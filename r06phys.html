6·phys · physics

Can a scale be perfectly in tune?

Can a scale be perfectly in tune? You tune a bass by ear: two notes that should agree, and a slow wah, wah, wah of loudness when they don't, which stops when you get it right. Every musician who has tuned an instrument has met a puzzle that took two thousand years to state cleanly. Tune every fifth pure, and the octaves come out wrong. Tune the thirds pure, and some fifths go sour. This page shows that the puzzle has no solution, and the reason is not acoustics or the ear. It is rung 4's technique that a number has only one prime list. The page sits on rung 6 because it is the next science page on the climb. Like the rung, it is about something that cannot be done, and about deciding what to do instead.

An interval is a ratio of frequencies, and stacking intervals multiplies their ratios. A pure fifth is threes over twos, and an octave twos alone; a number has only one prime list, so no stack of fifths ever lands on a stack of octaves. Every tuning gives something up; the only question is what.

Pitch is a frequency

A plucked string swings back and forth, and pushes the air as it goes; the pushes reach your ear as tiny swings of pressure. How many full swings, there and back, the string makes each second is its frequency. Frequency is what you hear as pitch: faster swinging, higher note.

From here on, the hertz, written Hz, is the unit of frequency: 1 Hz = 1/s, one full swing per second (5i's per). A string that swings 55 times each second has frequency 55 Hz.

For a string kept at one tension, the frequency is inversely proportional to the sounding length (5i's words). Half the length gives twice the frequency; two thirds of the length gives three halves of the frequency. The law of strings was set out in 1636. That is the whole of the monochord, a single string over a sliding bridge, the instrument on which the Greeks worked out their music.

The names of notes are a convention, and this page needs one line for them.

From here on, on this page, a note is named by a letter A to G, perhaps with ♭ (“flat”) after it, and a low number, as in A₄ or E₁. The letters and flats name the keys of a piano in order, A, B♭, B, C, D♭, D, E♭, E, F, G♭, G, A♭, and again. The low number says which run of those keys the note sits in. It is counted from the piano's lowest C, and goes up by one at each C.

On this page these capitals name notes, not statements or sets.

By international agreement, A₄, the A above the piano's middle C, is 440 Hz. Your bass's A string, A₁, is three octaves lower, and each octave down halves the frequency, so A₁ is 440 · ½ · ½ · ½ = 55 Hz, exactly.

An interval is a ratio, and stacking multiplies

The ear does not hear differences of frequency. It hears ratios. 55 Hz up to 110 Hz is a difference of 55 Hz. 220 Hz up to 440 Hz is a difference of 220 Hz. Both sound like the same step, because both are “times 2”.

From here on, the interval from a note of frequency f up to a note of frequency g is the ratio g : f, written as the fraction g/f (5i). The octave is the interval 2/1; the fifth, 3/2; the fourth, 4/3; the just major third, 5/4.

These are the musicians' words, said of intervals. 5g's third, fourth and fifth, the fractions 1/3, 1/4 and 1/5, keep their meanings; what the word is said of says which.

On the monochord: stop the string at half its length and you hear the octave; at two thirds, the fifth; at three quarters, the fourth. On your A string those are 110 Hz, 82.5 Hz and 220/3 Hz ≈ 73.33 Hz. There is a legend that an ancient teacher heard these ratios in the weights of a smith's hammers. It comes from writers centuries after him, and the physics in it is wrong. What survives is the real discovery behind it: the intervals which sound smooth are ratios of small numbers. The accepted word for that smoothness is consonance, from the Latin consonare, “to sound together”.

Going up one interval and then another multiplies by one ratio and then the other. So stacking intervals is multiplying fractions (5b), and coming down an interval divides by it.

\tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac43 @@ a fifth, then a fourth on top of it
= \tfrac{3 \cdot 4}{2 \cdot 3} = \tfrac{12}{6} @@ fractions-of-rationals ii: tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms
= \htmlClass{new}{2} @@ an octave: a fifth and a fourth fill it exactly

The tempting illegal move: intervals feel as if they add, a fifth “plus” a fourth making an octave. The ratios do not add: 3/2 + 4/3 = 17/6, nowhere near 2. The ear adds what the numbers multiply.

Notes an octave apart share a letter, so musicians treat a note and its octaves alike. To fit a ratio into the octave is to bring it to a ratio r with 1 ≤ r < 2 by dividing by 2, or multiplying by 2, as many times as needed. Stacks of the same interval need powers of a fraction, which the ladder has so far written only as r² (5e). Rung 4's recursion gives them at once.

From here on, for a rational r and a number k, r^k names p(k). Here p : ℕ → ℚ is the one function with p(0) = 1 and p(S(k)) = p(k) · r (4c's recursion). Read it “r to the power k”. For k = 2 it is 5e's r · r. For a number r it agrees with 4f's power, by power-of-a-fraction below with q = 1 (since ) and 5b's naming line.

Power-of-a-fraction technique: for numbers p and q with q ≠ 0, and every number k, (p/q)^k = p^k/q^k, with p^k and q^k 4f's powers.

(p/q)^0 = 1 = 1/1 = p^0/q^0 @@ the line above at 0; 5b's naming; power-zero twice
\text{assume } (p/q)^k = p^k/q^k @@ the induction hypothesis
(p/q)^{S(k)} = (p/q)^k \cdot (p/q) @@ the line above, the step
\phantom{(p/q)^{S(k)}} = (p^k/q^k)(p/q) = (p^k \cdot p)/(q^k \cdot q) @@ the hypothesis; fractions-of-rationals ii (5g); q^k ≠ 0 by no-zero-product
\phantom{(p/q)^{S(k)}} = \htmlClass{new}{p^{S(k)}/q^{S(k)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ power-of-a-next (4f), carried by the naming lines; induction

So a stack of fifths is a power of 3 over a power of 2. Two fifths up from C is (3/2)² = 9/4, past the octave; fit it, 9/8, the whole step from C to D. Keep going, one fifth at a time, fitting when needed, and you visit every key of the piano's octave. Musicians call that run of notes, each a fifth above the last, fitted into one octave, the cycle of fifths. (Going up by fifths, musicians name the seventh note F♯, read “F sharp”. They name the ones after it with sharps too, ♯ after the letter. This page uses the piano key's other name, with flats.)

Twelve fifths and seven octaves

After twelve fifths the piano is back on C, seven octaves up. Is the stack of fifths really back?

(3/2)^{12} = 3^{12}/2^{12} @@ the power-of-a-fraction technique
\phantom{(3/2)^{12}} = 531441/4096 \approx 129.75 @@ 3¹² = 531441, 2¹² = 4096 (4f, by hand); 5g's rounding
2^7 = 128 @@ seven octaves
(531441/4096)/128 = 531441/(4096 \cdot 128) @@ fractions-of-rationals iv (5g): dividing by a number multiplies the bottom
\phantom{(531441/4096)/128} = \htmlClass{new}{3^{12}/2^{19} = 531441/524288} @@ powers-add-their-counts (4f): 2¹² · 2⁷ = 2¹⁹ = 524288
\phantom{(531441/4096)/128} \approx 1.0136 @@ 5g's rounding to four places: not 1

That leftover ratio is the twelve-fifths comma (Pythagorean). It is about a quarter of the step between neighbouring piano keys, and the ear hears it at once. Start on your open A string, 55 Hz, and climb twelve pure fifths: you arrive at 55 · 531441/4096 = 29229255/4096 Hz ≈ 7136.05 Hz. Seven octaves up is 55 · 128 = 7040 Hz. The two notes are about 96 Hz apart, and they were meant to be the same note.

Why it never closes

Twelve is a near miss. Perhaps a bigger stack of fifths lands exactly on some stack of octaves? It never does, and the proof is the heart of this page. In plain words: a stack of fifths is a number made of threes over a number made of twos; a stack of octaves is made of twos alone. If they were equal, one number would have two prime lists, one with threes in it and one without. 4i's one-prime-list says that cannot happen. No-closing-fifths technique: for numbers m and n with m ≠ 0, (3/2)^m ≠ 2^n.

\text{assume } (3/2)^m = 2^n,\ \ m \ne 0 @@ aiming to refute: m fifths land exactly on n octaves
3^m/2^m = 2^n/1 @@ power-of-a-fraction; 5b's naming line for 2ⁿ
3^m \cdot 1 = 2^m \cdot 2^n @@ cross-multiplying (5b): cross-multiply
3^m = \htmlClass{new}{2^{m + n}} @@ one-changes-nothing; powers-add-their-counts (4f); as numbers, since the naming lines are one-to-one (ℕ-inside-ℤ, ℤ-inside-ℚ)
m + n \ne 0 @@ m = S(j) for some j (zero-or-a-next), so m + n = S(j + n) by next-on-the-left for plus, and next-never-empty
\begin{aligned}&\text{prime lists: } 3, 3, \cdots, 3 \text{ for } 3^m\\ &\quad ;\ \ 2, 2, \cdots, 2 \text{ for } 2^{m+n}\end{aligned} @@ the product of a list of m threes is 3^m (4i's product line and power-of-a-next, by induction on m); each entry at least the next
\text{the two lists are one list} @@ one-prime-list (4i): 3^m = 2^{m+n} has only one prime list
3 = 2 @@ their first entries, which exist since m ≠ 0 and m + n ≠ 0
\bot @@ 3 = S(2) ≠ 2, by never-its-own-next (3a)
\htmlClass{new}{(3/2)^m \ne 2^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute

The same conclusion comes by dividing alone. 2 divides 2^{m+n}, since m + n ≠ 0, so it would divide 3^m, a product of threes. So by prime-divides-a-list it would divide 3. But 3 = 2 · 1 + 1 leaves remainder 1 (division-with-remainder, one-split). Either way the conclusion is permanent. It is not that nobody has found the right number of fifths; there is no right number to find. The near misses do get better: 53 fifths overshoot 31 octaves by only about a fifth of a percent, which is why some theorists divided the octave into 53 steps. None is ever exact.

Notice what the proof used and what it did not. It never used 12, or the ear. It used one thing about 3/2: its top has a prime, 3, that the octave lacks. So it proves more. A stack of m fifths up, fitted down j times, is 3^m/2^{m+j}, and it lands on the starting note only if 3^m = 2^{m+j}: the same column. Fifths down are the same with top and bottom turned about. No fitting, up or down, rescues it.

The third, and a second comma

The table's E, four fifths up, is 81/64 ≈ 1.2656. But the smoothest major third, the one a choir drifts into, is 5/4 = 1.25. A scale with 5/4 in it was built in the second century. A treatise of 1558 made such thirds its centre, claiming that the consonances come from the numbers one to six. That system is just intonation. How far apart are the two thirds?

From here on, p ÷ q, read “p divided by q”, is a second way to write p/q. It needs no earning: it is 5b's dividing under another mark, and claims nothing new.

\tfrac{81}{64} \div \tfrac54 = \tfrac{81}{64} \cdot \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac45} @@ fractions-of-rationals iv: multiply by the reciprocal
= \tfrac{324}{320} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{81}{80}} \approx 1.0125 @@ fractions-of-rationals ii; cancel 4 (scaling): the syntonic comma

No-just-third technique: for all numbers m and j, 3^m/2^j ≠ 5/4. Suppose they were equal. Cross-multiplying (cross-multiplying) gives 3^m · 4 = 2^j · 5. So 5 divides 3^m · 2 · 2, a product of a list of threes and twos. So by prime-divides-a-list, 5 divides 3 or 2. But by divisor-not-past a divisor of a number other than 0 is at most that number, while 5 is more than both. Refute. ∎ No stack of fifths, fitted any way, produces a 5.

The difference is audible, and you can compute what you hear. A plucked string sounds not only its own frequency f but also, more softly, 2f, 3f, 4g and so on: its harmonics. And two tones close in frequency, sounding together, swell and fade in loudness; the number of swells each second, the beats, is the difference of their frequencies in hertz. So take A₁ at 55 Hz and the key a major third above it, D♭₂ (musicians writing in A call it C♯). Just, it is 55 · 5/4 = 68.75 Hz; by fifths, 55 · 81/64 = 4455/64 Hz ≈ 69.61 Hz.

5 \cdot 55 = 275 @@ the 5th harmonic of A₁, in Hz
4 \cdot 68.75 = 275 @@ the 4th harmonic of the just third: the same. Smooth
4 \cdot \tfrac{4455}{64} = \tfrac{4455}{16} \approx 278.44 @@ the 4th harmonic of the third by fifths
\tfrac{4455}{16} - 275 = \tfrac{55}{16} \approx \htmlClass{new}{3.44} @@ beats per second: the restless third

So why not tune everything just? Because the comma moves somewhere else. Take a just C major scale: C 1, D 9/8, E 5/4, F 4/3, G 3/2, A 5/3, B 15/8. The fifth from D up to A is (5/3)/(9/8) = 40/27, and 40/27 ÷ 3/2 = 80/81: a whole syntonic comma flat. Play a chord on D in that tuning and it howls. Musicians call such an interval a wolf. The comma did not go away; it hid between D and A.

Giving the comma away

If no tuning makes every interval pure, the remaining freedom is where to put the error. Spread it out, and you have a temperament. The one that won is equal temperament: make all twelve steps between neighbouring keys the same ratio, so that twelve of them stack to exactly 2. No fraction does that, as 6·phys.4.1c asks you to prove with no-closing-fifths's method. Rung 8 builds the number this needs.

That step was worked out to many places in China in 1584, and in the Netherlands around 1605, and tables for it were printed in France in 1636. Equal temperament keeps fifths almost pure, lets the thirds be rough, and in return every key sounds the same. The frets of your bass are placed for it. One of them needs nothing new: the 12th fret sits at exactly half the string, a pure octave.

Running it backwards: from two sounds to their interval

Given the interval, the page finds the note. Given two notes, run it back. Divide the higher frequency by the lower, and write the fraction in its lowest terms (5b). Fit it into the octave if it is 2 or more, and read the name off the list.

82.5\ \text{Hz and } 55\ \text{Hz} @@ two strings sounding together
82.5/55 = 165/110 = \htmlClass{new}{3/2} @@ divide; clear the decimal (5g); lowest terms
\text{a fifth} @@ the list: 3/2 is the fifth
220\ \text{Hz and } 82.5\ \text{Hz:}\ \ 220/82.5 = 8/3 @@ more than 2: wider than an octave
8/3 \cdot \tfrac12 = \htmlClass{new}{4/3} @@ fit: divide by 2 once. An octave and a fourth

And from an interval and its top note to its bottom note, divide: a fourth below A₂ at 110 Hz is 110 · 3/4 = 82.5 Hz, your E string. Running it backwards is how a tuner works: it hears a ratio and tells you how far from the name you are.

The open strings are a stack of fourths, E up to A up to D up to G: the cycle of fifths run backward. A common way to tune is by harmonics. Touch the E string lightly a quarter of the way along, near the 5th fret, and it sounds its 4th harmonic. Touch the A string a third of the way along, near the 7th fret, and it sounds its 3rd. With a tuner's E these are 4 · 41.20 ≈ 164.81 Hz and 3 · 55 = 165 Hz. That is about 0.19 beats per second, one slow swell every five seconds or so. Tune that beat away and you have made the fourth pure, 4/3, and E₁ becomes 41.25 Hz. Do the same from A up to D and from D up to G. Then G₂ ends at 55 · (4/3)² = 880/9 Hz ≈ 97.78 Hz, about 0.22 Hz flat of the fretted world's 98.00. Small, but chords up the neck will hear it. The pure intervals and the frets disagree because of a technique about primes.

Who said it first

The law of strings was set out by Marin Mersenne in 1636, and he printed the tables for equal temperament’s step; Zhu Zaiyu in China (1584) and Simon Stevin in the Netherlands (around 1605) had worked the step out first. The hammer legend is told of Pythagoras. The scale with 5/4 is Ptolemy’s; the treatise of 1558 is Gioseffo Zarlino’s.

Galileo, in the dialogue that founded modern physics, put the musical point plainly: what fixes an interval is not the length, thickness or tightness of the strings in themselves, but the ratio of the numbers of their vibrations, the pulses of air each sends to the ear in the same time; length and tension matter only because they change that number.

Galileo, Two New Sciences (1638), First Day, after Crew and de Salvio (1914)

Accepted names

The twelve-fifths comma is the Pythagorean comma; the 81/80 comma is the syntonic comma. Prime-divides-a-list is called Euclid’s lemma. Fitting a ratio into one octave is called octave reduction. The smoothness of intervals that are ratios of small numbers is consonance.
