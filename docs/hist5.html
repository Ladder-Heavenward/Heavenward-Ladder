The Foundations

Sets, logic, axioms for arithmetic and for geometry, and the first paradoxes that showed the foundations had cracks. On this page the question becomes what mathematics itself can and cannot prove.

75 climbers, in order of birth. Each of them climbed the same ladder you have climbed, and built part of it on the way. Under each name are the techniques they advanced, credited to them, and the climbers they met: who argued with whom, over what, and how it was resolved.

1850–1916Jørgen Pedersen Gram

A Danish actuary. In 1883 he straightened lists of functions and studied the grid that bears his name, fitting series by least squares.

Built into Functions Are Vectors With Lengths and Angles · The Closest Point · Straightening a Basis

1850–1925Oliver Heaviside

Self-taught telegraph engineer. He split the quaternion product into dot and cross with Gibbs, named impedance in 1886, and wrote Maxwell's equations in the form we use.

Advanced Heaviside’s impedance technique · Heaviside’s cross technique

Met Peter Guthrie Tait and Josiah Willard Gibbs over quaternions or vectors. Tait championed quaternions and attacked Heaviside's and Gibbs's vector methods. Resolved The vectors won; Tait came off worst. The notation of Gibbs's school became the standard one.

1851–1916Silvanus Thompson

Calculus Made Easy, the primer of 1910 that told its readers what one fool can do another can, is the ladder's companion on the Derivation floor.

Built into Polynomials and Rational Functions

1852–1939Ferdinand von Lindemann

Proved in 1882 that π is transcendental, which ended the quest to square the circle that Hobbes and the Greeks had joined.

Built into Constructions With Straightedge and Compass · An Angle Is an Arc; π · Two Famous Proofs · What Compass and Straightedge Can Build

Met Thomas Hobbes and John Wallis over squaring the circle. From 1655 Hobbes claimed proofs that squared the circle; Wallis refuted them, pamphlet after pamphlet, for more than twenty years. Resolved Hobbes withdrew some proofs but never the principle, and the quarrel ended only with his death. Lindemann settled the question itself in 1882: π is transcendental, so no straightedge and compass can square the circle.

1853–1932Wilhelm Ostwald

The chemist who with Mach doubted atoms and conceded in 1909 that their existence was proved.

Built into Why Does a Squeezed Gas Push Back? · Random Walks · Why Does Pollen Jiggle in Water?

Met Ludwig Boltzmann, Ernst Mach, Albert Einstein and Jean Perrin over whether atoms exist. Ostwald and Mach, energeticists, held that they do not; Boltzmann's theory of gases needed them, and he and Ostwald debated it at Lübeck in 1895. Resolved Einstein's theory of Brownian motion (1905) and Perrin's experiments on it (1908 to 1909) settled it. Ostwald accepted atoms in 1909; Boltzmann had died in 1906.

1854–1912Henri Poincaré

Took reasoning by recurrence to be a power of the mind that no logic could justify. He gave the disc and half-plane models of 1882, argued in 1902 that the choice of geometry is a convention, began topology, and found chaos in the three bodies.

Advanced Poincaré’s half-plane technique · Poincaré’s sphere-loops technique

1856–1922Andrey Markov

Chebyshev's student. He proved the stray-bound and introduced his chains in 1906, to show that the weak law of large numbers does not need independence.

Advanced Markov’s stray-bound technique · Markov’s share-of-time technique

1856–1927Carl Runge

Built the look-ahead steps of 1895 for statements on rates and showed in 1901 the wiggle at the ends of a fitted polynomial.

Advanced Runge’s look-ahead technique · Runge’s ends technique

1856–1894Thomas Stieltjes

Made asymptotic expansions rigorous in 1886, the year Poincaré did the same, and received the letter in which Hermite turned away from functions with no slope.

1856–1934Walther von Dyck

1857–1936Karl Pearson

Named the histogram in 1891, the correlation r in 1896 and the random walk in 1905, and built the chi-squared test; his quarrels with Fisher shaped a century of statistics.

Advanced Karl Pearson’s correlation

Met Ronald Fisher and Egon Pearson over Fisher's maximum likelihood, which Pearson criticised in 1917. Fisher answered in kind, and in 1919 would not take a post under him. Resolved Never settled between them. When Pearson retired in 1933 his department was split: Fisher took the Galton chair and Egon Pearson headed statistics.

1858–1932Giuseppe Peano

The marks ∈ and ∃ are his. He listed induction as an axiom in 1889, filled a square with a curve in 1890, and as a young editor in 1884 corrected the calculus textbooks with examples that still catch readers.

Advanced Peano’s induction technique · Peano’s multiplying · Peano’s many-futures technique · Peano’s square-filling technique

1858–1947Max Planck

The lump supposition of 1900, S = k log W written out, and the natural units of 1899.

Advanced Planck’s lump supposition · Planck’s gap-to-wavelength technique · Planck’s glow technique

Met Ludwig Boltzmann over whether the second law of heat is absolute. Planck held that it was. Resolved To find his radiation law in 1900, Planck had to accept Boltzmann's reading of the law as statistical, and used his methods; he called it an act of despair.

1859–1937Otto Hölder

1860–1940Vito Volterra

Built in 1881 the function whose slope exists everywhere, stays bounded, and yet has no Riemann area, and in 1926 wrote the predator and prey statements after a question about the sharks of the Adriatic.

Advanced Volterra’s loop-average technique

1862–1943David Hilbert

The axioms of geometry in 1899, the twenty-three problems of 1900, the rescue of Dirichlet's principle, the space of square-summable lists, the word eigen, the decision problem of 1928 with Ackermann, and the answer that taking excluded middle from the mathematician is like taking the telescope from the astronomer.

Advanced Hilbert’s two-sides-and-the-angle technique · Hilbert’s square-sum technique · Hilbert’s complete-closest technique · Hilbert’s half-power technique · Hilbert’s provable-mark technique

Met Leopold Kronecker, Georg Cantor and Richard Dedekind over whether completed infinities exist. Kronecker held that mathematics should rest on whole numbers and finite steps, and tried to stop Cantor's paper of 1877 in Crelle's Journal. Resolved Dedekind stepped in and the paper appeared, though Cantor never sent Crelle another. Set theory won: it was largely accepted, with Hilbert its strongest supporter.

Met Bernhard Riemann and Karl Weierstrass over the Dirichlet principle. Riemann took for granted that a least-energy function exists; Weierstrass showed in 1870 that such a least need not be reached. Resolved Hilbert later gave the principle a correct form, which made Riemann's proofs rigorous.

Met Georg Cantor, Kurt Gödel and Paul Cohen over the continuum hypothesis. Cantor proposed it and could not prove it; Hilbert put it first among his problems in 1900. Resolved Gödel showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved, and Cohen in 1963 that it cannot be proved. Gödel wrote to Cohen that his proof was a delight to read.

Met L. E. J. Brouwer and Arend Heyting over the excluded middle and completed infinity. Brouwer refused the law of excluded middle for infinite collections; Hilbert called it essential. In 1928 Hilbert had Brouwer removed from the board of Mathematische Annalen. Resolved Each side kept its ground. Heyting, Brouwer's student, carried intuitionism on and wrote its logic down as a formal system, so both logics can now be climbed side by side.

Met Kurt Gödel and Gerhard Gentzen over whether mathematics can prove itself consistent by finite means, Hilbert's programme. Resolved Gödel's theorems of 1931 showed it cannot in the original form. Gentzen proved arithmetic consistent in 1936 by a stronger induction, up to ε₀, and opened the proof theory that came after.

1871–1937Ernest Rutherford

Measured the falling activity of radioactive substances from 1900, spoke of a half-life by 1907, and counted flashes from radium with Geiger in 1910.

Built into How Sour Is It, and How Long Until Half Is Gone? · How Many Misprints on a Page? · Densities · Why Does Pollen Jiggle in Water? · How Does Heat Creep Into the Ground? · Why Can't Two Electrons Share a Place?

Met William Thomson and Charles Darwin over the age of the Earth. Thomson's estimate from cooling (1862) gave far too little time for Darwin's natural selection, and he opposed Darwin. Resolved Radioactivity overturned the estimate: the Earth has a heat source Thomson left out, and Thomson opposed Rutherford's radioactivity too.

1871–1928Ernst Steinitz

1871–1953Ernst Zermelo

The first list of axioms for sets, in 1908, with infinity and an empty set assumed outright; the axiom of choice that Borel, Baire and Lebesgue doubted; and the successor that takes x to {x}.

Advanced Zermelo’s infinity axiom · Zermelo’s non-empty technique

Met Bertrand Russell and Gottlob Frege over Frege's Basic Law V. Russell's letter of 16 June 1902, arriving as Frege's second volume was at the printer, showed that the law gives a contradiction. Resolved Frege conceded in an appendix that the law was false. Russell built his theory of types, and Zermelo's axioms of 1908 let a set be cut only from a set already given.

Met Kurt Gödel and Paul Cohen over the axiom of choice. Zermelo's proof of 1904 that every set can be well ordered rested on it, and many rejected the proof as non-constructive. Resolved Zermelo answered his critics in 1908 and gave set theory axioms. Gödel (1938) and Cohen (1963) showed that choice can neither be refuted nor proved from the other axioms.

1879–1955Albert Einstein

The photon and the grains of 1905, with the statement x² = 2Dt; Mercury's extra turning explained in 1915; and the argument with Bohr, from 1927 to his death, over whether the cloud is all there is.

Advanced Einstein’s photoelectric technique · Einstein’s frozen-shake technique · Einstein’s falling-room technique

Met Christiaan Huygens, Isaac Newton, Thomas Young and Léon Foucault over what light is. Huygens argued in 1678 that light is a wave; Newton held it to be a stream of particles, and in England few dared contradict Newton. Resolved Young explained interference by waves in 1803, against severe criticism. Foucault's measurement in 1850, that light is slower in water than in air, was the last nail in the particle theory's coffin. Einstein's light quanta of 1905 brought particles back beside the wave.

Met Ludwig Boltzmann, Wilhelm Ostwald, Ernst Mach and Jean Perrin over whether atoms exist. Ostwald and Mach, energeticists, held that they do not; Boltzmann's theory of gases needed them, and he and Ostwald debated it at Lübeck in 1895. Resolved Einstein's theory of Brownian motion (1905) and Perrin's experiments on it (1908 to 1909) settled it. Ostwald accepted atoms in 1909; Boltzmann had died in 1906.

Met Niels Bohr over whether quantum mechanics is complete. Einstein had grave doubts about Bohr's interpretation and challenged it with thought experiment after thought experiment. Resolved Bohr answered each one, and his view prevailed with most physicists.
