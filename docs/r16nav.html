16·nav · navigation

Where Am I Now?

Where am I now? For most of the history of the sea, a ship out of sight of land had no way to see its place. Its crew could measure only how fast it was going, and for how long. So they asked the question every watch, and answered it by adding up: so many knots for so many hours from the last place they were sure of. What makes it hard is that the adding never corrects itself. A small error in the speed is added in again every hour, and a ship can be sure of its sums and miles from where they say. This page reads the old method as 16c: the place is the area under the speed. Then it proves how fast an error in speed, or in the push of the engines, grows into an error in place.

The place now is the place at the start plus the area under the speed since. If the measured speed is always within a bound of the true one, the place is within that bound times the hours. If the measured push is within a bound of the true one, the place is within half that bound times the square of the hours. An unseen current moves the ship by its speed times the hours, and the log never shows it.

Place from speed

At sea, distance is counted in nautical miles, and speed in knots. A knot is one nautical mile an hour, and a nautical mile is 1852 m. Let a boat keep one course, a line drawn on the chart from its start. Let s(t) be its distance along that line after t hours, and v(t) its speed.

Dead-reckoning technique: where you are is where you started, plus the area under your speed. If v is continuous on [0, T], then s(T) = s(0) + ∫₀ᵀ v(t) dt. It is net-change (16·then), with Q = s. Its accepted name is dead reckoning.

Out of Monterey harbour, the course to Santa Cruz runs about 22 nautical miles across the bay. A navigator reads the log every half hour and writes the speed in the logbook.

\text{knots, read each half hour from } t = 0:\ 6.8,\ 7.4,\ 7.9,\ 7.6,\ 7.2,\ 6.9,\ 7.0 @@ seven readings over 3 hours
\text{left sum}: 0.5\,(6.8 + 7.4 + 7.9 + 7.6 + 7.2 + 6.9) = 21.9 \text{ nmi} @@ strip sums (15h): each half hour at its first reading
\text{right sum}: 0.5\,(7.4 + 7.9 + 7.6 + 7.2 + 6.9 + 7.0) = 22.0 \text{ nmi} @@ each half hour at its last reading
s(3) - s(0) \approx 22 \text{ nmi} @@ dead-reckoning: the boat should be off Santa Cruz

The old log was a board on a long line. Thrown over the stern, the board stayed put in the water while the line ran out. Knots tied along the line at equal spacing were counted as they ran out over a sand-glass of a few seconds. The spacing was set so that the number of knots counted was the speed in nautical miles an hour, which is why the unit is called a knot.

Errors that grow

A log never reads the true speed. Its reading u(t) differs from the true v(t) by a little: weed on the line, a gust, a sea running under the stern. Write e(T) for the error in place after T hours: the reckoned place less the true one.

Speed-error technique: a steady doubt in speed becomes a doubt in place that grows with the time run. Let u and v be continuous on [0, T], with the same start. Let |u(t) − v(t)| ≤ M for every t there. Then |e(T)| ≤ MT.

e(T) = \big(s(0) + \textstyle\int_0^T u\big) - \big(s(0) + \int_0^T v\big) @@ dead-reckoning, once with the log and once with the truth
= \textstyle\int_0^T \big(u(t) - v(t)\big)\,dt @@ areas-add (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{|e(T)| \le M\,T} \quad \blacksquare @@ area-bound (16a), with |u − v| ≤ M on [0, T]

The bound grows with T and nothing in the sums pulls it back. A log that reads 0.2 knots high may put the boat 0.2 nautical miles out after an hour, which no one would notice. After a day it may be 4.8 out. A fix, a place seen and measured, from a landmark, a star or a satellite, starts the sums again from a sure place.

Worse, the log measures the water flowing past the boat, not the ground beneath it. A current carries the water and the boat together, and the log does not see it. A current of c knots along the course for T hours moves the boat cT that no reckoning shows. A current across the course does the same sideways. The boat's distance across the line is its own area: ∫₀ᵀ c(t) dt, the area under the current's speed across.

\text{a current of } 0.5 \text{ kn along the course, } 10 \text{ h} @@ the log reads speed through the water
e(10) = -\textstyle\int_0^{10} 0.5\,dt = -5 \text{ nmi} @@ dead-reckoning: the true speed is u + 0.5; the boat is 5 nmi further on than reckoned
\text{a current of } 0.4 \text{ kn across the course, } 5 \text{ h}:\ 2 \text{ nmi off the line} @@ the across distance is ∫₀⁵ 0.4 dt

Run backwards twice

A submarine or an airliner can carry an instrument that feels its own push, its acceleration, with no log and no window. Its speed is the area under the acceleration, and its place is the area under the speed: 16·phys's two steps. Then the doubt is run backwards twice.

Doubled-error technique: run backwards twice, a small doubt grows with the square of the time. Let the measured and true accelerations be continuous on [0, T], within M of each other, with speed and place right at the start. Then the speed is out by at most Mt at each time t, and the place by at most MT²/2.

e_v(t) = \textstyle\int_0^t (a_m - a) @@ dead-reckoning one step down: the speed's error, with e_v(0) = 0
-Mt \le e_v(t) \le Mt @@ area-bound (16a)
e(T) = \textstyle\int_0^T e_v(t)\,dt @@ dead-reckoning again: the place's error, with e(0) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{-\tfrac{MT^2}{2} \le e(T) \le \tfrac{MT^2}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ smaller-function-smaller-area (15e); ∫₀ᵀ Mt dt = MT²/2 (the table)

Let the push be measured to within 0.001 m/s², about a ten-thousandth of the pull of the Earth. After an hour, 3600 s, the place may be out by ½ · 0.001 · 3600² = 6480 m, about 3.5 nautical miles. After ten hours the bound is a hundred times that. So such instruments are reset by fixes too, often.

Honda Point, 1923

On the evening of 8 September 1923, fourteen destroyers of the United States Navy steamed south along the Central Coast at 20 knots, in fog, bound for San Diego. Their place came from dead reckoning. Near Point Arguello they turned east, believing they had passed it and were entering the Santa Barbara Channel. They had not. They were north of their reckoning, and the turn took them onto the rocks at Honda Point. Seven destroyers were lost, and 23 sailors died.

At 20 knots, a doubt of half a knot, from the log or from a current, grows to several nautical miles over a day's run. That is the width of the passage they were aiming for.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

Dead reckoning is this principle put to work. A navigator out of sight of land never sees the boat's place change. She sees only its rate, on the log, and adds it up. Every instant of the passage is a middle between the last sure place and the next one, and it is described by a speed. The before and after are found from the middle, not the other way round. The price is that every doubt in the rate is added up too.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

A fix measures a before or an after. Reckoning fills the middle. A navigator needs both, and trusts neither alone for long.

A boat lies at anchor for an hour in a tidal current of 1 knot. Its log, which counts the water flowing past, reads 1 knot all hour. How far has the boat moved?

Who said it first

Bourne described the log: a piece of board with a line fastened to it, let fall over the stern so that it stays where it falls, the line let run out while a sand-glass or a counted time passes, and the length run out measured to give how far the ship goes in an hour.

William Bourne, A Regiment for the Sea (1574); paraphrase

Knots tied along the line came into use later, with a sand-glass timed to match their spacing. The nautical mile of 1852 m was agreed internationally in 1929.

In 16·nav.4.4, the navigator of the squadron was Lieutenant Commander Donald T. Hunter, aboard the flagship Delphy, under Captain Edward H. Watson. The bearings came from the radio direction-finding station at Point Arguello. A naval court found the squadron's commander and its navigator to blame; the losses at Honda Point remain the largest peacetime loss of ships in the history of the United States Navy.

Accepted names: dead reckoning (sometimes said to come from "deduced reckoning", a story with no early evidence); the chip log, or common log; set and drift for a current's direction and speed; inertial navigation for the instrument that feels its own push.
