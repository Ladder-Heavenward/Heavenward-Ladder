Do the Primes Hold Every Pattern?

Do the primes hold every pattern? A run is a list of numbers equally spaced, like 3, 5, 7 or 5, 11, 17, 23, 29. Colour every counting number red or blue, any way at all, and one colour holds runs of three; that much was settled in 1927, for runs of every length and any number of colours. A question of 1936 asked for more: does every set that holds a fixed share of the numbers, however small, hold runs of every length? For runs of three the answer came in 1953, by reading a set through the turnings of rung 32 on a clock, and for every length in 1975. The primes hold no fixed share of the numbers; among the first million, fewer than one in twelve is prime, and the share keeps falling. In 2004 they were shown to hold runs of every length anyway, by placing them as a dense set inside a larger set that behaves as if it were random. How large a set can be with no run of three at all is still argued: a construction of 1946 makes such sets surprisingly large, a proof of 2023 from two computer scientists brought the ceiling down almost to it, and the gap between them is open. This page proves the 1927 result for two colours and runs of three, builds the analysis of a set by turnings on a clock, and proves the 1953 result in full.

Every red and blue colouring of 1, …, 325 has a one-colour run of three, by blocks of five. On the clock of M, the turnings χ_r(x) = ω^(rx) are all the maps that turn plus into times, and Σ_r |ĝ(r)|² = M Σ_x |g(x)|². The count of runs of three in A is (1/M) Σ_r Â(r)² Â(−2r). A set of share δ with no run of three has a turning r ≠ 0 against which it is unbalanced by δ²N/12, so it is denser by δ²/48 on a run of length about δ√N; repeat, and the share would pass 1. So a set with no run of three has share at most about 1/log log N; since 2023, at most exp(−c (log N)^(1/12)). Here ω (little omega) means the turn by one hour of the clock of M.

sky17.1A Run of Three in Every Colouring

A run of three is x, x + d, x + 2d with d ≥ 1. 42j1 found that any colouring of the links among six people holds a one-colour triangle. The same kind of claim holds for runs. The colouring R R B B R R B B of 1, …, 8 has no one-colour run of three (a computer check of all 256 colourings of 8 finds four such, and of all 512 colourings of 9 finds none). So 9 is the least length that forces a run, for two colours. A bound with room to spare can be proved by hand.

Block-pattern technique: every red and blue colouring of 1, …, 325 holds a one-colour run of three.

\text{cut } 1, \ldots, 325 \text{ into } 65 \text{ blocks of } 5;\ \text{a block has } 2^5 = 32 \text{ colour patterns} @@ choices in stages multiply (4k6)
\text{two of blocks } 1, \ldots, 33 \text{ share a pattern: blocks } i \lt j \le 33 @@ the pigeonhole technique (08·cb5): 33 blocks, 32 patterns
\text{in block } i\text{, two of places } 1, 2, 3 \text{ share a colour, say red: } p \lt p' \le 3;\ p'' = 2p' - p \le 5 @@ three places, two colours
\text{place } p'' \text{ red in block } i\text{: a red run inside block } i\text{; else } p'' \text{ is blue in blocks } i \text{ and } j @@ the same pattern
k = 2j - i \le 65:\ \ \text{place } p'' \text{ of block } k \text{ red gives } (i, p), (j, p'), (k, p'') \text{ red} @@ steps 5(j − i) + (p′ − p) each
\htmlClass{new}{\text{blue gives } (i, p''), (j, p''), (k, p'') \text{ blue: a run either way}} \quad \blacksquare @@ steps 5(j − i)

Nesting the same idea, with blocks of blocks, proves the claim of 1927 in full: for every length and every number of colours, a long enough stretch of numbers forces a one-colour run of that length. It is held in trust here; for runs of three and any number of colours it also follows from sky17.6, since some colour holds at least its share of the numbers.

sky17.2Turnings on a Clock

On the clock ℤ/M of 43a2, let ω = e^(2πi/M), as in 32k1. For each hour r, the map χ_r(x) = ω^(rx) is well defined on hours, since ω^M = 1. It turns plus into times: χ_r(x + y) = χ_r(x) χ_r(y). These are the only such maps, and they are the stepped turnings of 32k1, now read as maps on the clock.

Clock-turnings technique: the maps χ from ℤ/M to the complex numbers other than 0 with χ(x + y) = χ(x)χ(y) are exactly the M maps χ_r(x) = ω^(rx), r = 0, …, M − 1; and Σ_x χ_r(x) χ_s(x)* = M when r = s, and 0 otherwise, with * the mirror (conjugate).

\chi(0) = \chi(0 + 0) = \chi(0)^2 \Rightarrow \chi(0) = 1 @@ χ(0) ≠ 0
\chi(1)^M = \chi(1 + 1 + \cdots + 1) = \chi(0) = 1 @@ M ones make the hour 0
\chi(1) = \omega^r \text{ for some } r \in \{0, \ldots, M - 1\} @@ the M-th roots of 1 are the powers of ω (21·cb20)
\chi(x) = \chi(1)^x = \omega^{rx} = \chi_r(x) @@ x ones
\textstyle\sum_x \chi_r(x)\chi_s(x)^* = \sum_x \omega^{x(r - s)} @@ the mirror of ω^(xs) is ω^(−xs)
\htmlClass{new}{= M \text{ if } r = s,\ \ 0 \text{ otherwise}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the geometric sum of 32k1

On the clock of 4, ω = i, and the four turnings take the hours 0, 1, 2, 3 to (1, 1, 1, 1), (1, i, −1, −i), (1, −1, 1, −1) and (1, −i, −1, i). Any two different rows, one mirrored, multiply and add to 0; for instance 1 + i·(−1)* + (−1)(1) + (−i)(−1)* = 1 − i − 1 + i = 0. The accepted name of a turning on a clock is a character.

sky17.3Energy on a Clock

For a function g on the clock, write ĝ(r) = Σ_x g(x) ω^(−rx), the stepped transform of 32k1 read on hours. A set A gives its indicator, 1 on A and 0 off it, and Â for short. The sizes of the coefficients add up to the size of the function, as 32e4 found for the transform on the line.

Clock-energy technique: Σ_r |ĝ(r)|² = M Σ_x |g(x)|²; so for a set A, Σ_r |Â(r)|² = M |A|, and at most M|A|/λ² hours r have |Â(r)| ≥ λ.

|\hat g(r)|^2 = \hat g(r)\hat g(r)^* = \textstyle\sum_x \sum_y g(x) g(y)^* \omega^{-r(x - y)} @@ the mirror of a sum is the sum of mirrors
\textstyle\sum_r |\hat g(r)|^2 = \sum_x \sum_y g(x) g(y)^* \sum_r \omega^{-r(x - y)} @@ finite sums swap
= \textstyle\sum_x g(x) g(x)^* \cdot M @@ the clock-turnings technique: only y = x survives
\#\{r : |\hat A(r)| \ge \lambda\} \cdot \lambda^2 \le \textstyle\sum_r |\hat A(r)|^2 = M|A| @@ a set’s indicator has |g|² = g
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_r |\hat g(r)|^2 = M \sum_x |g(x)|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clock-energy technique

Take A = {0, 1} on the clock of 4. Â(r) = 1 + ω^(−r) = 1 + (−i)^r gives 2, 1 − i, 0, 1 + i, with sizes squared 4, 2, 0, 2. They add to 8 = 4 · 2. Â(0) is always |A|; the other coefficients measure how unevenly A sits against each turning. If A were spread like coin tosses they would all be near √|A|; a coefficient near |A| is a turning that A follows.

sky17.4Counting Runs of Three by Turnings

Let P = {0, 1, …, N − 1} and put P on the clock of M = 2N + 1, an odd number. For x, y, z in P, x + z − 2y lies strictly between −2N and 2N, so x + z ≡ 2y on the clock only when x + z = 2y: then x, y, z is a run (with d = y − x, read either way), or x = y = z. The clock sees runs and nothing else. Write Λ(g₁, g₂, g₃) for the sum of g₁(x) g₂(y) g₃(z) over all x, y, z on the clock with x + z = 2y.

Run-tally technique: Λ(g₁, g₂, g₃) = (1/M) Σ_r ĝ₁(r) ĝ₃(r) ĝ₂(−2r); and for P = {0, …, N − 1}, Λ(1_P, 1_P, 1_P) ≥ N²/2.

[x + z - 2y = 0] = \tfrac1M \textstyle\sum_r \omega^{r(x + z - 2y)} @@ the clock-turnings technique, with s = 0
\Lambda = \tfrac1M \textstyle\sum_r \Big(\sum_x g_1(x)\omega^{rx}\Big)\Big(\sum_z g_3(z)\omega^{rz}\Big)\Big(\sum_y g_2(y)\omega^{-2ry}\Big) @@ finite sums swap
= \tfrac1M \textstyle\sum_r \hat g_1(-r)\hat g_3(-r)\hat g_2(2r) = \tfrac1M \sum_r \hat g_1(r)\hat g_3(r)\hat g_2(-2r) @@ r ↦ −r runs over every hour
\Lambda(1_P, 1_P, 1_P) = \#\{(x, z) \in P^2 : x + z \text{ even}\} = \lceil\tfrac N2\rceil^2 + \lfloor\tfrac N2\rfloor^2 @@ y = (x + z)/2 is then in P
\htmlClass{new}{\ge \tfrac{N^2}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ u² + v² ≥ (u + v)²/2

With N = 4, M = 9 and A = {0, 1, 3}: A has no run of three, so Λ(1_A, 1_A, 1_A) counts only the three triples x = y = z, and the sum of Â(r)² Â(−2r) over the nine hours, divided by 9, gives exactly 3 (checked by script). For P = {0, 1, 2, 3} the count is 2² + 2² = 8 = N²/2. A set with no run has a count as small as its size. A set of share δ spread like coin tosses would have about δ³N²/2. The next footing reads that gap through the turnings.

sky17.5A Large Coefficient

Let A ⊆ P have |A| = δN and no run of three. Split its indicator into the even part and the rest: 1_A = δ1_P + f, where f = 1_A − δ on P and 0 off it. Then Σ f = 0, so f̂(0) = 0, and Σ |f| = 2δ(1 − δ)N ≤ 2δN. Let μ be the largest |f̂(r)|. Since M is odd, r ↦ −2r runs over every hour, so the clock-energy technique holds for ĝ(−2r) as for ĝ(r).

Large-coefficient technique: if A ⊆ {0, …, N − 1} has |A| = δN, no run of three, and N ≥ 4/δ², then |f̂(r)| ≥ δ²N/12 for some hour r ≠ 0.

|A| = \Lambda(1_A, 1_A, 1_A) = \delta^3\Lambda(1_P, 1_P, 1_P) + \Lambda(f, 1_A, 1_A) + \delta\Lambda(1_P, f, 1_A) + \delta^2\Lambda(1_P, 1_P, f) @@ swap one slot at a time; Λ is linear in each slot
|\Lambda(f, g, h)| \le \tfrac1M\,\mu\,\big(\textstyle\sum_r |\hat g|^2\big)^{1/2}\big(\sum_r |\hat h|^2\big)^{1/2} = \mu\,\big(\sum|g|^2\big)^{1/2}\big(\sum|h|^2\big)^{1/2} @@ the run-tally technique, the largest |f̂| out, the product-bound technique (31a) on the rest; the clock-energy technique
|\Lambda(f, 1_A, 1_A)| \le \mu\delta N,\ \ \delta|\Lambda(1_P, f, 1_A)| \le \delta\mu\sqrt{\delta}\,N,\ \ \delta^2|\Lambda(1_P, 1_P, f)| \le \delta^2\mu N @@ Σ|1_A|² = δN, Σ|1_P|² = N; f may sit in any slot
\delta N \ge \tfrac{\delta^3 N^2}{2} - 3\delta\mu N \Rightarrow \mu \ge \tfrac{\delta^2 N}{6} - \tfrac13 @@ the run-tally technique for P
N \ge 4/\delta^2 \Rightarrow \tfrac{\delta^2 N}{12} \ge \tfrac13 @@ so μ ≥ δ²N/6 − δ²N/12
\htmlClass{new}{|\hat f(r)| \ge \tfrac{\delta^2 N}{12} \text{ for some } r \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ f̂(0) = 0

With δ = 0.1 and N = 10⁶: a set of 100 000 numbers below a million with no run of three must have a turning r on the clock of 2 000 001 against which its balanced part sums to at least 833 in size. A set spread like coin tosses would have every |f̂(r)|, r ≠ 0, near √(δN) ≈ 316. Having no run is a kind of order, and the turnings see it.

sky17.6A Denser Piece, Again and Again

A turning ω^(−rx) that f follows can be cut into pieces on which it barely moves. Then f, which sums to 0 over P, sums to something positive on some piece: A is denser there. The piece is a run of numbers, and a run with no run of three inside it is a smaller copy of the same problem.

Denser-piece technique: if A ⊆ {0, …, N − 1} has |A| = δN, no run of three, and N ≥ (1300/δ)², then there is a run Q of numbers, of length at least δ√N/2600, on which A has share at least δ + δ²/48.

K = \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor:\ \text{some } 1 \le q \le K \text{ has } \|qr/M\| \le 1/K @@ the pigeonhole technique: K + 1 values qr/M, q = 0, …, K, in K slots of the circle; ‖t‖ is the distance to a whole number
\text{cut each class of } P \text{ on dividing by } q \text{ into runs of step } q\text{, lengths between } L \text{ and } 2L,\ L = \lfloor\delta\sqrt N/1300\rfloor @@ each class has at least K ≥ L members
|\omega^{-r(x_0 + jq)} - \omega^{-rx_0}| \le 2\pi j\,\|qr/M\| \le 4\pi L/K \le \delta/48 \text{ for } j \lt 2L @@ a turning by angle 2πt moves a point at most 2π‖t‖; K ≥ √N/2
\tfrac{\delta^2N}{12} \le |\hat f(r)| \le \textstyle\sum_j \big|\sum_{Q_j} f\big| + \tfrac{\delta}{48}\sum_P |f| \le \sum_j \big|\sum_{Q_j} f\big| + \tfrac{\delta^2N}{24} @@ the large-coefficient technique; compare each term with its piece’s first; Σ|f| ≤ 2δN
\textstyle\sum_j \big(\big|\sum_{Q_j} f\big| + \sum_{Q_j} f\big) \ge \tfrac{\delta^2}{24}\sum_j |Q_j| @@ Σ over all pieces of Σ f is 0; Σ|Q_j| = N
\big|\textstyle\sum_{Q} f\big| + \sum_Q f \ge \tfrac{\delta^2}{24}|Q| \text{ for some piece } Q @@ the some-outcome technique (∞16), pieces weighted by size
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_Q f \ge \tfrac{\delta^2}{48}|Q|:\ \ |A \cap Q| \ge (\delta + \tfrac{\delta^2}{48})|Q|} \quad \blacksquare @@ |s| + s is 2s or 0

Runs-of-three technique: if N ≥ (2600/δ)^(4^(1 + 96/δ)), every set of at least δN numbers in {1, …, N} holds a run of three. Map Q onto {0, …, |Q| − 1} by x ↦ (x − x₀)/q: runs go to runs, so A ∩ Q has no run either. Repeat. Each round adds at least δ²/48 to the share, so the share doubles within 48/δ rounds, doubles again within 24/δ, and so on: it would pass 1 within 96/δ rounds. A round needs only length N ≥ (1300/δ)², and while N ≥ (2600/δ)⁴ the new length, at least δ√N/2600 (a whole part ⌊t⌋ is at least t/2 when t ≥ 1), is at least N^(1/4). From the starting N, after 96/δ rounds the length is still at least N^(4^(−96/δ)) ≥ (2600/δ)⁴. So the rounds cannot stop, and the share would pass 1: totally false.

Read backwards: a set with no run of three in {1, …, N} has share below about C/log log N. At N = 10⁶ that bound says nothing useful; the technique is a statement about the very large, and how large is the question of the last footings.

sky17.7Sparse Sets, Spheres and the Primes

How small must the share be? From below, a construction of 1946 builds large sets with no run of three, from spheres. Write numbers in base 10 with digits 0 to 4, so that adding two never carries.

Sphere-digits technique: if every number of S has digits only 0 to 4 in base 10, and the digits of each have the same sum of squares, then S holds no run of three.

x + z = 2y \text{ with } x, y, z \in S @@ suppose a run x, y, z
u + w = 2v \text{ digit by digit} @@ u, v, w the digit lists; digits ≤ 4 never carry, and 2y’s digits are 2v’s
|v|^2 = \big|\tfrac{u + w}{2}\big|^2 = \tfrac{|u|^2 + |w|^2}{2} - \tfrac{|u - w|^2}{4} = |v|^2 - \tfrac{|u - w|^2}{4} @@ the same sum of squares for u, v, w (18’s distance)
\htmlClass{new}{u = w,\ \text{so } x = y = z} \quad \blacksquare @@ no run with d ≥ 1

Three digits with squares adding to 17: S = {14, 41, 104, 140, 223, 232, 322, 401, 410}, nine numbers below 500 with no run of three (checked by script). With many digits and the base chosen with care, this gives sets of share about exp(−c√(log N)): far below any fixed share, and far above 1/log log N. In February 2023 two computer scientists proved that a set with no run of three has share at most exp(−c (log N)^(1/12)), and later in 2023 the 1/12 was raised to 1/9; a preprint of March 2026 claims 1/6, a claim this page has not revisited. The ceiling now has the same shape as the 1946 floor; only the power of log N differs.

Two more results are told and not proved. In 1975 every fixed share was shown to hold runs of every length, by an argument that cuts a set into pieces that behave regularly; a proof in 2001 extended this page’s turnings to longer runs. In 2004 the primes, though their share falls like 1/ln N (43g), were shown to hold runs of every length: they are placed as a set of fixed share inside a larger set, the almost-primes, that behaves like a random set in the ways that matter, and the 1975 result is carried over to it. The longest run of primes actually found, in 2019, has 27 terms.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞17.oq.1Must a set whose reciprocals add up without bound hold runs of every length? The primes are such a set (43g), and they do, by the 2004 proof. For runs of three, every such set holds one: proved in 2020, by pushing the ceiling of sky17.6 below 1/log N. For runs of four or more it is open.
∞17.oq.2How large can a set with no run of three be? The 1946 spheres give share about exp(−c (log N)^(1/2)); the ceiling since 2023 is exp(−c (log N)^(1/9)). Which power of log N is the truth? Many expect the spheres are close to best, and nobody has a proof either way.
∞17.oq.3Do the primes hold every pattern, or only runs? The 2004 proof gives runs; it does not give p and p + 2 both prime endlessly often, the twin pattern that 43·now left open with gaps of at most 246. Which patterns of primes are forced, and which can only be counted on average?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The claim about colourings was proved in 1927, a few years before the party problem of 42j was asked; it is one of the first results of the study of order forced in disorder. In 1936 two mathematicians asked whether colour mattered at all: perhaps any set with a fixed share of the numbers holds long runs. If so, the colouring claim follows, since some colour has at least its share.

In 1946 a construction of sets with no run of three, from points on a sphere, showed that such sets can be far larger than a first guess suggests, and in 1953 the case of runs of three was proved with turnings, the argument of this page. Longer runs resisted turnings, and when the general case was proved in 1975 it was by an intricate combinatorial argument. In 1977 a second proof appeared from an entirely different direction, the theory of dynamical systems.

In 2001 the argument by turnings was extended to runs of every length, with new measures of how evenly a set is spread. In 2004 two analysts combined these ideas with a set built from the methods of number theory, one that behaves like a random set and holds the primes as a fixed share, and proved that the primes hold runs of every length. The proof was posted in April 2004 and published in 2008. Searches by computer have kept pace only slowly: the longest run of primes known, found in 2019, has 27 terms, and a search in 2025 was still looking for 28.

For runs of three, the bounds crept. Every ceiling proved before 2023 had the shape N divided by a power of log N, while the 1946 floor did not move in its essential shape. In 2020 the logarithm was finally beaten, which settled the reciprocal question of ∞17.oq.1 for runs of three.

Then in February 2023 a graduate student at the University of Illinois and a computer scientist at UCLA, two people from computer science, posted a proof bringing the ceiling down to the shape of the 1946 floor. Within months the power was improved from 1/12 to 1/9 by the pair who had beaten the logarithm in 2020. A preprint of March 2026 claims 1/6 and has not been revisited here. What is argued now is whether the 1946 floor is the truth, with power 1/2, or whether still larger sets with no run of three exist; the gap between the proved powers and 1/2 is open.

A note on the record: the 2026 preprint has been seen in its arXiv listing, but its claim of 1/6 has not been checked against a second source for this page.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The claim of 1927 is van der Waerden’s theorem, and 9 is the van der Waerden number W(3; 2); the block-pattern technique is the standard proof of W(3; 2) ≤ 325. Runs are arithmetic progressions. The turnings on a clock are the additive characters of ℤ/M, the clock-turnings technique is their classification and orthogonality, and the clock-energy technique is Parseval’s (Plancherel’s) identity. The run-tally technique is the Fourier expression of the count of three-term progressions, and the large-coefficient and denser-piece techniques are the two halves of the density increment argument; the runs-of-three technique is Roth’s theorem, with Dirichlet’s approximation theorem from the pigeonhole technique. The sphere-digits technique is Behrend’s construction. The question of 1936 is the Erdős–Turán conjecture, proved as Szemerédi’s theorem; the 2001 measures are the Gowers norms; the 2004 result is the Green–Tao theorem, by a transference principle; ∞17.oq.1 is the Erdős conjecture on arithmetic progressions.

Who said it first

Bartel van der Waerden proved the colouring claim in 1927. Paul Erdős and Pál Turán asked the density question in 1936. Felix Behrend gave the sphere construction in 1946. Klaus Roth proved the case of three in 1953. Endre Szemerédi proved the general case in 1975, and Hillel Furstenberg gave the ergodic proof in 1977. Timothy Gowers gave the Fourier proof for every length in 2001. Ben Green and Terence Tao proved that the primes hold runs of every length in 2004. Thomas Bloom and Olof Sisask beat the logarithm in 2020 and improved the power to 1/9 in 2023. Zander Kelley and Raghu Meka proved the 1/12 bound in 2023. The 2026 preprint claiming 1/6 is by Rushil Raghavan.

A proof that is not allowed

“The primes have share 1/ln N, and the runs-of-three technique needs a share above about 1/log log N; since ln N grows faster than log log N, use the technique for larger and larger N.” Not allowed: the share of the primes falls below the share the technique needs, not above it. A fixed share is what every density argument spends. The primes need a different idea: a dense set inside a set that looks random.
