9·bio · biology

Is a cow just a big mouse?

Is a cow just a big mouse? A mouse weighs about 20 grams and a cow about 600 kilograms, some thirty thousand times as much. If a cow were simply a big mouse, every rate in its body (heat made, food burned, oxygen breathed) would be thirty thousand times a mouse's. It is not, by a long way. The rule that says how far off it is turns out to be one of the cleanest patterns in biology: a power of the body's mass with a fractional exponent, the three-quarters law (Kleiber's). This page is about what such a function does, and how to work with it using nothing but ratios of powers.

A power law has one habit: multiply the mass by some factor, and the output is multiplied by that factor raised to the law’s power, wherever you start. The three-quarters law’s power is three quarters, so per kilogram the rate falls as the mass grows: small animals burn hotter.

Metabolism, and its units

An animal's metabolism is the whole of the chemistry by which it burns food to stay alive. Its rate is measured by the heat it makes. A warm-blooded animal (a mouse, a cat, a person) keeps its body at a steady warmth whatever the weather, and at rest it makes heat as fast as it loses it. The basal metabolic rate is the rate measured at rest, warm, and some hours after a meal: what it costs just to stay alive. Biologists measure it in kilocalories per day.

From here on, a kilocalorie, written kcal, is the unit of energy 4184 J (7·phys's joule): the dietary calorie, about what it takes to warm 1 kg of water by 1 °C. A rate in kcal/day becomes watts by multiplying by (4184 J)/(1 kcal) and (1 day)/(86 400 s), two ways of writing 1 (5i).

What a power law does

From here on, a power law is a rule found by measuring that fits a function f : (0, ∞) → (0, ∞) with f(M) = a · M^b, for a positive real a and a rational b. Being measured, it is a law of nature, not a technique: the techniques on this page are about the function it fits. Here M^b is 8i's fractional power, and b is its exponent.

The study of how a living thing's measurements scale with its size by power laws is called allometry, and b is called the scaling exponent.

Power-law habit technique: for a power law f and positive reals k and M, f(kM) = k^b f(M); so f(M₂)/f(M₁) = (M₂/M₁)^b.

\tfrac{f(M_2)}{f(M_1)} = \tfrac{a M_2^b}{a M_1^b} = \htmlClass{keep}{\left(\tfrac{M_2}{M_1}\right)^{b}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the a cancels (fractions-of-rationals i read in ℝ); fraction-exponent techniques for a power of a quotient

That is the whole personality of a power law. Multiplying the mass by 2 always multiplies the output by the same factor, 2^b, whether you go from mouse to rat or from sheep to cow. Only the ratio of masses matters, and the constant u drops out of every comparison. The tempting illegal move is u(kM)^b = k · uM^b, as if the factor came out unchanged. It comes out as k^b. Only for b = 1, 5i's direct proportion, does doubling the mass double the output.

The three-quarters law

In 1932 an animal scientist at the University of California at Davis gathered the best measurements of basal metabolism he could trust, from a dove to a steer. Instead of assuming an exponent, he fitted one. It came out near 0.74. In 1947 he refitted on more animals and got about 0.756. He settled on a working rule still quoted: B ≈ 70 M^(3/4) kcal/day, with M the mass in kilograms. Some rows from his 1947 compilation, with what the rule predicts:

Across a range of masses of about thirty thousand, one formula with one exponent lands within about 7 percent for every row. The ratios of power-law habit make the rule easy to use by hand, when the mass ratio is a power you can undo. 16 = 2⁴, so 16^(3/4) = 2³ = 8: sixteen times the mass, eight times the rate. 10 000 = 10⁴, so 10 000^(3/4) = 10³: ten thousand times the mass, a thousand times the rate. A 16 kg animal is predicted at 70 · 8 = 560 kcal/day (the 14.1 kg dog measured 534), and a 256 kg one at 560 · 8 = 4480.

The proposal was to measure an animal’s metabolic size by its body weight to the three-quarters power. The heat production of mature animals of very different sizes, from rat to steer, was nearly proportional to it. It was more nearly so than to the two-thirds power that the older surface rule required.

An older rule, the surface law, said the exponent should be ⅔. Two French scientists argued it in 1838, and a German physiologist's measurements on dogs of different sizes in 1883 seemed to bear it out. Its reason comes from comparing an animal's skin with its bulk. That needs the geometry of rung 18 and the areas of rung 15, so this page can only compare the exponents. For a mass ratio of a million, ⅔ predicts 10⁶^(2/3) = 10⁴ times the rate. And ¾ predicts 10⁶^(3/4) = 10^(9/2) = 10⁴ · √10 ≈ 31 600 times. The two laws part company by a factor of √10 ≈ 3.16. The 1932 and 1947 data sit with ¾. Why ¾ is still argued. The leading explanations involve the branching networks that carry blood, and it is an open question, not settled science.

Per kilogram, and the mouse's appetite

Divide by the mass and the rule says how hot each kilogram of tissue runs.

\tfrac{B}{M} = \tfrac{70\,M^{3/4}}{M^{1}} @@ divide both sides by M (multiplying-keeps-answers by M⁻¹)
\phantom{\tfrac{B}{M}} = 70\,M^{\htmlClass{new}{-1/4}} @@ fraction-exponent techniques: 3/4 − 1 = −1/4
\tfrac{(B/M)_{\text{mouse}}}{(B/M)_{\text{woman}}} = \left(\tfrac{57.2}{0.021}\right)^{1/4} \approx \htmlClass{keep}{7.2} @@ power-law habit with b = −1/4: the minus turns the ratio over

The table agrees: 171 against 24 kcal per kg per day, a ratio of about 7.2. A gram of mouse burns about seven times what a gram of woman burns. In watts, the woman's 1368 kcal/day is 1368 · 4184/86 400 ≈ 66 W: about a dim lamp, all day, lying still. The mouse's 3.6 kcal/day is about 0.17 W. Per kilogram, that is about 1.2 W/kg against 8.3 W/kg. The rule's 70 kcal/day is about 3.4 W, so for a 70 kg person the rule predicts 3.4 · 70^(3/4) ≈ 82 W. The quarter power turns up elsewhere too, roughly. A mouse's heart beats some 600 times a minute and an elephant's some 30, a ratio of 20. Their masses, about 25 g and 4 tonnes (8·phys's tonne), stand in the ratio 160 000, whose fourth root is exactly 20.

Notice the shape of the per-kilogram law: a power law with a negative exponent. By 9e's reading of 1/x and the powers, it falls as M grows and never reaches 0. Going from a 21 g mouse to a 210 g rat, ten times the mass, cuts the rate per kilogram by 10^(1/4) ≈ 1.78. Going from a 60 kg person to a 600 kg cow, also ten times, cuts it by exactly the same factor. That sameness is power-law habit again.

Finding an exponent with whole-number comparisons

The three-quarters law was found by a method this ladder has not built yet. With only rung 8's powers you can still pin an exponent between fractions, and the argument is worth having. First, a base above 1 sorts its powers. Power-grows technique: for a real K > 1 and rationals b < c, K^b < K^c. K^c = K^b · K^(c−b) by fraction-exponent techniques. And K^(p/q) > 1 for positive integers p, q. Its q-th root ^q√K is above 1: if it were at most 1, so would its q-th power K be, by powers-keep-order. And a power of a real above 1 is above 1 (powers-keep-order). ∎ So a ratio of masses and a ratio of rates fix the exponent. If 16^b = 8, then b = ¾, since 16^(3/4) = 8 and power-grows allows no second b.

Bracket-an-exponent technique: for reals K, L > 1 and positive integers p, q, K^(p/q) < L exactly when K^p < L^q. Both sides are positive. Raising to the q-th power keeps order both ways (powers-keep-order and its converse), and (K^(p/q))^q = K^p by fraction-exponent techniques. ∎ So to ask whether a pair of animals has b above p/q, compare two whole-number powers of their ratios: no root is ever taken. Take the mouse and the cow: K = 600/0.021 ≈ 28 571 and L = 7877/3.6 ≈ 2188.

L = K^b,\ \ K \approx 28\,571,\ \ L \approx 2188 @@ power-law habit: the rate ratio is the mass ratio to the power b
\tfrac23:\ \ K^2 \approx 8.2 \cdot 10^8 \lt L^3 \approx 1.05 \cdot 10^{10} @@ bracket-an-exponent: K^(2/3) < L = K^b, so 2/3 < b (power-grows)
\tfrac34:\ \ K^3 \approx 2.33 \cdot 10^{13} \gt L^4 \approx 2.29 \cdot 10^{13} @@ bracket-an-exponent the other way: K^(3/4) > K^b, so b < 3/4
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac23 \lt b \lt \tfrac34},\ \text{just below } \tfrac34 @@ the two comparisons barely differ at ¾: b is very near it

The surface law's ⅔ is not even close for this pair: K² falls short of L³ by more than ten times. The mouse and the sheep, K ≈ 2210 and L ≈ 348, give K³ ≈ 1.08 · 10¹⁰ < L⁴ ≈ 1.47 · 10¹⁰. So for them b is a little above ¾. Each pair brackets the exponent; the picture brackets every pair you choose. Biologists draw these data on axes that turn every power law into a linear function whose step is the exponent. Running it backwards

On (0, ∞) a power law with b ≠ 0 is one-to-one, so 9d runs it backwards, and 9f says how. Undo the constant, then raise to the reciprocal power.

B = 70\,M^{3/4} @@ metabolism from mass
\tfrac{B}{70} = M^{3/4} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by 1/70
M = \htmlClass{new}{\left(\tfrac{B}{70}\right)^{4/3}} @@ raise both sides to 4/3: (y^(3/4))^(4/3) = y for y > 0 (fraction-exponent techniques)

Who said it first

The animal scientist at Davis was Max Kleiber, and the three-quarters law is Kleiber’s law. The surface law was argued by Pierre Sarrus and Jean-François Rameaux in 1838, and Max Rubner’s measurements on dogs in 1883 seemed to bear it out.

Kleiber proposed measuring an animal's metabolic size by its body weight to the three-quarters power, since the heat production of mature animals of very different sizes, from rat to steer, was nearly proportional to it, and more nearly so than to the two-thirds power that the older surface rule required.

Kleiber, “Body size and metabolism”, Hilgardia 6 (1932); and “Body size and metabolic rate”, Physiological Reviews 27 (1947)
