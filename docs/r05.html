Rung 5 · theory

Making room to undo

Rung 4 built the counting numbers and their arithmetic. Adding and multiplying never get stuck there: any two numbers have a sum and a product. Running them backwards does get stuck. No number added to 3 gives 0, and no number multiplied by 2 gives 1. This rung makes new numbers that answer those questions: the integers, which take away anything from anything, and the fractions, which divide anything by anything other than 0. It makes them out of nothing but pairs, and rung 2's tool for counting many things as one. Then it proves that the new numbers obey twelve techniques. From the rules alone come the facts usually announced without reason: anything times 0 is 0, two negatives multiply to a positive, and dividing by 0 is not just forbidden: there is no answer for it to give. It ends with a tension: the fractions are crowded, with another between any two, and yet they have holes.

Name the missing answer by the question that asks for it: a pair of numbers, standing for “what must be added to the second to make the first”. Count as one all the pairs that ask the same question. Define adding and multiplying on the pairs, and check that the answer never depends on which pair of a class you picked. Then do it again with multiplying in place of adding.

What it means to say every subtraction has an answer

A subtraction is a question about adding, asked backwards. “Five take away three” asks: what must be added to 3 to make 5? Among the counting numbers the answer is 2. 4g's at-most-is-adding technique says when such an answer exists: exactly when 3 ≤ 5. Ask it the other way, what must be added to 5 to make 3, and the counting numbers have nothing to offer. Adding to a number never takes it below where it started. The plainest case is 3 + x = 0. 5a proves, one move per line, that no number x does it: 3 + x is a next one, and 0 is nobody's next.

To say every subtraction has an answer is to say this. There is a collection of numbers, containing the counting numbers, with adding and multiplying that agree with rung 4's. In it, every question “what must be added to b to make k?” has exactly one answer. Not an answer by courtesy, not a rule that says “write a minus sign”. It is an object, built from the objects already in hand, that does the job, and a proof that it does.

One of the first to build numbers this way put the situation in a few sentences. Adding and multiplying can always be carried out, but their reverses, taking away and dividing, cannot. It was this limit that led people to create the negative numbers and the fractions.

Where the new numbers come from

The trick has three steps, and you will see it twice. First, name the answer by the question. The pair (5, 3) will stand for “what must be added to 3 to make 5”, and the pair (3, 5) for the question the counting numbers could not answer. A pair is a thing rung 2 can build, so the question has become an object.

Second, count as one all the pairs that ask the same question. (3, 5), (0, 2) and (10, 12) all ask for the same missing amount, so they must make one new number, not three. Saying “the same missing amount” seems to need the very taking-away you lack. The way round it is to move everything to one side, where only adding happens: (k, b) and (c, d) ask the same question exactly when k + d = b + c. That condition uses nothing but rung 4's adding. It is a sameness relation in 2e's sense, so it cuts the pairs into classes that never partly overlap. Each class is one integer.

Third, define adding and multiplying on the classes, by doing it to a pair from each. Then prove that the answer's class does not depend on which pairs you picked. This is the step people skip, and it is the one that makes the whole thing honest. A class has many members, and an operation that gave different answers for different members would not be an operation on classes at all. 5a has a tempting rule that fails exactly here.

Then do it all again. A division is a question about multiplying, asked backwards: what must 2 be multiplied by to make 1? The integers have no answer, and 5b proves it. So take pairs of integers, (1, 2) for that question. Count two pairs as one when they cross-multiply to the same thing. The classes are the fractions. One pair must be refused: no pair whose second entry is 0. Let even one such pair in and the counting-as-one stops sorting: everything is counted as one with everything else. That is the first face of the forbidden division, met while building, before any rule has been written down.

What you can do because you know it

Once the fractions are built, a list of twelve techniques can be checked for them. Adding and multiplying each ignore order and brackets. Adding 0 and multiplying by 1 change nothing. Every number has a negative that adds to it to give 0. Every number other than 0 has a reciprocal that multiplies with it to give 1. Multiplying spreads over adding. Every number is 0, positive, or the negative of a positive, exactly one. And positives added or multiplied stay positive. 5c proves each rule for the fractions by working on pairs, every line resting on a technique that rung 4 proved by induction. The list itself is the one a well-known calculus book sets at its start.

After that, nothing about pairs is needed. The rules are the whole licence. Every move you make with numbers and letters is one of the twelve techniques or a chain of them. That goes for collecting terms, spreading a product, taking something from both sides, or putting two fractions over one bottom. So the question “is this move allowed?” always has the same shape: which rule is it, and is its small print met? 5d shows what the rules force on their own, so that it is true in any system that obeys them. Anything times 0 is 0, a negative times a negative is a positive, and no number multiplied by 0 gives 1. That last fact is why dividing by 0 cannot be patched. Any rule that tried would break a rule, and 5d shows which.

And you can see the shape of the new numbers. Between any two fractions there is a third, so no fraction has a next one, as every counting number did. You would think a collection so crowded could have no gaps. 5e proves it does. Rung 4's no-double-square technique, read as a statement about fractions, says that no fraction multiplied by itself gives 2. Rung 8 fills the holes.

Every one of these is a beginning. People who have worked with fractions for fifty years still meet questions about them they cannot answer.

And 5·eng asks how gears change speed, and answers by counting teeth: a gear ratio is a fraction, a gear in the middle cancels, and gears on one shaft multiply. It carries a box on laws with a known exception rate.

The rest of the rung

The rung's second part makes the hands fast. 5f is doing the same change to both sides of a sameness. It gives the licensed techs for finding a missing number, run forwards and backwards, and the illegal ones: above all, dividing both sides by something that might be 0. 5g is fractions by hand: adding and multiplying them quickly, and putting them over one bottom. It also writes some of them with digits after a dot, the decimals. 5h is expanding and factoring: spreading a product over a sum, and running the spread backwards to find what was multiplied. 5i is ratio, proportion and units. There the cross-multiplying that defines a fraction turns out to be the whole theory of proportion. It ends with the benchmark that the rung's ordinary rules are complete. 5·chem counts atoms by weighing them, the mole and the balancing of reactions. 5·bio is the ratios of the pea crosses, where counting peas gave three to one and the fractions explained why. 5·cb2 calls back to rung 2: sameness relations and their classes seen again, now with the integers and the fractions as the worked examples.

At the end of the rung's first part you can take away anything from anything, divide anything by anything but 0, and know why each move is allowed. You know where the negatives and the fractions came from: classes of pairs, sorted by a relation that uses only adding, or only multiplying, of the numbers below. And you know one thing the fractions cannot do, which is the reason for rung 8.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 5d carries a box of the second kind of care, the kind that measures how wrong it might be instead of proving: laws with a known exception rate. A rule comes with its range and the direction it fails past the edge, as dividing does at 0. And 5a carries a box on the ledger: a negative number is not a thing you can count, but the entry that balances the books between two counts, and it exists along a series.

Who said it first

The few sentences on taking away and dividing are Richard Dedekind’s. The list of twelve techniques is the one Michael Spivak sets at the start of his Calculus. The pea crosses are Gregor Mendel’s.

Dedekind put the situation in a few sentences: adding and multiplying can always be carried out, but their reverses, taking away and dividing, cannot, and it was this limit that led people to create the negative numbers and the fractions.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), §I, trans. Beman, 1901

Kronecker is reported to have said, in a lecture, that the whole numbers were made by God and that everything else is the work of people. The remark reaches us second-hand, in a memoir written by Heinrich Weber after Kronecker's death, so it is given here as the report of a remark, in paraphrase. On this ladder even the counting numbers were made by hand, on rung 4, and this rung makes the rest from them.

reported in Weber, “Leopold Kronecker”, Jahresbericht der Deutschen Mathematiker-Vereinigung 2 (1893)
