Making room to undo

Rung 4 built the counting numbers: counting as an object, with adding, multiplying and powers as its motion, repeated. Run forwards, those motions never get stuck. Run backwards, they do: no number added to 3 gives 0, and no number multiplied by 2 gives 1. This rung builds no new kind of thing. It puts rung 2’s tools to work on rung 4’s statements. An integer is one of 2g’s classes: a name made of pairs of numbers, sorted by a sameness that uses only 4f’s adding. A fraction is the same move again, with 4g’s multiplying in place of adding. Then the new numbers are shown to obey twelve techniques, many of them rung 4’s carried over. From those alone come the facts usually announced without reason: anything times 0 is 0, two negatives multiply to a positive, and dividing by 0 has no answer to give. It ends with a tension: the fractions are crowded, with another between any two, and yet they have holes.

You know this one too. A lift has buttons below the ground floor, and a thermometer reads below zero. You split a pizza into halves, and a half into quarters, without anyone telling you what a fraction is. This rung only writes down what you already use, with the tools you built on rungs 2 and 4.

Pack the backward question into a name: a pair of numbers, sorted into 2g’s classes by a sameness made of adding. Work on any member, and check that the class of the answer stays put. Then run the same move with multiplying.

What it means to say every subtraction has an answer

A subtraction is a question about adding, asked backwards. “Five take away three” asks: what must be added to 3 to make 5? Among the counting numbers the answer is 2. Ask it the other way, what must be added to 5 to make 3, and the counting numbers have nothing to offer. The plainest case is 3 + x = 0, and 5b1 proves that no number x does it.

To say every subtraction has an answer is to say this. There is a collection of numbers, containing the counting numbers, with adding and multiplying that agree with rung 4's. In it, every question “what must be added to b to make k?” has exactly one answer. Not an answer by courtesy, not a rule that says “write a minus sign”. It is an object, built from the objects already in hand, that does the job, and a proof that it does.

One of the first to build numbers this way put the situation in a few sentences. Adding and multiplying can always be carried out, but their reverses, taking away and dividing, cannot. It was this limit that led people to create the negative numbers and the fractions.

Where the new numbers come from

The trick has three steps, and you will see it twice. First, name the answer by the question: the pair (3, 5) stands for the question the counting numbers could not answer (5b2). Second, count as one all the pairs that ask the same question, by a condition that uses nothing but rung 4's adding (5b3); each class is one integer. Third, define adding and multiplying on the classes by working on a member of each, and prove that the answer's class does not depend on the member picked (5b8). That third step is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes the whole thing honest.

Then do it all again. A division is a question about multiplying, asked backwards: what must 2 be multiplied by to make 1? The integers have no answer, and 5c proves it. So take pairs of integers, counted as one when they cross-multiply to the same thing: the classes are the fractions. One pair must be refused, any pair whose second entry is 0, and 5c5 shows that letting even one in stops the sorting. That is the first face of the forbidden division, met while building.

What you can do because you know it

Once the fractions are built, the twelve techniques of arithmetic can be checked for them (5d1): nine about adding and multiplying, three about which numbers are positive. 5d proves each by working on pairs, every line resting on a technique that rung 4 proved by induction.

After that, nothing about pairs is needed. The rules are the whole licence. Every move you make with numbers and letters is one of the twelve techniques or a chain of them. That goes for collecting terms, spreading a product, taking something from both sides, or putting two fractions over one bottom. So the question “is this move allowed?” always has the same shape: which rule is it, and is its small print met? 5e shows what the rules force on their own, so that it is true in any system that obeys them. Anything times 0 is 0, a negative times a negative is a positive, and no number multiplied by 0 gives 1. That last fact is why dividing by 0 cannot be patched. Any rule that tried would break a rule, and 5e shows which.

And you can see the shape of the new numbers. Between any two fractions there is a third, so no fraction has a next one, as every counting number did. You would think a collection so crowded could have no gaps. 5f proves it does. Rung 4's no-double-square technique, read as a statement about fractions, says that no fraction multiplied by itself gives 2. Rung 8 fills the holes.

Every one of these is a beginning. People who have worked with fractions for fifty years still meet questions about them they cannot answer.

And 5·eng asks how gears change speed, and answers by counting teeth: a gear ratio is a fraction, a gear in the middle cancels, and gears on one shaft multiply. It carries a box on laws with a known exception rate.

The rest of the rung

The rung's second part makes the hands fast. 5g is doing the same change to both sides of a sameness. It gives the licensed techs for finding a missing number, run forwards and backwards, and the illegal ones: above all, dividing both sides by something that might be 0. 5h is fractions by hand: adding and multiplying them quickly, and putting them over one bottom. It also writes some of them with digits after a dot, the decimals. 5i is expanding and factoring: spreading a product over a sum, and running the spread backwards to find what was multiplied. 5j is ratio, proportion and units. There the cross-multiplying that defines a fraction turns out to be the whole theory of proportion. It ends with the benchmark that the rung's ordinary rules are complete. 5·chem counts atoms by weighing them, the mole and the balancing of reactions. 5·bio is the ratios of the pea crosses, where counting peas gave three to one and the fractions explained why. 5·cb2 calls back to rung 2: sameness relations and their classes seen again, now with the integers and the fractions as the worked examples.

At the end of the rung's first part you can take away anything from anything, divide anything by anything but 0, and know why each move is allowed. You know where the negatives and the fractions came from: classes of pairs, sorted by a relation that uses only adding, or only multiplying, of the numbers below. And you know one thing the fractions cannot do, which is the reason for rung 8.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 5e carries a green box: laws with a known exception rate. A rule comes with its range and the direction it fails past the edge, as dividing does at 0. 5a carries a box on loose, then locked: ≈, about equal, lets a line state a guess together with how far it may miss, and later tools lock it tighter. And 5b carries a box on the ledger: a negative number is not a thing you can count, but the entry that balances the books between two counts, and it exists along a series.

Who said it first

The few sentences on taking away and dividing are Richard Dedekind’s. The list of twelve techniques is the one Michael Spivak sets at the start of his Calculus. The pea crosses are Gregor Mendel’s.

Dedekind put the situation in a few sentences: adding and multiplying can always be carried out, but their reverses, taking away and dividing, cannot, and it was this limit that led people to create the negative numbers and the fractions.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), §I, trans. Beman, 1901

Kronecker is reported to have said, in a lecture, that the whole numbers were made by God and that everything else is the work of people. The remark reaches us second-hand, in a memoir written by Heinrich Weber after Kronecker's death, so it is given here as the report of a remark, in paraphrase. On this ladder even the counting numbers were made by hand, on rung 4, and this rung makes the rest from them.
