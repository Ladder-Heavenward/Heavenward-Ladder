8a · proof

A Real Number Is a Cut

5f left a hole in the fractions: a clean split into a lower part and an upper part with nothing at the split. This step turns the lower part of such a split into a number. A real number will be a set of fractions with four properties, the properties every lower part has. The set of all of them is the reals, which the ladder will call the line. Order between reals is rung 2's oldest relation, being a part of. Every fraction sits among the reals as the set of fractions below it, with its order kept. And the hole of 5f is a cut too, one that belongs to no fraction: a new number.

A real number is the set of the fractions below it. One real is at most another when its fractions are among the other's.

8a1The Hole, Again

5f7 sorted every fraction into two heaps: the lower holds the fractions that are negative or square to less than 2, the upper the rest, which by no-fraction-squares-to-2 are the positive fractions squaring to more than 2. No-greatest-below showed that the lower heap has no greatest member. What is new here is that everything in the lower heap is below everything in the upper heap, proved below for any heap of this kind as the members-below-non-members technique. So the split is clean, and at the split there is no fraction: 5f's hole, which 5f promised rung 8 would fill.

8a2The Turn: The Split Is the Number

The old move was to stop looking for a number at the split and to let the split itself be the number. Whenever a splitting of the fractions is produced by no fraction, the inventor said, we create a new number, and regard it as completely fixed by the splitting.

A splitting is fixed by its lower heap: the upper heap is just the fractions left over. So the ladder takes the lower heap alone and asks what makes a set of fractions the lower heap of a split. Four things. It has something in it. It does not have every fraction, so the upper heap is not empty. It is closed downward: whatever is below a member is a member. And it has no greatest member, a choice that keeps one fraction from being written twice.

8a3What a Cut Is

From here on, a cut is a set α ⊆ ℚ with four properties. (i) α has a member: ∃r (r ∈ α). (ii) α misses some fraction: ∃s ∈ ℚ (s ∉ α). (iii) α is closed downward: ∀r ∈ α ∀s ∈ ℚ (s < r ⇒ s ∈ α). (iv) α has no greatest member: ∀r ∈ α ∃s ∈ α (r < s).

The < inside the conditions is 5d's order on fractions. A cut is used by unpacking: (i) and (ii) are somes, used by calling a member; (iii) and (iv) are everys, used with any member you have. A cut is made by proving all four, which this step does three times.

From here on, α (the Greek letter alpha, in its small form) names a cut, a real number. β (beta, in its small form) names another, and σ (sigma, in its small form) a third, when a third is needed. Small Greek letters for reals are a habit, like rung 0's small letters for things, not a technique. The capital Σ of 4k is a different mark and keeps its job.

8a4The Set of All Cuts, and Its Name

A cut is a part of ℚ, so it is a member of 𝒫(ℚ), the set of all the parts of ℚ (2g's Power). The condition “is a cut” is a statement about a set, built from ∈, < and rung 1's words. So, by Separation, there is a set of all of them:

From here on, ℝ names the set {α ∈ 𝒫(ℚ) : α is a cut}. Its members are the real numbers, or reals. Read it “R”, or “the reals”.

From here on, the line is the ladder's name for ℝ with the order defined below: “on the line” means “in ℝ”. No geometry is meant. The line is a word for the ordered set ℝ, and its picture is a picture of order: less to the left, greater to the right. The shape that shares the name waits for geometry, on rung 18. A line of a proof is still a line of writing.

Nothing has been assumed. By Power, Separation and same-members, from rung 2, ℝ is a set, and every real is a set of fractions. The pictures in this step set fractions out by size so the eye can follow the order; only left of and right of carry meaning in them.

8a5A First Technique: Members Sit Below Non-Members

Order on the fractions chains. 5d never wrote this down as a technique, so here it is. Chain technique for less on ℚ: x < y ∧ y < z ⇒ x < z.

a \lt b,\ \ b \lt c @@ given
b - a,\ \ c - b \text{ positive} @@ ﹀ 5d's <
(c - b) + (b - a) \text{ positive} @@ positive-sum on ℚ (5d)
(c - b) + (b - a) = c + ((-b) + b) + (-a) @@ regroup for plus on ℚ, turn-about for plus on ℚ (5d): regroup and commute
= c + 0 + (-a) = \htmlClass{new}{c - a} @@ negative on ℚ, plus-zero on ℚ; 5d's line for a − b
a \lt c \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap axiom on the positive line; ︿ <

Members-below-non-members technique: for a cut α, r ∈ α ∧ s ∉ α ⇒ r < s.

r \in \alpha,\ \ s \notin \alpha @@ given
s \lt r \lor s = r \lor r \lt s @@ any-two-compare on ℚ (5d): any two fractions compare, a proved or
\text{case } s \lt r:\ s \in \alpha,\ \bot @@ (iii) with the member r; clash with s ∉ α
\text{case } s = r:\ s \in \alpha,\ \bot @@ the swap axiom on r ∈ α; clash
\text{case } r \lt s:\ \htmlClass{new}{r \lt s} @@ the case itself
r \lt s \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases; the first two by everything permitted

So a cut and the fractions it misses are a lower and an upper heap: everything of the one below everything of the other. The either-way principle was not used: any-two-compare on ℚ's or is proved.

8a6Order Is Being a Part Of

From here on, for reals α and β, α ≤ β means α ⊆ β. Read it “α is at most β”. And α < β means α ≤ β ∧ α ≠ β (“α is less than β”). β ≥ α and β > α mean α ≤ β and α < β, as in 4h. Between reals these marks always mean this. Between fractions they keep 5d's meaning, and what stands on each side says which. Each of these marks is a statement with two gaps, as 0b's = is.

At-most-is-a-partial-order technique on ℝ: it is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive, in 6e's words. Reflexive: α ⊆ α is 2d's self-part. Antisymmetric: α ⊆ β and β ⊆ α give α = β by 2d's double-inclusion. Transitive: α ⊆ β and β ⊆ σ give α ⊆ σ by 2d's chain for part of. ∎ Three lines, each a technique of rung 2: order on the line is inherited, not invented. Whether any two reals compare is a different question, and 8b's.

8a7Each Fraction Is a Cut

From here on, for a rational r, r* names {s ∈ ℚ : s < r}, the set of the fractions below r. Read it “r star”, or “the cut of r”.

Fraction-cut technique: for every rational r, r* ∈ ℝ. It is a sift of ℚ, so a part of ℚ (sift-is-part). The four conditions, one at a time:

r - 1 \lt r @@ r − (r − 1) = 1, positive (times-one on ℚ, negative on ℚ and the techniques); 5d's <
\text{(i)}\ \ r - 1 \in r^{*} @@ ︿ the sift
\neg(r \lt r) @@ r − r = 0, and three-way on ℚ says 0 is not positive
\text{(ii)}\ \ r \notin r^{*} @@ ﹀ the sift: membership needs r < r
\text{(iii)}\ \ t \in r^{*},\ s \lt t \;\Rightarrow\; s \lt r,\ s \in r^{*} @@ ﹀; chain for less on ℚ; ︿ 
t \in r^{*} \;\Rightarrow\; t \lt \tfrac{t + r}{2} \lt r @@ another-between (5f), between t and r
\text{(iv)}\ \ \tfrac{t + r}{2} \in r^{*},\ \text{greater than } t @@ ︿ the sift; show an example
\htmlClass{new}{r^{*} \in \mathbb R} \quad \blacksquare @@ all four; ︿ the sift of 𝒫(ℚ)

Fractions-keep-order technique: for rationals r and s, r ≤ s ⇔ r* ≤ s*, and r < s ⇔ r* < s*; in particular r* = s* ⇔ r = s.

\text{assume } r \le s;\ \text{let } t \in r^{*} @@ to prove an if; chase a member
t \lt r,\ \text{so } t \lt s @@ ﹀; chain for less on ℚ if r < s, the swap axiom if r = s
t \in s^{*};\ \ r^{*} \subseteq s^{*} @@ ︿; t was anything; ︿ ⊆
\text{assume } r^{*} \subseteq s^{*} \text{ and } s \lt r @@ the other way; the second aiming to refute
s \in r^{*},\ \text{so } s \in s^{*},\ s \lt s,\ \bot @@ ︿; carry the ⊆ through; ﹀; ¬(s < s) as above
\neg(s \lt r),\ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{r \le s} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; any-two-compare on ℚ: the or of the other two

The sameness part follows by antisymmetry each side (at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ and 4h's ≤ on fractions with any-two-compare on ℚ), and the strict part by joining the two. So the cuts r* are a copy of the fractions, in the same order. 8d shows the copy adds and multiplies like the original, and only then gives the naming line that lets r stand for r*.

8a8A Real Is the Set of the Fractions Below It

Now the slogan becomes a technique. Members-are-the-fractions-below technique: for a real α and a rational r, r ∈ α ⇔ r* < α.

\text{assume } r \in \alpha;\ \text{let } t \in r^{*} @@ the rightward direction
t \lt r,\ \ t \in \alpha;\ \ r^{*} \subseteq \alpha @@ ﹀; (iii); ︿ ⊆
\text{if } r^{*} = \alpha:\ r \in r^{*},\ \bot @@ the swap axiom on r ∈ α; clash with fraction-cut's (ii)
r^{*} \ne \alpha;\ \ \htmlClass{new}{r^{*} \lt \alpha} @@ refute; join; ︿ <
\text{assume } r^{*} \subseteq \alpha \land r^{*} \ne \alpha @@ the leftward direction
\neg\forall t\,(t \in \alpha \Rightarrow t \in r^{*}) @@ else α ⊆ r*, and double-inclusion gives r* = α: clash; refute
\exists t\,(t \in \alpha \land t \notin r^{*}) @@ not-every, then negated-if forward (7e)
\text{call it } t:\ \neg(t \lt r),\ \text{so } r \le t @@ use the some; ﹀; any-two-compare on ℚ
\htmlClass{new}{r \in \alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ r = t: the swap axiom; r < t: (iii). Cases; the call closes

Read the rightward direction as the gesture of the step: every fraction in α is a fraction below α. The leftward one uses not-every, so it is classical: it finds a member of α beyond r without saying which.

8a9The Hole Is a Cut, and It Is No Fraction’s Cut

Let α = {r ∈ ℚ : r < 0 ∨ r² < 2}, the lower heap of the hole. Three small techniques first. Squares-keep-order technique on ℚ: 0 ≤ s < r ⇒ s² < r². If s = 0, then s² = 0 and r is positive, so r² is positive (positive-product on ℚ). If s is positive: s · s < r · s and r · s < r · r. Each is by 5f's keep-order for times on ℚ (multiply by a positive). Then use turn-about for times on ℚ and chain for less on ℚ. ∎

Hole's-step technique: for a rational r ≥ 0, with s = (2r + 2)/(r + 2): s − r = (2 − r²)/(r + 2) and s² − 2 = 2(r² − 2)/(r + 2)².

s - r = \tfrac{2r + 2}{r + 2} - \tfrac{r(r + 2)}{r + 2} @@ fractions-of-rationals i (5h): r over the same bottom; r + 2 positive, so not 0
= \tfrac{2r + 2 - r^2 - 2r}{r + 2} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{2 - r^2}{r + 2}} @@ fractions-of-rationals iii; spread on ℚ; collect
s^2 - 2 = \tfrac{(2r + 2)^2 - 2(r + 2)^2}{(r + 2)^2} @@ fractions-of-rationals ii and iii, over (r + 2)²
= \tfrac{4r^2 + 8r + 4 - 2r^2 - 8r - 8}{(r + 2)^2} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{2(r^2 - 2)}{(r + 2)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ square-of-a-sum (5i) twice; spread on ℚ; collect

This is 5f's no-greatest-below step: with r = p/q it is (2p + 2q)/(p + 2q). Read the two lines for sign. Below the hole, r² < 2: s − r is positive and s² − 2 is negative, so s is larger and still below. Above the hole, r² > 2: s − r is negative and s² − 2 is positive, so s is smaller and still above. Upward it is the climb 5f7 ran from 7/5; downward, from 3/2, it falls 10/7, 17/12, 58/41, 99/70. Neither heap has an end at the split. Written down, it is the no-least-above technique: r positive ∧ 2 < r² ⇒ ∃s (0 < s < r ∧ 2 < s²). Take s from hole's-step. Its top and bottom are positive, so s is positive. The signs of hole's-step's two lines do the rest, by negative-out on ℚ and positive-product on ℚ.

Hole's-heap-is-a-cut technique: α = {r ∈ ℚ : r < 0 ∨ r² < 2} is a real.

\text{(i)}\ \ -1 \lt 0,\ \text{so } -1 \in \alpha @@ add an option; ︿ the sift
\text{(ii)}\ \ \neg(2 \lt 0),\ \neg(4 \lt 2),\ \text{so } 2 \notin \alpha @@ 2 positive; 2² = 4 and 4 − 2 = 2 positive; neither (1c)
\text{(iii)}\ \ s \lt r,\ r \in \alpha:\ \text{case } r \lt 0:\ s \lt 0 @@ chain for less on ℚ
\quad \text{case } r^2 \lt 2:\ s \lt 0,\ \text{or } 0 \le s \text{ and } s^2 \lt r^2 \lt 2 @@ any-two-compare on ℚ on s and 0; squares-keep-order on ℚ; chain for less on ℚ
\quad s \in \alpha @@ add an option in each case; reason by cases
\text{(iv)}\ \ r \in \alpha:\ r \lt 0 \text{ gives } 0;\ \ r = 0 \text{ gives } 1 @@ any-two-compare on ℚ on r and 0; 0² = 0 < 2; 1² = 1 < 2
\quad 0 \lt r \text{ gives } \tfrac{2r + 2}{r + 2} @@ r² < 2 by remaining-option; no-greatest-below (5f), with hole's-step's s
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha \in \mathbb R} \quad \blacksquare @@ all four; ︿

Hole-is-no-fraction's-cut technique: for every rational r, α ≠ r*.

\text{assume } \alpha = r^{*} @@ aiming to refute
r \notin \alpha:\ \neg(r \lt 0) \land \neg(r^2 \lt 2) @@ fraction-cut's (ii); the swap axiom; ﹀; neither
r^2 \ne 2,\ \text{so } 2 \lt r^2;\ \ r \text{ positive} @@ no-fraction-squares-to-2 (5f); any-two-compare on ℚ; r = 0 would give 0 < 2
\text{call } s:\ 0 \lt s \lt r,\ \ 2 \lt s^2 @@ no-least-above; use the some
s \in r^{*} = \alpha,\ \text{so } s \lt 0 \lor s^2 \lt 2 @@ ︿; the swap axiom; ﹀ 
\bot @@ s < 0 clashes with 0 < s; s² < 2 with 2 < s² gives 2 < 2 by chain for less on ℚ
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha \ne r^{*}} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; r was anything

From here on, a real that is r* for no rational r is irrational. The hole's cut is irrational, by hole-is-no-fraction's-cut. A real that is some r* may be called rational, as its fraction is.

So the line holds something the fractions did not: a real sitting exactly at 5f's split. Every fraction of the lower heap is below it. Every fraction of the upper heap is not below it (by members-are-the-fractions-below: r ∈ α ⇔ r* < α). 5f opened a flag when it promised that rung 8 would fill its hole: that flag is closed here, since the hole now has a number at it. What this real gives when multiplied by itself is 8f's question, once 8d has said what multiplying cuts means.

8a11Running It Backwards

Handed a set of fractions and asked whether it is a real, check the four conditions in order. Expect (iii) and (iv) to need work. (iii) wants every negative let in when the condition talks about squares. (iv) wants a recipe for a larger member: a halfway fraction by another-between, or a step like hole's-step's. Handed a real and asked which fraction it is, look for r with α = r*. The least fraction not in α, if there is one, is r. If the fractions missed by α have no least member, α is irrational. Wanting α ≤ β, chase a member. Wanting r ∈ α from order, use members-are-the-fractions-below. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The move of letting the split itself be the number is Richard Dedekind’s. 8a.4.4’s old answer is his.

Dedekind’s move was to stop looking for a number at the split and to let the split itself be the number. Whenever a splitting of the fractions is produced by no fraction, he said, we create a new number, and regard it as completely fixed by the splitting.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), §IV, after Beman's translation (1901)

Accepted names: a cut is a Dedekind cut.
