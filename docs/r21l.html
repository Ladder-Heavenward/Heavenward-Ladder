21l · technique

The slope of sine by hand

Rung 13 gave rules for the slope of a function built from others: one inside another, two multiplied, one divided by another. Those rules never cared which functions they were given. This step hands them the sine, the cosine and the tangent, and finds the highest points, the lowest points and the bends of curves built from them.

The rules for slopes work on the circle’s coordinates exactly as on any other function. With them you can find where a curve made of waves is highest, lowest or flattest, and which way it bends.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 21g (the slopes of sin, cos and tan), 13e and 13i (the chain, product and quotient rules), 14a and 14g (tops, bottoms and bends). 21m runs the functions backwards.

21l1One inside another

Stands on 21g (slopes of sin and cos) and 13e (the chain rule).

Trig-chain technique: the slope of sin(g(x)) is cos(g(x)) times g′(x), and the slope of cos(g(x)) is −sin(g(x)) times g′(x). The outside is the circle’s coordinate, the inside anything with a slope.

(\sin x^2)' = \cos(x^2) \cdot 2x @@ inside x², slope 2x
(\sin^2 x)' = ((\sin x)^2)' = 2\sin x \cdot \cos x = \sin 2x @@ inside sin x, outside squaring; doubles (21d4)
\htmlClass{new}{(\cos^3 2x)' = 3\cos^2 2x \cdot (-\sin 2x) \cdot 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ three layers: cube, cos, times 2

21l2Products and quotients

Stands on 21l1, 21g3 (the slope of tan) and 13i (products and quotients).

Trig-quotient technique: sec′ = sec · tan, csc′ = −csc · cot and cot′ = −csc², each by the quotient rule on 1 / cos, 1 / sin and cos / sin.

\big(\tfrac{1}{\cos x}\big)' = \tfrac{0 \cdot \cos x - 1 \cdot (-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} @@ the quotient rule
\htmlClass{new}{\sec' x = \sec x\tan x} \quad \blacksquare @@ sin x / cos² x = (1 / cos x)(sin x / cos x)
(x\sin x)' = \sin x + x\cos x @@ the product rule

21l3Highest and lowest

Stands on 21l1, 21k4 (every answer) and 14a (a slope at a top is 0).

Trig-peaks technique: on a closed stretch, a wave-built function is highest or lowest at an end or where its slope is 0; every-answer finds the zeros of the slope.

f'(x) = 1 - 2\sin x = 0 \iff \sin x = \tfrac12 @@ slope of cos (21g)
x = \tfrac{\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{5\pi}{6} @@ every-answer (21k4), inside [0, 2π]
f(0) = 2,\ f(\tfrac{\pi}{6}) = \tfrac{\pi}{6} + \sqrt3,\ f(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6} - \sqrt3,\ f(2\pi) = 2\pi + 2 @@ check the flats and the ends
\htmlClass{new}{\text{highest } 2\pi + 2 \text{ at } 2\pi;\ \text{lowest } \tfrac{5\pi}{6} - \sqrt3 \text{ at } \tfrac{5\pi}{6}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 5π/6 − √3 ≈ 0.886 < 2

21l4Which way it bends

Stands on 21l1 and 14g (bends up and down).

Sine-bend technique: the sine bends down where it is above the floor and up where it is below, since its second slope is −sin. It changes its bend exactly where it crosses the floor.

(\sin x)'' = (\cos x)' = -\sin x @@ 21g, twice
\sin x > 0 \Rightarrow (\sin x)'' < 0 @@ bends down above the floor
\htmlClass{new}{(\sin kx)'' = -k^2\sin kx} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chain rule twice: a squeezed wave bends k² times as hard

So a wave squeezed to half its period bends four times as sharply.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The chain, product and quotient rules keep their names here. The second slope is the second derivative; “bends down” and “bends up” are concave down and concave up, and a place where the bend changes is a point of inflection.

Who said it first

In 21l.4.5, the first explanation is René Descartes’s, Dioptrics (1637); the quickest-route rule is Pierre de Fermat’s (1662), worked out with the method of slopes. Léon Foucault measured light in water in 1850 and found it slower.
