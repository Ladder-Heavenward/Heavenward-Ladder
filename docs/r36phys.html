Light as Energy, Colour as Perception

Light as energy, colour as perception: how can one light be both? A lamp in a kitchen in Truckee pours out energy that warms the counter, and the same lamp makes a colour that a cook calls warm white. The rung kept the two apart on purpose. It said that lights shone together add, that light through a filter is multiplied, that a molecule’s bonds pick the wavelengths it takes away, and that some light carries energy and no colour at all. Each of those was laid down, or held for this page. What makes the question hard is that every one of them is a guess from things, made by people who could not see a photon, and checked against meters and eyes for a long time. This page shows the guesses at work, with the rung’s own tools: sums of spectra, the three numbers, and a photon’s energy run backwards.

Two lamps whose light arrives independently add, wavelength by wavelength. Light through a filter keeps a share at each wavelength, and two filters keep the product of their shares. A molecule takes away the wavelength whose photon fits one of its gaps, so the gap fixes the colour, and the gap’s doubt passes to the wavelength unchanged. Light past the window adds energy and no colour, and a prism sorts out what the eye cannot. Every surface splits the light into what it sends back, passes and takes; a hot thing glows in a spread whose peak moves with its temperature, and a thin gas glows in the spikes of its own gaps.

36phys1Why Lights Add

Light arrives in photons (17·phys). Shine two lamps on one wall. In any second, the photons near a wavelength λ that reach a patch of the wall are those from the first lamp and those from the second, and no photon is from both. So the count is a count of two separate sets, and counts of separate sets add.

Counts-add technique: for two lamps whose light arrives independently, the amount at each wavelength of the two together is the sum of their amounts. Each photon at λ carries E(λ) (36b2), so the energies add too. This is the “together” that 36a3 laid down, and the reason it can be laid down.

N_1(\lambda),\ \allowbreak N_2(\lambda) \text{ the }\allowbreak \text{photons }\allowbreak \text{per }\allowbreak \text{second }\allowbreak \text{near } \lambda \text{ from }\allowbreak \text{each }\allowbreak \text{lamp} @@ the two sets share no photon
N(\lambda) = N_1(\lambda) + N_2(\lambda) @@ counts of separate sets add (2, 4)
\text{amount }\allowbreak \text{at } \lambda = N(\lambda)\,E(\lambda) = N_1(\lambda)E(\lambda) + N_2(\lambda)E(\lambda) @@ 36b2: each photon carries E(λ); 5d spreading
\htmlClass{new}{S = S_1 + S_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the definition of the sum of spectra (36a3)

The guess has an edge. Split one laser beam in two and bring the halves back together, and where the waves meet half a turn apart they cancel, as 32·eng’s headphones cancel noise. Then the amounts do not add; the waves do. Two ordinary lamps never keep time with each other for long enough to show it, and that is a fact by cases with a rate.

36phys2One Through the Other

A filter keeps a share t(λ) of the light at each wavelength, a number from 0 to 1, and stops the rest. 18·chem found the share for a path of length ℓ through a solution: t = 10^(−εℓc), with ε fixed by the molecule at that wavelength. So light S through the filter comes out as the spectrum t·S, wavelength by wavelength. That is multiplying, not adding: it is the pigment mix of 36h1 seen in the light itself.

Through-filters technique: light S through two filters with shares t₁ and t₂ comes out as t₁t₂S, wavelength by wavelength, in either order.

\text{after }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{first }\allowbreak \text{filter: } t_1(\lambda)\,S(\lambda) @@ the first keeps its share
\text{after }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{second: } t_2(\lambda)\,\big(t_1(\lambda)\,S(\lambda)\big) @@ the second keeps its share of what reached it
= t_1(\lambda)\,t_2(\lambda)\,S(\lambda) @@ 5d: grouping and turn-about for times
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{same }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{in }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{either }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{order}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 5d: turn-about for times

For two layers of one dye, t₁t₂ = 10^(−ε(ℓ₁ + ℓ₂)c): the paths add, as 18·chem’s absorbance technique says. A pot of mixed paint is not two filters in a row. Some light bounces back off grains near the top and never passes through anything, so the colour of a pot is not the product, and 36h6 says so.

36phys3A Gap Picks a Wavelength

A molecule holds its energy in steps. When a photon’s energy matches the gap ΔE between two steps, the molecule can take the photon and climb; a photon that fits no gap passes by. 36b2 gave the energy of a photon at λ as E(λ) = hc/λ, with hc about 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ joule metres. Run that backwards (9d), and the gap picks its wavelength.

Gap-to-wavelength technique: a molecule with an energy gap ΔE takes away light at λ = hc/ΔE, and a gap known to a share s gives the wavelength to the same share s.

E(\lambda) = hc/\lambda = \Delta E @@ the photon fits the gap
\lambda = hc/\Delta E = hc\,(\Delta E)^{-1} @@ 5b: divide both sides
\text{a share } s \text{ in } \Delta E \text{ gives }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{share } |{-1}|\,s = s \text{ in } \lambda @@ 13m: through a power k the share is multiplied by |k|
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda = hc/\Delta E,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{to }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{share }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{of } \Delta E} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines together

One example first. A dye whose gap is 3.40 × 10⁻¹⁹ J takes away light at 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ / 3.40 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 5.84 × 10⁻⁷ m, that is 584 nm, in the yellow. What is left is the short and long ends of W, and the dye looks blue to violet. If the gap is known to 1%, the wavelength is known to 1%, about 6 nm either way. A solvent that pulls on the molecule moves the gap a little, and the colour with it: the chain of 36g3, with its rate at every link.

36phys4Light the Eye Does Not See, and a Prism That Sees It

The warmth from a stove is light past 700 nm. The three curves are 0 there, so its three numbers are (0, 0, 0), and its energy is not 0: it warms the hand. Add it to any light and the colour stays and the energy grows. That is the spectrum D of 36c1, a light the eye does not see, and it is the two weightings of 36b4 pulled as far apart as they go.

Unseen-light technique: adding light from outside the window to any light keeps its colour and adds to its energy. Proof: col(S + D) = col(S) + col(D) = col(S) by three-numbers, since col(D) = (0, 0, 0); the energy of S + D is the energy of S plus that of D (36b2), and the energy of D is more than 0.

A prism sees what the eye drops. Glass slows short waves a little more than long ones, so a prism bends violet further than red, and each wavelength lands at its own place on a wall. Two metamers that look the same to the eye spread into two different patterns on the wall: a spike at 580 nm makes one line, and a red and a green light make two. The reduction technique of 36j7, seen with a piece of glass.

36phys5Every Surface Splits the Light Three Ways

Light that meets a pane of glass, a leaf or a wall goes one of three ways at each wavelength. Some is sent back, some passes through, and some is taken and becomes warmth. Each photon does exactly one of the three, so by the counting of 36·phys1 the three shares at λ add to 1. Write r(λ) for the share sent back (here r is a share, not 36e’s radius) and t(λ) for the share passed; the share taken is 1 − r − t, and none of the three is below 0. A filter’s t of 36·phys2 is one of the three; the colour of a wall is mostly its r.

How much a clear face sends back is laid down as measured, for light that meets it straight on: a face between air and a stuff of index n (23·phys’s n = c/v) sends back the share ((n − 1)/(n + 1))². For window glass, n is about 1.5, and the share is (0.5/2.5)² = 0.04. The ladder does not derive the law here; 34’s fields would.

Pane technique: a clear pane whose two faces each send back the share r, and which takes nothing, passes (1 − r)/(1 + r) of the light that meets it straight on. One example first: for glass, r = 0.04 and the pane passes 0.96/1.04 ≈ 0.923, a little more than the 0.96 × 0.96 = 0.9216 that counts only the straight path.

\text{straight }\allowbreak \text{through: } (1 - r)(1 - r) @@ pass the first face, then the second
\text{after }\allowbreak \text{one }\allowbreak \text{bounce }\allowbreak \text{back }\allowbreak \text{and }\allowbreak \text{one }\allowbreak \text{forward: } (1 - r)\,r\,r\,(1 - r) @@ the second face sends back r, the first sends back r again
\text{passed} = (1 - r)^2\,(1 + r^2 + r^4 + \cdots) @@ each round trip inside keeps r² more; counts add (36·phys1)
= \frac{(1 - r)^2}{1 - r^2} @@ the endless-ratio-sum technique (15i), ratio r² < 1
\htmlClass{new}{= \frac{1 - r}{1 + r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 − r² = (1 − r)(1 + r)

So a window passes about 92% and sends back about 8%, which is why a lit room is mirrored in its own window at night: outside is dark, and 8% of the room comes back.

36phys6Hot Things Glow

Anything warm sends out light of its own, and the hotter it is, the more and the shorter. 17·phys built the spectrum of a perfect glow at temperature T and its peak-moves technique: the glow is greatest at λmax with λmax·T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. A glow at T is laid down here as a spectrum of the rung, B_T, the same for every perfect glower at that temperature.

Glow-colour technique: the peak of a glow is inversely proportional to its temperature, so doubling T halves λmax; the sun, near 5772 K, peaks near 502 nm, inside the window, and a lamp filament near 2700 K peaks near 1073 nm, outside it. Proof: λmax = 2.898 × 10⁻³/T; for the sun 2.898 × 10⁻³/5772 ≈ 5.02 × 10⁻⁷ m, and for the filament 2.898 × 10⁻³/2700 ≈ 1.073 × 10⁻⁶ m.

Two lessons from the rung come with it. The sun’s peak is in the greens, and the sun is not green: a colour is col of the whole spectrum, the three curves weighing all of it, and a peak is one place on a spread (36a2). And a filament’s peak is in the warmth of 36·phys4: most of what an old bulb sends out has colour (0, 0, 0), which is why it is hot to touch.

So col(B_T), as T runs from cool to hot, is a curve through G: a dull red near 800 K, where only the tail of the glow reaches 700 nm, then orange, a yellowish white, white, and a bluish white past 7000 K. Lamps are sold by the T whose glow they look like, its accepted name the colour temperature: 2700 K for a warm white, 6500 K for daylight. The lamp in Truckee need not glow at all to be called 2700 K; it only needs three numbers near col(B_2700). That is a metamer of 36c1, put on a box.

36phys7A Thin Gas Glows in Lines

A hot solid glows in a spread. A thin hot gas glows in spikes. 35·phys found the levels of hydrogen, −13.6 eV/n² for n = 1, 2, 3, … (here n counts levels; it is not the index n of 36·phys5), and one fall, from 3 to 2, giving light of 656 nm. Every fall from a level m to a lower level n gives a photon of energy 13.6(1/n² − 1/m²) eV, and by 36·phys3 a photon of λ = hc/ΔE, with hc = 1239.84 eV·nm.

Lines technique: a thin gas sends out a sum of spikes, one at λ = hc/(E_m − E_n) for each fall it makes; and a cool layer of the same gas in front of a glow takes away spikes at exactly those wavelengths. Proof: each fall gives one photon that fits one gap (36·phys3), and counts add (36·phys1); a cool atom takes a photon only if it fits one of its gaps, and its gaps are the same ones.

m = 4:\ \ 13.6\,(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{16}) = 2.550\ \text{eV} \to 486.2\ \text{nm} @@ a blue-green
m = 5:\ \ 13.6\,(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{25}) = 2.856\ \text{eV} \to 434.1\ \text{nm} @@ a blue
m = 6:\ \ 13.6\,(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{36}) = 3.022\ \text{eV} \to 410.2\ \text{nm} @@ a violet; m = 7 gives 397 nm, at the window’s edge

So the colour of glowing hydrogen is col of four spikes, a sum of four points on the spike curve of 36c, and it comes out a pinkish purple, because the red line is strongest. And the sun’s light, a near-perfect glow, reaches us through its own cooler outer gas: at 656 nm, and at every other line of every gas there, a little is missing. The missing lines are each a gas’s signature, and they are how the stuff of a star is read from its light.

The accepted names

Light that adds by counts is incoherent light; light whose waves add and cancel is coherent, and the cancelling is interference. The share a filter keeps is its transmittance, and t = 10^(−εℓc) is the Beer–Lambert law. The gap a photon fits is an electronic transition, and the gap-to-wavelength technique is the Planck–Einstein relation run backwards. The warmth past 700 nm is infrared light. The prism’s sorting is dispersion, set by the glass’s refractive index changing with wavelength. A solvent that moves a dye’s colour shows solvatochromism. The shares sent back, passed and taken are reflectance, transmittance and absorptance; the face law is the Fresnel reflectance at normal incidence. The perfect glow is blackbody radiation, and λmax T = b is Wien’s displacement law; the curve col(B_T) through G is the Planckian locus, and a lamp’s T is its correlated colour temperature. Hydrogen’s falls to level 2 are the Balmer series, and the missing lines of the sun are Fraunhofer lines, read by spectroscopy.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton held that white light is a sum of coloured lights, and showed in the 1660s that a second prism does not change a colour the first has picked out. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe argued in his Theory of Colours of 1810 that colour is made at the edges of light and dark; that argument is the standing question of this page. William Herschel found the warmth past the red end in 1800 with a thermometer in a prism’s band. Thomas Young showed in the years from 1801 to 1803 that two parts of one light can cancel. August Beer gave the law of the share kept in 1852. Augustin-Jean Fresnel gave the share sent back by a face in 1823. Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped the sun’s dark lines in 1814, and Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen showed in 1859 that a gas takes away the lines it sends out. Johann Balmer found the rule of hydrogen’s visible lines in 1885 from four measured wavelengths.
