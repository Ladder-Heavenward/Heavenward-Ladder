The New Science

Letters for numbers, the printed book, the telescope, and the first laws read off from measurements. On this page the symbol takes over from the sentence, and nature is asked questions in the form of equations.

29 names, in order of birth. Each line says what the person did that the ladder uses; the links under it go to the steps where the work stands, with the name kept to the cellar there.

1501–1576Gerolamo Cardano

His Ars Magna of 1545 printed the solution of the cubic and met the square roots of negatives, which he set aside as subtle and useless. He also counted chances at dice.

1512–1594Gerardus Mercator

Drew the heading map of 1569 on which a steady course is a straight line, spacing the lines of latitude by hand without a formula.

1526–1572Rafael Bombelli

In L'Algebra of 1572 he gave rules for calculating with square roots of negatives and used them to reach the real answers of cubics.

1540–1603François Viète

The founder of letters for knowns and unknowns. He wrote the coefficients as sums of roots, gave the law of cosines its form in 1579, and solved the three-real-roots cubic by the triple angle.

1546–1601Tycho Brahe

The measurer. His sightings, good to two minutes of arc, were what Kepler's circles could not fit, and his observatory used the first product-by-sums before logarithms.

On the ladder at 18h, 18·phys, 21d, 23·phys

1548–1620Simon Stevin

In the Netherlands he found the push of water by thin strips, trapping it between too little and too much, drew the wreath of balls that gives the triangle of forces, and worked out the equal-tempered step.

On the ladder at 6·phys, 8·cb6, 15·eng, 18·eng, 19b, 21·phys, 44c

1550–1617John Napier

In 1614 he published the first tables of logarithms, built from two moving points, and turned the multiplying of astronomers into adding.

On the ladder at 17, 17a, 17c, 21d

1561–1630Henry Briggs

Visited Napier in 1615 and recomputed the tables so that the log of 1 is 0 and the log of 10 is 1; his tables of 1624, to fourteen places by hand, served for three and a half centuries.

1564–1642Galileo Galilei

The old experimenter with the board. Two New Sciences of 1638 gave the law of falling, the strength of beams and the first look at the endless; the Dialogue of 1632 gave the earth its motion and the sea its tides, wrongly.

1571–1630Johannes Kepler

Found from Tycho's sightings that the planets ride ellipses with the sun at a focus, and measured wine barrels by slicing them into thin discs.

1577–1643Paul Guldin

The Jesuit who attacked Cavalieri's heap of lines, and whose rule turns a spun figure's volume into an area times a path.

1588–1648Marin Mersenne

The friar whose letters joined the mathematicians of Europe. He set out the law of strings in 1636, printed the tables for equal temperament, and timed wide swings slower.

1588–1679Thomas Hobbes

The philosopher who quarrelled with Wallis from 1655 to 1679 over squaring the circle and over a horn of endless length and finite volume.

1595–1632Albert Girard

Claimed in 1629 that a polynomial of degree n has n roots, stated the sums of roots for every degree, and published the area of a spherical triangle as its angle excess.

1596–1650René Descartes

La Géométrie of 1637 set pairs out across and up, so that a curve became an equation. He called the roots of negatives imaginary, set Fermat a test with his leaf, and gave the rule of signs.

1598–1647Bonaventura Cavalieri

In 1635 he made the heap of lines into a method for areas and volumes, and was attacked for it by Guldin.

1601–1665Pierre de Fermat

A lawyer at Toulouse. He found tangents and extremes before the calculus, counted chances with Pascal, made descent famous, and wrote his last claim in a margin. The ladder earns his little theorem and his descent on the Advanced floor.

1608–1647Evangelista Torricelli

In 1641 he found the horn of endless length and finite volume, in 1643 the draining rule, and in the 1640s saw that a tangent problem and an area problem were one.

1614–1684Claude Clerselier

Descartes's defender, who answered Fermat that light cannot choose the quickest path, having no knowledge of where it is going.

1616–1703John Wallis

His Arithmetica infinitorum of 1656 guessed areas from patterns in sums, gave the product for π, and provoked both Hobbes and the young Newton.

1620–1684Edme Mariotte

In France the gas law is credited to him, from 1676. He broke beams of wood and glass and found them weaker than Galileo's figure.

1620–1687Nicholas Mercator

No relation to the map-maker. In 1668 he gave the area under the hyperbola as an endless sum, and studied the tuning of fifty-three.

1623–1662Blaise Pascal

The arithmetical table of 1654, the chances of dice with Fermat, the gambler's ruin, the pressure of water in a tall thin tube, and the essay on the geometrical spirit that the ladder's first rung echoes.

1629–1695Christiaan Huygens

Wrote the first book on averages in 1657, found the force fleeing the centre as v²/r, corrected Galileo's pendulum with the cycloid in 1673, and shared the catenary with Leibniz.

1630–1677Isaac Barrow

Newton's teacher at Cambridge. His lectures of 1670 gave the link between tangent and area as a technique of geometry, and the partial-fraction proof of the secant's area.

1635–1703Robert Hooke

Boyle's assistant at the J tube, and the author of the spring law. He saw cells through his microscope and argued with Newton over light and gravity.

1638–1675James Gregory

Proved in 1668 that the area under the secant is a logarithm, and found in 1671 the series for the arctangent that Madhava had found before him.

1643–1727Isaac Newton

Fluxions in the 1660s, the binomial series, the method of guessing roots, and the Principia of 1687, whose first lemma came close to the limit. The law of gravitation, the cooling law and the laws of motion are his; so are the dots.

1646–1716Gottfried Leibniz

The d-mark, published 1684, and the calculus of infinitely small quantities. He wrote numbers in twos, held that nature makes no leaps, measured force by mv², and quarrelled with Newton's friends over who was first.
