the top · history

The New Science

Letters for numbers, the printed book, the telescope, and the first laws read off from measurements. On this page the symbol takes over from the sentence, and nature is asked questions in the form of equations.

29 climbers, in order of birth. Each of them climbed the same ladder you have climbed, and built part of it on the way. Under each name are the techniques they advanced, credited to them, and the climbers they met: who argued with whom, over what, and how it was resolved.

1501–1576Gerolamo Cardano

His Ars Magna of 1545 printed the solution of the cubic and met the square roots of negatives, which he set aside as subtle and useless. He also counted chances at dice.

Advanced Cardano’s cube-formula technique · Cardano’s one-real technique

1512–1594Gerardus Mercator

Drew the heading map of 1569 on which a steady course is a straight line, spacing the lines of latitude by hand without a formula.

Built into How Can a Small Map Be True to the Land? · A City Is Two Turns · Can a Flat Map Tell the Truth? · Why Fly Over the Pole to Asia?

1526–1572Rafael Bombelli

In L'Algebra of 1572 he gave techniques for calculating with square roots of negatives and used them to reach the real answers of cubics.

Built into Pairs That Square to −1 · Numbers That Turn · Identities · Finding Every Root · The Pairs Looked Back On · Cubics and Quartics

1540–1603François Viète

The founder of letters for knowns and unknowns. He wrote the coefficients as sums of roots, gave the law of cosines its form in 1579, and solved the three-real-roots cubic by the triple angle.

Advanced Viète’s three-real-roots technique

1546–1601Tycho Brahe

The measurer. His sightings, good to two minutes of arc, were what Kepler's circles could not fit, and his observatory used the first product-by-sums before logarithms.

Built into Circles and Conics · Why Do Planets Travel on Ellipses? · The Sum Formulas From Turns · How Does a Lens Bend Light?

Met Johannes Kepler over whose model of the heavens was right. Kepler, a Copernican, thought Tycho's model mistaken, yet came to Prague to work on his observations. Resolved Tycho died in 1601 and Kepler succeeded him. Tycho's records of Mars led Kepler to the ellipse, published in 1609.

1548–1620Simon Stevin

In the Netherlands he found the push of water by thin strips, trapping it between too little and too much, drew the wreath of balls that gives the triangle of forces, and worked out the equal-tempered step.

Advanced Stevin’s slope technique

1550–1617John Napier

In 1614 he published the first tables of logarithms, built from two moving points, and turned the multiplying of astronomers into adding.

Built into Turning Multiplying Into Adding · The Log as an Area · ln(xy) = ln x + ln y · The Sum Formulas From Turns

1561–1630Henry Briggs

Visited Napier in 1615 and recomputed the tables so that the log of 1 is 0 and the log of 10 is 1; his tables of 1624, to fourteen places by hand, served for three and a half centuries.

Built into How Fast Can an Enzyme Go? · Turning Multiplying Into Adding · ln(xy) = ln x + ln y · pˣ for Any Real x · How Many Terms · Knots by Hand

1564–1642Galileo Galilei

The old experimenter with the board. Two New Sciences of 1638 gave the law of falling, the strength of beams and the first look at the endless; the Dialogue of 1632 gave the earth its motion and the sea its tides, wrongly.

Advanced Galileo’s ratio · Galileo’s falling-distance technique

1571–1630Johannes Kepler

Found from Tycho's sightings that the planets ride ellipses with the sun at a focus, and measured wine barrels by slicing them into thin discs.

1577–1643Paul Guldin

The Jesuit who attacked Cavalieri's heap of lines, and whose technique turns a spun figure's volume into an area times a path.

Built into The Squeeze · Measuring What Is Under a Curve · Partitions, Lower and Upper Sums · Volumes by Cross Sections

Met Bonaventura Cavalieri over Cavalieri's indivisibles. Guldin argued in his Centrobaryca (1635 to 1641) that a surface is not a heap of lines, and that infinitely many lines cannot be compared with each other. Resolved Cavalieri answered in 1647, after Guldin's death, in Six Geometrical Exercises. The answer sharpened his method, and that book became the one the next generation learned from.

1588–1648Marin Mersenne

The friar whose letters joined the mathematicians of Europe. He set out the law of strings in 1636, printed the tables for equal temperament, and timed wide swings slower.

Advanced Mersenne’s law of strings

Met René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat over whose method for tangents was sound. Asked by Mersenne for his view, Fermat criticised Descartes's optics; Descartes attacked Fermat's method for tangents, maxima and minima, with Mersenne carrying the letters (1638). Resolved Referees found for Fermat, and Descartes conceded that the method was very good.

1588–1679Thomas Hobbes

The philosopher who quarrelled with Wallis from 1655 to 1679 over squaring the circle and over a horn of endless length and finite volume.

Built into The Area Under x, Worked to the End · Improper Integrals · Distance-Keepers Form a Group · An Angle Is an Arc; π · Adding up Slices

Met John Wallis and Ferdinand von Lindemann over squaring the circle. From 1655 Hobbes claimed proofs that squared the circle; Wallis refuted them, pamphlet after pamphlet, for more than twenty years. Resolved Hobbes withdrew some proofs but never the principle, and the quarrel ended only with his death. Lindemann settled the question itself in 1882: π is transcendental, so no straightedge and compass can square the circle.

1595–1632Albert Girard

Claimed in 1629 that a polynomial of degree n has n roots, stated the sums of roots for every degree, and published the area of a spherical triangle as its angle excess.

Advanced Girard’s root technique · Girard’s excess-area technique

1596–1650René Descartes

La Géométrie of 1637 set pairs out across and up, so that a curve became an equation. He called the roots of negatives imaginary, set Fermat a test with his leaf, and gave the technique of signs.

Advanced Descartes’ plane · Descartes’ chart technique

Met Pierre de Fermat and Marin Mersenne over whose method for tangents was sound. Resolved Referees found for Fermat, and Descartes conceded that the method was very good.

1598–1647Bonaventura Cavalieri

In 1635 he made the heap of lines into a method for areas and volumes, and was attacked for it by Guldin.

Advanced Cavalieri’s cross-sections technique · Cavalieri’s slices technique

1601–1665Pierre de Fermat

A lawyer at Toulouse. He found tangents and extremes before the calculus, counted chances with Pascal, made descent famous, and wrote his last claim in a margin. The ladder earns his little technique and his descent on the Advanced floor.

Advanced Fermat’s descent · Fermat’s flat-top technique · Fermat’s bend technique · Fermat’s straightest-light technique · Fermat’s little technique

Resolved Referees found for Fermat, and Descartes conceded that the method was very good.

Met Blaise Pascal over how to share the stakes of a game cut short. Pascal wrote to Fermat in 1654, and the two worked the problem by letter. Resolved Not a quarrel but a meeting: the letters founded the theory of chance, and the two are counted its joint founders.

Met Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor over Fermat's note in his Diophantus that he had a proof the margin was too small to hold. Wiles announced a proof on 23 June 1993; a gap was found that September. Resolved With Taylor's help, Wiles closed the gap in September 1994; the proof is now accepted.

1608–1647Evangelista Torricelli

In 1641 he found the horn of endless length and finite volume, in 1643 the draining statement, and in the 1640s saw that a tangent problem and an area problem were one.

1614–1684Claude Clerselier

Descartes's defender, who answered Fermat that light cannot choose the quickest path, having no knowledge of where it is going.

Built into Optimising · Does Light Take the Shortest Path?

1616–1703John Wallis

His Arithmetica infinitorum of 1656 guessed areas from patterns in sums, gave the product for π, and provoked both Hobbes and the young Newton.

1620–1684Edme Mariotte

In France the gas law is credited to him, from 1676. He broke beams of wood and glass and found them weaker than Galileo's figure.

Built into Does Every Gas Follow the Same Law? · Will the Shelf Hold? · Where Will a Loaded Beam Crack?

1620–1687Nicholas Mercator

No relation to the map-maker. In 1668 he gave the area under the hyperbola as an endless sum, and studied the tuning of fifty-three.

Built into Turning Multiplying Into Adding · Can a Flat Map Tell the Truth? · Last Time on the Octave and the Bar

1623–1662Blaise Pascal

The arithmetical table of 1654, the chances of dice with Fermat, the gambler's ruin, the pressure of water in a tall thin tube, and the essay on the geometrical spirit that the ladder's first rung echoes.

Advanced Pascal’s proof · Pascal’s row-step technique

Met Pierre de Fermat over how to share the stakes of a game cut short. Pascal wrote to Fermat in 1654, and the two worked the problem by letter. Resolved Not a quarrel but a meeting: the letters founded the theory of chance, and the two are counted its joint founders.

1629–1695Christiaan Huygens

Wrote the first book on averages in 1657, found the force fleeing the centre as v²/r, corrected Galileo's pendulum with the cycloid in 1673, and shared the catenary with Leibniz.

Built into Curves Traced in Time · sin h / h → 1 · Can Forces Be Added Like Arrows? · Why Does a Pendulum Keep Time? · Curvature by Hand · The Best Path, by Hand · Entering Statistics · Averages as Integrals · No System Beats a Fair Game · Rhythm, and Falling Into Step · Last Time on the Octave and the Bar · Continued Fractions

Met Isaac Newton, Thomas Young, Léon Foucault and Albert Einstein over what light is. Huygens argued in 1678 that light is a wave; Newton held it to be a stream of particles, and in England few dared contradict Newton. Resolved Young explained interference by waves in 1803, against severe criticism. Foucault's measurement in 1850, that light is slower in water than in air, was the last nail in the particle theory's coffin. Einstein's light quanta of 1905 brought particles back beside the wave.

1630–1677Isaac Barrow

Newton's teacher at Cambridge. His lectures of 1670 gave the link between tangent and area as a technique of geometry, and the partial-fraction proof of the secant's area.

Advanced Barrow’s one-plus-a-power technique

1635–1703Robert Hooke

Boyle's assistant at the J tube, and the author of the spring law. He saw cells through his microscope and argued with Newton over light and gravity.

Advanced Hooke’s steady-swing technique

Met Isaac Newton over light and the inverse square. Hooke claimed the sound parts of Newton's theory of colour in 1672, and later claimed the inverse-square law of gravity as his own idea. Resolved Never settled between them. Newton denied Hooke the credit in 1686, and the dispute stayed bitter until Hooke died in 1703.

1638–1675James Gregory

Proved in 1668 that the area under the secant is a logarithm, and found in 1671 the series for the arctangent that Madhava had found before him.

1643–1727Isaac Newton

Fluxions in the 1660s, the binomial series, the method of guessing roots, and the Principia of 1687, whose first lemma came close to the limit. The law of gravitation, the cooling law and the laws of motion are his; so are the dots.

Advanced Newton’s tangent step · Newton’s third law of motion · Newton’s law of cooling · Newton’s round technique · Newton’s leftover technique · Newton’s shift-to-rest technique

Met Gottfried Leibniz over who found the calculus first. Newton had his fluxions by 1671 but kept them unpublished; Leibniz printed his d in 1684. The Royal Society's report of 1713, which found for Newton, was written by Newton himself. Resolved Not between them: the quarrel was still open when Leibniz died in 1716. The ladder keeps both marks, Leibniz's d beside Newton's dot.
