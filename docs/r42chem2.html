How Does One Molecule Turn Into Another?

How does one molecule turn into another? 42·chem drew molecules as graphs and counted them. A reaction rewrites the graph: a link breaks, another forms. Organic chemists predict which, with one idea: a pair of electrons flows from where electrons are plenty to where they are short. Two paths do the same rewrite in different ways, and a rate experiment tells them apart: one goes in a single step and turns the carbon inside out; the other waits for a link to break first and loses track of which way the carbon faced. This page draws reactions as moves of electron pairs, reads their speed from rate laws, and uses 20·chem’s handedness to see which hand a carbon ends up with.

A reaction moves pairs of electrons: from a giver, rich in electrons, to a taker, short of them. Drawn as curved arrows on the graph. One step: rate ∝ both concentrations, and the carbon flips like an umbrella. Two steps: rate ∝ one, since the link breaks first, and both hands form. Mirror twins (20·chem) share a graph, so the path decides the hand.

42chem21A Reaction Rewrites the Graph

Sarah mixes bromomethane with a solution of hydroxide. The bromine leaves the carbon and an OH takes its place: methanol forms.

\text{HO}^- + \text{CH}_3\text{Br} \to \text{CH}_3\text{OH} + \text{Br}^- @@ one link broken, one made
\text{the graph: C-Br link out, C-O link in} @@ 42·chem’s graph, rewritten
\text{the rest of the molecule untouched} @@ reactions are local
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a small group of atoms decides how a molecule reacts}} @@ OH, C=O, C-Br, each its own habits

From here on, on this page, a functional group is such a small piece of the graph whose habits travel with it from molecule to molecule.

42chem22Electrons Flow From Plenty to Short

42chem23One Step or Two?

For bromomethane the hydroxide pushes in as the bromine leaves, in one step. For tert-butyl bromide, whose carbon is crowded by three CH₃ groups, the bromine leaves first, alone, and hydroxide arrives after.

\text{one step: rate} = k[\text{HO}^-][\text{RBr}] @@ both must meet
\text{two steps, slow one first: rate} = k[\text{RBr}] @@ hydroxide waits
\text{double } [\text{HO}^-]:\ \text{rate } \times 2 \text{ or } \times 1 @@ the experiment that tells
\htmlClass{new}{\text{CH}_3\text{Br}:\ \times 2;\ \ (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}:\ \times 1} @@ one step, two steps

Which concentrations a rate depends on says which molecules meet in the slowest step. Crowding blocks the one-step path; a crowded carbon can hold a positive charge while it waits, so the two-step path takes over.

42chem24Same Graph, Other Hand

20·chem found that a carbon with four different neighbours comes in two forms that no turn carries one onto the other. Both forms have the same atoms joined the same way.

\text{mirror twins: the same graph} @@ the same dots, the same links
\text{the graph records who is joined, not which way round} @@ joins without directions
\text{so a rewrite of the graph does not say which hand comes out} @@ the path must say
\htmlClass{new}{\text{carvone: one hand smells of spearmint, the other of caraway}} @@ the nose reads the hand

A reaction drawn on the graph alone cannot say which hand its product has; the path the electrons take decides it, and 20·chem’s look from one neighbour tells which hand came out.

42chem25Which Hand Comes Out

\text{one step: hydroxide enters opposite the leaving Br} @@ from behind
\text{the three other neighbours flip, like an umbrella in wind} @@ one hand becomes the other
\text{two steps: the waiting carbon is flat} @@ three neighbours in a plane
\htmlClass{new}{\text{attack from either face: half each hand}} @@ the handedness is lost

A one-step path flips the carbon to the other hand; a two-step path through a flat middle gives both hands equally. So the hand of the product, and the rate law, each tell which path ran. Drug makers who need one hand choose the one-step path.

Accepted names

The giver is a nucleophile, the taker an electrophile, the leaving bromide a leaving group; curved arrows are arrow pushing, the path a reaction mechanism. One step is SN2, two steps SN1 through a carbocation. Handedness is chirality, the twins enantiomers, the flip Walden inversion, half each hand a racemic mixture.

Who said it first

Jacobus van ’t Hoff and Joseph Le Bel explained mirror twins by carbon’s pyramid in 1874. Paul Walden found the flip in 1896, and Edward Hughes and Christopher Ingold sorted the one-step and two-step paths by their rate laws in the 1930s.
