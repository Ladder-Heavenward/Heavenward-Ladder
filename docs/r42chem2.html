How Does One Molecule Turn Into Another?

How does one molecule turn into another? 42·chem drew molecules as graphs and counted them. A reaction rewrites the graph: a link breaks, another forms. Organic chemists predict which, with one idea: a pair of electrons flows from where electrons are plenty to where they are short. Two paths do the same rewrite in different ways, and a rate experiment tells them apart: one goes in a single step and turns the carbon inside out; the other waits for a link to break first and loses track of which way the carbon faced. This page draws reactions as moves of electron pairs, reads their speed from rate laws, and uses 20·chem’s handedness to see which hand a carbon ends up with.

A reaction moves pairs of electrons: from a giver, rich in electrons, to a taker, short of them. Drawn as curved arrows on the graph. One step: rate ∝ both concentrations, and the carbon flips like an umbrella. Two steps: rate ∝ one, since the link breaks first, and both hands form. Mirror twins (20·chem) share a graph, so the path decides the hand.

42chem21A Reaction Rewrites the Graph

Sarah mixes bromomethane with a solution of hydroxide. The bromine leaves the carbon and an OH takes its place: methanol forms.

\text{HO}^- + \text{CH}_3\text{Br} \to \text{CH}_3\text{OH} + \text{Br}^- @@ one link broken, one made
\text{the graph: C-Br link out, C-O link in} @@ 42·chem’s graph, rewritten
\text{the rest of the molecule untouched} @@ reactions are local
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a small group of atoms decides how a molecule reacts}} @@ OH, C=O, C-Br, each its own habits

From here on, on this page, a functional group is such a small piece of the graph whose habits travel with it from molecule to molecule.

2chem9 called these parts with names, and the hydroxyl group was the first. The page’s reaction, in gap statements.

One gap of the carbon changes its partner. Nothing else in the statement moves. The open gap, and the charge that marks it, pass from the oxygen to the bromine.

42chem22Electrons Flow From Plenty to Short

\text{Br pulls the link’s electrons toward itself} @@ it is more electron-hungry than C
\text{so the carbon is a little short: } \delta^+ @@ a taker
\text{HO}^- \text{ has spare pairs: a giver} @@ a negative charge
\htmlClass{new}{\text{O’s pair forms the C-O link; the C-Br pair leaves with Br}} @@ two curved arrows

Mechanism technique: draw each reaction as pairs of electrons moving from a giver to a taker, one curved arrow per pair; a link forms where a pair arrives and breaks where a pair leaves. Charge is kept: the minus goes from O to Br.

“More electron-hungry” is 2chem7’s second relation: bromine holds the shared part closer than carbon. That relation is not symmetric, and it is what gives a bond a direction for an arrow to follow. An arrow starts at a pair that some atom holds and ends at an atom left short.

2·chem’s reading checks become the checks on a mechanism. A carbon has four gaps and never five names (2chem5). So the oxygen cannot simply arrive: as its pair comes in, the pair to bromine must go out. That is why the two arrows are drawn together. After every arrow, count each atom’s gaps again. A step that leaves a carbon with five partners cannot be read, and is not a step.

42chem23One Step or Two?

For bromomethane the hydroxide pushes in as the bromine leaves, in one step. For tert-butyl bromide, whose carbon is crowded by three CH₃ groups, the bromine leaves first, alone, and hydroxide arrives after.

\text{one step: rate} = k[\text{HO}^-][\text{RBr}] @@ both must meet
\text{two steps, slow one first: rate} = k[\text{RBr}] @@ hydroxide waits
\text{double } [\text{HO}^-]:\ \text{rate } \times 2 \text{ or } \times 1 @@ the experiment that tells
\htmlClass{new}{\text{CH}_3\text{Br}:\ \times 2;\ \ (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}:\ \times 1} @@ one step, two steps

Which concentrations a rate depends on says which molecules meet in the slowest step. Crowding blocks the one-step path; a crowded carbon can hold a positive charge while it waits, so the two-step path takes over.

Both molecules are R-Br, and 2chem11 said that what is written of R-Br is meant for every R. That holds for the rewrite: for every R, the bromine’s gap passes to the oxygen. It does not hold for the path or the speed. With R a single carbon and its hydrogens there is one step. With R a carbon crowded by three others there are two. R is a fresh name as far as b is concerned. What b cannot see, the room round the carbon, decides the path. 13chem7 and 17chem2 found the same: R keeps the reaction and changes the number.

42chem24Same Graph, Other Hand

20·chem found that a carbon with four different neighbours comes in two forms that no turn carries one onto the other. Both forms have the same atoms joined the same way.

\text{mirror twins: the same graph} @@ the same dots, the same links
\text{the graph records who is joined, not which way round} @@ joins without directions
\text{so a rewrite of the graph does not say which hand comes out} @@ the path must say
\htmlClass{new}{\text{carvone: one hand smells of spearmint, the other of caraway}} @@ the nose reads the hand

A reaction drawn on the graph alone cannot say which hand its product has; the path the electrons take decides it, and 20·chem’s look from one neighbour tells which hand came out.

42chem25Which Hand Comes Out

\text{one step: hydroxide enters opposite the leaving Br} @@ from behind
\text{the three other neighbours flip, like an umbrella in wind} @@ one hand becomes the other
\text{two steps: the waiting carbon is flat} @@ three neighbours in a plane
\htmlClass{new}{\text{attack from either face: half each hand}} @@ the handedness is lost

A one-step path flips the carbon to the other hand; a two-step path through a flat middle gives both hands equally. So the hand of the product, and the rate law, each tell which path ran. Drug makers who need one hand choose the one-step path.

In 20chem7’s terms the one-step path refills the carbon’s gaps in the mirrored order, and the two-step path loses the order and gives both.

42chem27A Double Bond Made, a Double Bond Spent

Substitution changed the partner in one gap. Two more rewrites change two gaps at once, on neighbouring carbons. In the first, each of the two carbons lets one partner go, and the two freed gaps are filled by each other: one partner now fills two gaps, and 2chem4’s double bond has been made. In the second, the reverse: the second pair between two carbons opens, each carbon has a gap free, and each takes a new partner.

\text{C}_1(\text{C}_2, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{H}),\ \text{C}_2(\text{C}_1, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{Br}) @@ before
\htmlClass{new}{\text{C}_1(\text{C}_2, \text{C}_2, \text{H}, \text{H}),\ \text{C}_2(\text{C}_1, \text{C}_1, \text{H}, \text{H})} @@ two partners leave: an H from C₁, the Br from C₂
\text{C}_1(\text{C}_2, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{H}),\ \text{C}_2(\text{C}_1, \text{H}, \text{H}, \text{Br}) @@ two partners arrive: the same line read upward

Double-bond technique: removing one partner from each of two neighbouring carbons makes a double bond between them, and adding one partner to each carbon of a double bond spends it; every atom’s count of gaps is kept in both.

Each is the other run backwards. With substitution these are three of the rewrites a first course in the subject is built on. Which carbon gets which partner, and one step or two, are settled as in 42chem23, by which carbon can better hold a charge while it waits.

42chem28Which Part Answers

2chem10 quoted the last step in making the painkiller paracetamol: 4-aminophenol is treated with acetic anhydride. That molecule has two parts that could answer, the OH and the NH₂, each with an unshared pair to give. The anhydride has a carbon left short by two oxygens, a taker. Only the nitrogen ends up joined to it. Why not the oxygen?

Both are givers. 2chem7’s second relation ranks them: oxygen holds its electrons closer than nitrogen does. A pair that is held less closely is given more readily. So the nitrogen answers first, and with one measure of anhydride it is the only one that answers.

Readier-giver technique: when a molecule has two parts that could give a pair to the same taker, the one whose atom holds its pair less closely answers first.

That technique is one line of a wider one. List every place in the molecule that could give a pair and every place that could take one. A reaction joins the readiest giver to the readiest taker that can reach each other. “Readiest” is settled by how closely the pair is held and by charge. “Can reach” is settled by crowding (42chem23). When the part a chemist wants changed is not the readiest, the readier one is first capped with a part that can be taken off again afterwards.

The same question is asked inside one part. A double bond has two carbons, and a newcomer H⁺ can go to either. It goes to the one that leaves the positive charge on the carbon with more carbons round it, since that carbon holds a charge better (42chem23 used the same fact). So a chemist asks three things in order. Which part? Which atom of the part? Which hand (42chem25)?

Accepted names

The giver is a nucleophile, the taker an electrophile, the leaving bromide a leaving group; curved arrows are arrow pushing, the path a reaction mechanism. One step is SN2, two steps SN1 through a carbocation. Handedness is chirality, the twins enantiomers, the flip Walden inversion, half each hand a racemic mixture.

Who said it first

Jacobus van ’t Hoff and Joseph Le Bel explained mirror twins by carbon’s pyramid in 1874. Paul Walden found the flip in 1896, and Edward Hughes and Christopher Ingold sorted the one-step and two-step paths by their rate laws in the 1930s.

Accepted names: more rewrites

Making a double bond by removing two partners is an elimination, and spending one by adding two is an addition. Choosing which of two parts reacts is chemoselectivity, and which atom of a part regioselectivity. A cap that is taken off again afterwards is a protecting group.
