18·∎ · conclusion

Geometry is algebra on pairs of numbers

A point is a pair of numbers, and the distance between two points is a square root. From those two things, every fact this rung proves about right angles, straight ways, lines, circles and conics is a calculation with real numbers.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 18e conics by distances is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

Since rung 9 the ladder has drawn pairs of reals as dots, and said no shape was meant. The survey of rung 18 promised to build the shapes from one definition, a distance between pairs. Then a figure is a set of pairs, a fact about a figure is a fact about real numbers, and an equation settles what a drawing only suggests.

18a defined distance and the dot product, and proved the right-triangle technique (Pythagoras'). 18b bounded the dot product and proved that the straight way is the shortest. 18c showed that the two descriptions of a line agree, and proved one line through two points and one parallel through a point. 18d made the points as far from A as from B into a line, and put every triangle on exactly one circle. 18e found the parabola, the ellipse and the hyperbola from distances, each with an equation of the second degree. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

Once a point is a pair of reals and distance is the root of the sum of the squared differences, right angles, straight ways, lines, circles and conics obey techniques about real numbers, proved by algebra.

Let a point be a pair (x, y) of reals, and the plane be ℝ².

Let d(P, Q) = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²).

Let u ⊥ v mean u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ = 0.

Then the squares on two displacements add up to the square on their sum exactly when the two are perpendicular.

No side of a triangle is longer than the other two together. The two together equal it only when the three points lie on one segment.

A point with a direction, and an equation ax + by = c, describe the same lines.

Through two different points there is exactly one line. Through a point off a line there is exactly one line that never meets it.

The points as far from A as from B make a line. Three points not on one line lie on exactly one circle.

The points whose distance to a fixed point is e times their distance to a fixed line make a parabola when e = 1, an ellipse when e < 1, and a hyperbola when e > 1. Each has an equation of the second degree.

The proof

Each part below is a technique already proved on a step of this rung. The proof cites them by name and joins them. Nothing in it looks at a figure.

d(P, Q) = |Q - P| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2} @@ the definition of distance (18a); the root exists by square-root and square-never-negative
d(P, Q) \ge 0,\ \ d(P, Q) = 0 \iff P = Q,\ \ d(P, Q) = d(Q, P) @@ the distance-laws technique
|u + v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 + 2\,u \cdot v @@ the right-triangle technique (Pythagoras')
\htmlClass{new}{|u + v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 \iff u \perp v} \quad \blacksquare @@ take away |u|² + |v|² from both sides (adding-keeps-answers)
  
|u \cdot v| \le |u|\,|v| @@ the dot-bound technique
d(P, R) \le d(P, Q) + d(Q, R) @@ the triangle technique in the plane, with u = Q − P and v = R − Q
\htmlClass{new}{d(P, R) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, R) \iff Q \in PR} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equality-on-the-segment technique
  
\{P + tv\} = \{X : n \cdot X = n \cdot P\},\ \ n = (-v_2, v_1) @@ the two-descriptions-agree technique
P \ne Q:\ \text{exactly one line holds } P \text{ and } Q @@ the line-through-two-points technique
D = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{one common point} @@ the two-lines-meet technique
\htmlClass{new}{P \notin \ell \Rightarrow \text{one line through } P \text{ misses } \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ the parallel-through-a-point technique

Euclid had to ask for the last line as a postulate. Here it is a technique about ℝ².

d(X, O) = r \iff (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2 @@ the definition, squared; both sides are not negative (powers-keep-order)
d(X, A) = d(X, B) \iff 2(B - A) \cdot X = |B|^2 - |A|^2 @@ the perpendicular-bisector technique: a line, since B − A ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{A, B, C \text{ not collinear} \Rightarrow \text{one circle holds all three}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the circle-through-three technique: the bisectors of AB and BC meet once (two-lines-meet)
  
d(P, (0, p)) = d(P, \{y = -p\}) \iff x^2 = 4py @@ the parabola technique
d(P, F_1) + d(P, F_2) = 2a \iff \tfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 @@ the ellipse technique: a > c ≥ 0, b² = a² − c²
|d(P, F_1) - d(P, F_2)| = 2a \iff \tfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 @@ the hyperbola technique: 0 < a < c, b² = c² − a²
\htmlClass{new}{d(P, F) = e\,d(P, \ell):\ e = 1,\ e \lt 1,\ e \gt 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-family technique: a parabola, an ellipse, a hyperbola

Every line above is a statement about real numbers. Rung 8's order and roots and rung 5's algebra decided each one. That is what it means to say that geometry is algebra on pairs of numbers. One thing is left out on purpose: how big an angle is. The rung lays that down as an axiom in 18f, and rung 21 returns to it.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 18f triangles and parallels by hand: angles, triangles, congruence, similarity: Euclid's triangles and parallels, worked by hand. Two angles are equal when their openings are equal. Two sides and the angle between them become a calculation, where Euclid laid one triangle on another. The angles of a triangle add to 180°, with sizes measured by halving the quarter turn, i, and similar triangles come from scaling.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18g lines in coordinates: Lines and figures worked in coordinates: the five forms of a line, the half-planes on either side of it, and the best corner of a region. The medians meet at the average of the corners, the three heights meet in one point by three lines of algebra, and a triangle's area comes from its corners.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18h circles and conics: circles and conics as working tools: the same-product technique for chords through a point, the length of a touching line from outside, the sorting of any equation Ax² + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0, and the two ends of an orbit, α(1 ± e).its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18i curves traced in time: a curve can be a pair of numbers that move with time, (x(t), y(t)). Its slope is y′/x′, its speed is √(x′² + y′²), and its length is the area under its speed, so length too becomes algebra on pairs.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18j constructions with straightedge and compass: the old compass and straightedge, checked in coordinates. Every point they can reach is got from the starting points by + − × ÷ and square roots, which is why some old constructions can never be done.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·chem how much colour is in the glass?: a ray through a round glass tube crosses a chord of length 2√(r² − d²), and that chord sets how much light the sample takes out.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·cb13 slopes of circles and curves given by equations (the implicit cases 13l deferred): slopes of curves given by equations, now that circles are built: a touching line meets its radius at a right angle, and the flat and upright touching lines of an oval come from where the top or bottom of y′ is 0.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·cb16 a cone, cut by a plane: 16j’s flat walls meet 18e’s curves. A tilted plane cuts the double cone x² + y² = z² in an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola, and the tilt alone decides which.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·nav how can a small map be true to the land?: a map is the grid of pairs laid over a shape that was there first. Its scale is the ratio of similar triangles, so a surveyor's measured base line and two sightings fix every other distance.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·logi how many of each loaf should a bakery bake?: It shows limits drawn as lines turning a choice among tens of thousands of plans into a check of a few corners, and sliding a line tells what one more unit of each limit is worth.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·phys why do planets travel on ellipses?: It shows that distances alone settle the fit: measured places whose distance from the sun is a straight line in x are a cut of a cone, with the sun at a focus.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·eng why are bridges built from triangles?: Three-sides and similar triangles carried into a frame of bars show why a triangle keeps its shape while four bars rack, and find every bar's force as a scaled copy of the bar itself.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 18·econ how much do buyers flinch?: The buyers' flinch is a slope on 17·then's log–log plot and a ratio of two pieces of a drawn line, and the demand that keeps revenue fixed is a hyperbola by 18e's distances.its line appears when you finish it.

That is the first geometry built on numbers. Every figure in it is a set of pairs, and every proof is a calculation you can check line by line. You have only just begun to draw with numbers.
