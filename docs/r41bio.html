Why Does Life Use Metal?

Why does life use metal? Joy reads the back of a jar of vitamin tablets to a customer: iron, zinc, copper, manganese, molybdenum, and a vitamin built round cobalt. None of them is in the gap statements of 2·chem, where a living thing was carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. This page asks what each metal does that those four cannot, and what it costs the cell to keep it.

A metal atom in a protein does one of four jobs: it holds a charge in place, it pulls on a bond, it passes one electron, or it holds a small molecule. Which job it does can be read from whether its charge can change.

41bio1About Half of All Proteins

Joy’s jar is small, and the amounts are in thousandths of a gram. Yet about half of all proteins hold a metal atom somewhere in their fold. In most of them the metal does not take part in a reaction. It holds the fold in shape. In the rest it widens the list of reactions a protein can run.

The side groups of a protein are R groups of 9·chem2: an OH, an NH₂, a COOH, an SH. Each can lend or take a pair of electrons, as 42chem21 drew with an arrow. None of them can easily take one electron alone, hold an oxygen molecule without reacting with it, or pull hard on a bond without giving up a proton. Those are the jobs given to metals.

Sorting technique: sort a metal in a living thing by whether its charge can change. If it cannot, the metal holds or pulls. If it can, the metal passes electrons or holds a small molecule.

41bio2The Metals That Only Hold Charge

Sodium and potassium are always +1, calcium and magnesium always +2. They are never anything else in a cell, so they cannot pass electrons. What the cell uses is where they are. Sodium and potassium are kept on opposite sides of a membrane. Calcium is kept out, and let in as a signal.

Magnesium is the commonest of them inside a cell, at 10 to 15 thousandths of a mole per litre. It sits on phosphate groups. A chain of three phosphates carries several negative charges side by side (43bio holds that chain), and a magnesium ion beside it cancels two of them. The fuel molecule of 19·bio is used by enzymes with its magnesium on.

Magnesium also lets go fast: a water molecule on it is swapped about 600,000 times a second. A metal that lets go that fast can be lent to a molecule and taken back.

41bio3A Metal That Pulls

Zinc is always +2 as well, and it holds on harder than magnesium. An enzyme sets a zinc ion beside the oxygen of a C=O group. In 2·chem’s relation h, oxygen already holds the shared part closer than carbon. With zinc’s +2 beside it, the oxygen holds it closer still. The carbon is left barer, and a group with a spare pair can attack it. Enzymes that break down sugar use zinc this way.

Zinc does the same to water. A water molecule bound to zinc gives up a proton far more readily than free water: in 17chem2’s terms its pKa is sharply lowered. So the enzyme has an OH with a negative charge ready at a pH where free water would give almost none.

Pulling technique: a metal ion of fixed charge beside an atom makes that atom hold its shared electrons closer, so the neighbour atom becomes easier to attack and a bound water gives up its proton more readily.

41bio4One Electron at a Time

The organic carriers of 19·bio pass electrons in pairs. The carrier written NAD takes two at once with a proton, and its pull on them is fixed at about −0.32 volts. An iron atom takes or gives one: Fe²⁺ becomes Fe³⁺ and back (38chem32). A pair-carrier cannot hand its two electrons straight to a one-electron taker.

Between them stands a ring molecule made from a B vitamin, which can take two electrons and give them out one at a time. Its pull is set by the protein round it, anywhere from about −0.45 to +0.15 volts.

The vitamin ring: two in, one out at a time, pull set between −0.45 and +0.15 V.

So the chain of 19·bio runs from pair-carriers through the ring to metals. The metal’s pull can also be tuned: the atoms that grasp it, their arrangement in space (38chem35’s arrangements), and the protein behind them shift it. Iron held by sulfur has been tuned across more than 1.2 volts.

41bio5A Wire Made of Cages

The commonest form of iron in the chain is a cage: four iron atoms and four sulfur atoms at the corners of a small cube, hung in the protein by the SH groups of four cysteines (43bio2).

The charge of the cage is counted as 38chem32 counts.

-\text{8} @@ four sulfur, each −2
\text{+4 + 6 = +10} @@ two Fe²⁺ and two Fe³⁺
\htmlClass{new}{\text{+10 }-\text{ 8 = +2}} @@ cage

The cages do not touch. They sit 10 to 15 Å apart, and an electron crosses the gap without any atom moving. The rate falls with the distance r in ångströms as

e^{-11} \approx 0.000017 @@ at r = 10
\htmlClass{new}{e^{-16.5} \approx 0.00000007} @@ at r = 15

Through empty space the number in the exponent is 3 to 5, so at r = 10 the factor is at most e−30.

The protein between two cages carries the electron far better than empty space would. Even so, five ångströms more costs a factor of about 250. That is why the cages stand in a row, each within reach of the next, and the hops still run a hundred million to ten thousand million times a second. A proton, about 1,800 times heavier, crosses less than 1 Å this way.

Spacing technique: the rate of an electron’s hop between two metal sites falls by a fixed factor for each added ångström, so a protein carries electrons a long way by many short hops.

41bio6The Ring That Holds Oxygen

38chem37 held the ring in blood: iron in the middle of a flat ring of four nitrogens. A fifth nitrogen, from the protein, grasps the iron from below. The sixth place is open, and an oxygen molecule sits there and leaves again unchanged. Holding oxygen without reacting with it is the job no carbon group does.

An adult body holds 3 to 4 grams of iron.

In the ring in blood: 2.6 g.

Stored, mostly in the liver: about 1 g.

In the proteins that run reactions: about 4% of the whole.

The iron is used again and again. Very little is taken in or lost in a day.

The open place is also the weak point. Carbon monoxide and cyanide sit in it and do not leave, which is how both kill.

The same ring in other proteins does not let oxygen go. There the iron is driven up to +4 for an instant and puts an oxygen atom into a plain C-H bond. Cholesterol is made with several such steps.

Iron is not the only metal that can carry oxygen. The horseshoe crab carries it on copper, and its blood is blue.

41bio7The Rare Ones

Cobalt sits in vitamin B12, in a ring like the one in blood. It takes three charges, +1, +2 and +3, and it holds a carbon atom directly: a metal to carbon bond. That bond can break two ways. Evenly, one electron goes to each side, leaving a carbon with a lone electron that can pull a hydrogen off almost anything. Unevenly, both electrons go to one side, and 42chem21’s arrow describes it. One vitamin gives the cell both kinds of break.

Two other jobs are done by metals in clusters, and by nothing else.

Turning the nitrogen of the air into ammonia takes eight electrons delivered to one molecule. The cluster that does it holds iron, molybdenum and sulfur round a single carbon atom.

Splitting water in a leaf is done by a cluster of four manganese atoms and one calcium.

41bio8What Metal Costs

A metal that can pass one electron can pass it to the wrong thing. Fe²⁺ meeting hydrogen peroxide hands over one electron and makes a fragment with a lone electron that reacts with whatever it hits first. So no cell lets iron go about loose.

Iron is also hard to get. In water with air in it, at pH 7, Fe³⁺ dissolves to about 10⁻¹⁸ moles per litre.

10⁻¹⁸ mol/L × 6 × 10²³ per mol ≈ 600,000 ions in a litre.

A bacterium’s volume is about 10⁻¹⁵ L, so about 1 chance in 1,000,000,000 of one loose ion in a cell’s worth of water.

Bacteria answer by sending out small molecules that grasp Fe³⁺ harder than almost anything known and carrying them back in. In the blood, iron travels on a protein made to carry it, and cells take that protein in whole. Almost every bacterium needs iron, so holding iron back is one way a body starves an infection.

The last cost is sorting. The +2 metals grasp a protein in a fixed order of strength, manganese weakest and copper strongest, whatever the protein. A protein that needs manganese would have to see 10,000 times more manganese than copper to get the right one. The cell does not manage that by amounts. It folds the manganese protein in a room where copper is not, and moves it afterwards.

The choice of iron is older than the air. Until about 2.4 billion years ago there was no free oxygen, and iron stayed dissolved as Fe²⁺. Life built its chemistry on a metal that was everywhere, and has paid to keep it since the air changed.

Cost technique: a metal whose charge can change must be carried, stored and handed over by proteins made for it, since loose it is both scarce and harmful, and the cell gets the right metal into the right protein by place.

Accepted names

A metal that pulls on a bond acts as a Lewis acid. NAD is nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide; the vitamin ring is a flavin (FMN, FAD), from riboflavin. The cage is an iron-sulfur cluster, [4Fe-4S], a cubane. An electron crossing a gap without an atom moving is tunnelling, and the theory of its rate is Marcus theory. The ring is heme; the nitrogen below it is the proximal histidine; the proteins that put oxygen into C-H are cytochromes P450. The blue carrier is hemocyanin. B12 is cobalamin; the even break is homolysis, the uneven heterolysis. The nitrogen cluster is FeMo-co of nitrogenase; the water cluster is the oxygen-evolving complex. Fe²⁺ with peroxide is the Fenton reaction, giving the hydroxyl radical. The grasping molecules are siderophores (enterobactin); the blood carrier is transferrin, the store ferritin. The order of grasp is the Irving-Williams series. The rise of oxygen is the Great Oxidation Event.
