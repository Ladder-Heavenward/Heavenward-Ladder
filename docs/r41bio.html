41·bio · biology

How Are All Living Things Related?

How are all living things related? Every cell of a person, a horse, a chicken and a tuna makes a small protein, cytochrome c, of about a hundred links in a chain, that passes electrons along inside the cell. The chains are not quite the same. A person’s and a horse’s differ at 12 places, a person’s and a tuna’s at 21. If kinds of living thing split from shared ancestors, and the chains changed a little at a time after each split, then the counts should fit a tree: the further back two kinds split, the more their chains differ. Whether they fit is a question the counts can answer, and this page asks it. It counts how many trees there could be, and finds that for twenty kinds there are more than there are grains of sand on the Earth, so no one finds the tree by trying them all. It finds an exact test for whether a table of distances comes from a tree, and the table for the four animals passes. It builds the tree, places its root, and tries to date its splits with a clock that runs at a steady rate. The clock runs, roughly, and its errors are part of what it says.

Trees with n named ends and three links at every inside dot number 1 · 3 · 5 ⋯ (2n − 5). Distances along a tree pass a four-point test: of the three ways to pair four ends, the two largest sums are equal. The cytochrome c differences of four animals pass it, and give a tree with link lengths. Rooted by the midpoint and timed by a clock, the tree dates the splits roughly; the clock’s errors are measured. Seen differences undercount the changes, and a rare-events count corrects them.

41·bio.1How Many Trees

A tree of descent here is a tree whose ends are the kinds being compared, named, and whose other dots, the shared ancestors, each have exactly three links. It has no root yet: the root is placed later.

Graft technique: there are 1 · 3 · 5 ⋯ (2n − 5) trees of descent with n named ends, for n ≥ 3.

\text{a tree of descent with } k \text{ ends has } 2k - 2 \text{ dots and } 2k - 3 \text{ links} @@ ends-count: k + 3(k − 2) = 2(dots − 1) gives k − 2 inside dots
\text{a new end } k + 1 \text{ grafts onto the middle of any one of the } 2k - 3 \text{ links} @@ each graft gives a different tree
\text{every tree with } k + 1 \text{ ends comes from one graft: take end } k + 1 \text{ off and close the gap} @@ undoing the graft
T(k + 1) = (2k - 3)\,T(k),\ \ T(3) = 1 @@ induction (4c)
\htmlClass{new}{T(n) = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 5):\ \ T(4) = 3,\ T(10) = 2\,027\,025,\ T(20) \approx 2.2 \times 10^{20}} \quad \blacksquare @@

For 50 kinds the count has 75 digits. So a tree is not found by trying them all and keeping the best. It is built from the data by a recipe, and then tested.

41·bio.2A Test for Tree Distances

Give each link of a tree a length at least 0. The tree distance between two ends is the total length of the one path between them (41b2). A table of distances fits a tree when some tree with lengths has exactly those tree distances.

Four-point technique: if a table fits a tree, then for every four ends p, q, r, s, of the three sums d(p, q) + d(r, s), d(p, r) + d(q, s), d(p, s) + d(q, r), the two largest are equal.

\text{the paths among } p, q, r, s \text{ form a tree with four ends; say } p, q \text{ on one side of a middle stretch of length } m \ge 0 @@ the shape of four ends: pairs split by a middle stretch, possibly of length 0
d(p, q) + d(r, s) = \text{the four end links} @@ the two paths miss the middle
d(p, r) + d(q, s) = d(p, s) + d(q, r) = \text{the four end links} + 2m @@ each path crosses the middle once
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the two largest sums are equal, and the smallest is less by } 2m} \quad \blacksquare @@

The other way also holds, and is proved by building the tree four ends at a time; it is stated here. The pairing with the smallest sum shows which ends sit together, and half the gap gives the middle stretch.

41·bio.3Four Animals

Line up the chains of cytochrome c by 41l’s alignment-route technique: for these four the chains have the same length, 104 links, and the best route needs no gaps. Count the places where two chains differ.

\text{human–horse } 12,\ \text{human–chicken } 13,\ \text{human–tuna } 21,\ \text{horse–chicken } 11,\ \text{horse–tuna } 19,\ \text{chicken–tuna } 17 @@ places differing, as tabulated in 1967
\text{pairings: } 12 + 17 = 29,\ \ 13 + 19 = 32,\ \ 21 + 11 = 32 @@ the three sums
\text{the two largest are equal: the table fits a tree, with human and horse together, } m = (32 - 29)/2 = 1.5 @@ the four-point technique, and its converse
\text{human } \tfrac12(12 + 13 - 11) = 7,\ \ \text{horse } 5,\ \ \text{chicken } 4.5,\ \ \text{tuna } 12.5 @@ each end link from three distances
\htmlClass{new}{\text{check: human–tuna } 7 + 1.5 + 12.5 = 21} \quad \blacksquare @@ and the other five

A table of real counts need not fit exactly, and this one does, which is luck in the rounding as much as biology. For many kinds the recipes build a tree that fits the table as nearly as can be, and then ask, by resampling the places of the chain, how often each split comes back.

41·bio.4A Root and a Clock

If chains change at a steady rate on every line of descent, every end is the same distance from the root. Then the root is the midpoint of the longest path between two ends. Here that path is human to tuna, 21; its midpoint is 10.5 from each end, which lies on the tuna’s link, 2 from the middle stretch.

\text{root: tuna on one side; chicken, then horse and human on the other} @@ the midpoint
\text{root to human } 10.5,\ \text{to horse } 8.5,\ \text{to chicken } 6.5,\ \text{to tuna } 10.5 @@ a steady clock would make these equal
\text{mammals and birds split about } 320 \text{ million years ago} @@ from fossils
\tfrac{13}{2} \text{ changes per line in } 320 \text{ million years: one per about } 49 @@ human–chicken
\htmlClass{new}{\text{human–tuna: } \tfrac{21}{2} \cdot 49 \approx 520;\ \ \text{human–horse: } 6 \cdot 49 \approx 290 \text{ million years}} \quad \blacksquare @@ fossils say about 430 and about 90
Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The steady clock is a law under the small case, guessed in 1962 from proteins like this one. It holds in one sense: across many proteins and many kinds, differences grow with the time since the split. It fails in another. One protein over a few kinds can be off by a factor of three, as horse and human are here.

A law is stated with its range and its rate of exceptions, and the exceptions are part of the measurement.

Today’s clocks are calibrated on many fossils, let the rate drift from line to line within a stated spread, and give each date a range. A date without its range is a law with its exceptions hidden.

The clock puts the horse and human split at 290 million years, and fossils at about 90. Is that a reason to doubt the tree, the clock, or neither?

41·bio.5Changes Nobody Saw

Two chains that differ at a place may have changed there twice, and two that agree may have changed and changed back. So the count of differences undercounts the changes, more so the further apart two kinds are. A guess that corrects it: changes strike each place as rare events (22j), at an average of d per place, and a place shows a difference whenever it was struck at least once.

\text{chance a place was never struck: } e^{-d} @@ the rare-events technique, k = 0
\text{share of places that differ: } p = 1 - e^{-d},\ \ d = -\ln(1 - p) @@ undo exp by ln (17)
\text{human–tuna: } p = 21/104 \approx 0.202,\ \ d \approx 0.226,\ \ 104\,d \approx 23.5 @@ about 2.5 changes nobody saw
\htmlClass{new}{\text{human–chicken: } 13 \to 13.9;\ \ \text{the correction grows with the distance}} \quad \blacksquare @@

The guess ignores that a change at a place can land back on what was there, and that some places hardly ever change, and better corrections count both. The direction of the correction is sure: the far distances are stretched more than the near ones, which pushes the human and tuna split back, away from the fossil date. A model fixes one error and shows the next.

The accepted names

A tree of descent is a phylogenetic tree, here unrooted and binary; its inside dots are hypothetical common ancestors. The graft count is the number of unrooted binary trees, (2n − 5)!!. The four-point technique is the four-point condition (Peter Buneman, 1971), and a table that fits a tree is a tree metric, or additive. A table from a clock is ultrametric, and the steady clock is the molecular clock. The correction is the Poisson correction; for DNA its relative is the Jukes–Cantor distance (1969). Building a tree from distances is done by neighbour joining (Naruya Saitou and Masatoshi Nei, 1987); the resampling of places is the bootstrap (Joseph Felsenstein, 1985).

Who said it first

Charles Darwin drew a tree of descent in a notebook in 1837, with the words "I think" above it. Emanuel Margoliash and others sequenced cytochrome c from many species in the early 1960s, and Walter Fitch and Margoliash built a tree from the differences in 1967. Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling proposed the molecular clock in 1962, and Motoo Kimura's neutral theory of 1968 gave a reason for it, the one in 41·bio.7.4.

A proof that is not allowed

“Human and tuna differ at 21 places and human and horse at 12, so the tuna split from us 21/12 times as long ago as the horse did.” Not allowed: it assumes the steady clock, the very law the page tests, and ignores the changes nobody saw. On these numbers it would put the horse split at about 245 million years, if the tuna split is 430.
