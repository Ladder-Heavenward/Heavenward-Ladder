Entering Statistics

Since the second rung, the statistics annexes have been guessing from things: from counts of heads, from misprints on a page, from polls and rainfall. They did it carefully, and they never said what a chance is. This rung says. It starts with a plain question, how long is a set, and finds that not every set has a length. The sets that do have one make a family on which length adds exactly over countably many pieces. With that, a function can be totalled by slicing its heights instead of its inputs, and limits pass through totals with almost no demands. Then comes the turn the whole floor has been waiting for. A chance is a statement that names a size, with the size of everything set to one. An average is a total. From there the rung proves that averages of many tries settle, that a whole count can be stored in one function, that sums of many small parts take the shape of a bell, and that no betting system beats a fair game. The rest of the rung uses these to estimate, to test, and to update a belief from evidence. Guessing from things becomes a technique.

On rung 1 the ladder told you that all of this was stuff you already knew. It was true. Every line since has been sentences, collections and runs, the things you started with. It was also so far short of the whole truth that it was nearly a trick. Look down: you can see now how far it is from what you knew to what you can do with it.

Chance is a size with total one, and an average is a total. So averages of many independent tries settle, sums take the bell’s shape, and fair games cannot be beaten, and guessing from things under unpredictable variation becomes a technique.

What it means to say it

To say that size and chance add over countably many pieces is to say that the sized sets of the line, and the events of a chance space, keep exact books. Cut one into countably many separate pieces and the sizes of the pieces add to the size of the whole, with nothing made and nothing lost. Everything else on the rung is built on that one demand.

A chance is a statement that names a size with total one, so averages are totals, and the techniques of size give the laws of averages, sums and fair games. The rung proves it, and with it the step from guessing to technique.

Where it comes from

An Italian physician and two French mathematicians counted chances in games of dice, and a Dutch scientist wrote the first book on averages in 1657. A Swiss mathematician proved the first law of large numbers in 1713, a French mathematician in London found the bell in 1733, and an English minister’s essay of 1763 turned a given chance around. A French mathematician gathered all of it in 1812, and a German mathematician built least squares on the bell. Sets were measured by countable covers in 1898, the new integral was built in 1902, and a set with no size was found in 1905. A Russian mathematician laid chance on size in 1933, and his axioms are the ones on this rung. Three statisticians in England built testing in the 1920s and 1930s, and argued about it for the rest of their lives.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things, each from the ones before.

First, outer size gives intervals their lengths (30a1) and adds at most (30a2); yet once choice is allowed, some set has no size at all (30a4). The sets that pass the split test do have sizes (30b1), and their sizes add exactly over countably many separate pieces (30b3).

Second, a function can be totalled by slicing its heights (30c3), and the new total agrees with rung 15’s area wherever that works (30c4).

Third, limits pass through totals when the functions rise (30d1), or when one function with a finite total fences them all (30d3); and a total on the plane can be taken in either order (30e3).

Fourth, a chance is a statement that names a size with total one. Given chances (30f2), independence (30f2) and the turned-around chance (30f3) follow from the rules of size, and an average is a total (30g1).

Fifth, averages of many independent tries settle at the true average, with a width that shrinks like one over the root of the number of tries (30h2, 30h4); a count is stored in one function (30i1); and every rescaled sum of many independent parts heads to the bell (30j3).

Sixth, in a fair game no system that stops in time can change the average fortune (30k2, 30k3).

Here the ladder names its last kind of guessing in full. Rung 0 named induction over the arrangement, which is deduction, and induction over the series, which is mathematics. The science annexes guessed by induction under the small case: induction under the case with predictable variation, where each case behaves like the next. Statistics is induction under the large case: induction under the case with unpredictable variation, where no single case can be predicted and only the crowd of cases can be measured. Until this rung, the lessons never argued from the world, and guessing from things lived in the green boxes and the annexes. From here on it is a technique, with its own proofs.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Rung 30 carries all eight green boxes, and here each one becomes part of a technique. 30b carries the ledger: size keeps exact books over countably many separate pieces, and a set that would break the books is left out. 30c carries two sizes of truth: a total is a claim about the whole function and cannot see a set of size 0. 30d carries loose, then locked: each point settles at its own pace, and one fence locks the totals. 30f carries laws with a known exception rate: a chance is a law stated with its exception rate, exact about the many and silent about the one. 30g carries counting, and the triangle: the spreads of independent parts add, so their widths combine like the sides of a right triangle. 30h carries the cloud: an average from tries is reported with its width, σ over the root of the number of tries. 30i carries connections, not families: a chance law is known by what its chance function does. 30j carries the middle of a change: the bell is the shape a sum passes through when its parts have faded into their spread. The annexes carry their own boxes. 30·then carries laws with a known exception rate: a test that calls counts too good when their total falls in its lowest 5% is wrong one time in twenty on honest counts, and that stated rate makes its verdict on the friar’s peas rigorous though anexact. 30·bio carries the cloud: drift moves an allele’s share by a width each generation, so a change is called selection only past two widths. 30·phys carries the cloud: a body’s energy in touch with a room has a computed width, so sealed and open bodies give the same heat capacities. 30·comp carries laws with a known exception rate: a decoder is wrong at a rate bounded before any message is sent. 30·econ carries the middle of a change: a month of prices passes through the bell, and a loss line is checked by counting the days it is passed. 30·logi carries laws with a known exception rate: selling 79 tickets promises a bump on about 1 flight in 23, and that rate is only as good as the estimate of who shows. 30·nav carries counting, and the triangle: errors east and north add like the squares of a right triangle’s sides, and the chance of being far off decides whether to pass a rock in fog. 30·now carries the cloud: a drop of 49 points, give or take 21, is a size with its width, not a dot that passed or failed.

What follows

With those six, the techniques compute. Chances are counted by the rules, densities give chances of continuous measurements, random walks wander and return, waiting times and arrivals are timed, and chance that steps from state to state settles into a steady mix. Then the statistics itself: estimates from samples with their widths, the parameter that makes the data most likely, tests of whether a difference is real and which test is best, beliefs updated by the turn-around rule, and how the numbers were got in the first place. 30w looks back at the axioms that recur under every technique. The annexes take these into the jiggle of pollen, the spread of genes, the sharing of energy, noisy lines, markets, overbooked flights, finding your own position, and the question of how many published findings survive.

What you can do because you know it

You can say what a chance is, compute averages and spreads, bound how often a measurement strays, and give an estimate with its width. You can tell whether a sum of many parts will look like a bell, test whether a difference is likely to be real, and update a belief from evidence by an exact rule.

From here on, a guess from things comes with its width and its exception rate, or it is not finished.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The no-size set spends rung 6’s axiom of choice, and the rung says so where it does. The rung spends rung 8’s least upper bound, rung 15’s area, rung 22’s endless sums, rung 23’s storing functions, and rung 28’s double integrals and change of variables. One step is held in trust: that matching average-functions give matching limits, on 30j, whose proof waits much higher up the ladder. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say size and chance add over countably many pieces, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Gerolamo Cardano, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat counted chances in games of dice, and Christiaan Huygens wrote the first book on averages in 1657. Jacob Bernoulli proved the first law of large numbers in 1713, Abraham de Moivre found the bell in 1733, and Thomas Bayes’ essay of 1763 turned a given chance around. Pierre-Simon Laplace gathered all of it in 1812, and Carl Friedrich Gauss built least squares on the bell.

Émile Borel measured sets by countable covers in 1898, Henri Lebesgue built the new integral in 1902, and Giuseppe Vitali found a set with no size in 1905. Andrey Kolmogorov laid chance on size in 1933, and his axioms are the ones on this rung. Ronald Fisher, Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson built testing in the 1920s and 1930s, and argued about it for the rest of their lives.

Accepted names: the axioms of this rung are Kolmogorov’s axioms; turning a given chance around is Bayes’ theorem; the new integral is the Lebesgue integral; the set with no size is a Vitali set; the bell is the Gaussian or normal distribution.
