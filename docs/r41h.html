41h · proof

Games

A penalty taker in soccer picks a side of the goal, and the keeper picks a side to dive, both in the same instant. A taker who always picks the same side is easy to stop. A keeper who always dives the same way is easy to beat. So each should be unpredictable, but how unpredictable, and in what proportion? This step treats a game where one player’s gain is the other’s loss as a grid of numbers, and lets each player choose a side by chance, with chances of their own choosing. Each can then guarantee a certain average whatever the other does. The taker’s best guarantee from below, and the keeper’s best guarantee from above, turn out to be the same number. That was proved in 1928 by a fixed-point argument; here it comes in a few lines from the last step, since each player’s question is a program, and the two programs are twins. The step ends with games where both can gain or both can lose, and states, without proof, what can then still be said.

In a game of gain against loss, each player mixes choices by chance. The most the first can guarantee is at most the least the second can hold it to, and the two are equal, because the two players’ questions are twin programs. In a two-by-two game with no safe choice, each best mix makes the other player indifferent. In games that are not gain against loss, there is always a pair of mixes neither player would change alone, and it can be bad for both.

41h1A grid of gains

A game of gain against loss is an m × n grid M. The row player picks a row i, the column player a column j, at once, and the column player pays the row player Mᵢⱼ; a negative number is a payment the other way. A mix for the row player is a list x of chances xᵢ ≥ 0 adding to 1 (30f); a mix y for the column player likewise. Choosing independently, the average payment is xᵀMy, by 30g’s average-adds technique.

In stone, paper, scissors, with a win paid 1, a loss −1 and a draw 0, every row loses to some column, so no fixed choice is safe. The mix (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) earns 0 on average against every column.

Guarantee-order technique: what the row player can guarantee is at most what the column player can hold it to: the largest over x of the least over y of xᵀMy is at most the least over y of the largest over x.

\text{for each } x \text{ and } y:\ \min_{y'} x^{\mathsf T}My' \le x^{\mathsf T}My \le \max_{x'} x'^{\mathsf T}My @@ a least is at most any value, a largest at least
\min_{y'} x^{\mathsf T}My' \le \max_{x'} x'^{\mathsf T}My \text{ for every } x, y @@ the outer two
\htmlClass{new}{\max_x \min_y x^{\mathsf T}My \le \min_y \max_x x^{\mathsf T}My} \quad \blacksquare @@ the left side does not depend on y, nor the right on x

Against a fixed mix x, the column player does best with a single column: xᵀMy is a mix of the column averages, so it is least at the least of them. So the row player’s guarantee is the largest over x of the least entry of xᵀM. The largest and least exist: the mixes form a closed and bounded set, and the averages are continuous (34d).

41h2The minimax technique

Minimax technique: in every game of gain against loss, the largest over x of the least over y of xᵀMy equals the least over y of the largest over x; that common number is the value of the game.

\text{add one number to every entry so that all are above } 0 @@ every average moves by that number; both sides move alike
\text{row player: best } v \text{ with } x^{\mathsf T}M \ge v \text{ in every column};\ \ u = x / v @@ v > 0, since entries are
\Rightarrow \text{least } \textstyle\sum u_i \text{ with } M^{\mathsf T}u \ge 1,\ u \ge 0;\ \text{it equals } 1/v @@ the mixes x are the u scaled to add to 1
\text{column player: least } w \text{ with } My \le w \text{ in every row};\ \ z = y / w @@ the same, the other way
\Rightarrow \text{largest } \textstyle\sum z_j \text{ with } Mz \le 1,\ z \ge 0;\ \text{it equals } 1/w @@ a program as in 41g, with A = M, b = 1, c = 1
\text{the two are twin programs; } z = 0 \text{ is allowed and large } u \text{ cover} @@ 41g2
\htmlClass{new}{1/v = 1/w,\ \ \text{so } v = w} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-prices technique

So in a game of gain against loss there is nothing to gain by reading the other player’s mind, once both play their best mixes. A player who announces a best mix in advance loses nothing by it. That is not true of fixed choices, and it is the reason to mix.

41h3The penalty

A safe choice is a row whose least entry equals the value: a row player who has one need not mix. In a two-by-two game with no safe choice for either player, each best mix uses both choices; then the other player’s two choices must earn the same against it, or the other would use only the better one.

Indifference technique: in a two-by-two game with no safe choice, each player’s best mix makes the other player’s two choices earn the same.

\text{taker left, keeper left } 0.58;\ \text{left, right } 0.95;\ \text{right, left } 0.93;\ \text{right, right } 0.70 @@ chances of scoring, close to those measured on professional penalties
0.58\,x + 0.93\,(1 - x) = 0.95\,x + 0.70\,(1 - x) @@ the taker kicks left with chance x; the keeper is indifferent
0.23 = 0.60\,x,\ \ x \approx 0.383 @@ the taker kicks left about 38% of the time
0.58\,q + 0.95\,(1 - q) = 0.93\,q + 0.70\,(1 - q),\ \ q \approx 0.417 @@ the keeper dives left with chance q
\htmlClass{new}{\text{value} \approx 0.93 - 0.35 \cdot 0.383 \approx 0.796} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same from either side, by the minimax technique

A guess under the small case can be checked against this. If takers play as the technique says, their scoring chance should be the same whichever side they choose. Studies of thousands of penalties have found it close to the same, within the spread their counts allow, which is a law in the ladder’s sense with a stated spread, and not a proof that the players reason this way.

41h4When both can gain

In most games one player’s gain is not the other’s loss: each has a grid of their own. A pair of mixes is an equilibrium when neither player can do better by changing their own mix alone.

\text{both silent: 1 year each; both confess: 5 years each} @@ each may stay silent or confess
\text{one confesses, one silent: the one who confesses goes free, the other 10 years} @@ a grid for each player
\text{whatever the other does, confessing is shorter: } 0 < 1,\ \ 5 < 10 @@ each player’s own grid
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the one equilibrium: both confess, 5 years each, against 1 year each if both kept silent}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no change alone helps

Equilibrium stated: every game with finitely many choices for finitely many players has an equilibrium in mixes. The proof, of 1950, finds it as a kept point of a continuous map of the set of mixes to itself, by the kept-point technique of 34h on a set of that shape; it is not given here. The minimax technique is the case of two players and gain against loss, where an equilibrium is a pair of best mixes and every equilibrium pays the value. In other games equilibria can be many, pay differently, and, as the two accused show, leave everyone worse off than a choice no one would make alone.

The accepted names

A game of gain against loss is a two-person zero-sum game, a mix a mixed strategy, and a fixed choice a pure strategy. The minimax technique is von Neumann's minimax theorem, and a pair of safe choices is a saddle point. The indifference technique is the indifference principle. The two accused are the prisoner's dilemma (Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher's game of 1950, named by Albert Tucker). An equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, and its existence is Nash's theorem.

Who said it first

Émile Borel wrote about mixed strategies from 1921 and solved games with 3 and 5 choices, and doubted for a time that the minimax held in general. John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in 1928, and with Oskar Morgenstern wrote Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944). David Gale, Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker proved the link with linear programming in 1951. John Nash proved that equilibria exist in 1950, by Brouwer's and then Kakutani's fixed point theorems. Ignacio Palacios-Huerta studied 1417 penalty kicks from professional matches in 2003; the four chances on this page are close to his.

A proof that is not allowed

“The row player's best mix earns v against every column. The column player's best mix holds every row to w. Play them against each other: the payment is at least v and at most w, so v ≤ w; and since both are best, v = w.” Not allowed: v ≤ w is the guarantee-order technique, and “both are best” does not give equality. That is the whole content of the minimax technique.
