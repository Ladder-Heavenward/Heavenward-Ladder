30d · proof

When limits pass through integrals

Rung twenty-three asked when a limit of functions can pass through an integral, and needed the functions to settle everywhere at once. That demand is strict. Many lists of functions settle point by point, each at its own pace, and still behave. The new total does better. If the functions only rise, their totals rise to the total of the limit. If they do not rise, the total of the limit is still no more than the lowest the totals keep coming back to. And if every function in the list stays under one fixed function with a finite total, the limit passes through the integral with no other demand. These three techniques are what the rest of the rung, and all of chance, stands on.

Rising lists pass their limit through the total; any list of positive functions loses no total in the limit; and a list fenced by one function with a finite total passes its limit through exactly.

30d1Rising lists

Rising-limit technique: if 0 ≤ f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ ⋯ are sized and fₙ(x) settles at f(x) at every x, then ∫fₙ dm settles at ∫f dm.

A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots \text{ sized},\ \ A = \bigcup A_n @@ a rising list of sets
A = A_1 \cup (A_2 \setminus A_1) \cup (A_3 \setminus A_2) \cup \cdots @@ separate sized pieces (30b2)
\htmlClass{new}{m(A_n) \to m(A)} \quad \blacksquare @@ countable-adding (30b3): m(Aₙ) is a partial sum
  
\textstyle\int f_n \le \int f_{n+1} \le \int f @@ a step below fₙ is below fₙ₊₁ and below f
\textstyle\int f_n \to L \le \int f @@ a climb under a ceiling arrives (22a)
s = c_i \text{ on } A_i \text{ a step function} \le f,\ \ 0 < c < 1 @@ now show L is at least ∫s
E_n = \{f_n \ge c\,s\} \text{ rises to the whole line} @@ where s(x) > 0, f(x) ≥ s(x) > c s(x), and fₙ(x) settles at f(x)
\textstyle\int f_n \ge c \sum_i c_i\, m(A_i \cap E_n) \to c \int s @@ fₙ ≥ c s on Eₙ; rising sets, above
L \ge c \textstyle\int s \text{ for every } c < 1 \text{ and every step } s \le f @@ let c rise to 1
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int f_n\,dm \to \int f\,dm} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∫f is the least upper bound of such ∫s

Now the total adds: if f and g are sized and at least 0, ∫(f + g) dm = ∫f dm + ∫g dm. Take rising steps sₙ ↑ f and tₙ ↑ g (30c3). Then sₙ + tₙ ↑ f + g, and step totals add (30c1). The total also scales: ∫cf dm = c∫f dm for c ≥ 0.

30d2Nothing is gained in the limit

For a list of reals u₁, u₂, …, let vₙ be the greatest lower bound of uₙ, uₙ₊₁, uₙ₊₂, …. The vₙ rise. From here on, the number they head to is the lower limit of the list: the lowest value the list keeps coming back near. If the list settles, its lower limit is where it settles.

Lower-limit technique: if fₙ ≥ 0 are sized, the total of their lower limit is at most the lower limit of their totals.

g_n = \inf_{k \ge n} f_k \text{ is sized, and } g_n \uparrow \liminf f_k @@ {gₙ ≥ t} sits between countable sized sets (30c2); the vₙ above
g_n \le f_k \text{ for every } k \ge n \Rightarrow \textstyle\int g_n \le \inf_{k \ge n} \int f_k @@ step-total keeps order (30c1)
\textstyle\int g_n \to \int \liminf f_k @@ rising-limit (30d1)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ let n grow on both sides

The two sides can differ. Let fₙ be 1 on [n, n + 1] and 0 elsewhere. Every fₙ has total 1. At every x, fₙ(x) is 0 once n > x, so the lower limit of the fₙ is 0, with total 0. The bump slid off to the right and took its total with it.

30d3A fence makes the limit pass

Fenced-limit technique: if fₙ(x) settles at f(x) at every x, and |fₙ| ≤ g for every n with ∫g dm finite, then ∫fₙ dm settles at ∫f dm.

g + f_n \ge 0,\ \ g - f_n \ge 0 @@ the fence
\textstyle\int (g + f) \le \liminf \int (g + f_n) = \int g + \liminf \int f_n @@ lower-limit (30d2); totals add (30d1)
\textstyle\int (g - f) \le \int g - \limsup \int f_n @@ the same, with −fₙ; limsup is the upper limit
\limsup \textstyle\int f_n \le \int f \le \liminf \int f_n @@ take away the finite ∫g
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int f_n\,dm \to \int f\,dm} \quad \blacksquare @@ the upper limit is at most the lower limit, so the list settles

The fence is what the sliding bump of 30d2 lacked: no single g with a finite total stands over all of [n, n + 1] for every n. Compare 23e, which passed a limit through rung fifteen’s area only when the list settled everywhere at once. The fence asks less. The list may settle at its own pace at each point.

Anexact, yet rigorous · loose, then locked

2e gathered things loosely under a rule, then locked them. A list of functions that settles point by point is loose: each point goes at its own pace, and the totals can slide away, as the bump did.

Let each point settle at its own pace, then lock the whole list under one fence with a finite total.

The fence is the lock. Once one function with a finite total stands over the whole list, no total can escape, and the limit passes through exactly. Nothing about the pace has changed. Only the room to escape is gone.

The fraction of a lake frozen each night creeps toward its winter value, at its own pace in each bay. Which fence would let you pass the limit through the lake’s total?

The accepted names

The rising-limit technique is the monotone convergence theorem. The lower-limit technique is Fatou’s lemma, and the lower and upper limits are the limit inferior and limit superior, lim inf and lim sup. The fenced-limit technique is the dominated convergence theorem, and the fence g is a dominating function.

Who said it first

Henri Lebesgue proved the fenced limit, first for a constant fence, in 1902 and in general by 1908. Beppo Levi proved the rising limit in 1906, and Pierre Fatou proved the lower limit in 1906 in his work on series.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each fₙ is under g, so f is under g, so ∫f is at most ∫g, so ∫fₙ settles at ∫f.” Not allowed: being bounded by the same number does not make a list settle. The proof needs lower-limit twice, once from each side.
