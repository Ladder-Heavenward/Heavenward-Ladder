26l · technique

The factorial past the counting numbers

The factorial multiplies the counting numbers up to a point: one times two times three, and so on. It makes sense only at whole numbers. What would half a factorial be? There is no list of numbers to multiply. But there is an area. The area under x to a power times the shrinking growth function, from zero out to forever, comes out to exactly the factorial of that power whenever the power is a whole number, and it makes sense for every positive power at all. This step builds that area, proves it is the factorial at the whole numbers, and finds its value at one half: the square root of pi, a turn hiding inside a factorial. Then it carries the new function below zero, past the place where its area stops making sense, by the one rule the factorial obeys at every step. That is the first taste of an idea the ladder meets again much higher up: a function can outlive the formula that first defined it.

An area from zero out to forever gives the factorial at every whole number and a value at every positive number in between. At one half the value is the square root of pi. The step from each value to the next carries the function below zero, everywhere but the negative whole numbers and zero.

26l1An area for the factorial

Two-ends technique: for every real s > 0, ∫ from 0 to ∞ of x^(s − 1) e^(−x) dx converges, at the end that runs off and at the end where the power may climb.

e^x \ge \tfrac{x^m}{m!} \text{ for } x \ge 0 \text{ and whole } m @@ exp as a sum (22j3): every piece is ≥ 0
x \ge 1:\ \ x^{s-1}e^{-x} \le m!\,x^{s-1-m} \le \tfrac{m!}{x^2} @@ a whole m ≥ s + 1; then x^(s + 1 − m) ≤ 1 for x ≥ 1 (17g)
\textstyle\int_1^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx \text{ converges} @@ comparison for areas (16i3) with out-to-forever, p = 2 (16i2)
0 < x \le 1:\ \ x^{s-1}e^{-x} \le x^{s-1} @@ e^(−x) ≤ 1
\textstyle\int_0^1 x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx \text{ converges} @@ in-to-0 with p = 1 − s < 1 (16i2; its proof runs for real p with 17g’s real powers); comparison, run at the bad end
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx \text{ converges for every } s > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ both pieces converge

From here on, for real s > 0, Γ(s) = ∫ from 0 to ∞ of x^(s − 1) e^(−x) dx. Γ is a capital gamma. Accepted name: the gamma function.

At s = 1 the power is gone: Γ(1) = ∫ from 0 to ∞ of e^(−x) dx = the limit of 1 − e^(−b), which is 1. The shrinking growth function beats every power out to forever, and that is what makes the area finite there.

26l2The factorial at the whole numbers

Factorial-step technique: for every real s > 0, Γ(s + 1) = s Γ(s); so Γ(n + 1) = n! for every whole n ≥ 0.

\int_c^b x^s e^{-x}\,dx = \Big[-x^s e^{-x}\Big]_c^b + s\int_c^b x^{s-1}e^{-x}\,dx @@ parts (16e): x^s against the slope of −e^(−x); 0 < c < b
b^s e^{-b} \le \tfrac{m!\,b^s}{b^m} \to 0 \text{ as } b \to \infty @@ e^b ≥ b^m/m! with a whole m > s (22j3)
c^s e^{-c} \to 0 \text{ as } c \to 0^+ @@ s > 0 (17g), and e^(−c) ≤ 1
\Gamma(s+1) = s\,\Gamma(s) @@ the limits at both ends (16i1); both integrals converge (26l1)
\Gamma(1) = 1 @@ 26l1
\htmlClass{new}{\Gamma(n+1) = n\,\Gamma(n) = n\cdot(n-1)! = n!} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on n (4c), from Γ(1) = 1 = 0!

So Γ(5) = 4! = 24 and Γ(6) = 5! = 120. The step rule also links values that are not whole. Γ(7/2) = (5/2)(3/2)(1/2) Γ(1/2) = (15/8) Γ(1/2), so every Γ at a half is fixed by the one value Γ(1/2).

The shift by one is a nuisance kept for history: Γ at n + 1 is n factorial. The area does not care where the counting starts.

26l3Half a factorial

Γ(1/2) = ∫ from 0 to ∞ of x^(−1/2) e^(−x) dx. Put x = y², and it becomes twice the area under the bell e^(−y²) from 0 to ∞. That area has no antiderivative in any table. It is found by squeezing the bell between two powers whose areas rung 23 already knows.

Bell-area technique: ∫ from 0 to ∞ of e^(−y²) dy = √π/2; so Γ(1/2) = √π.

1 + t \le e^t \text{ for every real } t @@ ln u ≤ u − 1 (17a), with u = e^t
(1 - x^2)^n \le e^{-nx^2} \text{ on } [0, 1],\ \ e^{-nx^2} \le (1 + x^2)^{-n} \text{ for } x \ge 0 @@ t = −x² and t = x²; powers of numbers ≥ 0 keep order; (e^t)ⁿ = e^(nt) (17e)
\int_0^1 (1 - x^2)^n\,dx = I_{2n+1},\ \ \int_0^\infty \tfrac{dx}{(1 + x^2)^n} = I_{2n-2} @@ x = cos t; x = tan t with 1 + tan² = sec² (21n), then the limit; Iₘ = ∫₀^(π/2) sinᵐ (23k4), the same area for cos by the complement (21e3)
\sqrt n\,I_{2n+1} \le G \le \sqrt n\,I_{2n-2},\ \ G = \int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}\,dy @@ smaller function, smaller area (15e); y = √n x (16d); G converges by comparison with e^(−y) beyond 1 (16i3)
m\,I_m I_{m-1} = (m-1)\,I_{m-1}I_{m-2} = \cdots = 1\cdot I_1 I_0 = \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ Iₘ = ((m − 1)/m) Iₘ₋₂ (23k4)
n I_{2n+1}^2 = \tfrac{n\pi}{2(2n+1)}\cdot\tfrac{I_{2n+1}}{I_{2n}} \to \tfrac{\pi}{4},\ \ n I_{2n-2}^2 = \tfrac{n\pi}{2(2n-1)}\cdot\tfrac{I_{2n-2}}{I_{2n-1}} \to \tfrac{\pi}{4} @@ the line above with m = 2n + 1 and m = 2n − 1; the ratios head to 1 (23k4)
\htmlClass{new}{G = \int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}\,dy = \tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ G² is squeezed between the two (10d1)
\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \int_0^\infty x^{-1/2}e^{-x}\,dx = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-y^2}\,dy = \sqrt\pi @@ x = y², dx = 2y dy (16d) on [c, b], then the limits

So Γ(1/2) = √π ≈ 1.7725, and Γ(3/2) = (1/2)√π, Γ(5/2) = (3/4)√π, and on. Half a factorial is a turn in disguise: π came in through the areas under powers of sin, the same road 23k took to the √(2π) in the size of n!.

The bell is even, so its area over the whole line is √π. Stretched, ∫ over the line of e^(−y²/2) dy = √(2π). That number will matter on every rung that touches chance.

26l4Past the formula

The area defines Γ(s) only for s > 0. Below 0 the power x^(s − 1) climbs too fast near 0, and the area is not finite. The step rule does not care. Run it backwards: Γ(s) = Γ(s + 1)/s.

Step-down technique: for every real s other than 0, −1, −2, …, the value Γ(s + n)/(s(s + 1)⋯(s + n − 1)) is the same for every whole n with s + n > 0; for s > 0 it is the area Γ(s); and with it Γ(s + 1) = s Γ(s) holds for every such s.

\frac{\Gamma(s+n+1)}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+n)} = \frac{(s+n)\,\Gamma(s+n)}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+n)} = \frac{\Gamma(s+n)}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+n-1)} @@ the factorial-step technique (26l2) at s + n > 0
\text{one value, whichever whole } n \text{ with } s + n > 0 @@ the line above, from each n to the next
s > 0:\ n = 0 \text{ gives } \Gamma(s) @@ the empty product is 1
s = 0, -1, -2, \ldots:\ \text{one factor of the bottom is } 0 @@ no value there
\htmlClass{new}{\Gamma(s) = \tfrac{\Gamma(s+n)}{s(s+1)\cdots(s+n-1)} \text{ for } s \ne 0, -1, -2, \ldots} \quad \blacksquare @@ and Γ(s + 1) = sΓ(s) by the first line
  
\Gamma(-\tfrac12) = \frac{\Gamma(\tfrac12)}{-\tfrac12} @@ the step-down technique with n = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\Gamma(-\tfrac12) = -2\sqrt\pi \approx -3.5449} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell-area technique (26l3)

That is continuing a function past its formula. The area was a formula that worked only for s > 0. The step rule carried its values past the edge. The geometric sum did the same without a name: 1 + z + z² + ⋯ is 1/(1 − z) only for |z| < 1, but 1/(1 − z) goes on everywhere but 1.

In the plane such a continuation is not a free choice. If two functions have complex slopes on a disc |z − w| < R and agree on a smaller disc round w, they have the same slopes at w, so the same pieces uₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(w)/k! (23e3), and each is its own sum on the whole disc (26g2): they agree on all of it. A function with a complex slope cannot be changed in one place without changing everywhere. The integral for Γ(s) also makes sense for complex s with floor part above 0, since |x^(s − 1)| = x^(floor part − 1) (26b2). Following Γ through the plane is work for much higher up the ladder.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Γ is the gamma function, and the two-ends technique says its defining integral, Euler’s integral of the second kind, converges for s > 0. The factorial-step technique is the functional equation Γ(s + 1) = sΓ(s). The bell-area technique is the Gaussian integral, ∫ e^(−x²) dx = √π over the line; its proof here is a squeeze through Wallis’s integrals. The step-down technique is the continuation of Γ to the negative non-integers, where it has poles at 0, −1, −2, …. Continuing a function past its formula is analytic continuation, and the fact that two functions with complex slopes agreeing near a point agree on a whole disc is the start of the identity theorem. The integral in 26l.4.4 is the beta function, B(m, n) = Γ(m)Γ(n)/Γ(m + n).

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler, in letters to Christian Goldbach of 1729 and 1730, gave the factorial values between the whole numbers, first as an endless product and then as an integral; Daniel Bernoulli found a product of his own in a letter of 1729. Adrien-Marie Legendre named it Γ in 1809 and shifted it by one. Pierre-Simon Laplace found ∫ e^(−x²) = √π in 1774, in his work on chance. Harald Bohr and Johannes Mollerup proved in 1922 that Γ is the only function with f(1) = 1, f(s + 1) = s f(s), and ln f bending up, which answers 26l.4.3 and 26l.4.5 one way.
