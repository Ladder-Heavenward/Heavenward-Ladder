Where Does a Satellite Hang Still in the Sky?

Where does a satellite hang still in the sky? A television dish on a roof is bolted in place, pointed once, and never moved, yet it stays locked on a satellite tens of thousands of kilometres away. The satellite is falling around the earth at over three kilometres a second. It looks still because it goes round exactly as fast as the earth turns. This page finds the turning time to match, finds the height from 21·nav2’s circle speed and 17g’s powers, shows why it works only over the equator, and what slowly tilts it, and prices keeping it in place with 25·nav’s plane turns.

A circle orbit at radius r goes at √(GM/r), so it takes 2πr^(3/2)/√(GM). Set that equal to one turn of the earth against the stars, and r is fixed: 42,164 km from the centre. Its plane must be the equator’s, or the satellite swings north and south each day. The moon and sun tilt it about 0.85° a year, and undoing that costs about 46 m/s a year.

26phys21One Turn a Day

Mike installs satellite dishes. For a satellite to stay over one spot, it must go round the earth’s axis in exactly the time the earth takes to turn once.

From here on, on this page, a day means one turn of the earth against the stars: 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds, or 86,164 seconds. The 24-hour day is a little longer, because the earth also moves along its path round the sun, and must turn a little further to face the sun again.

26phys22The Height From the Circle-Speed Rule

v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}} @@ circle speed (21·nav2)
T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi\, r^{3/2}}{\sqrt{GM}} @@ once round
r = \left(\dfrac{GM\,T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} @@ powers for any real exponent (17g)
GM = 3.986 \times 10^{14} \text{ m}^3/\text{s}^2,\ T = 86\,164 \text{ s}:\ r \approx 4.2164 \times 10^7 \text{ m} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{height} = 42\,164 - 6\,378 \approx 35\,786 \text{ km},\ \ v \approx 3.07 \text{ km/s}} @@ above the equator

Day-orbit technique: a circle orbit that goes round once a day has radius (GM T²/4π²)^(1/3), 42,164 km for the earth; only there does a satellite keep pace with the turning ground.

26phys23Only Over the Equator

Every orbit’s plane passes through the earth’s centre. A satellite in a tilted orbit at the right height crosses the equator twice a day, going north half the day and south the other half. Seen from the ground it traces a small figure eight about a point on the equator.

\text{tilt } i:\ \text{the satellite swings from } i \text{ north to } i \text{ south each day} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only tilt } 0 \text{ keeps it at one point}} @@ the equator’s plane

So every satellite that hangs still is in one ring above the equator, and space in that ring is shared out among the world’s nations by international agreement, slot by slot.

26phys24The Moon and Sun Tilt It

The moon and sun do not orbit in the equator’s plane. Their pulls lean on the satellite’s orbit, a little each day, and its tilt creeps up by about 0.75° to 0.95° a year, depending on where the moon’s path lies in its long cycle. The earth’s bulge at the equator also tugs the satellite slowly east or west along the ring.

Left alone, a satellite drifts into a figure eight that grows each year, and a fixed dish slowly loses it.

26phys25Keeping Station With Small Pushes

To undo the tilt, the satellite turns its speed back into the equator’s plane, using 25·nav’s plane-turn technique.

\Delta v = 2v\sin\dfrac{\Delta}{2} @@ plane turn (25·nav)
\Delta = 0.85°:\ 2 \times 3\,075 \times \sin 0.425° \approx 46 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about 46 m/s a year, against about 2 m/s a year to hold its place east and west}} @@ the north-south pushes are most of the fuel

A still satellite spends most of its fuel undoing the tilt that the moon and sun give it; when the fuel runs out, its working life is over. The last of the fuel lifts it a few hundred kilometres above the ring, out of the way. Mike points a dish once at a satellite’s published longitude, and it stays on signal for years, because the satellite’s operators make these pushes every few weeks.

Accepted names

The still orbit is the geostationary orbit; any orbit of one day is geosynchronous. A turn against the stars is a sidereal day. The pushes are station keeping, north-south and east-west, and the higher ring for spent satellites is the graveyard orbit.

Who said it first

The writer Arthur C. Clarke set out the use of geostationary satellites for communication in 1945; the first working one was launched in 1964 (not yet revisited for the dates).
