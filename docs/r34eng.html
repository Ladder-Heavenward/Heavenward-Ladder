How Long Does It Take to Harden Steel?

How long does it take to harden steel? A gear tooth must be hard on its face, to resist wear, and tough inside, so it does not snap. Heat-treaters give it that by baking soft steel in a carbon-rich gas: carbon creeps in from the surface, and the outer skin hardens. Doubling the depth of the skin takes four times as long. This page follows carbon atoms wandering through iron as 30n’s random walk, finds they obey 33a’s heat equation, finds the depth grows as the square root of the time with 33b, finds hotter is faster by an exponential (17), and reads the same equation in a layer of clay settling under a building.

Carbon atoms hop at random between gaps in the iron crystal, so their spread grows as the square root of the time, and their share obeys the heat equation. With the surface held carbon-rich, the hardened depth is a fixed multiple of √(Dt). D grows as e^(−Q/RT), so a little more heat goes a long way. Water squeezed out of clay obeys the same equation, so a clay layer settles in a time that grows as its thickness squared.

34eng1Atoms Wander Through a Crystal

Randy runs a heat-treating shop. Iron atoms sit in a regular crystal; carbon atoms are small enough to sit in the gaps between them. At high heat, each carbon atom hops to a neighbouring gap millions of times a second, each hop in a random direction.

From here on, on this page, c(x, t) is the share of carbon at depth x below the surface after time t, and D is how fast carbon spreads in the iron, its spreading rate, in square metres a second.

\text{hops independent, each averaging 0} @@ a random walk (30n)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread after time } t \approx \sqrt{2Dt}} @@ not in step with t: as its square root

34eng2The Heat Equation Again

Through any thin slice of steel, carbon flows from where it is more crowded to where it is less, at a rate in step with the slope of the share. What flows in and does not flow out builds up.

\text{flow} = -D\,\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial x} @@ down the slope
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D\,\dfrac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2}} @@ the heat equation (33a), with carbon for heat

Everything 33a and 33b found about heat holds for carbon in steel, with D in place of the heat-spreading rate.

34eng3The Depth Grows as the Square Root of the Time

Hold the surface at a high carbon share c_s, in a soft steel of low share c₀. The solution of 33b for a held surface depends on x and t only through x/√(Dt), and is made from the area under the bell curve (30j).

\dfrac{c - c_0}{c_s - c_0} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\dfrac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) @@ a fixed shape in x/√(Dt) (33b)
\text{hard where the share is a quarter of the way up: } \operatorname{erfc}(z) = 0.25,\ z \approx 0.81 @@ 
x \approx 1.63\sqrt{Dt} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{D = 2 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}^2/\text{s},\ t = 4 \text{ h}:\ x \approx 1.63\sqrt{2.88 \times 10^{-7}} \approx 0.87 \text{ mm}} @@ near 925 °C

Square-root depth technique: when something spreads by random hops from a held surface, the depth it reaches is a fixed multiple of √(Dt). Randy cuts and etches a test bar after each bake: the hard skin shows as a dark band, and its measured depth sits on the √(Dt) curve.

34eng4Hotter Is Faster, by an Exponential

A carbon atom must squeeze past iron atoms to hop, which needs a burst of heat energy. The share of atoms with enough energy grows as an exponential of the temperature.

D = D_0\,e^{-Q/(RT)} @@ Q ≈ 148 kJ per mole for carbon in hot iron; T in kelvin
\dfrac{D(1273 \text{ K})}{D(1198 \text{ K})} = e^{\frac{148\,000}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{1198} - \frac{1}{1273}\right)} \approx e^{0.88} \approx 2.4 @@ exp and ln (17)
\htmlClass{new}{75 \text{ degrees hotter: } 2.4 \text{ times faster}} @@

34eng5Twice as Deep Takes Four Times as Long

x \propto \sqrt{t}:\ \ t \propto x^2 @@ 
0.87 \text{ mm in } 4 \text{ h} \Rightarrow 1.74 \text{ mm in } 16 \text{ h} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{or } 1.74 \text{ mm in about } 6.7 \text{ h at } 75 \text{ degrees hotter}} @@ 16 ÷ 2.4

Doubling the hardened depth takes four times as long at the same heat, so heat-treaters raise the heat instead, as far as the steel’s grain allows. A shop quoting a job reads the bake time straight from the depth the drawing asks for, squared.

34eng6A Clay Layer Settling Under a Building

A building on clay squeezes the clay, but the clay can only settle as water seeps out of its tiny pores, and the water pressure in the pores obeys the same equation, with the clay’s settling rate c_v in place of D. A layer drained top and bottom has water travelling at most half its thickness, H.

\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} = c_v\,\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} @@ u the extra water pressure (33a)
90\% \text{ settled at } t \approx 0.85\,\dfrac{H^2}{c_v} @@ the slowest shape fading (33b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a 4 m layer: } H = 2,\ t \approx 3.4 \text{ years};\ \ \text{an 8 m layer: about 14 years}} @@ twice as thick, four times as long

A clay layer settles in a time that grows as the square of the distance its water must travel. Engineers set markers under a new building and read them each year; the settlement follows the curve, rising as √t at first and then levelling off. To speed it, they drive drains into the clay to shorten the water’s path.

Accepted names

Baking in carbon gas is carburizing, a kind of case hardening; the flow law is Fick’s first law and the equation Fick’s second law; D is the diffusion coefficient, e^(−Q/RT) its Arrhenius form, and erfc the complementary error function. The clay’s settling is consolidation, and c_v the coefficient of consolidation.

Who said it first

Adolf Fick set out the spreading laws in 1855; Svante Arrhenius the exponential in 1889; Karl Terzaghi the theory of consolidation in the 1920s (not yet revisited for the dates; D and c_v here are round teaching values).
