46k · technique

Symmetry Groups of Equations, by Hand

Finding the group of an equation by hand takes four moves, always in the same order. Find the roots. Build the field they make and its degree, one floor at a time. Name a few symmetries by where they send the generators of the field, and check that their count matches the degree. Then read the middle fields off the part-groups. The step runs this on the two equations 46c and 46d used, and then on a third, x to the fourth minus two, whose roots sit at the corners of a square in the plane and whose group is the square’s own group of rung 19. Its ten part-groups give ten fields, and the step finds every one. Last, a shortcut for cubics: whether the discriminant is a square decides the group without building anything.

Roots, then the field and its degree, then symmetries by where the generators go, counted against the degree, then the middle fields. The fourth power less two has a field of degree eight and the square’s group of eight symmetries. An irreducible cubic has the turns of three as its group exactly when its discriminant is a rational square, and all six shuffles otherwise.

46k1The Recipe, on x² − 2 and x³ − 2

Group-by-hand technique: find the roots; build K one generator at a time and multiply the degrees; name symmetries by where they send the generators, each generator to a root of its own polynomial over the floor below; stop when the count reaches [K : F]. The count-the-symmetries technique (46c3) says every choice allowed at each floor is a symmetry, so the stopping rule is exact.

x^2 - 2:\ K = \mathbb Q(\sqrt2),\ [K : \mathbb Q] = 2,\ \ \sqrt2 \mapsto \pm\sqrt2 @@ one floor, two choices
\text{group } \{e, (1\ 2)\},\ \text{middle fields } \mathbb Q,\ K @@ the middle-field technique
x^3 - 2:\ \ \sqrt[3]2 \mapsto \sqrt[3]2, \omega\sqrt[3]2, \omega^2\sqrt[3]2;\ \ \omega \mapsto \omega, \omega^2 @@ floor 1: three roots of x³ − 2; floor 2: two roots of x² + x + 1 over ℚ(∛2)
\htmlClass{new}{3 \cdot 2 = 6 \text{ symmetries}:\ G = S_3,\ \text{generated by } \sigma = (1\ 2\ 3),\ \tau = (2\ 3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the count equals [K : ℚ] = 6; 46d3 has its six middle fields

The second floor’s polynomial is the one over the floor below, not over ℚ. When a generator’s polynomial factors over the floor below, it has fewer choices there.

46k3x⁴ − 2: Every Middle Field

Fixed-by-hand technique: to find the field fixed by a part-group H, find a member of K that H leaves alone and whose field has degree |G|/|H|; that field is K^H. By the middle-field technique the degree pins it down.

\{e, r, r^2, r^3\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(i);\ \ \{e, r^2, s, r^2s\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(\sqrt2);\ \ \{e, r^2, rs, r^3s\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(i\sqrt2) @@ size 4, degree 2: r fixes i; r² and s fix α² = √2; rs(iα²) = −i(iα)² = iα²
\{e, r^2\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(\sqrt2, i) @@ size 2, degree 4: r² sends α to −α and fixes i and α²
\{e, s\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(\alpha);\ \ \{e, r^2s\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q(i\alpha) @@ s fixes α; r²s(iα) = (−i)(−α)
\{e, rs\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q((1 + i)\alpha);\ \ \{e, r^3s\} \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q((1 - i)\alpha) @@ rs(α) = iα, rs(i) = −i: (1 − i)iα = (1 + i)α
\htmlClass{new}{\{e\} \leftrightarrow K,\ \ G \leftrightarrow \mathbb Q:\ \ \text{ten in all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the middle-field technique: no field is missing

Closed middles: the three of degree 2, and ℚ(√2, i), since their part-groups are normal (each holds r², and size 4 is half). ℚ(α) is not closed: r sends α to iα outside it. (1 + i)α is a root of x⁴ + 8, so ℚ((1 + i)α) has a polynomial of its own.

46k4A Cubic’s Group From Its Discriminant

Square-discriminant technique: an irreducible cubic over ℚ has group A₃, the turns of its three roots, when its discriminant Δ is the square of a rational number, and S₃ when it is not.

\delta = (r_1 - r_2)(r_1 - r_3)(r_2 - r_3),\ \ \delta^2 = \Delta \in \mathbb Q @@ 46b4
\text{a swap of two roots sends } \delta \text{ to } -\delta;\ \text{a turn keeps it} @@ an odd shuffle changes the sign of an odd number of the factors
\delta \in \mathbb Q \iff \text{every member of } G \text{ fixes } \delta \iff G \subseteq A_3 @@ K^G = ℚ (46d2); δ ≠ 0, roots different
3 \text{ divides } |G| \text{, so } G \text{ is } A_3 \text{ or } S_3 @@ the every-root technique: irreducible, so G moves root 1 to all three
\htmlClass{new}{G = A_3 \iff \Delta \text{ is a rational square}} \quad \blacksquare @@ δ = ±√Δ
  
y^3 - 3y - 1:\ \ \Delta = 108 - 27 = 81 = 9^2 @@ −4p³ − 27q², p = −3, q = −1
\text{group } A_3:\ K = \mathbb Q(2\cos 20^\circ),\ \text{degree } 3 @@ one root makes the whole field: with y = 2cos 20°, the other two are 2 − y² and y² − y − 2
x^3 - 2:\ \ \Delta = -27 \cdot 4 = -108 @@ p = 0, q = −2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{not a square: group } S_3} \quad \blacksquare @@ as 46k1 found by building the field

The accepted names

The square’s group is the dihedral group D₄ (or D₈, counting members), and the lattice of ten subgroups with its ten intermediate fields is the standard worked example of the Galois correspondence. The square-discriminant technique is the discriminant test for the Galois group of a cubic. The sum of 46k.6.4 is the trace from K to the fixed field.

Who said it first

The example x⁴ − 2 became the textbook case through Emil Artin’s Galois Theory (1942) and the books that followed it. The test by the discriminant goes back to Joseph-Louis Lagrange’s study of the cubic (1770–71), read in the language of groups.

A proof that is not allowed

“r sends α to iα and fixes i, so r sends iα to i · i = −1.” Not allowed: r(iα) = r(i)r(α) = i · iα = −α. A symmetry keeps products, and is applied to each factor; α is not i.
