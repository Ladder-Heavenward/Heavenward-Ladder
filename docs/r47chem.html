Why Are Rubies Red and Sapphires Blue?

Why are rubies red and sapphires blue? Both are the same clear crystal of aluminium oxide; a ruby has a trace of chromium in it, a blue sapphire traces of iron and titanium. Copper sulfate solution is blue, nickel salts green, and the colour changes when the water round the metal is swapped for something else. The colour comes from a metal atom’s five d shapes, which 38·chem drew. Alone they share one energy. Surrounded by six neighbours at the corners of an octahedron, they split into two levels, and an electron hopping between them takes one colour out of white light. This page splits the levels by symmetry, using rung 48’s groups, keeps their average fixed, reads the gap as a colour, and decides whether electrons spread out or pair up.

Six neighbours on the axes push hardest on the two d shapes that point at them: those rise, the three pointing between fall. Two up by 0.6Δ, three down by 0.4Δ, so the average stays put. Light of energy Δ is absorbed; the colour seen is the rest. Electrons pair up in the low level when Δ is bigger than the cost of pairing, and spread to the high level when it is smaller.

46chem1Five Shapes in a Cage of Six

A titanium ion in water holds six water molecules, one on each end of the x, y and z axes. Their electrons push on the ion’s d electrons.

d_{x^2-y^2},\ d_{z^2}:\ \text{lobes along the axes} @@ 38·chem’s shapes
d_{xy},\ d_{yz},\ d_{xz}:\ \text{lobes between the axes} @@ pointing at no neighbour
\text{lobes facing a neighbour: pushed harder, energy up} @@ like charges
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one level of five splits into two and three}} @@ the two that point, the three that do not

From here on, on this page, Δ is the energy between the upper two and the lower three, and the six neighbours are ligands.

46chem2Symmetry Says Two and Three

Why exactly two and three, and not some other split? The octahedron’s turnings and reflections form a group (47), and each turning carries d shapes into blends of each other.

\text{a turning of the octahedron permutes the axes} @@ x to y to z
d_{xy} \to d_{yz} \to d_{xz} @@ the three trade places: one level
d_{x^2-y^2},\ d_{z^2} \text{ blend into each other only} @@ the two trade among themselves
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shapes the group mixes must share an energy}} @@ the group’s matrices (47·cb27) sort them

Crystal-field technique: shapes that the neighbours’ symmetry carries into each other must share an energy, so the group of the surroundings says how many levels the d shapes split into and how many shapes each holds. Four neighbours at the corners of a pyramid split them three over two instead, with a gap 4/9 the size.

46chem3The Average Stays Put

2 \cdot u - 3 \cdot \ell = 0,\ \ u + \ell = \Delta @@ the average level unmoved
u = 0.6\Delta,\ \ \ell = 0.4\Delta @@ solve the pair
\text{one electron in a low shape: } -0.4\Delta @@ below the unsplit level
\htmlClass{new}{\text{electrons in low shapes give the ion extra hold}} @@ counted in units of Δ

The split keeps the average energy of the five shapes where it was: the two rise 0.6Δ, the three fall 0.4Δ. An ion gains 0.4Δ for each electron in a low shape and loses 0.6Δ for each in a high one.

46chem4The Gap Is a Colour

Titanium in water has one d electron, in a low shape. Light whose energy is Δ lifts it to a high one.

E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} @@ Planck’s relation (36b)
\text{absorbed near } \lambda = 500 \text{ nm} @@ green, measured
\Delta = \dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \cdot 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{500 \times 10^{-9}} \approx 4.0 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} @@ per ion; about 240 kJ per mole
\htmlClass{new}{\text{green taken out of white: red and blue pass, purple seen}} @@ the colour is what is left

The colour of a metal’s compound is white light minus the colour whose energy matches Δ. Swap the neighbours and Δ changes: ammonia splits more than water, which splits more than chloride, so the colour shifts. In ruby, chromium’s neighbours split it to take out green and violet, leaving red.

46chem5Spread Out or Pair Up

Iron with six d electrons: fill the three low shapes first? Two electrons in one shape repel each other, at a cost P.

\text{paired low: } 6 \text{ low}:\ 6(-0.4\Delta) = -2.4\Delta,\ \ 3 \text{ pairs} @@ tight
\text{spread: } 4 \text{ low}, 2 \text{ high}:\ -1.6\Delta + 1.2\Delta = -0.4\Delta,\ \ 1 \text{ pair} @@ loose
\text{paired low wins} \iff 2.4\Delta - 0.4\Delta > 2P @@ two extra pairs
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta > P:\ \text{pair up};\ \ \Delta < P:\ \text{spread out}} @@ which neighbours decides

Electrons pair up in the low shapes when the gap is larger than the cost of pairing, and spread out when it is smaller. Spread-out electrons each carry 38·phys’s spin, a tiny magnet, so the choice shows: iron with water is magnetic, iron with cyanide is not.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

This is crystal field theory, refined with bonding as ligand field theory. The two levels are eg (upper) and t2g (lower), named for their irreducible representations of the octahedral group Oh; Δ is Δo, the crystal field splitting energy. The gain is the crystal field stabilisation energy; the order of neighbours the spectrochemical series; paired and spread are low spin and high spin.

Who said it first

Hans Bethe worked out the splitting by group theory in 1929, and John Hasbrouck Van Vleck developed it for magnetism in the 1930s; Van Vleck shared the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physics. Ryutaro Tsuchida set out the spectrochemical series in 1938.
