29g · proof

Shortest paths

Rung 14 found the best value of a function by asking where its slope is 0. This step asks a bigger question. Among all paths between two points, which one is shortest? Which shape of wire lets a bead slide down fastest? The thing to make best is no longer a number chosen from a line, but a whole curve chosen from endlessly many. The same trick works: nudge the best curve a little, and the total cannot change at first. That gives one equation every best curve must obey. On a surface, it says what the surface’s straight lines are. And it pays back the triangle-total that 29f held in trust.

A curve that makes a total least obeys one equation, found by nudging it. On a surface, shortest paths turn only out of the surface, never along it, and a triangle of them has total bend equal to its excess.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 14a (a slope is 0 at a best point inside), 16e (parts), 28h (either order), 29d, 29e and 29f. 29l runs the equation by hand.

29g1The best-path equation

Stands on 14a, 16e, 28h, 11.

Let L(x, y, p) have continuous second partial slopes. For a curve y(x) from (a, A) to (b, B), its total is J[y] = ∫ₐᵇ L(x, y, y′) dx. A nudge is a function η with η(a) = η(b) = 0 and a continuous slope, so y + tη has the same ends for every t.

Best-path technique: if y makes J least (or greatest) among curves with the same ends, then d/dx (∂L/∂p) = ∂L/∂y along y. Here ∂L/∂p is the slope of L along its third input, taken at p = y′.

j(t) = J[y + t\eta] \text{ is least at } t = 0 @@ y + tη is one of the curves; y is best
j^{\prime}(0) = 0 @@ flat-top (14a)
j^{\prime}(0) = \int_a^b \big(L_y\,\eta + L_p\,\eta^{\prime}\big)\,dx @@ chains (28c) inside the total; the slope passes inside by either-order (28h)
\int_a^b L_p\,\eta^{\prime}\,dx = \big[L_p\,\eta\big]_a^b - \int_a^b \big(\tfrac{d}{dx}L_p\big)\eta\,dx @@ parts (16e)
\int_a^b \big(L_y - \tfrac{d}{dx}L_p\big)\,\eta\,dx = 0 \text{ for every nudge } \eta @@ η is 0 at both ends
h = L_y - \tfrac{d}{dx}L_p,\ \ h(c) > 0 \Rightarrow h > 0 \text{ on } (c - \delta, c + \delta) @@ continuity keeps the sign near c (11)
\eta = (x - c + \delta)^2(x - c - \delta)^2 \text{ there, } 0 \text{ elsewhere} \Rightarrow \int h\,\eta > 0 @@ a bump nudge, with a continuous slope
\htmlClass{new}{h = 0:\ \ \tfrac{d}{dx}\,\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial p} = \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial y}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for h(c) < 0; the line above contradicts the one before it

Check it on the flat plane. Length is ∫√(1 + y′²) dx, so L = √(1 + p²), with Ly = 0. The equation says y′/√(1 + y′²) is constant, so y′ is constant. The shortest curve between two points is a straight line, as it should be.

29g2Paths on a surface

Stands on 29g1, 29d1, 29e5.

On a surface, a path is two functions u(s), v(s), and its length is ∫ √(E u′² + 2F u′v′ + G v′²) ds (29d1). Nudge u alone, then v alone: each obeys the best-path equation. A path that obeys both is a straightest path of the surface. Accepted name: geodesic. Every shortest path is a straightest path.

Path-equations technique: in a ruler du² + g² dv², a shortest path traced at speed 1 obeys u″ = g gu v′² and (g² v′)′ = g gv v′².

\Lambda = \sqrt{u^{\prime 2} + g^2 v^{\prime 2}} = 1 \text{ along the path} @@ the length’s integrand, at speed 1
\Lambda_{u^{\prime}} = u^{\prime}/\Lambda,\ \ \Lambda_u = g\,g_u\,v^{\prime 2}/\Lambda @@ slopes of a square root (13)
\tfrac{d}{ds}\,u^{\prime} = g\,g_u\,v^{\prime 2} @@ best-path (29g1) for u, with Λ = 1 put in after the slopes
\Lambda_{v^{\prime}} = g^2 v^{\prime}/\Lambda,\ \ \Lambda_v = g\,g_v\,v^{\prime 2}/\Lambda @@ the same for v
\htmlClass{new}{u^{\prime\prime} = g\,g_u\,v^{\prime 2},\ \ (g^2 v^{\prime})^{\prime} = g\,g_v\,v^{\prime 2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ best-path for v

The curves v = constant, traced as u = s, obey both: v′ = 0 and u″ = 0. So the curves straight out are straightest. They are shortest too: any path from u = a to u = b has length ∫√(u′² + g² v′²) ds ≥ ∫|u′| ds ≥ b − a. On the sphere, with u the distance from the north pole, this proves the meridians are shortest, and so, by turning the sphere, every arc of a great circle shorter than half of it.

29g3Turning along a straightest path

Stands on 29g2.

Measure the heading of a path by ψ, the angle from the straight-out direction ru to the path’s direction. At speed 1, u′ = cos ψ and g v′ = sin ψ.

Heading technique: along a straightest path in the ruler du² + g² dv², the heading changes by dψ = −gu dv.

u^{\prime} = \cos\psi,\ \ v^{\prime} = \sin\psi / g @@ the two directions r_u and r_v/g are at right angles and of length 1
u^{\prime\prime} = -\sin\psi\;\psi^{\prime} @@ chain technique (13, 21)
-\sin\psi\;\psi^{\prime} = g\,g_u\,\frac{\sin^2\psi}{g^2} @@ path-equations (29g2)
\psi^{\prime} = -g_u\,\frac{\sin\psi}{g} = -g_u\,v^{\prime} @@ divide by −sin ψ, where it is not 0
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{d\psi}{dv} = -g_u} \quad \blacksquare @@ chains: dψ/dv = ψ′/v′

On the flat plane in polar coordinates g = u, so dψ = −dv: a straight line crossing the rays from the origin turns, relative to them, exactly as fast as the rays fan out.

29g4A triangle’s total is its excess

Stands on 29g2, 29g3, 29e5, 29f3.

Round a point A of a surface, the straightest paths leaving A at speed 1 in every direction give a straight-out ruler du² + g² dv² near A, with g = 0 and gu = 1 at A, as in polar coordinates on the flat plane. The basement says where this comes from.

Triangle-total technique: a triangle with straightest sides, small enough to sit in such a ruler round one corner, has total bend equal to its excess α + β + γ − π.

A \text{ at } u = 0;\ AB \text{ along } v = 0,\ AC \text{ along } v = \alpha;\ BC:\ u = h(v) @@ the straight-out ruler round A
\iint K\,dA = \int_0^{\alpha}\!\!\int_0^{h(v)} -\frac{g_{uu}}{g}\,g\,du\,dv @@ straight-out (29e5), ruler-area (29d2)
= \int_0^{\alpha} \big(g_u(0, v) - g_u(h(v), v)\big)\,dv = \alpha - \int_0^{\alpha} g_u(h(v), v)\,dv @@ g_u undoes g_uu (16); g_u = 1 at A
-\int_0^{\alpha} g_u\,dv = \psi_C - \psi_B @@ heading technique (29g3) along BC
\beta = \pi - \psi_B,\ \ \gamma = \psi_C @@ BA points along −r_u at B; CA along −r_u at C
\htmlClass{new}{\iint_T K\,dA = \alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

This is what 29f held in trust. Cut the surface finely, so each triangle is small enough, and the excess-count and hole-count techniques stand on proved ground. On the sphere it agrees with 29b: the total bend of a triangle is its area times 1/R², which is its excess.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Choosing a best curve is the calculus of variations, and J is a functional. The best-path equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation, and a nudge is a variation. The fact that ∫hη = 0 for every nudge forces h = 0 is the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations. Straightest paths are geodesics. The straight-out ruler round a point is geodesic polar coordinates; that the straightest paths from A cross the circles round A at right angles, so that F = 0 there, is Gauss’s lemma. The triangle-total technique is the local Gauss–Bonnet theorem for geodesic triangles.

Who said it first

Johann Bernoulli posed the fastest-slide problem in 1696. Leonhard Euler found the equation for general problems in 1744, by cutting curves into many straight pieces, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange found the nudging method in a letter to Euler in 1755; Euler named the subject the calculus of variations in 1766. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the geodesic triangle form, and the lemma about polar coordinates, in 1827. Karl Weierstrass showed in 1870 that a best curve need not exist, which sent the subject back to its foundations.

Where the straight-out ruler comes from

Leave A along every direction at speed 1 on straightest paths. Rung 24’s existence technique, carried to pairs of functions, gives one path for each direction. Call the distance travelled u and the starting direction v. These are coordinates near A, and E = 1 since each path has speed 1. That F = 0 is Gauss’s lemma: nudging a straightest path’s direction does not change its length at first (29g1), and the slope of F along u is exactly that first change. So the ruler is du² + g² dv², and near A it looks like the flat plane’s polar one, g ≈ u.

The seam closer

In 29g.4.5, the mathematician of 1851 is Bernhard Riemann, and the claim he trusted is Dirichlet’s principle. The total of 1870 is Karl Weierstrass’s example. David Hilbert rescued the principle in 1900, under extra demands on the total and on the curves allowed.
