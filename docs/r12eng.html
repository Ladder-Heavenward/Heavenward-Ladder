12·eng · engineering

Will the shelf hold?

Will the shelf hold? Whoever hangs a shelf asks it before the books go on. So does a builder laying a plank across a scaffold, and an engineer drawing a floor. The one sure test is to load the thing until it breaks, and that destroys it. Three things make the question hard. The load moves: books shift, and a painter walks along the plank. The wood varies from board to board. And no measurement is exact. Engineers answer with three habits. Find where the load does the most harm. Check that worst case against what the part can take. Keep a margin for everything nobody measured. This page builds the three habits from 12·phys's turning effects. Rung 12 earns its keep twice. The highest-and-lowest technique promises a worst case before anything is computed. And 12e's rounding says which way to round.

A plank on two supports presses on them in the reverse ratio of the load's distances to them. A single load bends the wood under it with a turning effect that grows with its distance from each support, and is greatest when the load stands at the middle: a quarter of the load times the span. A load spread evenly over the span bends it most at the middle too, by an eighth of the total load times the span. A part passes when what it can take is at least the safety factor times the worst load. Round loads up and strengths down.

The words

From here on, on this page, a beam is a stiff bar, as in 12·phys, held up at two or more places along it. Each such place is a support. A plank across two trestles, a shelf on two brackets and a floor joist resting on two walls are all beams. The span is the distance between two supports. The load is everything the beam carries, its own weight included.

From here on, on this page, the push of a support is the force, in newtons, with which it holds the beam up. Its accepted name is the support's reaction. A load spread evenly along a beam is given by its weight per metre, w, in N/m. Thirty kilograms of books on each metre of shelf is w ≈ 30 · 9.8 = 294 N/m.

Page letters, said out loud. P is a load at one place, in newtons (not 7·phys's power). d is where it stands, measured from the left support A, at 0. L is the span, so the right support B is at L (12·phys's L was a bar's length). R_A and R_B are the pushes of A and B. w is a spread load per metre. M(x) is the turning effect at a cut, defined below (not 12c's greatest value).

The physics premise comes from 12·phys. A beam at rest does not move and does not turn. So two things are true. The forces on it balance: the pushes up add to the loads down. And the turning effects about the place of any support add to 0, with each push counted as a weight below 0, as 12·phys.3.1b counted a balloon. A beam at rest is a lever that balances, with its supports pushing as weights below 0. The premise has the accepted name static equilibrium.

How hard each support pushes

Support-pushes technique: a load shares itself between two supports in the reverse ratio of its distances to them. Precisely: a load P at d, on a beam with supports at 0 and L, gives the pushes R_B = Pd/L and R_A = P(L − d)/L. The beam's own weight is set aside here; it adds its own pushes, found the same way.

P\,(d - 0) + (-R_A)(0 - 0) + (-R_B)(L - 0) = 0 @@ turning effects about A add to 0 (the premise; 12·phys's turning effect W(x − p) with p = 0)
Pa - R_B L = 0 @@ 5i's collecting; the push at A has distance 0
R_B = \tfrac{Pa}{L} @@ adding-keeps-answers, then multiplying-keeps-answers by 1/L (5g); L > 0
R_A + R_B = P @@ the forces balance (the premise)
\htmlClass{new}{R_A = \tfrac{P(L - d)}{L}} \quad \blacksquare @@ adding-keeps-answers; one bottom (5h)

Read it against 12·phys's balance-formula. Treat the two pushes as weights at 0 and L. Their balance point is (R_A · 0 + R_B · L)/(R_A + R_B) = Pd/P = d. The load stands at the balance point of the pushes that hold it. So the technique is the balance-formula run backwards.

P = 90 \cdot 9.8 = 882 \text{ N},\ \ d = 0.6 \text{ m},\ \ L = 2.4 \text{ m} @@ a painter with tools, a quarter of the way along a plank on two trestles
R_B = \tfrac{882 \cdot 0.6}{2.4} = \htmlClass{new}{220.5} \text{ N} @@ support-pushes
R_A = 882 - 220.5 = \htmlClass{new}{661.5} \text{ N} @@ the nearer trestle takes three quarters: 1.8 : 0.6 = 3 : 1

Where the wood is bent hardest

Picture the plank sawn through at a place x. The left piece would turn about the cut. What stops it is the wood at x, which holds the two pieces together. So the wood at x carries a turning effect, equal and opposite to that of everything left of the cut. The greater it is, the harder the wood there is bent. A board breaks where that turning effect first passes what the wood can take.

From here on, on this page, the turning effect at the cut x is M(x): the net turning effect about x of every force on the beam left of x, with a push up counted positive. For a single load P at d, M(x) = R_A x when x ≤ d, and M(x) = R_A x − P(x − d) when x ≥ d. Its accepted name is the bending moment, measured in N · m.

x \le d:\ \ M(x) = R_A\,x @@ only the push at A lies left of the cut
x \ge d:\ \ M(x) = R_A x - P(x - d) = Pa - R_B x = R_B(L - x) @@ collect; R_A − P = −R_B; Pa = R_B L (support-pushes)
M(d) = R_A d = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{P\,d(L - d)}{L}} @@ both pieces agree at d: a joint (11e), so M is continuous on [0, L]

Both pieces are linear functions (9e). The first rises from 0 at A, the second falls to 0 at B, and they meet under the load. For one load, the wood is bent hardest right under it. For the painter, M(0.6) = 661.5 · 0.6 = 396.9 N · m.

The worst place for a load that moves

The painter walks. Where is she most dangerous? Before computing anything, rung 12 says there is an answer. Wherever she stands, the hardest bend along the plank is g(d) = Pd(L − d)/L, a polynomial in d. So g is continuous (11d) on the closed interval [0, L], and highest-and-lowest promises a place where it is greatest. Completing the square finds it.

Worst-place technique: a single load does the most harm at the middle of the span, where it bends the wood with a turning effect of PL/4.

g(d) = \tfrac{P}{L}\,d(L - d),\ \ 0 \le d \le L @@ the hardest bend, with the load at d
d(L - d) = \tfrac{L^2}{4} - \big(d - \tfrac L2\big)^2 @@ completing the square (8i)
g(d) = \tfrac PL\Big(\tfrac{L^2}{4} - \big(d - \tfrac L2\big)^2\Big) \le \tfrac PL \cdot \tfrac{L^2}{4} @@ square-never-negative; P/L > 0 keeps the order
\htmlClass{new}{g(d) \le \tfrac{PL}{4}},\ \text{equal exactly at } d = \tfrac L2 \quad \blacksquare @@ a square is 0 only at 0
P = 882,\ L = 2.4:\ \ \tfrac{PL}{4} = \htmlClass{new}{529.2} \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} @@ a third more than at a = 0.6

The promise matters more than the formula. With two painters, or a pile of paint tins, there is no tidy g. The physics premise of 12·phys is still true: moving the loads a little changes every turning effect a little. So the hardest bend is still continuous in where the loads stand, on a closed interval of places, and a worst place still exists. The engineer can then search for it, by trying places or by halving. A search for a worst case that might not exist could go on finding worse cases for ever. 12c's x on [0, 1) does exactly that.

A load spread out

Books lie all along a shelf. By 12·phys's premise, a stretch of even load turns things as if all of it sat at its middle. A load w per metre over the span [0, L] weighs wL and acts at L/2. So by support-pushes each support pushes wL/2. Left of a cut at x lies a stretch of length x, weighing wx, acting at x/2.

Spread-load technique: a load spread evenly over a span bends the wood hardest at the middle, with a turning effect of wL²/8.

M(x) = \tfrac{wL}{2}\,x - wx \cdot \big(x - \tfrac x2\big) @@ the push at A, and the books left of the cut acting at their middle
= \tfrac w2\,x(L - x) @@ collect (5i)
= \tfrac w2\Big(\tfrac{L^2}{4} - \big(x - \tfrac L2\big)^2\Big) @@ completing the square, as before
\htmlClass{new}{M(x) \le \tfrac{wL^2}{8}},\ \text{equal exactly at } x = \tfrac L2 \quad \blacksquare @@ square-never-negative

Houses in the United States are framed with upright wall studs 16 inches apart, about 0.406 m. Shelf brackets are screwed into studs, so a common span is two gaps, about 0.81 m. A metre of hardback books weighs roughly 20 to 30 kg.

w = 30 \cdot 9.8 = 294 \text{ N/m},\ \ L = 0.81 \text{ m} @@ the heavier end of the range: see "rounding the wrong way" below
R_A = R_B = \tfrac{294 \cdot 0.81}{2} \approx 119.1 \text{ N} @@ spread-load: each bracket takes half
\tfrac{wL^2}{8} = \tfrac{294 \cdot 0.6561}{8} \approx \htmlClass{new}{24.1} \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} @@ at the middle of the shelf

The square of the span matters. Double the span and the hardest bend is four times as great, on the same books per metre. That is why long shelves sag in the middle and short ones do not.

The margin: a safety factor

From here on, on this page, the capacity of a part is the greatest load, or turning effect, it can take without failing. It is measured by breaking samples. The safety factor of a design is the ratio capacity : worst load, read as the fraction capacity/worst. A design passes a safety factor s when the part's capacity is at least s times the worst load it will meet.

Why a margin at all? Boards of one kind break at different loads. People overload what they were told not to. Wood weakens with damp and with years. And every calculation on this page simplified something. The United States' workplace safety rules ask each part of a scaffold to hold its own weight and at least 4 times the greatest load it is meant to carry. The ropes that hang a scaffold must hold at least 6 times.

\text{own weight } 15 \text{ kg}:\ \ w = \tfrac{147}{2.4} = 61.25 \text{ N/m},\ \ \tfrac{wL^2}{8} = 44.1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} @@ spread-load, at the middle
\text{the painter at worst}:\ \ \tfrac{PL}{4} = 529.2 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} @@ worst-place, also at the middle
44.1 + 4 \cdot 529.2 = \htmlClass{new}{2160.9} \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} @@ turning effects of separate loads add (each is 12·phys's Σ); both are greatest at the same place
\text{capacity} \ge 2400 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m} \Rightarrow \text{passes} @@ if tests break planks of this kind at no less than 2400 N · m

Rounding the wrong way, on purpose

Every number in a check is rounded somewhere. Engineers round each one the way that makes the check harder to pass. Loads round up and strengths round down. Books at 20 to 30 kg per metre are taken as 30. A test series that broke planks at 2437, 2511 and 2480 N · m is written as 2400, below the least of them.

12e met the same choice. A trap [1.414, 1.4145] for √2 can be reported cut off or rounded, and you say which. An engineer whose halving traps a worst turning effect in [2160, 2161] N · m writes 2161. One whose halving traps a capacity in [2400, 2401] writes 2400. The reason is the cost of each mistake. A shelf that looks weaker than it is costs a few more screws. A shelf that looks stronger than it is falls down.

When a push turns into a pull

Support-pushes can be used for a load past a support too, with d > L. Then R_A = P(L − d)/L is below 0: A would have to pull the plank down. A trestle cannot pull. So the plank lifts off A and tips. Here only the plank's own weight, on the other side of B, holds it down.

\begin{aligned}&\text{plank } 2.7 \text{ m, trestles at } 0 \text{ and } 2.4,\\ &\quad \ \ 147 \text{ N at its middle } 1.35\end{aligned} @@ a 0.3 m overhang at the right
\text{about } B:\ \ 147(1.35 - 2.4) + 882(2.7 - 2.4) + (-R_A)(0 - 2.4) = 0 @@ the premise, with the painter at the very end
-154.35 + 264.6 + 2.4R_A = 0 @@ collect
R_A \approx \htmlClass{new}{-45.9} \text{ N} @@ below 0: A must pull, and cannot. The plank tips

The same rules limit how far a plank may stick out past its support, unless it is fixed down. A plank must reach at least 6 inches past the middle of its support, so it cannot slip off. A short plank may reach at most 12 inches past it, so it cannot tip.

\text{a negative push is a small push}\,? @@ it is a pull the support must supply; a trestle cannot, and the plank tips

Running it backwards

Handed the two pushes, find where a single load stands: d = L · R_B/(R_A + R_B). Handed a worst turning effect, find the load: P = 4 · worst/L, or w = 8 · worst/L². Handed a capacity and a safety factor, find the greatest load the part may carry: divide the capacity by the factor first. Handed a span that fails, shorten it: the bend falls with the square of the span.

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

The scientist of 1638 in 12·eng.4.4 is Galileo Galilei, in Two New Sciences, whose Second Day is the first book on the strength of beams. He took the wood of the whole break to pull equally hard, which made his beams about three times too strong. Edme Mariotte broke beams of wood and glass in the 1670s and 1680s and found them weaker than Galileo's figure. The full account of how the pull is shared across a break came only in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Galileo asks why a large beam breaks under its own weight where a small one of the same shape stands, and answers that strength grows more slowly than weight as all the sizes grow together.

Galileo Galilei, Two New Sciences (1638), Second Day; paraphrase

Accepted names

The push of a support is the reaction. The turning effect at a cut is the bending moment, and its graph along the beam the bending moment diagram. A beam on two end supports is simply supported; one fixed at one end only, sticking out, is a cantilever. A beam with more supports than the premise can solve for, as in 12·eng.3.1c, is statically indeterminate. The scaffold rules quoted are the United States' Occupational Safety and Health Administration's, in its scaffold standard for construction.
