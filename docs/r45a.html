45a · technique

Rings Whose Ideals Are Finitely Made

In the whole numbers and in polynomials of one letter, every ideal has one maker (44c). With two letters that fails: the polynomials with no constant term in x and y need both x and y. What survives is weaker and enough: every ideal is made by finitely many members. This step shows that finitely made ideals and rising chains that stop are the same condition, and finds a ring where both fail.

A ring has finitely made ideals exactly when every rising chain of ideals I₁ ⊆ I₂ ⊆ ⋯ stops rising. One letter: one maker. Endlessly many letters x₁, x₂, …: the chain (x₁) ⊆ (x₁, x₂) ⊆ ⋯ rises for ever.

45a1Finitely Made

(a_1, \ldots, a_k) = \{r_1a_1 + \cdots + r_ka_k\} @@ the smallest ideal holding them (44b)
\text{in } F[x]:\ (x^2 - 1,\ x^3 - 1) = (x - 1) @@ one maker, the highest common divisor (44c)
\text{in } F[x, y]:\ (x, y) \ne (g) \text{ for any } g @@ g would divide x and y, so g is a constant
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y) \text{ is finitely made, by two}} @@ two letters, two makers

An ideal is finitely made when finitely many of its members make it; in polynomials of two letters, one maker is not enough, but finitely many can be.

45a2Rising Chains Stop

U = I_1 \cup I_2 \cup \cdots \text{ is an ideal} @@ any two members lie in one Iₙ
U = (a_1, \ldots, a_k),\ \text{each } a_j \in I_{n_j} @@ if every ideal is finitely made
U \subseteq I_N,\ N = \max n_j \Rightarrow I_N = I_{N+1} = \cdots @@ the chain stops
\htmlClass{new}{\text{conversely: an ideal not finitely made gives a chain that never stops}} @@ add one member at a time

Finite-maker technique: every ideal of R is finitely made exactly when every rising chain of ideals in R stops rising after finitely many steps.

45a3A Ring Where Chains Rise for Ever

(x_1) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2) \subsetneq (x_1, x_2, x_3) \subsetneq \cdots @@ each new letter is outside
x_{n+1} \notin (x_1, \ldots, x_n) @@ set x₁ = ⋯ = xₙ = 0: x_{n+1} is not 0
(x_1, x_2, \ldots) \text{ is not finitely made} @@ by the technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{finitely many letters will be needed}} @@ the next step

With endlessly many letters an ideal can need endlessly many makers; with finitely many, it never does, which is the next step.

45a4Moves to Watch Out For

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

\text{every ideal of } F[x, y] \text{ has one maker}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> (x, y) needs two (45a1)
\text{a union of ideals is never an ideal}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-a">out of arrangement</span> a rising union is one (45a2)
\text{chains stopping means ideals have one maker}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> finitely many makers, not one (45a2)
x_{n+1} \in (x_1, \ldots, x_n)\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-s">out of sequence</span> setting the first n letters to 0 leaves x_{n+1} (45a3)
\text{the condition fails in } \mathbb Z\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> ℤ has one maker for every ideal (45a1)

Accepted names

A ring whose ideals are finitely made is Noetherian; rising chains stopping is the ascending chain condition. (a₁, …, aₖ) is the ideal generated by them.

Who said it first

Emmy Noether made the chain condition the centre of ring theory in her paper on ideal theory of 1921.
