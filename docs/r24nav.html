Can a Submarine Know Where It Is Without Looking Out?

Can a submarine know where it is without looking out? Deep under the ice there are no stars, no radio and no coast. A submarine can still feel every push it is given: a weight on a spring inside a sealed box stretches whenever the boat speeds up. Add up the pushes and you have the speed; add up the speed and you have the place. This page does that with rung 24’s rate statements, finds how fast a tiny error in the spring grows, finds that a slight tilt is far worse, and then finds a remarkable cure: level the box by the place it computes, and the error stops growing and swings back and forth every 84 minutes.

Position obeys x″ = a with a known start, so x is the twice-taken area under a. A steady error b in a grows the position error as ½bt². A tilt θ makes the box read gθ of gravity as a push. If the box is kept level by the computed position, an error y tilts it by y/R and feeds back −(g/R)y: y″ = −(g/R)y swings with period 2π√(R/g), about 84 minutes, instead of growing.

24nav1Adding Up the Pushes

Jean navigates a submarine. A sealed box holds a small weight on a spring for each direction; how far the spring stretches gives the boat’s acceleration a(t) along it. From the start, the speed and the place follow from rate statements.

v\prime = a,\ \ v(0) = v_0 @@ speed from acceleration: an initial value problem (16f)
v(t) = v_0 + \textstyle\int_0^t a @@ area is a change (16c)
x\prime = v,\ \ x(0) = x_0 @@ place from speed
\htmlClass{new}{x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \textstyle\int_0^t\!\int_0^s a} @@ the place, from pushes alone

From here on, on this page, the error is the computed place less the true place. The box never looks outside, so every error in a is added up twice.

24nav2A Small Steady Error

Say the spring reads a little too much, by a steady b = 0.001 m/s², about a ten-thousandth of gravity.

e\prime\prime = b,\ \ e(0) = 0,\ e\prime(0) = 0 @@ the error’s own statement
e(t) = \tfrac12 b t^2 @@ take the area twice (16c)
t = 1 \text{ hour} = 3600 \text{ s}:\ e = \tfrac12 \cdot 0.001 \cdot 3600^2 @@ 3600² is about 13 million
\htmlClass{new}{e \approx 6.5 \text{ km after an hour}} @@ and four times that after two

Twice-added technique: a steady error in acceleration grows the error in place as the square of the time. A box good enough for an hour is four times worse for two.

24nav3Gravity Looks Like a Push

A weight on a spring cannot tell gravity from a push. If the box tilts by a small angle θ, part of gravity lies along a sideways spring, and the box reads it as the boat speeding up.

\text{sideways reading} = g\sin\theta \approx g\theta @@ sin h / h → 1 (21f)
\theta = 0.001:\ g\theta \approx 0.0098 \text{ m/s}^2 @@ ten times the spring error of 24nav2
e = \tfrac12 g\theta t^2 @@ the twice-added technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after an hour: about } 64 \text{ km}} @@ from a tilt of a sixteenth of a degree

The box must be held level to within a tiny angle, or gravity floods the reading. Wheels spinning fast inside the box, gyroscopes, hold it steady as the boat rolls. But level by what? The earth is round, so “down” turns as the boat travels.

24nav4Levelled by Its Own Answer

Keep the box square to the line from the computed place to the earth’s centre. Then an error y in place, along the ground, tilts the box from true level by y/R, R the earth’s radius. That tilt reads as a push of g·y/R, aimed back toward the truth.

\text{tilt} = y/R @@ an arc over a radius (21a)
y\prime\prime = -\tfrac{g}{R}\,y @@ the tilt’s push pulls the error back
y = A\cos(nt) + \tfrac{B}{n}\sin(nt),\ \ n = \sqrt{g/R} @@ the steady-swing technique (24h)
2\pi\sqrt{R/g} = 2\pi\sqrt{6\,371\,000/9.81} \text{ s} @@ R in metres, g in m/s²
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 84 \text{ minutes}} @@ the error swings, and does not grow

Levelling technique: a box kept level by its own computed place turns an error in place into a swing with period 2π√(R/g), about 84 minutes, instead of a growth. 84 minutes is also the time for a pendulum as long as the earth’s radius, and for a craft skimming the ground in orbit.

24nav5Errors That Still Grow

The swing tames errors in level, not all errors. The spinning wheels drift slowly, and a drift in heading is not pulled back by gravity.

\text{heading error } \varepsilon = \omega t @@ a steady drift ω
\text{sideways speed error} = v\,\varepsilon = v\omega t @@ running a little off course
\text{sideways place error} = \tfrac12 v\omega t^2 @@ area is a change (16c)
\htmlClass{new}{v = 250 \text{ m/s},\ \omega = 0.01°/\text{h},\ 10 \text{ h}:\ \approx 7.9 \text{ km}} @@ an airliner over an ocean

Inertial navigation is excellent over minutes and drifts over hours, so it is always mixed with fixes from outside: a star, a radio signal, a known sea floor. Between fixes it carries the place; at each fix it is set right. How best to mix the two is a question for a later rung.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Finding the place from felt pushes is inertial navigation; the spring is an accelerometer and the spinning wheels gyroscopes. A steady error in a reading is a bias, and a slow turn of a gyro is drift. Levelling by the computed place is Schuler tuning, and its 84 minutes the Schuler period. Mixing inertial navigation with outside fixes is sensor fusion, often by a Kalman filter.

Who said it first

Max Schuler showed in 1923 that a device kept level by its own motion over the round earth swings with a period of about 84 minutes. Charles Stark Draper’s laboratory built the inertial systems that guided submarines under the polar ice in the 1950s and the Apollo craft to the moon.
