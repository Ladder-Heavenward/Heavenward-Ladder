28l · technique

Polynomials That Hug a Surface, by Hand

Rung twenty three hugged a curve with a polynomial that matched its height and its slopes at one point. A surface can be hugged the same way. The flat sheet of the last step matches the height and the two partial slopes. One more layer matches the second slopes too: how fast each partial slope changes along each knob. Those second slopes sit in a small square grid, and since the order of the knobs does not matter, the grid is the same when flipped across its middle. The grid decides what a flat spot looks like. A bowl has a bottom, a dome has a top, and a mountain pass is a saddle: the lowest point on the ridge, and the highest point of the road through it. Two numbers of the grid tell the three apart. The step ends with a ceiling on how wrong the hugging polynomial can be.

Walk along a straight line through the point, and the surface becomes a curve, which rung twenty three already knows how to hug. Gathered back, the second slopes make a square grid. At a flat point, the grid’s corner and its determinant tell a bottom from a top from a saddle. Bounds on the next slopes cap the error.

28l1The Hug of Degree 2

Let f(x, y) have second partial slopes fxx, fxy, fyx, fyy (28d1), all continuous near p. Then fx and fy are smoothly sloped (28b3), and fxy = fyx (28d2). The second-slope grid of f at p is

\(H(p) = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx}(p) & f_{xy}(p) \\ f_{xy}(p) & f_{yy}(p) \end{pmatrix},\quad h^{\mathsf T}H(p)\,h = f_{xx}h_1^2 + 2f_{xy}h_1h_2 + f_{yy}h_2^2.\)

It is the same when flipped across its middle. Its number hᵀH(p)h, for an arrow h = (h₁, h₂), is the second slope of f along h.

Surface-match technique: if the second partial slopes of f are continuous on a disc around p, then f(p + h) = f(p) + ∇f(p) · h + ½ hᵀH(p)h + E(h), where E(h)/|h|² heads to 0 as h heads to 0. The polynomial of degree 2 in h, without E, is the hug of f at p.

g(t) = f(p + th),\ \ 0 \le t \le 1 @@ f along the segment from p to p + h, inside the disc
g'(t) = f_x(p + th)\,h_1 + f_y(p + th)\,h_2 @@ the roads-add technique (28c3)
g''(t) = h^{\mathsf T}H(p + th)\,h @@ roads-add again, on f_x and f_y; f_xy = f_yx (28d2)
g(1) = g(0) + g'(0) + \tfrac12 g''(c),\ \ 0 < c < 1 @@ the match-error technique (23b1) with n = 1
E(h) = \tfrac12\,h^{\mathsf T}\big(H(p + ch) - H(p)\big)h @@ subtract the hug
|E(h)| \le \tfrac12\big(|\Delta_{xx}| + 2|\Delta_{xy}| + |\Delta_{yy}|\big)|h|^2 @@ Δ the changes in the entries; |h₁|, |h₂| ≤ |h|
\htmlClass{new}{E(h)/|h|^2 \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ each Δ heads to 0: the second slopes are continuous at p (28a1)
  
f = e^x\cos y,\ \ p = (0, 0):\ \ f = 1,\ \ \nabla f = (e^x\cos y,\ -e^x\sin y) = (1, 0) @@ the one-knob technique (28a2)
f_{xx} = e^x\cos y = 1,\ \ f_{xy} = -e^x\sin y = 0,\ \ f_{yy} = -e^x\cos y = -1 @@ at (0, 0)
f(x, y) \approx 1 + x + \tfrac12(x^2 - y^2) @@ the surface-match technique
\htmlClass{new}{f(0.1, 0.2) = 1.0831;\ \ \text{the hug gives } 1.085} \quad \blacksquare @@ the flat sheet 1 + x gives 1.1: the hug is ten times closer

The hug of eˣ cos y is the product of the hugs of eˣ and of cos y from 23h, cut off at degree 2: (1 + x + x²/2)(1 − y²/2) with the terms of degree 3 and 4 dropped. Building hugs from known hugs works on surfaces too, and is often quicker than taking slopes.

28l2Top, Bottom or Saddle

A flat point of f is a point p where ∇f(p) = 0: the tangent plane there is level. A saddle is a flat point where f is higher than f(p) along one line through p and lower along another, at points as near p as you like, as on a mountain pass.

Grid-test technique: let the second partial slopes of f be continuous near a flat point p. Let A = fxx(p), B = fxy(p), C = fyy(p), and D = AC − B², the determinant of H(p). If D > 0 and A > 0, f has a bottom at p. If D > 0 and A < 0, a top. If D < 0, a saddle. If D = 0, this test cannot tell.

Q(h) = Ah_1^2 + 2Bh_1h_2 + Ch_2^2 = A\big(h_1 + \tfrac{B}{A}h_2\big)^2 + \tfrac{D}{A}h_2^2 @@ complete the square; A ≠ 0, since AC > B² ≥ 0
Q(h) > 0 \text{ for } h \ne 0 @@ two squares with positive weights: 0 only when h₂ = 0 and then h₁ = 0
Q(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) \ge m > 0 \text{ for every } \theta @@ the highest-and-lowest technique (12c) on [0, 2π]: the least value is reached, and it is positive
Q(h) \ge m\,|h|^2 @@ Q(sh) = s²Q(h); write h = |h|(cos θ, sin θ)
f(p + h) - f(p) = \tfrac12 Q(h) + E(h) \ge \tfrac12 m|h|^2 - \tfrac14 m|h|^2 @@ the surface-match technique (28l1) with ∇f(p) = 0; |E(h)| ≤ ¼m|h|² once |h| is small
\htmlClass{new}{f(p + h) > f(p) \text{ for } 0 < |h| \text{ small: a bottom}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for the top, the same lines for −f

The saddle. If D < 0 and A ≠ 0, then Q(1, 0) = A and Q(−B, A) = A(AC − B²) = AD have opposite signs. If A = 0, then B ≠ 0, and Q(1, s) = 2Bs + Cs² changes sign with s near 0. Along an arrow h with Q(h) > 0, the curve g(t) = f(p + th) has g′(0) = 0 and g″(0) = Q(h) > 0, so a bottom at t = 0 by the second-test technique (14f2). Along an arrow with Q(h) < 0 it has a top. So p is a saddle.

f = x^3 + y^3 - 3xy:\ \ \nabla f = (3x^2 - 3y,\ 3y^2 - 3x) = 0 @@ y = x², then x⁴ = x
(0, 0) \text{ and } (1, 1) @@ the only real answers
H = \begin{pmatrix} 6x & -3 \\ -3 & 6y \end{pmatrix}:\ \ D(0,0) = -9,\ \ D(1,1) = 36 - 9 = 27 @@ the second partial slopes
(0, 0): D < 0,\ \text{a saddle};\ \ f(t, -t) = 3t^2,\ \ f(t, t) = 2t^3 - 3t^2 @@ higher along one line, lower along the other
\htmlClass{new}{(1, 1): D > 0,\ A = 6 > 0,\ \text{a bottom},\ f = -1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the grid-test technique

28l3How Wrong the Hug Can Be

23b2 put a ceiling on the error of a matching polynomial from a bound on the next slope. The same works on a surface, one line at a time.

Hug-ceiling technique: let f be smoothly sloped to the order needed on a disc holding p and p + h. If every second partial slope has size at most K there, the sheet’s error is at most ½K(|h₁| + |h₂|)². If every third partial slope has size at most K there, the hug’s error is at most K(|h₁| + |h₂|)³/6.

g(t) = f(p + th):\ \ g''(t) = \textstyle\sum_{i,j} f_{ij}(p + th)\,h_ih_j @@ as in 28l1: two roads each time, so four terms
|g''(t)| \le K\,\textstyle\sum_{i,j}|h_i||h_j| = K(|h_1| + |h_2|)^2 @@ every second partial slope has size at most K
g^{\prime\prime\prime}(t) = \textstyle\sum_{i,j,k} f_{ijk}(p + th)\,h_ih_jh_k,\ \ |g^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)| \le K(|h_1| + |h_2|)^3 @@ roads-add once more (28c3): eight terms
\text{sheet error} = \tfrac12 g''(c),\ \ \text{hug error} = \tfrac16 g^{\prime\prime\prime}(c_2) @@ the match-error technique (23b1) with n = 1 and n = 2
\htmlClass{new}{|\text{sheet error}| \le \tfrac12K(|h_1| + |h_2|)^2,\ \ |\text{hug error}| \le \tfrac16K(|h_1| + |h_2|)^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the error-ceiling technique (23b2), once each
  
\text{third partial slopes of } e^x\cos y \text{ are } \pm e^x\cos y,\ \pm e^x\sin y @@ each slope turns cos into −sin or sin into cos, and keeps eˣ
K = e^{0.1} \approx 1.1052 \text{ on } 0 \le x \le 0.1 @@ |cos|, |sin| ≤ 1, and eˣ rises
\tfrac16 K(0.1 + 0.2)^3 \approx 0.0050 @@ the hug-ceiling technique
\htmlClass{new}{|1.08314 - 1.085| \approx 0.0019 \le 0.0050} \quad \blacksquare @@ 28l1’s hug, inside its fence

The fence holds the true value without knowing it. That is what makes a hug safe to use: the answer comes with a number that says how far off it can be, and the number shrinks like the cube of the step.

Harder questions that rely on your own making. The step is complete without them; the rung's angel wings need them.

28l.4.1buildBuild a polynomial f of degree 2 whose only flat point is (1, −2), a saddle, with f(1, −2) = 5.

28l.4.2edgeEach has D = 0 at its flat point (0, 0). Decide each: bottom, top or saddle, in the sense of 28l2.

28l.4.3converseTurn the grid-test technique round: at a bottom, with continuous second slopes, must D > 0? Prove that A ≥ 0, C ≥ 0 and D ≥ 0 there, and find a bottom with D = 0.

The accepted names

The second-slope grid is the Hessian matrix, and its determinant D the Hessian (determinant). The hug is the second-order Taylor polynomial of f at p, and the surface-match technique is Taylor’s theorem in two variables. The number hᵀHh is a quadratic form; a form positive for every h ≠ 0 is positive definite, and completing the square to show it is the idea behind the Cholesky factorization. The grid-test technique is the second derivative test. A flat point is a critical (or stationary) point, and a saddle a saddle point. Bottoms and tops among neighbours are local minima and maxima.

Who said it first

Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the conditions for a maximum or minimum of a function of two variables in 1759, by completing the square. Otto Hesse studied the determinant of second derivatives in 1844, and James Joseph Sylvester named it after him in 1851. Giuseppe Peano showed in 1884, in his edition of Angelo Genocchi’s calculus, that a function can have a minimum along every line through a point and still no minimum there; his example was (y − x²)(y − 2x²).
