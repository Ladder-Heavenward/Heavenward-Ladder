Why do stone arches stand?

Why do stone arches stand? A stone arch has no glue worth the name. Its blocks are cut, stacked on a wooden frame, and the frame is taken away. Some of those arches have stood for two thousand years. Stone is strong when it is squeezed and weak when it is pulled: a block of granite holds up a tower kilometres tall, yet snaps under a pull a tenth as hard. So an arch must be shaped so that every block is only ever pushed. Builders found such shapes by eye and by rule of thumb, and many arches fell while they learned. What makes the question hard is that the push inside the stone cannot be seen. This page shows, with rung twenty-five’s numbers of the plane and rung twenty-four’s rules on rates, what shape a hanging chain takes and why, why the same shape turned upside down only pushes, why a bridge deck turns the chain into a parabola, and why an arch stands by its shape far more than by its strength.

A force is a number of the plane, and the forces on anything still add to nothing. Split the pull along a hanging chain into its across part and its up part. The across part is the same all along, since a weight pulls straight down. The up part grows by the weight of each piece, and that is a rule on the chain’s rates with one answer. Turn the whole picture upside down and turn every force around: the forces still add to nothing, and every pull has become a push. Hang an even deck from the chain and its shape is a parabola. Stretch a standing arch and it still stands, so its shape matters long before its stone gives way.

25eng1Forces are numbers of the plane

A force has a size and a direction, so it is a number of the plane: across part plus i times up part. A measured law, never seen broken in still things: the forces on anything that stays still add to 0. Cut a hanging chain at two points. The piece between is pulled at each end along the chain, and pulled down by its weight. The weight has no across part.

P_{\text{left}} + P_{\text{right}} + W = 0,\ \ W = -iw\ell @@ the piece is still; w per metre of chain, ℓ metres long
\text{across parts: } -H(x_1) + H(x_2) + 0 = 0 @@ each end pulls outward along the chain
H(x_1) = H(x_2) @@ for every two cuts
\text{the pull at } x \text{ points along } 1 + i\,y'(x) @@ along the chain: across 1, up y′ (13)
\htmlClass{new}{P(x) = H\,(1 + i\,y'(x)),\ \ \text{up part } V = H\,y'} \quad \blacksquare @@ H the same all along

Steady-across technique: along a hanging chain, the across part of the pull is the same everywhere, and the up part is that across part times the slope. At the lowest point the slope is 0, so the pull there is all across, H. Toward the ends it grows, since it carries more chain.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the triangle: a motion split into parts

The pull in a chain is one thing, along the chain. Split it along the across and up directions, and the weight touches only one of them. That is why H is the same all along: nothing ever pushes across.

Split each force along directions chosen so that the loads touch as few parts as possible, and say which directions you chose.

Split along the chain and square to it instead, and the weight touches both parts, and nothing is steady. The chain is the same either way. The directions chosen decide what is easy to see.

A chain hangs on a steep hillside, its two ends at very different heights. Is its across pull still the same all along? What about a chain lying in a river current that pushes it sideways?

25eng2The rule a hanging chain obeys

The up part V = Hy′ grows by the weight of each piece. A piece from x to x + h is about √(1 + y′²) h long, by 18i. So V′ = w√(1 + y′²). Write c = H/w, a length.

H\,y'' = w\sqrt{1 + y'^2} \iff y'' = \tfrac1c\sqrt{1 + y'^2} @@ V′ = w √(1 + y′²), with V = Hy′
\text{try } y = c\cosh\tfrac{x}{c},\ \ \cosh u = \tfrac{e^u + e^{-u}}{2},\ \ \sinh u = \tfrac{e^u - e^{-u}}{2} @@ exp (17)
y' = \sinh\tfrac{x}{c},\ \ y'' = \tfrac1c\cosh\tfrac{x}{c} @@ slopes of exp
1 + \sinh^2 u = \cosh^2 u @@ multiply out: the cross terms are ½ and −½
\htmlClass{new}{y = c\cosh\tfrac{x}{c} \text{ obeys the rule, lowest at } x = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked by substitution (24f); one answer, since √(1 + p²) changes no faster than p (24d)

Hanging-chain technique: a chain hanging under its own weight takes the shape y = c cosh(x/c), where c is its lowest pull over its weight per metre. And y = c cosh(x/c) is y = cosh x stretched by c, the map z ↦ cz of 25f. So every hanging chain is one curve, stretched and slid. A power line strung 300 m between towers near The Dalles with c = 1000 m sags 1000(cosh 0.15 − 1) ≈ 11.27 m, and is 2000 sinh 0.15 ≈ 301.13 m long.

25eng3Upside down, it only pushes

Model a chain as weights at joints, with light links between. At each joint, the pulls of its two links and its weight add to 0. Now turn the picture upside down: flip every point across the floor line, z ↦ z̄. For the forces, flip each one and turn it half round: F ↦ −F̄.

P_1 + P_2 + W = 0,\ \ W = -iw @@ a joint of the chain
-\bar P_1 - \bar P_2 - \bar W = -\overline{(P_1 + P_2 + W)} = 0 @@ the flip of a sum is the sum of the flips (9·cb8)
-\bar W = -\overline{(-iw)} = -iw = W @@ the flip of −iw is iw: the weight is unchanged
-\bar P_1 \text{ lies along the flipped link, pointing toward the joint} @@ the flip keeps the link’s line; −1 reverses its sense
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the flipped chain stands under the same weights, every pull now a push}} \quad \blacksquare

Upside-down technique: a hanging chain turned upside down is an arch that stands under the same loads by pushing alone. In 1675 this was put as a riddle: as hangs the flexible line, so but inverted will stand the rigid arch. Stone can push and cannot pull. So an arch in the shape of a hanging chain, loaded as the chain was, asks of its stone only what stone does well.

25eng4The parabola under an even deck

A suspension bridge hangs a heavy, level deck from light cables. Now the load is spread evenly across, q newtons for each metre of span, not along the cable. So V′ = q.

H\,y'' = q @@ V = Hy′ grows by q per metre across
y = \tfrac{q}{2H}\,x^2 @@ lowest at x = 0
s = \tfrac{q}{2H}\big(\tfrac{L}{2}\big)^2 \Rightarrow H = \tfrac{qL^2}{8s} @@ sag s over span L
\cosh u = 1 + \tfrac{u^2}{2} + \tfrac{u^4}{24} + \cdots @@ the power sum of exp (23h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a shallow chain is nearly a parabola, and an even deck makes it one}} \quad \blacksquare

Even-deck technique: a cable carrying a level deck evenly hangs as a parabola, and its across pull is qL²/(8s): the flatter the cable, the harder it pulls. The Golden Gate’s main span is 1280 m and its cables sag about 145 m. So H = (L/8s) qL ≈ 1.10 times the weight of the whole span’s deck, and the pull at a tower is √(1.10² + 0.5²) ≈ 1.21 times it. An arch under an even deck is that parabola turned upside down. The Bixby Creek Bridge on the Big Sur coast (1932) stands its deck on posts over a single concrete arch about 98 m across.

25eng5Shape, not strength

An arch stands if some line of pushes, joint to joint, balances every block and stays inside the stone. Call it the push line. Stretch the arch and its push line by z ↦ cz, with c > 0 real. Every weight grows, by c³ for a solid of the same stone, and every force can be scaled by the same c³.

\textstyle\sum F_j = 0 \Rightarrow \sum c^3 F_j = c^3 \sum F_j = 0 @@ multiply a sum by a real (25a)
z \mapsto az \text{ keeps every direction, and sends inside to inside} @@ a stretch (25f)
\text{the stretched push line balances the stretched arch, inside its stone} @@ both lines above
\text{the squeeze on the stone: force } \propto c^3,\ \text{ face } \propto c^2,\ \text{ so } \propto c @@ squeeze per square metre
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a model that stands, stands when stretched, until the squeeze crushes it}} \quad \blacksquare

Shape technique: whether an arch stands depends on its shape, not its size, until it is so large that its stone is crushed. Granite takes a squeeze of about 130 million newtons per square metre and weighs about 2700 kg per cubic metre. A granite column crushes its own base only at 130 × 10⁶/(2700 × 9.8) ≈ 4.9 km tall. Every arch ever built is far below that. Arches fall by changing shape, when a support spreads and the push line leaves the stone, not because the stone gives way.

The accepted names

The hanging chain’s curve is the catenary, and a its parameter; cosh and sinh are the hyperbolic cosine and sine. Forces adding to 0 is static equilibrium, and the across pull the horizontal tension. The push line is the line of thrust; an arch shaped to keep it at the centre is a funicular arch. The shape technique, together with its rule that one push line inside the stone is enough, is the safe theorem of limit analysis. The squeeze per square metre is stress, and what crushes the stone its compressive strength.

Who said it first

Galileo Galilei thought a hanging chain a parabola (1638); Joachim Jungius showed it was not (1669). Jakob Bernoulli set the problem, and Gottfried Leibniz, Christiaan Huygens and Johann Bernoulli solved it in 1691. Robert Hooke published the riddle in 1675, as an anagram in Latin. Antoni Gaudí designed with hanging models of strings and weights. Jacques Heyman made the shape technique into an engineer’s method in the 1960s. In 25·eng.7.5, the dome is St Peter’s in Rome: Le Seur, Jacquier and Boscovich reported in 1742; Giovanni Poleni hung a chain loaded like the dome (1748) and found a push line inside it; five iron rings were added.
