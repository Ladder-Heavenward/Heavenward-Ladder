Can a Positive Test Be Probably Wrong?

Can a positive test be probably wrong? A clinic screens for an illness that 1 person in 1,000 has. The test catches everyone who has it, and gives a false yes to 5 well people in 100. Claire tests positive. Most people, and many doctors, guess she almost surely has the illness. The count says she almost surely does not. This page counts ten thousand people through the test, using 2·stat’s groups and 5·stat’s chance of one thing given another, finds that a rare illness and a good test give mostly false yeses, and works out what a second test adds. Each result is a yes or a no, and, as 7g said, each is data for a better guess, not a verdict.

Picture 10,000 people. Count the sick, and of them the ones who test positive; count the well, and of them the ones who test positive. Claire is one of the positives. Her chance of being sick is the sick positives over all the positives.

7stat1Ten Thousand People, Counted

Claire’s clinic screens for an illness that 1 person in 1,000 has. Rather than reason about chances, count people. Take 10,000 who come in.

10\,000 \times \tfrac{1}{1000} = 10 @@ sick
\htmlClass{new}{10\,000 - 10 = 9\,990} @@ well

From here on, on this page, the share of people who have the illness is its base rate: here 1 in 1,000. Every count below starts from it.

7stat3Of the Positives, How Many Are Sick?

Claire tested positive, so she is one of the 510 in the positive column. Her chance of being sick is the chance of sick given positive, which 5·stat found as a count over a count: the cases with both, over the cases with the given.

\dfrac{\text{sick and positive}}{\text{positive}} = \dfrac{10}{510} @@ cases with both, over cases with the given (5·stat)
\htmlClass{new}{\approx \tfrac{1}{51} \approx 2\%} @@ about 2 in 100

Count-the-positives technique: to find the chance that a positive result is right, count a large group through the test and divide the sick positives by all the positives. Claire’s chance of being sick rose from 1 in 1,000 to about 1 in 51. The test did its job; it did not make her probably sick.

7stat4A Rare Illness and a Good Test

The well positives, 500, swamp the sick positives, 10, because the well are 999 in 1,000 of the people. A small share of a huge group beats all of a tiny one.

base ratesick positiveswell positivessick, given positive
1 in 1,00010500about 2%
1 in 100100495about 17%
1 in 101,000450about 69%

The same test, given to people more likely to be sick, gives positives that are far more likely right. That is why doctors test people with symptoms before people without, and why a screening of everyone needs a second look at every positive.

This was put to a group of doctors and students at a teaching hospital in 1978, with these numbers. Nearly half of them answered 95%. A doctor who sketches the table above gets about 2%, with no more than multiplying and dividing.

7stat5Test Again: The Second Positive

Claire takes a second test of the same kind, whose errors do not follow the first. Count the 510 positives through it.

\text{sick: all } 10 \text{ positive again} @@ the test catches all of them
\text{well: } 500 \times \tfrac{5}{100} = 25 \text{ positive again} @@ the false-yes share again
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{10}{10 + 25} = \dfrac{10}{35} \approx 29\%} @@ sick, given two positives

After the first positive, the 510 positives are the new group, with a base rate of 10 in 510. The second test starts from there. Each positive result moves the guess, and the next result starts from the moved guess. A third positive would take Claire past 8 in 10. Each yes is data, as 7g said; none is a verdict on its own.

Accepted names

The chance that a sick person tests positive is the test’s sensitivity, and the chance that a well person tests negative is its specificity (here 95%). The chance that a positive is sick is the positive predictive value. Forgetting the base rate is the base rate fallacy, and the counting method is Bayes’ rule done with natural frequencies.

Who said it first

Ward Casscells, Arno Schoenberger and Thomas Graboys put this question to staff and students at Harvard Medical School in 1978; about half answered 95%, and few gave the right answer of about 2%. Gerd Gigerenzer and others later showed that counting people, as this page does, makes the right answer easy to reach.
