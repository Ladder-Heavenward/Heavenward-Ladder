14h · technique

Optimising

A ferry sets a fare, a shop decides how much to order, a driver picks a speed. Each wants the most of something or the least of something else, and each choice pulls two ways. A higher fare brings more money per ticket and fewer tickets. A bigger order costs fewer orders and more shelf. Write the thing to make best as a function of the one choice, and find the interval of choices that make sense. Then the last three steps do the rest: 14a's candidates on a closed interval, or 14f's single critical point on any interval. The work is in the writing. Once the function is on the step, finding its best is a few lines.

Name the choice. Write the goal as a function of it, using every condition to remove other unknowns. Find the interval of sensible choices. Then compare candidates (candidates) or use a single critical point (one-critical-point), and check the answer means something.

14h1The steps

First, say in words what is to be made greatest or least, and what is chosen. Second, name the choice with one letter and write the goal as a function of it. If the goal depends on more than one unknown, use the problem's conditions to write the others in terms of the choice. Third, find the interval of choices that make sense: prices are not negative, speeds lie between limits, an order is more than nothing. Fourth, find the best. On a closed interval, compare the candidates (candidates). On any interval, a single critical point that is a bottom is the least (one-critical-point), and one that is a top the greatest. Fifth, answer the question asked, with units, and check it: is it a whole number when it must be, and does it lie in the interval?

Accepted name: optimisation, and the best value is the optimum.

14h2Two parts that pull apart

Many costs have one part that falls as the choice grows and one that rises. When the parts are k/x and mx, the answer has a shape worth knowing.

Equal-parts technique: for positive reals k and m, the function k/x + mx on (0, ∞) has its least value 2√(km), at x = √(k/m), and there the two parts are equal: k/x = mx.

f(x) = \tfrac{k}{x} + mx,\ \ f'(x) = -\tfrac{k}{x^2} + m @@ quotient for slopes (13f), slope-of-a-line; continuous on (0, ∞)
f'(x) = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; x^2 = \tfrac{k}{m} \;\Leftrightarrow\; x = \sqrt{k/m} @@ the only critical point in (0, ∞)
f''(x) = \tfrac{2k}{x^3} \gt 0 @@ second-test: a bottom
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{k}{x} = mx} \text{ at } x = \sqrt{k/m},\ \ f = 2\sqrt{km} @@ one-critical-point: the least value on (0, ∞); k/x = m·x is the line x² = k/m again ∎

At the best choice, the falling part and the rising part cost the same. A shop that finds its ordering costs well above its shelf costs is ordering too little.

x + \tfrac1x \ge 2 \text{ for } x \gt 0,\ \text{equal only at } 1 @@ equal-parts with k = m = 1
x = \sqrt{s/t} \text{ for positive } s, t:\ \ \sqrt{\tfrac st} + \sqrt{\tfrac ts} \ge 2 @@ the line above
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{s + t}{2} \ge \sqrt{st}} @@ multiply by √(st)/2: the halfway mean is at least the root of the product, as 8g found

14h3Worked: the order that costs least

A writer for factory managers in 1913 asked how many parts to make or buy at a time. Order Q items at a time and run through D in a year, so there are D/Q orders, each with a fixed cost K. Between orders, the stock falls steadily from Q to 0. So on average Q/2 items sit on the shelf, each costing h to hold for a year.

T(Q) = \tfrac{KD}{Q} + \tfrac{h}{2}Q \text{ on } (0, \infty) @@ the goal: yearly cost, in dollars
\text{a store in Eureka: } D = 1800 \text{ bags},\ K = \$25,\ h = \$1 @@ the numbers
Q = \sqrt{\tfrac{2KD}{h}} = \sqrt{90000} = \htmlClass{new}{300} @@ equal-parts with k = KD = 45000 and m = h/2 = ½
T(300) = 150 + 150 = \$300 @@ ordering costs and shelf costs equal, as equal-parts says

Its accepted name is the economic order quantity. Six orders a year, every two months.

14h5Worked: the speed that costs least, and the wrong goal

C(v) = \tfrac{2000}{v} + 0.2v \text{ cents per km},\ \ 40 \le v \le 110 @@ a driver paid 2000 cents an hour, so 2000/v a kilometre; fuel 0.2v a kilometre
C'(v) = -\tfrac{2000}{v^2} + 0.2 = 0 \text{ at } v = 100 @@ equal-parts's critical point, inside [40, 110]
C(40) = 58,\ \ C(100) = 40,\ \ C(110) \approx 40.18 @@ candidates
\text{least: } 40 \text{ cents per km at } 100 \text{ km/h} @@ wages and fuel 20 cents each

The same costs per hour are 2000 + 0.2v², least at the slowest speed allowed. That answers a different question, the cost of an hour on the road, when the trip is a fixed distance. Writing the goal is the step where problems go wrong.

The 1913 article told factory managers that the number of parts to make at one time is a balance. On one side is the interest tied up in stock; on the other, the cost of setting up each run. A formula, not the foreman's habit, should strike it.

14h7Running it backwards

Handed a best choice and asked what the problem's numbers must have been, set the slope of the goal to 0 at that choice and solve for the unknown number. Handed an answer, check it against the interval, the units, and whether the best was a top or a bottom. Handed a cost of the shape k/x + mx, check that the two parts are equal at the claimed best. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Harris told factory managers that the number of parts to make at one time is a balance between the interest tied up in stock and the cost of setting up each run, and that a formula, not the foreman's habit, should strike it.

Harris, “How many parts to make at once”, Factory, The Magazine of Management (1913); paraphrase

The writer of 1913 is Ford Whitman Harris, and the best-order formula is often called Harris’s formula, or the Wilson formula. The closer’s principle of least time is Pierre de Fermat’s (1662); the answer is Claude Clerselier’s, defending René Descartes.
