22f · proof

Sums Against Areas

When the pieces of a sum come from a curve that falls, each piece is a strip one unit wide standing under or over the curve. So the endless sum and the area under the curve out to forever go together: both settle, or both wander. That turns questions about sums into questions about areas, which the sixteenth rung can answer. It settles a whole family at once: the sums of one over a power of the counting numbers settle exactly when the power is more than one. And it puts a fence round totals nobody can find: one plus a quarter plus a ninth and so on settles below two, though its exact total waits for a much later rung.

If the pieces come from a falling curve, the sum and the area under the curve settle or wander together. One over the counting numbers raised to a power settles exactly when the power is more than one. The area also fences in the total.

22f1Strips Beside the Curve

Area technique for sums: let f be positive and falling on [1, ∞), and uₙ = f(n). Then Σ uₙ settles exactly when ∫₁^∞ f settles. On [k, k + 1] the strip of height f(k + 1) lies under the curve and the strip of height f(k) lies over it.

f(k+1) \le \int_k^{k+1} f \le f(k) @@ f falls: lowest at the right end, highest at the left (15b)
u_2 + \cdots + u_n \le \int_1^n f \le u_1 + \cdots + u_{n-1} @@ add the strips from k = 1 to n − 1
\int_1^\infty f \text{ settles} \Rightarrow s_n \le u_1 + \int_1^\infty f @@ a ceiling for the running totals
\textstyle\sum u_n \text{ settles} \Rightarrow \int_1^n f \le \sum u_n @@ a ceiling for the areas, which climb in n
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum u_n \text{ and } \int_1^\infty f \text{ settle together}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the climb technique on each side (22a2)

22f2The p-Sums

p-sum technique: Σ 1/nᵖ settles exactly when p > 1. The curve 1/xᵖ falls for p > 0. 16i found ∫₁^∞ x^(−p) dx settles exactly when p > 1. For p ≤ 0 the pieces do not head to 0.

\int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = \tfrac{b^{1-p} - 1}{1 - p} \ (p \ne 1),\ \ \int_1^b \tfrac{dx}{x} = \ln b @@ 16c and 17a
p > 1:\ \int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx = \tfrac{1}{p - 1} @@ b¹⁻ᵖ → 0
p \le 1:\ \text{the areas pass every ceiling} @@ ln b and b¹⁻ᵖ both do
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum \tfrac{1}{n^p} \text{ settles} \Leftrightarrow p > 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the area technique (22f1)

So the line between settling and wandering runs exactly through the harmonic sum. Σ 1/n wanders. Σ 1/n^1.01 settles. The p-sums are the main yardstick for comparison after the geometric sums: use them when the pieces shrink like a power of n rather than like a power of a fixed number.

22f3A Fence Round the Total

Fence technique for sums: if f is positive and falling, the total of Σ f(n) lies between ∫₁^∞ f and f(1) + ∫₁^∞ f; and the tail after n pieces lies between ∫_{n+1}^∞ f and ∫ₙ^∞ f. These are the inequalities of 22f1, with the area run out to forever.

\int_1^\infty \tfrac{dx}{x^2} = 1 @@ 22f2 with p = 2
1 \le \textstyle\sum \tfrac{1}{n^2} \le 1 + 1 = 2 @@ the fence technique
s_{10} = 1.5497\ldots,\ \ \tfrac{1}{11} \le \text{tail} \le \tfrac{1}{10} @@ ten pieces, and the tail fenced by areas
\htmlClass{new}{1.6406 < \textstyle\sum \tfrac{1}{n^2} < 1.6498} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the fences to s₁₀

Ten pieces and two areas pin the total to within a hundredth. Its exact value is a famous number that involves π. This ladder can only fence it for now. The proof waits for rung 32.

22f4A Gap That Settles

The harmonic sum wanders, but 22f1 says how fast: its running totals Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n stay within 1 of ln n. The gap between them does better than that. It settles.

g_n = H_n - \ln n @@ the running total less the area ∫₁ⁿ dx/x
g_n - g_{n+1} = \ln(n+1) - \ln n - \tfrac{1}{n+1} = \int_n^{n+1}\Big(\tfrac1x - \tfrac{1}{n+1}\Big)dx \ge 0 @@ 1/x ≥ 1/(n+1) on the strip: gₙ falls
g_n \ge \tfrac1n > 0 @@ 22f1: Hₙ − 1/n = u₁ + ⋯ + uₙ₋₁ ≥ ∫₁ⁿ dx/x = ln n
\htmlClass{new}{g_n \to \gamma \text{ for some } \gamma \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fall technique (22a2)

The fall technique gives a number without giving its digits. Computed, γ ≈ 0.5772. Nobody knows whether γ is a fraction. That question has been open for nearly three hundred years.

22f.4.5seamA young mathematician announced the total of 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯, a number built from π. To find it he treated the sine as a polynomial with endlessly many factors, one for each place it is 0. Others objected that a rule for polynomials need not carry over to the endless, and that the sine might hide other zeros. He checked his total to many places against the running totals, and published. Proofs that met the objections came much later.

The accepted names

The area technique for sums is the integral test; the p-sums are the p-series; the fence technique gives the integral test remainder estimate. γ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. The total of 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯ is π²/6; finding it is the Basel problem.

Who said it first

Colin Maclaurin (1742) and Cauchy (1827) laid sums beside areas. Pietro Mengoli posed the Basel problem in 1650, the Bernoulli brothers failed at it, and Leonhard Euler solved it in 1734, aged twenty-seven, by a step that was not justified for a century. Euler also found γ (1734), and Lorenzo Mascheroni computed it, partly wrongly, in 1790. In 22f.4.5, the objections were raised by Daniel Bernoulli and others; Euler gave a second proof in 1741, and the product for the sine was put on firm ground by Karl Weierstrass (1876).
