26a · proof

e to an Imaginary Power

The growth function was built for real inputs. Its endless sum, one plus x plus x squared over two and so on, makes sense for any number you can add and multiply, and the new numbers can be added and multiplied. So feed it a number off the line and see what comes out. Feed it a quarter turn times an angle, and the sum splits in two: one half is the endless sum for the cosine, the other half is the endless sum for the sine. Growth, fed a turn, turns. The famous line that ties together e, i, pi, one and zero falls out at the end.

The growth function’s endless sum settles for every complex input. Fed a turn, it gives cosine plus i times sine. So every complex number is a length times e to a turn.

26a1Sums of Complex Numbers

A sum Σ zₖ of complex numbers settles at S when its running totals do, by 25c2’s distance: |Sₙ − S| heads to 0. It settles exactly when the floor parts and the upright parts both settle, since each part is at most the size and the size is at most the two parts’ sizes added.

Plane-size technique: if Σ |zₖ| settles, then Σ zₖ settles. 22e’s settles-in-size technique, applied to each part.

|a_k| \le |z_k|,\ \ |b_k| \le |z_k| \text{ for } z_k = a_k + b_k i @@ a part is at most the size (25c2)
\textstyle\sum |a_k|,\ \sum |b_k| \text{ settle} @@ the comparison technique (22e)
\textstyle\sum a_k,\ \sum b_k \text{ settle} @@ the settles-in-size technique (22e)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum z_k = \sum a_k + i\sum b_k \text{ settles}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum-of-sums technique (22d)

So every power sum Σ cₖzᵏ whose sizes Σ |cₖ||z|ᵏ settle has a total, and 23c’s reach works in the plane: it settles inside a disc |z − w| < R and wanders outside.

26a2The Growth Function on ℂ

From here on, for every z in ℂ, e^z = exp z = 1 + z + z²/2! + z³/3! + ⋯. Accepted name: the complex exponential.

\textstyle\sum |z|^k/k! = e^{|z|} @@ exp as a sum (22j3), at the real number |z|
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum z^k/k! \text{ settles for every } z \in \mathbb{C}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plane-size technique (26a1)
x \in \mathbb{R} \Rightarrow \exp x \text{ is rung 17's } e^x @@ 22j3: the same sum, on the floor

So the new e^z agrees with rung 17’s on the floor. Nothing was redefined: the old function was always this sum, and the sum never needed its input to be real.

26a3Growth, Fed a Turn, Turns

Turn-growth technique: for every real θ, e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ. The even pieces of the sum are the cosine sum, and the odd pieces, divided by i, are the sine sum.

i^{2m} = (-1)^m,\ \ i^{2m+1} = (-1)^m i @@ i² = −1, and powers multiply
\textstyle\sum_k \frac{(i\theta)^k}{k!} = \sum_m \frac{(i\theta)^{2m}}{(2m)!} + \sum_m \frac{(i\theta)^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!} @@ even and odd pieces: each settles in size (22e), so the sum-of-sums technique (22d) splits the total
= \textstyle\sum_m (-1)^m\frac{\theta^{2m}}{(2m)!} + i\sum_m (-1)^m\frac{\theta^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!} @@ the first line
\htmlClass{new}{e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sums for cos and sin (23b2)

Put θ = π. Then cos π = −1 and sin π = 0, so e^(iπ) = −1, or e^(iπ) + 1 = 0. Five numbers met on five different floors, in one line.

And |e^(iθ)| = √(cos²θ + sin²θ) = 1. So 21·cb20’s out-and-round form r(cos θ + i sin θ) is r e^(iθ): every complex number other than 0 is a length times e to a turn.

The accepted names

The turn-growth technique is Euler’s formula, and e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 is Euler’s identity. The form re^(iθ) is the polar form, or exponential form, of a complex number. The plane-size technique says absolute convergence implies convergence in ℂ.

Who said it first

Roger Cotes found an equivalent of the turn-growth technique, in logarithms, in 1714. Abraham de Moivre’s formula (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos nθ + i sin nθ dates from 1722 and 1730. Leonhard Euler wrote e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ in his Introductio of 1748, by comparing the sums as this step does.
