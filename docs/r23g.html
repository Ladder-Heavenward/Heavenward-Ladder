23g · proof

Two famous proofs

Two numbers have been with you for a long time: the growth number e from the seventeenth rung, and π from the fifth, first guessed as twenty-two sevenths. Neither has ever been written as a fraction, and this step proves neither can be. Both proofs work the same trick. Suppose the number were a fraction. Then some quantity built from it would have to be a whole number. But endless sums and areas pin that quantity strictly between zero and one, and no whole number lives there. For e the quantity is the leftover of its own endless sum. For π it is an area under a polynomial times a sine.

Neither e nor π is a fraction: both are irrational, in 8a's word. In each proof, a fraction would make some quantity a whole number, and the tools of these rungs trap it strictly between zero and one.

23g1e is not a fraction

Stands on 23b2 (e = Σ 1/k!) and 15i (the geometric sum).

Irrationality technique for e: no whole numbers p and q make e = p/q.

e = p/q,\ \ q \ge 1 @@ suppose so, toward ⊥
x = q!\Big(e - \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{q} \tfrac{1}{k!}\Big) @@ the leftover after q + 1 pieces, times q!
x = q!\,\tfrac{p}{q} - \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{q} \tfrac{q!}{k!} \text{ is a whole number} @@ q!/q and each q!/k! with k ≤ q are whole
x = \tfrac{1}{q+1} + \tfrac{1}{(q+1)(q+2)} + \cdots > 0 @@ e = Σ 1/k! (23b2), times q!
x < \tfrac{1}{q+1} + \tfrac{1}{(q+1)^2} + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{q} \le 1 @@ each factor is at least q + 1; the geometric sum (15i)
\htmlClass{new}{0 < x < 1 \text{ and } x \text{ whole: } \bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ no whole number lies strictly between 0 and 1

The proof used that the sum for e settles fast: its leftover after q + 1 pieces is smaller than 1/q!, small enough to beat the q! that a fraction with bottom q would bring.

23g2π is not a fraction

Stands on 21g (sin′ = cos, cos′ = −sin), 16c (evaluating by antiderivatives), 23a1 (slopes of a polynomial) and 22h2 (xⁿ/n! → 0).

Irrationality technique for π: no whole numbers p and q make π = p/q. Suppose they do, and fix a whole n, to be chosen large.

f(x) = \tfrac{x^n(p - bx)^n}{n!},\ \ f(\pi - x) = f(x) @@ π − x = (p − bx)/b and p − q(π − x) = bx: the two factors swap
f^{(j)}(0) \text{ is whole for every } j @@ f is Σ cₘxᵐ/n! with whole cₘ and m ≥ n; f^{(j)}(0) = j!cⱼ/n!, whole (23a1)
f^{(j)}(\pi) = (-1)^j f^{(j)}(0) \text{ is whole} @@ the swap, and the chain rule
F = f - f'' + f^{(4)} - \cdots + (-1)^n f^{(2n)},\ \ F'' + F = f @@ f has degree 2n, so f^{(2n+2)} = 0
\big(F'\sin - F\cos\big)' = F''\sin + F\sin = f\sin @@ the product rule, sin′ = cos, cos′ = −sin (21g)
\int_0^\pi f(x)\sin x\,dx = F(\pi) + F(0) \text{ is whole} @@ evaluating by antiderivatives (16c); sin 0 = sin π = 0, cos π = −1
0 < f(x)\sin x \le \tfrac{\pi^n p^n}{n!} \text{ on } (0, \pi) @@ x < π, p − bx < p, 0 < sin x ≤ 1
0 < \int_0^\pi f\sin < \pi \cdot \tfrac{(\pi p)^n}{n!} < 1 \text{ for } n \text{ large} @@ (πp)ⁿ/n! heads to 0 (22h2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a whole number strictly between } 0 \text{ and } 1: \bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last two lines

5b named π with the guess 22/7 and promised it would be sharpened at each tool. Rung 21 measured it. This step closes the question of whether any fraction could be it exactly. None can. The guesses can only get closer.

23g3Beyond fractions

Stands on 23g1 and 23g2.

A fraction is a statement that names a root of a first-degree equation with whole coefficients, qx − p = 0. √2 is irrational, but it is a root of x² − 2 = 0. Are e and π roots of some polynomial with whole coefficients? They are not: no polynomial with whole coefficients, other than 0, has e or π as a root. That claim is stated here and held in trust. Its proofs reach for tools much higher up the ladder.

From here on, a real is algebraic when it is a root of a polynomial with whole coefficients, not all 0, and transcendental when it is not.

The second fact settles one of the oldest questions there is. With ruler and compass you can build only lengths that are algebraic. A square with the area of a circle of radius 1 would need a side of √π. So no ruler-and-compass recipe squares the circle.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The proof for e is Fourier's proof (1815). The proof for π here is Ivan Niven's (1947). That e is transcendental is Hermite's theorem (1873); that π is transcendental is the Lindemann theorem (1882), and with Weierstrass's extension (1885) the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem. Squaring the circle is the problem of quadrature of the circle.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler showed e is irrational in 1737, by its endless fraction; Joseph Fourier's short proof is the one above. Johann Heinrich Lambert showed π is irrational in 1761, by an endless fraction for tan. Charles Hermite proved e transcendental in 1873, and Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π transcendental in 1882, ending the quest to square the circle that began in ancient Greece. Whether e + π is a fraction is still open.
