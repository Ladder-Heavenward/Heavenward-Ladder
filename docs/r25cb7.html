25·cb7 · newsflash for 7·phys

Batteries

7·phys said what a battery does: it lifts charge, so that each coulomb leaving one end carries energy it spends on the way round. It did not say where that energy comes from. 6·chem said who gives up electrons, and left a flashlight in a drawer with a question: why has its zinc not given its electrons away? Both answers are one answer. A battery is a change in which one metal hands electrons to another, built so that the electrons can only go the long way, through the wire. Its voltage is the energy of that change, counted per coulomb. That voltage sags as the battery runs, by a rule with a log in it. And run backwards, with a charger or a smelter pushing, the same change counts out metal by the coulomb.

A cell keeps its giver and its taker apart, so the electrons must go round through the wire. Its voltage is the difference of two measured pulls. Times the charge of a mole of electrons, it is the energy the change can hand out as work. As the cell runs, its products pile up and its voltage sags by a log of their ratio, until the change is at rest and the cell is dead. Pushed backwards, every mole of electrons drives a fixed amount of change, whatever the voltage.

25·cb7.1Two Halves, Kept Apart

Put a zinc strip in a solution of zinc ions, and a copper strip in a solution of copper ions. Join the two solutions by a salty bridge that lets ions through, and join the strips by a wire. Zinc atoms give up electrons and go into solution. The electrons run through the wire to the copper strip, where copper ions take them and come out as metal. The zinc and the copper ions never touch. The only road between them is the wire.

Each half has a measured pull on electrons, its reduction voltage, taken against a hydrogen half set at 0. A few, in volts, at the standard state (each dissolved ion at 1 mol/L, 25 °C): lithium −3.04, aluminium −1.66, zinc −0.76, iron −0.44, lead −0.13, hydrogen 0, copper +0.34, silver +0.80, oxygen in acid +1.23, chlorine +1.36. These are measured laws, not proofs.

\mathrm{Zn} \to \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + 2e^- @@ zinc’s number goes 0 to +2: it gives (6·chem)
\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2e^- \to \mathrm{Cu} @@ copper’s number goes +2 to 0: it takes
\mathrm{Zn} + \mathrm{Cu^{2+}} \to \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + \mathrm{Cu} @@ add the halves: 2 electrons given, 2 taken
E^\circ = E_{\text{taker}} - E_{\text{giver}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) @@ the taker pulls harder by this much, per coulomb
\htmlClass{new}{E^\circ = 1.10\ \text{V}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the voltage of the cell at the standard state

Cell technique: a cell’s voltage is the taker’s pull less the giver’s, and the half with the lower pull is the one that gives. The voltage does not depend on how many electrons are written in the balanced change. A voltage is energy per coulomb, and doubling the change doubles both.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change: two sides at once

6·chem left a flashlight in a drawer and asked why its zinc had not given up its electrons. The answer is the principle itself. The zinc can give only if, in the same event, something takes. In the drawer the taker sits behind a wall, and the only road to it is a wire that is not there.

A cell runs only when both of its halves can run at once.

A cell is a change split in two on purpose, with the wire as its middle. Close the switch and both halves run at once, at the same rate, electron for electron. Open it and both stop. Neither half ever runs alone.

A copper wire is dropped into a beaker of silver ions, with no second beaker and no outside wire. Which side gives, which side takes, and where is the middle of the change now?

25·cb7.2Volts Into Energy

A volt is a joule for each coulomb. So the energy a cell hands out is its voltage times the charge that passes. Count the charge by the mole. One electron carries 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and a mole is 6.02214076 × 10²³ of them.

F = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23} \cdot 1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} @@ a mole of electrons; both numbers fixed by agreement
F \approx 96\,485\ \text{C per mole} @@ 19·bio’s F, 96.5 kJ per mole per volt
\text{energy} = n F E @@ n moles of electrons, each coulomb lifted E volts (7·phys)
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta G = -nFE} \quad \blacksquare @@ counted negative when the change hands energy out

From here on, ΔG is the energy a change can hand out as work, per mole of the change as written. It is negative when the change hands energy out. ΔG° is its value at the standard state.

Energy technique: a cell that moves n moles of electrons through E volts hands out nFE joules, and ΔG = −nFE. The zinc and copper change of 25·cb7.1 has n = 2, so ΔG° = −2 × 96 485 × 1.10 ≈ −212 kJ for each mole of zinc. Unlike the voltage, ΔG doubles when the change is written twice. A car battery in Truckee is six lead cells of 2.04 V in a row, 12.24 V in all.

25·cb7.3Why a Battery Sags

19·bio found that crowding a dissolved substance costs RT ln(ratio) per mole. A running cell crowds its products and thins its starting stuff. Gather the crowding of every substance into one ratio, Q: the products’ concentrations over the starting stuff’s, each to the power of its count in the change. For the zinc and copper cell, Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺]. The metals are pure and do not enter.

\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q @@ the ratio-coin technique (19·bio), for every substance at once
-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q @@ the energy technique, on both sides
E = E^\circ - \tfrac{RT}{nF}\ln Q @@ divide by −nF
\tfrac{RT}{F} = \tfrac{8.314 \cdot 298.15}{96\,485} \approx 0.02569\ \text{V},\ \ \tfrac{RT}{F}\ln 10 \approx 0.0592\ \text{V} @@ at 25 °C
\htmlClass{new}{E = E^\circ - \tfrac{0.0592}{n}\log_{10} Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ log₁₀ Q = ln Q / ln 10 (17c)

Sag technique: each tenfold rise in the ratio of products to starting stuff lowers the voltage by 0.0592/n volts. With [Zn²⁺] = 1 and [Cu²⁺] = 0.01, Q = 100 and E = 1.10 − 0.0296 × 2 ≈ 1.04 V. The voltage sags slowly, by a log, and the cell dies when E = 0. Then ln Q = nFE°/RT, so log₁₀ Q ≈ 2 × 1.10/0.0592 ≈ 37.2. Long before that, the copper ions are gone.

25·cb7.4Pushed Backwards, Counted by the Coulomb

Push a cell with an outside voltage larger than its own, and its change runs backwards. A charger does this to a phone. A smelter does it to aluminium ore, which no cell would give up freely: aluminium’s pull is −1.66 V. Each electron pushed through does one electron’s worth of change, whatever the voltage.

\text{charge} = I\,t @@ current is charge per second (7·phys)
\text{moles of electrons} = \tfrac{It}{F},\ \ \text{moles of metal} = \tfrac{It}{nF} @@ n electrons for each atom
\mathrm{Al^{3+}} + 3e^- \to \mathrm{Al}:\ \ n = 3,\ \ 26.98\ \text{g per mole} @@ the smelter’s change
4.2 \times 10^{8}\ \text{kg a year} \approx 1.56 \times 10^{10}\ \text{moles of Al} @@ the Kitimat smelter’s yearly output
\htmlClass{new}{I \approx \tfrac{1.56 \times 10^{10} \cdot 3 \cdot 96\,485}{3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{8}\ \text{A}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the whole current, shared among the pots

Counting technique: the metal a current makes depends only on the charge that passes, It/(nF) moles, and the voltage decides only the energy it costs. At about 4 V a pot, a kilogram of aluminium takes 4 × 3 × 96 485/0.02698 joules, about 12 kWh. That is why the smelter sits by a river and its own dam.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A cell that hands energy out is a galvanic, or voltaic, cell; one driven backwards is an electrolytic cell. The zinc and copper cell is the Daniell cell. The giving strip is the anode, where oxidation happens; the taking strip is the cathode, where reduction happens. The salty bridge is a salt bridge. The pulls are standard reduction potentials against the standard hydrogen electrode, and E° the standard cell potential, or electromotive force. F is the Faraday constant. ΔG is the Gibbs free energy change. Q is the reaction quotient, and the sag technique is the Nernst equation. The counting technique is Faraday’s laws of electrolysis. Two halves of one metal at different strengths make a concentration cell.

Who said it first

Alessandro Volta described his pile in 1800. John Frederic Daniell built the zinc and copper cell in 1836. Michael Faraday counted electrolysis by the charge in 1833 and 1834. Walther Nernst gave the sag technique in 1889, and Josiah Willard Gibbs the free energy in the 1870s. Charles Martin Hall and Paul Héroult each found the aluminium smelting route in 1886. In 25·cb7.6.5, Volta held that the touching of two unlike metals is the push, the contact theory; Faraday, after his counting, held that no push comes without chemical change, and the quarrel ran from the 1800s into the 1890s.
