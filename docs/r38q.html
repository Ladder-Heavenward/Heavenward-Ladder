38q · technique

Proofs With No Detours at All

A detour makes a mark and takes it apart at once, and one detour can be cut out (38p). Cutting one can make others, so whether all can be cut is a real question. Write proofs as claims “from all of these, one of those,” with each move adding one mark on one side, and one move, the cut, that brings in a lemma and uses it up. Every proof can be rewritten with no cuts. A proof with no cuts uses only parts of its own foot. This step writes the claims, cuts the cuts, and reads off what a cut-free proof can contain.

G ⇒ D: if all of G hold, one of D does. Moves add a mark on the left or right. Cut: from G ⇒ D, A and A, G ⇒ D, get G ⇒ D. Every proof becomes cut-free, each cut pushed upward until it vanishes, the bigger first. A cut-free proof uses only parts of its foot, so ⇒ with nothing on either side has no proof.

38q1Claims With Two Sides

G \Rightarrow D:\ \text{all of } G \text{ give one of } D @@ two lists of statements
\dfrac{G \Rightarrow D, A \quad G \Rightarrow D, B}{G \Rightarrow D, A \wedge B} @@ and on the right
\dfrac{A, G \Rightarrow D}{G \Rightarrow D, \neg A} @@ not on the right: move A across
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cut: } \dfrac{G \Rightarrow D, A \quad A, G \Rightarrow D}{G \Rightarrow D}} @@ a lemma A proved and used up

Claim technique: a claim G ⇒ D says that all of G give one of D; each move adds one mark on one side, except the cut, which proves a lemma and uses it. The claims reached are the same as 38b’s columns reach (38c, 38d).

38q2Cutting Every Cut

\text{left: } G \Rightarrow A \text{ and } G \Rightarrow B;\ \ \text{right: } A, G \Rightarrow D @@ the cut’s two inputs end in ∧ moves
\text{replace by one cut on } A @@ a smaller lemma
\text{measure: (size of the cut mark, height of the cut)} @@ compared first by size
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each rewrite lowers the measure; the measure cannot fall for ever}} @@ a falling run (29·cb3)

Cut-free technique: every proof in the claim calculus can be rewritten into a proof of the same claim with no cuts; each rewrite trades a cut for cuts on smaller marks or higher in the proof, and the measure falls. The cost: a proof of n lines can grow to a tower of 2s of height about n.

38q3Only Parts of the Foot

\text{every move but the cut keeps its inputs as parts of its output} @@ nothing comes from outside
\text{every line of a cut-free proof is made of parts of its foot} @@ the parts property
\Rightarrow \text{ (nothing on either side) has no parts to start from} @@ no proof without cuts
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so pure logic reaches no clash}} @@ from within, by counting parts

A cut-free proof builds its foot from its own parts, so it can be found by working backward from the foot, and the empty claim has none: pure logic is clash-free. Machines that search for proofs work this way.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The claim calculus is the sequent calculus LK; claims are sequents; cutting every cut is cut elimination, Gentzen’s Hauptsatz; the parts property is the subformula property. The tower is the non-elementary cost of cut elimination (Statman, Orevkov 1979).

Who said it first

Gerhard Gentzen introduced sequents and proved cut elimination in 1934 and 1935, in the same paper as natural deduction.
