39c · proof

A Wave Can Carry Power, or a Message

The same wave on the same wire can be asked two different questions. The first is how much it can do: how warm it makes a kettle, how bright a lamp. That is power, and it goes as the squares of the wave’s sizes, moment by moment, and also, by the energy technique of the rung on sums of turnings, frequency by frequency. The second is what it says: which pattern it is, out of all the patterns it could have been. That is information, and it is counted in the bits of the statistics floor. The two questions part company at once. The wave from a wall socket carries over a thousand watts into a kettle and no message at all, because once its frequency and size are known, every later moment of it is known too. A thin wire to a radio carries almost no power and a great deal of message. How much message depends on how many times a second the wave may change, which the step on samples counts, and on how many levels the noise lets a reader tell apart. Electrical engineering asks how much power reaches the load. Electronics asks how much of the pattern survives. The next steps build the part where the two meet, a small pattern steering a large power.

Power is the average of the square, and the energy technique shares it out frequency by frequency. Information is which pattern, out of how many: a wave fixed in advance says nothing. A wire passing frequencies up to B carries at most 2B changes a second, and noise sets how many levels each change can take.

39c1Power, Frequency by Frequency

A voltage v across a resistance R turns energy into heat at the rate v²/R watts (7·phys: power is current times voltage, and the current is v/R). For a wave that repeats, what matters is the average of v² over a turn. 32b3’s energy-kept technique says that average is the sum of the energies of the wave’s turnings, and 32e4 says the same for a wave that never repeats, with a total over every frequency in place of a sum.

Power-by-frequency technique: the average power a wave delivers to a resistance is the sum, frequency by frequency, of the average powers its turnings would deliver alone.

v = V s(\theta):\ \ \overline{v^2} = V^2\,\overline{s} = \tfrac12 V^2 @@ s² = s, since s is 0 or 1 (39a1); on for half of each turn
\text{0th turning } \tfrac12 V:\ \ \tfrac14 V^2 @@ a steady level’s square
\text{first turning } \tfrac{2V}{\pi}\cos\theta:\ \ \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{2V}{\pi}\big)^2 = \tfrac{2}{\pi^2}V^2 \approx 0.203\,V^2 @@ the average of cos² is ½ (21c: cos² + sin² = 1, and the two take turns)
\text{the rest}:\ \ \tfrac12 V^2 - \tfrac14 V^2 - \tfrac{2}{\pi^2}V^2 \approx 0.047\,V^2 @@ the energy-kept technique: the parts add to the whole
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the first turning carries } \tfrac{8}{\pi^2} \approx 81\% \text{ of the swinging power}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0.203 out of 0.25

A wall socket in Portland gives 60 Hz with a peak of about 170 V. Its average square is 170²/2 ≈ 14 400 V², the square of 120 V, the number on the label. A 1500 W kettle on it has R = 120²/1500 = 9.6 Ω and draws 12.5 A. All of that power sits at one frequency.

39c2A Single Frequency Carries No Message

30·comp counted information as surprise: an outcome of chance p tells log₂(1/p) bits, and a choice among n equally likely patterns tells log₂ n bits. Information is a question about the crowd of patterns a wave could have been, not about the wave alone.

Known-wave technique: a wave fixed by numbers the reader already has tells the reader nothing: its information is 0 bits, whatever its power.

v(t) = V\cos(2\pi f t + \phi),\ \ V, f, \phi \text{ known} @@ the socket: 170 V, 60 Hz, and its start read once
\text{every later } v(t) \text{ is one value of a known function} @@ a function gives single values (9a)
\text{one pattern possible, with chance } 1 @@ the crowd of patterns has one member
\htmlClass{new}{\log_2 1 = 0 \text{ bits}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the surprise of a sure thing (30comp1)

Power and message are not rivals; they are different questions about the same function. The socket answers the first loudly and the second not at all. A square wave whose 1s and 0s come from a coin answers the second: each moment it may change, it chooses between two, one bit, and 39b showed its first turning alone keeps that bit. A message lives in what the reader could not have predicted.

39c3How Many Changes a Second

A wire never passes every frequency. A copper pair, a fibre and the air each pass a band up to some top frequency B and lose what lies above. 32g1 proved that a signal with no frequency above B is fixed by its samples at the spacing 1/(2B), and 32g2 rebuilt it from any list of samples at all.

Changes-per-second technique: a wire that passes frequencies up to B carries at most 2B free values a second, and with M levels to choose from at each, at most 2B log₂ M bits a second.

\text{a signal with top frequency } B \text{ is fixed by } f(k/2B) @@ the samples-are-coefficients technique (32g1)
\text{any list of samples gives such a signal} @@ the sample-rebuild technique (32g2): the sum of samples times shifted sincs
\text{one second holds } 2B \text{ samples, each free} @@ the spacing is 1/(2B)
\text{each sample one of } M \text{ levels}:\ \ M^{2B} \text{ patterns a second} @@ the product technique (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{\log_2 M^{2B} = 2B\log_2 M \text{ bits a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the log of a power (17c)

With two levels, 1 and 0, that is 2B bits a second. An old telephone line passes about 3100 Hz: at most 6200 changes a second. More bits need more levels at each change, and the levels are where noise comes in.

39c4Noise Sets the Levels

39b4 showed that a reader confuses two levels when the noise is not small beside the gap between them. With signal power S and noise power N, the levels a reader can tell apart grow only like √(1 + S/N): the size of the signal against the size of the noise, not their powers. So each change carries about ½ log₂(1 + S/N) bits, and 2B changes a second carry B log₂(1 + S/N). 30·comp named this the capacity of a line with hiss and held its proof in trust; here it is used, and its proof waits much higher up the ladder.

Capacity, used: a wire passing frequencies up to B, with signal power S over noise power N, carries at most B log₂(1 + S/N) bits a second, and codes come as near that as you like.

B = 3100 \text{ Hz},\ S/N = 3000 @@ an old telephone line (30·comp)
\sqrt{1 + 3000} \approx 54.8 \text{ levels},\ \ 6200 \log_2 54.8 \approx 35\,800 @@ the changes-per-second technique, with M the levels
3100 \log_2 3001 \approx 35\,800 \text{ bits a second} @@ the capacity, the same number: ½ log₂ M² = log₂ M
\htmlClass{new}{B = 20 \text{ MHz},\ S/N = 1000:\ \ 20\cdot10^6 \log_2 1001 \approx 199 \text{ million bits a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one channel of a home wireless link on a good day

Here the two questions of this step separate cleanly. Electrical engineering asks how much power reaches the load: the kettle, the motor, the lamp. Electronics asks how much of the pattern survives the trip: S against N, B, and the code. Once S/N is large, doubling the power buys only half a bit more on each change, B more bits a second, since log₂(1 + 2S/N) ≈ 1 + log₂(S/N). Doubling B doubles the bits. The part where the two questions meet is the transistor of 39e: a small signal, carrying the pattern, steering a large power, and its gain is the ratio between them.

The accepted names

The average power of v on R is v_rms²/R, with v_rms the root mean square, 120 V for a North American socket. The energy-kept technique is Parseval’s identity, and for the transform Plancherel’s; power frequency by frequency is the power spectrum. 2B changes a second is the Nyquist rate, and 2B log₂ M Hartley’s law. B log₂(1 + S/N) is the Shannon–Hartley theorem, and S/N the signal-to-noise ratio, often given in decibels, 10 log₁₀(S/N): 1000 is 30 dB. The low-S/N regime of 39c.6.2 is the power-limited regime.

Who said it first

Marc-Antoine Parseval stated his sum in 1799. Harry Nyquist counted the changes a line can carry in 1924 and 1928, and Ralph Hartley the bits, by the log of the number of levels, in 1928. Claude Shannon proved the capacity of a line with hiss in 1948, with the samples of 32g as its tool; Edmund Whittaker (1915) and Vladimir Kotelnikov (1933) had the sampling result before him. The Atlantic cable quarrel of 39c.6.4 was between Wildman Whitehouse, who raised the voltage, and William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, whose theory of the cable’s smear (1855) and sensitive mirror galvanometer made the 1866 cable work.

A proof that is not allowed

“The capacity is B log₂(1 + S/N). Make S as large as you like, and the bits a second are as many as you like, so power is message after all.” Not allowed as a reading of the formula: the bits grow, but as the log of the power, and only while B is fixed. Power is spent on telling levels apart. The message is in the choice among them, and a wave with one possible pattern carries 0 bits at any S.
