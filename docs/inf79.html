Why Can’t Sorting Beat n log n?

Why can’t sorting beat n log n? Rung 22 proved the floor: a sort that learns only by comparing needs at least log₂ n! comparisons on some list. This step reads that proof as one arrow, from the n! orders into the rows of answers, and an arrow that is one-to-one can only lead to a set at least as large. Counting is a map where arrows lead between ascending sizes. Then it asks what the proof leaves open. The floor in whole numbers is met for five items, by a sort of seven comparisons. For twelve items it is not met: the floor says 29 and the truth is 30, found by a machine search in the 1960s. The least number is now known up to 22 items, the last three values settled in 2023, and it is open from 23 on. A sort that learns in another way is outside the floor’s plan, and how fast whole numbers can be sorted is open too.

A comparing sort of at most k questions gives a one-to-one arrow, orders → rows of k answers, so n! ≤ 2ᵏ. S(n) is the least k that works: S(n) ≥ ⌈log₂ n!⌉. S(5) = 7 = ⌈log₂ 120⌉. S(12) = 30 > 29. With j questions left, each answer must leave at most 2ʲ orders. Looks with b outcomes: n! ≤ bᵏ.

inf79.1The Floor, as One Arrow

22l’s answer-count technique says that a sort which learns only by comparing makes at least log₂ n! comparisons on some list of n different items. Read its proof as an arrow. A comparing sort asks a question, “is this item less than that one?”, and chooses its next question from the answers so far. Suppose it never asks more than k. Run it on an order σ of the n items and write down its answers, yes or no, filled out to a row of length k. Call the row a(σ).

Orders-into-rows technique: the arrow a, from the n! orders to the 2ᵏ rows of k answers, is one-to-one; so n! ≤ 2ᵏ.

a(\sigma) = a(\tau) \Rightarrow \text{the sort asks the same questions on } \sigma \text{ and on } \tau @@ each question is chosen from the answers so far
\text{same questions, same answers} \Rightarrow \text{the same rearrangement } \rho \text{ is applied to both} @@ the sort moves items only by what it has learned
\rho(\sigma) = \text{sorted} = \rho(\tau) \Rightarrow \sigma = \tau @@ a rearrangement is undoable (19a): apply ρ⁻¹ to the sorted list
a \text{ is one-to-one} \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{n! \le 2^k} \quad \blacksquare @@ n! orders in 2ᵏ boxes, no box with two (42i1)

The proof never looks inside the sort. It names one arrow and asks where it can lead: a one-to-one arrow leads only to a set at least as large. The arrangement of the questions and their sequence are hidden in a; the count is all that shows.

inf79.2The Floor in Whole Numbers

Let S(n) be the least k for which some comparing sort of n different items never asks more than k questions. A number of questions is whole, so the technique above gives S(n) ≥ ⌈log₂ n!⌉, the least whole k with 2ᵏ ≥ n!. For n = 2, 3, 4, 5 the orders number 2, 6, 24, 120 and the floors are 1, 3, 5, 7. A floor says what cannot be done. Whether it can be met is another question, answered only by showing a sort.

Five-in-seven technique: five different items can be sorted with 7 comparisons; so S(5) = 7.

\text{compare two pairs; name them so that } a < b,\ c < d @@ 2 comparisons; e waits
\text{compare } b \text{ with } d\text{; name them so that } b < d @@ 3 so far: a < b < d and c < d
\text{place } e \text{ in the chain } a < b < d\text{: against } b\text{, then against } a \text{ or } d @@ 5 so far: a chain of four
c < d\text{: place } c \text{ among the chain’s items below } d @@ at most three of them
\text{at most three items: against the middle one, then one more} @@ 7 in all
2^6 = 64 < 120 \le 128 = 2^7 \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{S(5) = 7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the floor is 7, and this sort meets it

Merge sort on five items can take 8: one for a pair, three for a triple, four to merge. The sort above pairs first and inserts after, each insertion into a chain of three, the longest that two questions can search. No question is spent where its two answers are far from evenly likely.

inf79.3When the Floor Is out of Reach

To meet the floor a sort has no question to spare. Suppose it may ask k questions, and after one of them j are left.

Tight-split technique: with j questions left, the orders still possible on each answer number at most 2ʲ.

Y = \text{the orders still possible after the answer yes} @@ and N after no
\text{the rest of the sort tells the orders of } Y \text{ apart in at most } j \text{ questions} @@ it must sort every one of them
a|_Y : Y \to \{\text{yes}, \text{no}\}^j \text{ is one-to-one} @@ the orders-into-rows technique, on the rest
\htmlClass{new}{|Y| \le 2^j \text{ and } |N| \le 2^j} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for N

Four items have 24 orders and a floor of 5. After a < b there are 12 left and 4 questions. Ask c against d and each answer leaves 6. Ask b against c and the answer c < b leaves 8, which is 2³ exactly: legal, with nothing to spare from then on.

For twelve items, 12! = 479,001,600 lies between 2²⁸ and 2²⁹, so the floor is 29. Every way of asking that keeps to the tight split at every question was tried by machine in 1964, and each one ran out: S(12) = 30. The one-to-one arrow into 2²⁹ rows exists as a map of sets. No sort is that arrow.

inf79.4The Search by Machine

The sort of ∞79.2, pairing first and then inserting into chains whose lengths are one less than a power of 2, was given for any n in 1959. For n up to 11 it meets the floor. Past that, each value of S(n) has taken a machine search to settle, and each time so far the 1959 sort has turned out to be the best there is.

  • S(12) = 30, one above the floor (searched 1964, published 1965).
  • S(13) = 34 (1994, and again by another search in 2002); S(14) = 38 and S(22) = 71 (2004); S(15) = 42 and S(19) = 58 (2007).
  • S(16) = 46, S(17) = 50 and S(18) = 54 (preprint 2022, dated 2023 in its own table). The floor for 16 is 45, and 45 had been conjectured.
  • From 23 on S(n) is open: S(23) is 75 or 76. The 1959 sort is known not to be best at n = 47 (1981).

On 7 June 2023 a learning system was reported to have found shorter machine routines for sorting a handful of items, by playing a game whose moves add one instruction at a time. The routines went into a standard library. They save instructions, not comparisons: the cost counted is another one, and the floor of this page does not speak of it.

inf79.5Outside the Plan

The floor is a statement about a plan: learn only by comparing. 39l’s counting sort is outside it. It looks at an item and reads which of m values it has, an answer with m outcomes, not 2. The arrow of the first footing still exists; it leads somewhere larger.

Many-outcomes technique: a sort that learns by k looks, each with at most b outcomes, has n! ≤ bᵏ.

a(\sigma) = \text{the row of the } k \text{ outcomes} \in \{1, \dots, b\}^k @@ filled out to length k as before
a \text{ is one-to-one} @@ the same three lines as the orders-into-rows technique
\htmlClass{new}{n! \le b^k} \quad \blacksquare @@ n! orders in bᵏ boxes

With b = 2 this is the floor. With n looks of m ≥ n outcomes each, mⁿ ≥ nⁿ ≥ n!, so nothing forbids sorting in n looks, and counting sort does it. For whole numbers that each fit one machine word, sorts are known that take about n · log log n steps (2004), and about n · √(log log n) on average with random choices (2002). Whether n steps are enough is open.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞79.oq.1What is S(23), 75 or 76? And is there a way to find S(n) that does not try every tight split by machine? The floor is one arrow; what second arrow, if any, explains the extra comparison from 12 to 19 items and at 22?
∞79.oq.2Can n whole numbers, each fitting one machine word, be sorted in about n steps? The best known sorts are within a factor of log log n, and no floor above n has been proved.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The sort that pairs and then inserts was published in 1959. For up to 11 items it meets the floor of counted orders.

In 1964 a machine search showed that twelve items need 30 comparisons, one above the floor. The 1959 sort uses 30, so it stayed best there. In 1981 it was shown not to be best for 47 items.

Searches in 1994, 2002, 2004 and 2007 settled 13, 14, 15, 19 and 22 items. A preprint of 2022 settled 16, 17 and 18, and put the first open case at 23.

For whole numbers in machine words the comparison floor was first passed in the 1990s; the sorts of 2002 and 2004 are the best known, and the question of n steps stays open.

The accepted names

The floor is the information-theoretic lower bound for comparison sorting; the questions and answers drawn as a branching figure are a decision tree, and a row of answers is a path to a leaf. S(n) is the minimum number of comparisons, the subject of minimum-comparison sorting. The sort that pairs and inserts is merge insertion, or the Ford-Johnson algorithm. The crowded box is the pigeonhole principle. Sorting whole numbers that fit a word is integer sorting on the word RAM. The learning system of 2023 is AlphaDev, and the library is LLVM’s libc++.

Who said it first
