Can a Computer Try Every Route?

Can a computer try every route? A delivery van leaves its depot, calls at every stop on its list, and comes home. Which order of stops makes the drive shortest? With four stops a driver can try every order on the back of an envelope. With twenty, the orders are more than a fast computer can try in a lifetime. What makes it hard is that each new stop does not add to the work; it multiplies it. This page shows, with 4k’s counting and 22k’s ladder of growth, how many tours there are, and why trying them all soon outruns any machine. Then it shows a quick rule of thumb, always driving to the nearest stop not yet visited, that builds a tour at once. Last, it shows how to tell, without knowing the best tour, how far off the quick one can be.

With the depot fixed, the stops can be put in order in a factorial number of ways, and each tour is counted twice, once each way round. Each extra stop multiplies the count by the new number of stops, so a machine a thousand times faster buys only two more stops. The nearest-stop rule takes about half the square of the stops in looks, and its tour can be well off the best. Every tour uses two links at every stop, so half the sum of each stop’s two shortest links is a floor under the best tour, and any tour found can be measured against that floor.

22logi1Counting the Tours

A van leaves its depot, calls at each of n stops once, and comes home. A tour is the order of the stops. Roads are taken to be the same length both ways.

\text{orders of the } n \text{ stops} = n! @@ arrangements (4k)
\text{each order driven backwards is the same loop} @@ same roads, same length
\text{no order is its own backwards, for } n \ge 3 @@ its first stop and its last would trade places
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tours} = \tfrac{n!}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the orders pair off, two to a tour

Tour-count technique: a depot and n stops, n at least 3, make n!/2 different tours. Four stops make 12 tours. Six make 360. Ten make 1 814 400.

22logi2Why the Count Outruns Any Computer

Grant a computer that checks a billion tours a second, which is generous. 15 stops take it about 11 minutes. 20 stops take about 38 years. A computer running since the universe began, some 4.35 × 10¹⁷ seconds, would have finished every tour of 26 stops, but not of 27.

\tfrac{(n+1)!/2}{n!/2} = n + 1 @@ one more stop
\tfrac{n!}{c^n} \text{ grows at least twice over at each step once } n \ge 2d @@ the next factor is (n + 1)/c ≥ 2: powers of c lose to factorials (22k)
\tfrac{22!/2}{20!/2} = 21\cdot 22 = 462 < 1000 < 21\cdot 22\cdot 23 @@ from 20 stops
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a machine } 1000 \text{ times faster buys two more stops}} \quad \blacksquare @@ at 20 stops; fewer as n grows

One-more-stop technique: each extra stop multiplies the tours by the new number of stops, so a faster machine buys only a stop or two. Speed grows by factors; the count grows by a factor that itself keeps growing. No machine that tries every tour keeps up.

22logi3The Nearest-Stop Rule

A rule of thumb: from where you are, drive to the nearest stop not yet visited; when none is left, drive home. With n stops it looks at n links, then n − 1, and so on down to 1.

n + (n - 1) + \cdots + 1 = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} @@ doubled-sum (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{looks} = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 20 stops: 210 looks, not 1.2 × 10¹⁸ tours

A bike courier works the street grid of downtown Portland, where a block is 200 feet. Its shop O is at (0, 0), and its six stops are A (0, −4), B (6, 5), C (2, 3), D (3, −1), E (0, 3) and F (4, −3), in blocks east and north. A link’s length is the blocks along the streets, east-west plus north-south.

O \to E \to C \to D \to F \to A \to B \to O @@ nearest each time: 3, then 2, 5, 3, 5
3 + 2 + 5 + 3 + 5 + 15 + 11 = 44 @@ the last two links are long: A to B, and B home
O \to A \to F \to D \to B \to C \to E \to O = 4 + 5 + 3 + 9 + 6 + 2 + 3 = 32 @@ the best of all 360 tours
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{44}{32} = 1.375} @@ the quick tour is 37.5% longer

Nearest-stop rule: the rule builds a tour in about half the square of the stops in looks, but it leaves the far stops for last and can come home long. On stops scattered at random, it tends to run about a quarter over the best. There are layouts where it does far worse, and its worst grows, slowly, with the number of stops.

22logi4A Floor Under the Best Tour

The best tour of many stops cannot be found by trying. So how far off is a quick tour? Find a length that no tour can beat, and measure against that.

\text{a tour enters and leaves each place once} @@ two links at every place, the depot too
\text{tour} = \tfrac12\sum_{\text{places}} (\text{its two links}) @@ each link touches two places, so the sum counts it twice
\text{its two links} \ge \text{its two shortest links} @@ two links from one place, to two other places
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tour} \ge \tfrac12\sum_{\text{places}} (\text{two shortest links})} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every tour, so for the best

Two-links floor technique: every tour is at least half the sum, over the places, of each place’s two shortest links. For the courier the floor is 28.5 blocks. So the nearest-stop tour of 44 is at most 44/28.5 ≈ 1.54 times the best, known without trying a single other tour. The best is 32, so the floor was 11% short of it.

The largest cases are not closed by trying the orders either. In 2006 a tour of 85 900 points, the places a machine must touch on a chip, was proved shortest, at 142 382 641 units. It is still the largest case on the list of tours proved shortest.

The accepted names

The question of the shortest tour is the travelling salesman problem, and trying every tour is brute force. The nearest-stop rule is the nearest-neighbour heuristic; a rule that gives a good tour fast, with no promise of the best, is a heuristic. The two-links floor is a lower bound of the kind called a relaxation; its stronger cousin, the one-tree bound, gives the Held–Karp bound. A distance counted along a street grid is the taxicab, or Manhattan, distance. Problems like this one, for which no method is known that is fast every time, are called NP-hard, and the question in 22·logi.6.5 is the P versus NP problem.

Who said it first

Karl Menger posed the messenger problem in Vienna in 1930, and noted that the nearest-stop rule does not give the best tour. George Dantzig, Ray Fulkerson and Selmer Johnson (1954) found the best tour through 49 cities of the United States, by floors and cuts rather than by trying. Daniel Rosenkrantz, Richard Stearns and Philip Lewis (1977) showed that the nearest-stop tour is never more than about half the log of the number of stops times the best, when no detour is shorter than the direct link, and that layouts exist where it is off by a factor that grows that way. David Applegate, Robert Bixby, Vašek Chvátal and William Cook found the best tour through all 24 978 towns of Sweden (2004) and through 85 900 points of a computer chip (2006).

In 22·logi.6.5, Kurt Gödel asked John von Neumann in a letter of 1956 whether such questions could be settled in steps that grow only like a power of their size, and seemed to hope so. Jack Edmonds (1965) took a power of the size as the meaning of a good method, and guessed that this problem has none. Stephen Cook (1971) and Richard Karp (1972) showed that a good method for it would give one for thousands of other problems at once. Since 2000 a prize of a million dollars has stood for either answer.
