19·logi · logistics

Why Ship Gas as a Cold Liquid?

Why ship gas as a cold liquid? Natural gas is mostly methane, and as a gas it takes up so much room that no ship could carry enough of it to be worth the trip. Cooled to about −162 °C it becomes a liquid six hundred times smaller. But keeping it that cold is a fight: heat leaks in through every wall, and some of the cargo boils away each day. This page finds the shrink from 9·chem’s gas law, the cooling from 19·eng’s expanding gas, the leak through the tank wall from 13·eng2, the daily loss as a share, and the distance past which a ship beats a pipeline.

A liquid is about 600 times denser than the same gas at room conditions, so a ship carries 600 times more. Heat leaks in at kAΔT/L, and each unit of heat boils a fixed mass of cargo, so a fixed share boils off each day. A pipeline costs more per kilometre; a ship costs more to start and less per kilometre; past the distance where the two cost lines cross, ship.

19logi1About Six Hundred Times Smaller

Methane as a gas, at room temperature and normal pressure, weighs about 0.7 kg per cubic metre. As a liquid at −162 °C it weighs about 420 to 450 kg per cubic metre.

\dfrac{\text{liquid density}}{\text{gas density}} \approx \dfrac{430}{0.72} @@ the same mass, two volumes
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 600} @@ a cubic metre of liquid holds 600 cubic metres of gas

Squeezing the gas instead, by the gas law (9·chem), would take about 600 times normal pressure for the same shrink, needing walls far too thick and heavy for a ship. Cooling is the cheaper way to make the gas small.

19logi2Cooling by Expansion

To cool a gas, squeeze it, let the heat of squeezing flow away into cooling water, then let it expand quickly. A gas that expands too fast for heat to flow in does work pushing outward, and pays for that work out of its own heat, so it cools (19·eng).

P\,V^{\gamma} \text{ stays the same, so } T\,V^{\gamma - 1} \text{ stays the same} @@ too fast for heat to flow (19·eng), with the gas law
\text{double the volume, } \gamma \approx 1.3:\ T \to T \cdot 2^{-0.3} \approx 0.81\,T @@ in kelvin
\htmlClass{new}{300 \text{ K} \to \text{about } 245 \text{ K}} @@ about −28 °C from one doubling

One expansion is not enough to reach 111 K (−162 °C), so plants run the loop again and again, each cold gas cooling the next stream, like 19·eng’s engine run backwards. That costs energy: about a tenth of the gas’s energy goes into making it liquid.

19logi3Heat Leaks in Through the Tank Wall

The cargo sits about 180 °C colder than the sea air. Heat flows in through the tank’s insulation at the rate 13·eng2 found for a wall.

\text{rate} = \dfrac{k\,A\,\Delta T}{L} @@ through a layer (13·eng2)
k = 0.02 \text{ W/(m·K)},\ A = 6\,000 \text{ m}^2,\ \Delta T = 180 \text{ K},\ L = 0.3 \text{ m} @@ foam a third of a metre thick
\htmlClass{new}{\text{rate} = \dfrac{0.02 \cdot 6\,000 \cdot 180}{0.3} = 72\,000 \text{ W}} @@ about 72 kW

No insulation stops it, only slows it. Doubling the foam halves the leak but takes cargo space. Designers trade the two.

19logi4Boil-Off: A Little Lost Every Day

The cargo stays at its boiling point, so every joule that leaks in boils some liquid off: about 510,000 joules boil one kilogram of methane. The boiled gas is drawn off and usually burned in the ship’s engines.

72\,000 \text{ W} \times 86\,400 \text{ s} \approx 6.2 \times 10^9 \text{ J a day} @@ seconds in a day
\dfrac{6.2 \times 10^9}{510\,000} \approx 12\,000 \text{ kg a day} @@ boiled
\text{tank holds about } 40\,000 \text{ m}^3 \times 430 \text{ kg/m}^3 \approx 17 \times 10^6 \text{ kg} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{share a day} \approx \tfrac{12\,000}{17 \times 10^6} \approx 0.07\%} @@ within the 0.1% to 0.15% ships plan for

Boil-off technique: heat leaks in at a steady rate, so a steady share r of the cargo boils off each day, and after n days the cargo is (1 − r)ⁿ of what was loaded, close to 1 − nr when nr is small. At 0.15% a day, a 20-day voyage loses about 3%. A shipping planner works out each voyage’s loss this way and prices it into the cargo.

19logi5Ship or Pipeline: The Break-Even Distance

A pipeline costs a lot for every kilometre of pipe. A ship route costs a great deal to start, for the liquefying plant and the receiving terminal, but little more for each extra kilometre of sea. So each has a cost line in the distance (18c), and the lines cross.

\text{pipeline: } c_p = 1.0 \cdot d @@ per 1,000 km
\text{ship: } c_s = 3.0 + 0.2 \cdot d @@ big start, small slope
1.0\,d = 3.0 + 0.2\,d \Rightarrow d = 3.75 @@ where the lines cross
\htmlClass{new}{\text{past about } 3\,750 \text{ km, the ship is cheaper}} @@ for these numbers

Below the crossing distance, pipe; above it, ship. Pipelines under the sea cost more per kilometre than on land, which moves the crossing closer. The real crossing depends on prices that change, but its shape does not: a line with a big start and small slope always overtakes a steep line from zero. An energy planner draws these two lines for each new gas field before choosing.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The cold liquid is liquefied natural gas, LNG. The fast expansion with no heat flow is adiabatic expansion; the gas boiled off is boil-off gas, and its daily share the boil-off rate. The receiving plant is a regasification terminal.

Who said it first

Carl von Linde built a machine to liquefy air by repeated expansion in 1895. The first ship cargo of LNG crossed the Atlantic in 1959. The cost figures in footing 5 are made-up round numbers; real break-even distances are often quoted in the low thousands of kilometres (not yet revisited).
