Functions are arrows with lengths and angles

Rung 27 made arrows of lists and of functions alike, and gave them grids, readings and stretches. It never said how long an arrow is, or when two arrows stand at right angles. Lists had the dot product since rung 12, but a function has no corners to measure. This rung asks for one thing, a product of two arrows that is straight in each slot, indifferent to order and positive on every arrow but 0, and finds that it is enough. The product gives every arrow a size and every two arrows an angle. It finds the closest point of any flat piece, as a shadow with its miss at right angles, and least squares turns out to be that shadow. It straightens any basis, gives every symmetric map right-angled axes, and makes every grid a turn, a stretch and a turn. Then it turns to functions. Sized by their totals, with differences on a set of size zero ignored, they fill spaces with no holes and endlessly many directions, where readings along right angles never hold more energy than the arrow, and rebuild it when no direction is left out. Polynomials, built from coin tosses, come near every continuous function at once.

One product, straight in each slot, indifferent to order and positive, gives arrows lengths and angles. With it, closest points are shadows with misses at right angles, symmetric maps have right-angled axes, and every grid is a turn, a stretch and a turn. Functions, sized by totals, are arrows in complete spaces with endlessly many directions, where readings along right angles keep energy.

The class · read it through, then go to 31a

What it means to say it

To say that functions are arrows with lengths and angles is to say that one product does for a function what the dot did for a list of two numbers. It says how long the function is, how far it is from another, and when two functions stand at right angles, so that one carries nothing of the other. Everything else on the rung is built on that one product.

A product that is straight in each slot, indifferent to order and positive gives every arrow a size and every two arrows an angle; with it, the closest point of a flat piece is a shadow whose miss is at right angles, and functions sized by totals become arrows in a complete space with endlessly many directions. The rung proves it.

Where it comes from

A French mathematician proved the product bound for lists in 1821, a Russian mathematician for integrals in 1859, and a German one again in 1888. The same French mathematician found in 1829 that symmetric grids have real stretches, and an English mathematician proved in 1852 that the signs of a quadratic form cannot be changed. An Italian and a French mathematician found the turn, the stretch and the turn in 1873 and 1874. A Danish actuary straightened lists of functions in 1883, and a German mathematician did it in general in 1907. In 1885 a German mathematician proved that polynomials come near every continuous function, thirteen years after he had built a continuous function with no slope anywhere, and in 1912 a mathematician in Kharkiv built the near polynomials from coin tosses. Between 1904 and 1910 a German mathematician built the plane with endlessly many directions, and in 1907 a Hungarian and an Austrian mathematician showed that the functions sized by the square of their totals have no holes. A Polish mathematician built complete spaces with a size, with no product, around 1920.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, a product gives sizes: parts at right angles add their squared sizes (31a1), every product is bounded by the sizes (31a3), and the size of a difference is a distance, with the triangle (31a4).

Second, the closest point of a flat piece is a shadow. On a right-angled list it is the sum of the readings times the arrows (31b2), and on any free list it solves one grid of products (31b3), so the best line of 13·stat is a shadow (31b4). Any free list can be straightened into a right-angled one building the same pieces (31c1), and every grid with free columns is right angles times a staircase (31c3).

Third, a symmetric map has real stretches (31d1) and a right-angled basis of axes (31d3), so a quadratic form read along its axes is a sum of squares whose signs no change of directions can change (31e1, 31e2), which explains 28l’s test for flat points (31e4). Every grid is a turn, a stretch and a turn (31f2), and its largest pieces give the closest simpler grid (31f4).

Fourth, functions have sizes: the highest height, the total and the energy (31g1, 31g2). Two functions that agree but for a set of size zero are taken as the same, and that bends the same-members axiom (31g3); then the spaces of the total and of the energy have no holes (31g4). In a complete product space, readings along a right-angled list never hold more energy than the arrow (31h2), rebuild it and keep its energy exactly when no direction is left out (31h3), and every closed flat piece has one closest point (31h4). Without a product, a complete space with a size still has continuous straight maps exactly when they are bounded (31i2), and with one, every bounded reading is a product (31i3).

Fifth, every continuous function on a closed interval has polynomials within any ε at every input at once, built from the averages of coin tosses (31j1, 31j2); and they come near even a function with no slope anywhere, in height though never in slope (31j3).

Anexact, yet rigorous. Rung 31 carries four green boxes, and in each the measuring kind of care becomes geometry. 31a carries counting, and the triangle: parts at right angles add their squared sizes for any product, so a signal and a hum at right angles combine like the sides of a right triangle. 31g carries two sizes of truth: in a space built on totals a function is known by what its totals say, and its value at one point is not a fact about it, as no meter reports one instant. 31j carries loose, then locked: averages of coin tosses settle at each input, and one bound that serves every input at once locks them into one polynomial. 31l carries the cloud: a correlation from a sample is the cosine of an angle that wobbles, reported with its width. The annexes carry their own. 31·stat carries the cloud: a model’s miss on held-back data is reported with its width, and once used to choose, it flatters. 31·then carries the cloud: a strength read off a calibration line is reported with its width, most of it the wobble of the unknown’s own reading.

What follows

With those five, the techniques compute. Shadows and least squares are found by hand, correlation and the fitted line are read as one angle, curves are put through given points with the best points chosen, stretches are found by machine, and the directions of most spread are found in a cloud of data. The annexes take these into models that fit too well, calibration lines and power laws, swaying towers, insurance and lottery tickets, lines at counters, polarized sunglasses, and pictures stored small.

What you can do because you know it

You can measure the size of a function and the angle between two, find the closest point of any flat piece by a shadow, fit a line or a curve by least squares and say what the fit leaves out, and straighten any basis. You can find the axes of a symmetric map, read the signs of a quadratic form, split any grid into a turn, a stretch and a turn, and keep its largest pieces. You can say when a list of functions leaves no direction out, and approximate any continuous function by a polynomial at every input at once.

From here on, a function is an arrow, and the question of how close two functions are has an answer.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down as an axiom. The rung bends one: 2c’s same-members axiom, which says two things are the same when they have the same members, is bent on 31g, where two functions are taken as the same when they agree but for a set of size zero, and the rung says so where it does. It spends rung 8’s least upper bound, rung 12’s dot, rung 15’s even continuity, rung 25’s turning numbers, rung 27’s arrows, grids and stretches, and rung 30’s sizes, totals and averages of tries. One equality is held in trust: that the sines on 0 to 1 leave no direction out, which rung 32 proves. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say functions are arrows with lengths and angles, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the product bound for sums in 1821, Viktor Bunyakovsky for integrals in 1859, and Hermann Schwarz again in 1888. Cauchy found that symmetric grids have real stretches in 1829, and James Joseph Sylvester proved that the signs of a quadratic form cannot be changed in 1852. Eugenio Beltrami in 1873 and Camille Jordan in 1874 found the turn, the stretch and the turn. Jørgen Pedersen Gram straightened lists of functions in 1883, and Erhard Schmidt in general in 1907.

Karl Weierstrass built a continuous function with no slope anywhere in 1872 and proved in 1885 that polynomials come near every continuous function; Sergei Bernstein built the near polynomials from coin tosses in 1912. David Hilbert built the plane with endlessly many directions from 1904 to 1910, Frigyes Riesz and Ernst Fischer proved L² complete in 1907, and Riesz and Maurice Fréchet showed that bounded readings are products that year. Stefan Banach set out complete spaces with a size in 1920 and 1932, and in 1931 he and Stefan Mazurkiewicz showed that most continuous functions have no slope anywhere.

Accepted names: a product is an inner product, and a space with one an inner product space; a complete one is a Hilbert space, and a complete space with a size a Banach space. The product bound is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, straightening is the Gram–Schmidt process, the right-angled axes of a symmetric map are the spectral theorem, the fixed signs are Sylvester’s law of inertia, and the turn, stretch and turn is the singular value decomposition. The energy bound is Bessel’s inequality and its equality Parseval’s identity. Bounded readings as products is the Riesz representation theorem, and polynomials near every continuous function is the Weierstrass approximation theorem, with Bernstein polynomials.
