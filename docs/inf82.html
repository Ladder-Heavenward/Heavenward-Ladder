How Fast Can Two Grids Be Multiplied?

How fast can two grids be multiplied? The school rule for the product of two n by n grids uses n³ products of numbers. In 1969 it was found that two by two grids can be multiplied with seven products in place of eight, and that the saving compounds when the entries are themselves grids: the count falls from n³ to about n^2.807. No rule can use fewer than n² products, since the answer has n² entries that may be anything. Between 2 and 2.807 lies a number ω, the true exponent, and nobody knows it. The hunt for it is a hunt in arrow math: the product is one map out of a tensor product, a rule is that map written as a sum of simple terms, and the fewest terms is a rank for three spaces where rung 47 had two. The record stood at 2.371339 from 2024, and in August 2026 it fell to 2.371177.

An r-product rule: AB = Σₛ αₛ(A)·βₛ(B)·Cₛ, with readings αₛ, βₛ and fixed grids Cₛ. Two by two: r = 7. Stacking: r products for m by m give rᵏ for mᵏ by mᵏ, about n^(log_m r) steps; ω ≤ log₂ 7 ≈ 2.807. Floor: the Cₛ build all n by n grids, so r ≥ n²; ω ≥ 2. Three spaces: t = a⊗a⊗b + a⊗b⊗a + b⊗a⊗a is a limit of two-term members and needs three terms.

inf82.1Seven Products for Two by Two

The product C = AB of two n by n grids has entries cik = Σj aijbjk, as in 27d. The school rule makes n³ products of numbers and nearly as many additions. For n = 2 that is 8 products. Adding two grids is cheap beside multiplying them, so the products are what a better rule must save.

Seven-products technique: the four entries of the product of two 2 by 2 grids are sums and differences of seven products, each a sum of a’s times a sum of b’s, with the a’s on the left.

M_1 = (a_{11} + a_{22})(b_{11} + b_{22}) @@ a sum of a’s times a sum of b’s
M_2 = (a_{21} + a_{22})\,b_{11},\quad M_3 = a_{11}(b_{12} - b_{22}) @@ the a’s on the left
M_4 = a_{22}(b_{21} - b_{11}),\quad M_5 = (a_{11} + a_{12})\,b_{22} @@ the same
M_6 = (a_{21} - a_{11})(b_{11} + b_{12}) @@ the same
M_7 = (a_{12} - a_{22})(b_{21} + b_{22}) @@ seven products in all
c_{11} = M_1 + M_4 - M_5 + M_7 @@ multiply out: twelve terms, ten cancel in pairs, a₁₁b₁₁ + a₁₂b₂₁ is left
c_{12} = M_3 + M_5,\quad c_{21} = M_2 + M_4 @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{c_{22} = M_1 - M_2 + M_3 + M_6} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out; no line moved an a past a b

That is 7 products and 18 additions, against 8 products and 4 additions. For two grids of four numbers it is no gain at all. The last reason in the proof is where the gain lies.

inf82.2Stacking a Rule on Itself

Call it an r-product rule for m by m grids when there are r products, each one reading of A times one reading of B in that order, and every entry of AB is a fixed mix of the products. A reading of A is a fixed mix of its entries. Multiplied out with each a left of each b, the two sides of such a rule are the same sum of terms aijbkl. So the rule holds as well when the entries are square grids of one size: grids add and spread as numbers do, and the one thing they do not do, swap in a product, is never asked of them.

Stacking technique: an r-product rule for m by m grids, with r > m², gives an rᵏ-product rule for mᵏ by mᵏ grids, and multiplies n by n grids in about C·nlog_m r steps, along the powers of m.

\text{cut an } m^k \text{ by } m^k \text{ grid into } m^2 \text{ blocks of size } m^{k-1} @@ blocks multiply by the same rule, order kept (27d)
\text{the rule: } r \text{ products of blocks, and a fixed number of sums of blocks} @@ it holds for entries that are grids
P(k) = r\,P(k - 1),\ P(0) = 1 \Rightarrow P(k) = r^k @@ each product of blocks by the same rule again; induction (4c)
T(n) = r\,T(n/m) + c\,n^2 @@ a sum of blocks costs at most n² additions
\htmlClass{new}{T(n) \sim C\,n^{\log_m r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the master technique (22l2): r/m² > 1, the bottom wins

With m = 2 and r = 7 this is the n2.807 that 39m took on trust, since log₂ 7 ≈ 2.8074. Call an exponent e reachable when some rule multiplies n by n grids in at most C·nᵉ steps, and write ω for the greatest lower bound of the reachable exponents. Every r-product rule for m by m grids gives ω ≤ log_m r. A rule with 21 products for three by three grids would beat seven for two by two, since log₃ 21 ≈ 2.771; one with 22 would not.

inf82.3The Floor Is n Squared

Write a rule out in full. Each product is αₛ(A)·βₛ(B), a reading of A times a reading of B, and it is handed to the entries of the answer in fixed shares, which make a fixed grid Cₛ. So an r-product rule says AB = Σₛ αₛ(A)·βₛ(B)·Cₛ, for s from 1 to r.

Square-floor technique: every r-product rule for n by n grids has r ≥ n²; so ω ≥ 2.

AB = \textstyle\sum_{s=1}^{r} \alpha_s(A)\,\beta_s(B)\,C_s @@ the rule, for all A and B
B = I:\quad A = \textstyle\sum_s \alpha_s(A)\,\beta_s(I)\,C_s @@ AI = A
C_1, \dots, C_r \text{ build every } n \text{ by } n \text{ grid} @@ A is a mix of them, with counts αₛ(A)βₛ(I)
r \ge n^2 @@ the exchange technique (27b): n² free grids, one for each entry
\htmlClass{new}{n^2 \le \text{products},\quad 2 \le \omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ a rule with fewer than n² steps cannot even write the answer

So the fewest products for two by two grids is between 4 and 7, and ω is between 2 and 2.807. Read it with the arrows of 47h. The map (A, B) ↦ AB is straight in each slot, so it is exactly one linear map μ out of G ⊗ G, where G is the space of n by n grids. A term αₛ(A)·βₛ(B)·Cₛ is the simplest such map there is: one reading of each slot, and one grid. A rule is μ written as a sum of r of them, and the order of the products, and how the sums are arranged, has gone out of sight. For two spaces the fewest terms was the rank of a grid, found by the methods of rung 27. Here three spaces are in play, two of readings and one of grids, and no such method is known.

inf82.4Fewer Terms in the Limit

With three spaces something happens that two never allow. Let a and b be free vectors of a space V, and build V ⊗ V ⊗ V as (V ⊗ V) ⊗ V, by 47h1 used twice. Its simplest members are the single terms u ⊗ v ⊗ w. Take t = a ⊗ a ⊗ b + a ⊗ b ⊗ a + b ⊗ a ⊗ a.

Limit-of-two technique: t is the limit, as ε heads to 0, of members with two terms; and t itself cannot be written with two.

(a + \varepsilon b) \otimes (a + \varepsilon b) \otimes (a + \varepsilon b) = a \otimes a \otimes a + \varepsilon\,t + \varepsilon^2 u + \varepsilon^3\, b \otimes b \otimes b @@ straight in each slot; u holds the three terms with two b’s
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\big[(a + \varepsilon b)^{\otimes 3} - a^{\otimes 3}\big] = t + \varepsilon\,u + \varepsilon^2\, b \otimes b \otimes b \to t @@ two terms on the left, for every ε ≠ 0
t = a \otimes S_a + b \otimes S_b,\quad S_a = a \otimes b + b \otimes a,\ \ S_b = a \otimes a @@ collect the first slot
t = u_1 \otimes x_1 + u_2 \otimes x_2 \Rightarrow S_a, S_b \text{ are mixes of } x_1, x_2 @@ suppose two terms, each xᵢ a single v ⊗ w; read the count of a, then of b, in the first slot
S_a, S_b \text{ free} \Rightarrow x_1, x_2 \text{ are free mixes of } S_a, S_b @@ two free members of what two members build (27b)
p\,S_a + q\,S_b \text{ has grid } \begin{pmatrix} q & p \\ p & 0 \end{pmatrix},\ \det = -p^2 @@ counts on a ⊗ a, a ⊗ b, b ⊗ a, b ⊗ b
\text{rank } 1 \text{ only when } p = 0:\ \text{the multiples of } S_b @@ a single v ⊗ w has rank 1 (47h2), so its determinant is 0 (27e)
\htmlClass{new}{x_1, x_2 \text{ both multiples of } S_b:\ \text{not free}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so no two terms give t

So “at most two terms” is not kept by limits when there are three slots. In 1979 this was put to use. A rule for the grid product may be allowed to hold only in the limit, with ε left in it, and it still gives a bound on ω when it is stacked. The records since count terms in this looser way.

inf82.5The Exponent Since 1969

Each entry is a bound proved on ω, with its year.

  • 1969: 2.8074, the seven products.
  • 1978 to 1981: 2.796, then 2.780 with a rule that holds in the limit, then 2.522.
  • 1990: 2.3755. One three-way member, far from the grid product itself, is raised to high powers by stacking, and grid products are found inside the powers.
  • 2010 to 2024: 2.3737, 2.3729, 2.3728639, 2.3728596, 2.371866, 2.371552, 2.371339, each a finer study of the powers of that same member.
  • August 2026: 2.371177. The search at the heart of the method was set up afresh and run with tools from machine learning, and the numbers found were rounded to fractions and checked again exactly.
  • Small sizes are open too. Three by three grids have had a rule of 23 products since 1976, and at least 19 are needed. For four by four, stacking seven on itself gives 49; a machine search of 2022 found 47 where 1 + 1 = 0, and one of 2025 found 48 for complex numbers.

It has also been proved that the member used since 1990 cannot bring the bound down to 2, whatever power is taken. If ω is 2, as many who work on it expect, the proof needs a new starting point. And none of the rules after 1969 is run on a machine: their gain shows only for grids far larger than any that can be stored.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞82.oq.1Is ω = 2? The floor is 2 and the best bound of August 2026 is 2.371177. No rule is known that reaches 2, and no proof that none does; the member studied since 1990 is proved unable to settle it.
∞82.oq.2What is the fewest number of products for three by three grids? It is at least 19 and at most 23, and the 23 has stood since 1976.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The seven products of 1969 showed that the n³ of the school rule is not the cost of the product, and opened the question of ω.

From 1978 the bound fell by new ideas every few years: rules that hold in the limit, and in 1990 the method of powers that every record since has refined.

From 2010 the gains have been in the third decimal place and beyond. In 2022 and 2025 machine searches found new rules for small sizes, and in 2026 a machine search took part in a record for ω itself.

The accepted names

A grid is a matrix, and ω is the exponent of matrix multiplication. An r-product rule is a bilinear algorithm, and the fewest products is the rank of the matrix multiplication tensor ⟨n, n, n⟩, a member of a tensor product of three spaces. The seven products are Strassen’s algorithm; stacking is the recursive, or tensor power, construction. The fewest terms in the limit is the border rank, and a rule that holds in the limit is an approximate bilinear algorithm; the member t is the W tensor, of rank 3 and border rank 2. The member used since 1990 is the Coppersmith–Winograd tensor and the method of powers is the laser method. Rules too large ever to run are called galactic algorithms. Arithmetic where 1 + 1 = 0 is the field of two elements.

Who said it first

Volker Strassen, “Gaussian elimination is not optimal” (1969), and the laser method (1986). Victor Pan (1978). Dario Bini, Milvio Capovani, Grazia Lotti and Francesco Romani, on approximate rules (1979). Arnold Schönhage (1981). Don Coppersmith and Shmuel Winograd (1990). Andrew Stothers (2010), Virginia Vassilevska Williams (2012), François Le Gall (2014), Josh Alman and Virginia Vassilevska Williams (2020), Ran Duan, Hongxun Wu and Renfei Zhou (2022), and the papers of 2024 by Vassilevska Williams, Yinzhan Xu, Zixuan Xu and Zhou, and by Alman, Duan, Vassilevska Williams, Xu, Xu and Zhou. The record of August 2026: Emilien Dupont, Marvin Eisenberger, Borislav Kozlovskii, Abbas Mehrabian, Francisco Ruiz, Abigail See, Renfei Zhou, Josh Alman, Virginia Vassilevska Williams and Matej Balog.
