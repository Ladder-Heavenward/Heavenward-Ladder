23i · technique

How many terms

A sum that settles gives a number only when you stop adding, and then the question is how wrong you are. This step answers it by hand. There are three ways to fence what you left out, and the cheapest one that applies is the one to use. A fence that is narrow is not yet an answer to so many places: the whole fence, slips of rounding included, has to round the same way. With that settled, the step computes the growth constant and the sine of a tenth to six places, shows why a small angle can stand in for its own sine, and shows how a calculator moves any input close to the centre before it adds a single piece.

Fence the leftover the cheapest way that applies. A value is known to so many places when its whole fence rounds the same way. Small angles are nearly their own sines. Move the input near the centre before adding anything.

23i1Fences for the leftover

Cheapest-fence technique: to fence the leftover of a sum at x, use the bracket when the signs take turns and the sizes fall, a geometric sum when each piece is at most k times the one before with k < 1, and the error ceiling otherwise, and choose the sum whose pieces shrink fastest at x. The bracket says the leftover is smaller than the next piece. The geometric fence says it is at most the next piece over 1 − k. The error ceiling needs a ceiling on a slope.

\ln 2 = 1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots,\ \ \text{leftover after } n \text{ pieces} < \tfrac{1}{n+1} @@ the bracket (22g1)
\tfrac{1}{n+1} < 0.0000005 \Rightarrow n \ge 2\,000\,000 @@ two million pieces
\ln 2 = 2\big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac{1}{3 \cdot 3^3} + \tfrac{1}{5 \cdot 3^5} + \cdots\big),\ \ \text{each piece under } \tfrac19 \text{ of the last} @@ 23h3; the ratio of neighbours is (2m − 1)/(9(2m + 1))
\text{six pieces: } 0.69314707\ldots,\ \ \text{leftover} \le \tfrac{\text{next piece}}{1 - 1/9} < 0.00000011 @@ the leftover technique (22i2); the next piece is 2/(13 · 3¹³)
\htmlClass{new}{0.69314707 < \ln 2 < 0.69314719,\ \text{so } \ln 2 = 0.693147 \text{ to six places}} \quad \blacksquare @@ six pieces against two million

The fence was never the hard part. The choice of sum was. Both sums settle at ln 2, and one of them is useless for computing. A sum whose pieces shrink by a fixed factor gains a fixed number of places with every piece.

23i2Six places of e

Rounding-fence technique: a total is known to d places when every number in its fence, with the slips of rounding added, rounds to the same d places. A fence narrower than half a unit in the last place is needed, and it is not enough: it must also miss the boundary halfway between two roundings.

1 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.166666667 + 0.041666667 + 0.008333333 + 0.001388889 + 0.000198413 + 0.000024802 + 0.000002756 @@ 1/k! for k = 0 to 9, each rounded to nine places
= 2.718281527,\ \ \text{slips} \le 10 \times 0.0000000005 = 0.000000005 @@ each rounding moves a piece by at most half a unit in the ninth place
\text{leftover} \le \tfrac{1/10!}{1 - 1/11} = \tfrac{11}{10 \cdot 10!} < 0.000000304 @@ from 1/10! on, each piece is at most 1/11 of the one before (22i2)
2.718281522 < e < 2.718281836 @@ the fence, slips included
\htmlClass{new}{e = 2.718282 \text{ to six places}} \quad \blacksquare @@ every number in the fence rounds to it

Nine pieces would not do. Their fence runs from 2.7182787 to 2.7182819, and the two ends round to different sixth places. Ten pieces leave the lower end at 2.718281522, only 0.000000022 above the boundary 2.7182815 that decides the rounding. Had it fallen below, an eleventh piece would settle it. That is all the rounding-fence technique asks: add pieces until the whole fence rounds one way.

23i3Small angles

Small-angle technique: for |θ| ≤ h, with θ in radians, sin θ is θ to within h³/6, and cos θ is 1 − θ²/2 to within h⁴/24. The proof skips a term: sin has no θ² piece, so its match of degree 1 is already its match of degree 2, and the error is one power smaller than it looks.

T_1 = T_2 = \theta \text{ for } \sin @@ the θ² coefficient of sin is 0 (23a3)
\sin\theta - \theta = R_2(\theta) = -\tfrac{\cos c}{3!}\,\theta^3 @@ the match-error technique (23b1) with n = 2; the third slope of sin is −cos
|\sin\theta - \theta| \le \tfrac{h^3}{6} @@ |cos c| ≤ 1
T_2 = T_3 = 1 - \tfrac{\theta^2}{2} \text{ for } \cos,\ \ \cos\theta - T_3(\theta) = \tfrac{\cos c}{4!}\,\theta^4 @@ no θ³ piece; the fourth slope of cos is cos
\htmlClass{new}{|\sin\theta - \theta| \le \tfrac{h^3}{6},\ \ \big|\cos\theta - \big(1 - \tfrac{\theta^2}{2}\big)\big| \le \tfrac{h^4}{24}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every |θ| ≤ h
  
\sin 0.1 = 0.1 - \tfrac{0.001}{6} + \tfrac{0.00001}{120} - \cdots @@ the sum for sin (23b2)
0.1 - 0.000166667 = 0.099833333 @@ two pieces
0 < \text{leftover} < \tfrac{0.1^5}{120} < 0.0000000834 @@ the bracket (22g1): the next piece is positive
0.099833333 < \sin 0.1 < 0.099833417 @@ the fence
\htmlClass{new}{\sin 0.1 = 0.099833 \text{ to six places}} \quad \blacksquare @@ both ends round to it; a third piece gives 0.0998334167

Degrees must be turned into radians first. 1° is π/180 = 0.0174533, and the technique says sin 1° is that to within 0.0000009. The Moon, 384 400 km away on average, fills about 0.52° of sky, which is 0.0090757 radians. Its width is about 384 400 × 0.0090757 = 3489 km. The exact triangle, with a tangent, gives a width 0.02 km larger. The angle was measured to two figures, so the small-angle step costs nothing that the measurement had not already lost. The Moon is in fact about 3475 km across.

23i4Bring the input near the centre

Bring-near technique: before adding any pieces, move the input close to the centre with facts that are exact: take whole half turns off an angle, split off a power of 2 inside a log, and split an exponent into a whole part and a small part. The sums then shrink fast from the first piece. Only the facts you used to move the input need to be known to more places.

3\pi = 9.424777961,\ \ 10 - 3\pi = 0.575222039 @@ π to ten figures
\sin 10 = \sin(3\pi + 0.575222039) = -\sin 0.575222039 @@ sin(A + π) = −sin A, from the sum formula (21d): each half turn flips the sign
\sin 0.575222039 = 0.575222039 - 0.031721616 + 0.000524803 - 0.000004134 + \cdots @@ the sum for sin (23b2)
0 < \text{leftover} < 0.00000002 @@ the bracket: the next piece is x⁹/9!, about 0.000000019
\htmlClass{new}{\sin 10 = -0.544021 \text{ to six places}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the four pieces give 0.544021092, and the fence rounds one way

The slip in 3π is three times the slip in π, so π to ten figures is plenty here. For sin 1000 it would not be: 1000 − 318π multiplies the slip in π by 318. Every calculator key for sin, cos, ln and eˣ works this way: move the input near the centre with exact facts, then add a handful of pieces.

\text{the fence is } [0.1234564,\ 0.1234566], \text{ narrower than } 0.0000005, \text{ so six places are known}\,? @@ it straddles 0.1234565: its ends round to 0.123456 and 0.123457

The accepted names

The leftover is the truncation error, and the slips are rounding error. The cheapest-fence technique gathers the remainder estimates for alternating series, for series bounded by a geometric series, and the Lagrange bound. A value known to d places by the rounding-fence technique is correctly rounded; a total too close to a rounding boundary to settle cheaply is the table-maker's dilemma. The small-angle technique is the small-angle approximation. Moving the input near the centre is argument reduction (or range reduction). The sum for π in the build challenge is Machin's formula.

Who said it first

Henry Briggs computed logarithms to fourteen places by hand and published them in 1624. John Machin reached a hundred places of π in 1706 with the formula of the build challenge. Leonhard Euler printed e to twenty-three places in 1748.
