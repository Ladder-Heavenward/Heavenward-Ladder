Can One Letter of DNA Be Changed Without Cutting?

Can one letter of DNA be changed without cutting? The last step found the cutter and the crowd of near places around its target, and ended on a worry: a cut through both strands is mended by the cell in a hurry, and sometimes mended wrong. Since 2016 a second family of tools has changed a single letter in place instead, with no cut through both strands. This page stays with the ideas and the numbers. It counts what a change of one letter can be once the partner strand is counted too; it treats whether an edit took, and whether it took anywhere else, as chances, and asks how a chance too small to see is bounded; it prices the first approved treatment, which turns out to cut after all, and asks who can reach it; and it counts what happens to an edit that passes from parents to children, which is where the twins born in 2018 come in, and why nearly everyone in the field condemned their making. The last question is not arithmetic: who decides.

The 12 changes of one letter into another make 6 kinds of change of a letter pair, and the first two letter editors make 2 of them. An edit with bystanders is a tree of chances, and the clean branch has chance p(1 − b)ᵏ. If n reads show no unwanted change, its rate is at most 3/n at the 5% level, and hundreds of millions of treated cells make a rate of one in a million a few hundred cells. The first approved treatment, of 2023, cuts a switch rather than changing the sickle letter, and its price of $2.2 million is set against care costs by a discounted sum. An edit on one copy reaches each child with chance 1/2: its expected carriers stay at 1 per generation while the line dies out with chance 1.

inf30.1One Letter, Two Strands

A change of one letter is the smallest edit there is. At one place A, C, G or T becomes one of the other three, so a genome of 3 × 10⁹ places has 9 × 10⁹ possible changes of one letter. Many inherited diseases come from exactly one: sickle cell disease from one letter in the gene for a part of haemoglobin, an A that became a T.

But a letter never sits alone. The partner strand holds its pair (34·bio.1), so a change on one strand is a change of the pair, and the same change read from the other strand looks different. A change of A to G on one strand is, read on the other, a change of T to C.

Pair-kinds technique: the 12 changes of one letter into another fall into 6 kinds of change of a letter pair, two changes to a kind, each the other read on the partner strand.

4 \cdot 3 = 12 @@ a letter, then one of the 3 others (4k6)
X \to Y \text{ on one strand} = X' \to Y' \text{ on the other} @@ A′ = T, T′ = A, C′ = G, G′ = C (34·bio.1)
X \to Y \ne X' \to Y' @@ X′ ≠ X: no change is its own partner
\htmlClass{new}{12 / 2 = 6 \text{ kinds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ separate piles of two (4k5)

The letters come in two shapes. A and G are larger, C and T smaller, and every pair joins one of each. Of the 6 kinds, 2 keep the shape of each letter, G·C to A·T and A·T to G·C, and 4 swap it. The first letter editors, reported in 2016 and 2017, make exactly those 2. In plain words: a cutter blunted so that it cannot cut both strands holds the place, a second protein joined to it changes the chemistry of one letter so that it reads as another, and the cell’s own upkeep copies the change across to the partner strand. A later kind, reported in 2019, writes a short new stretch from a template it carries, and reaches all 6 kinds and small losses and gains as well.

If changes that cause disease were spread evenly over the 6 kinds, an editor that makes one kind would reach one in six of them. The 2017 report counted nearly half of the known disease-causing changes of one letter as the single kind its editor turns back, because that kind of change is the commonest on its own.

inf30.2Did It Change, and Only There?

In one cell the editor acts on its letter, or it does not. Within a few letters of its place it can also change a second letter of the same kind as the target, a bystander. So the outcome in one cell is a path through a tree: target changed or not, each bystander changed or not.

Clean-edit technique: if the target is changed with chance p and each of k bystanders with chance b, all independently, the chance that the target alone is changed is p(1 − b)ᵏ. The branch-product technique (30l2) along the one clean path.

p = 0.6,\ \ b = 0.1,\ \ k = 2 @@ suppose
p(1 - b)^2 = 0.6 \cdot 0.81 = 0.486 @@ branch-product (30l2)
p\big(1 - (1 - b)^2\big) = 0.6 \cdot 0.19 = 0.114 @@ target and at least one bystander (30l2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{of } \allowbreak \text{a } \allowbreak \text{million } \allowbreak \text{cells: } \allowbreak \text{about } \allowbreak \text{486 } \allowbreak \text{000 } \allowbreak \text{clean, } \allowbreak \text{114 } \allowbreak \text{000 } \allowbreak \text{with } \allowbreak \text{a } \allowbreak \text{bystander } \allowbreak \text{beside } \allowbreak \text{the } \allowbreak \text{edit}} \quad \blacksquare @@ averages add (30g1)

Whether a bystander matters depends on what it changes: some leave the protein as it was, and some do not. Independence is the guess most likely to be wrong, since two letters side by side meet the same protein in the same moment. In a real treatment these numbers are measured, not computed. The computing says what has to be measured, and how many cells a measured share means.

inf30.3Counting What Is Rare

An unwanted change somewhere else in the genome is rare in each cell. The question is how rare, and it is answered by reading copies of that place drawn from many cells. Suppose each read shows the change with chance f, independently, and n reads show it not once.

Nothing-seen technique: if n independent looks each see a thing with chance f, and none of them sees it, then any f above 3/n would have given that result with chance below 5%.

P(\text{none in } n) = (1 - f)^n @@ independent looks (30f2)
(1 - f)^n \le e^{-fn} @@ 1 − f ≤ e^(−f): eˣ lies above its tangent at 0 (17)
f > 3/n \Rightarrow e^{-fn} < e^{-3} \approx 0.0498 @@ e³ ≈ 20.1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{none } \allowbreak \text{seen } \allowbreak \text{in } \allowbreak \text{n } \allowbreak \text{reads, } \allowbreak \text{so } \allowbreak \text{f } \allowbreak \text{≤ } \allowbreak \text{3/n } \allowbreak \text{at } \allowbreak \text{the } \allowbreak \text{5\% } \allowbreak \text{level}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 3000 reads bound f by 1 in 1000

This bounds a rate. It never promises none. A treatment may return hundreds of millions of edited cells to a patient. If an unwanted change sits in one cell in a million, 2 × 10⁸ cells hold about 200 that carry it, and by the rare-events technique (22j2) the chance that none does is about e^(−200). Reads cannot see one in a million unless millions of them cover that one place. What a safety review weighs is not whether an unwanted change exists, but where it sits, how many cells carry it, and whether any of those cells could grow out of control.

inf30.4The First Approved Treatment

In November 2023 the United Kingdom approved the first treatment made with this family of tools, for sickle cell disease and a related blood disease, and the United States approved it for sickle cell in December 2023. It does not change one letter. It cuts, in a stretch of DNA that works as a switch, keeping the unborn baby’s form of haemoglobin turned off after birth. With the switch broken, the baby’s form comes back and stands in for the faulty adult form. For the first approved treatment, the answer to this page’s question is that it did not try.

In the trial reviewed for the American approval, 29 of 31 patients followed long enough had no severe pain crisis for at least a year. The list price in the United States was $2.2 million, for one treatment, given once. It needs the patient’s own blood-forming cells, months in a specialist hospital, and chemotherapy strong enough to empty the bone marrow, with the loss of fertility that can bring.

Lifetime-care technique: a cost C each year for n years, from next year on, discounted at a rate r a year, is worth (C/r)(1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ) today, and C/r if it never ends. The endless-ratio-sum technique (15i3) with ratio 1/(1 + r), less its tail after year n.

\textstyle\sum_{i \ge 1} C(1 + r)^{-i} = \dfrac{C/(1 + r)}{1 - 1/(1 + r)} = \dfrac{C}{r} @@ endless-ratio-sum (15i3)
\textstyle\sum_{i > n} C(1 + r)^{-i} = (1 + r)^{-n}\,\dfrac{C}{r} @@ the tail is the whole sum, n years later
C = 40\,000,\ \ r = 0.03,\ \ n = 50 @@ suppose
C/r \approx 1.33 \text{ million};\ \ 50 \text{ years:}\ 1.33 \cdot (1 - 0.228) \approx 1.03 \text{ million} @@ 1.03⁻⁵⁰ ≈ 0.228
\htmlClass{new}{2.2 \text{ million} > 1.03 \text{ million}} \quad \blacksquare @@ at these supposed costs, care saved alone does not match the price

The supposed costs are a choice, and every study of the price makes a different one: dearer care, years of work regained, a lower rate. About 7.7 million people were living with sickle cell disease in 2021, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa and India, where the specialist hospitals the treatment needs are few. At the American price, treating them all would cost about $17 trillion. Who can reach the treatment is set by the price, by the hospital and by the chemotherapy, and only the first is a matter of arithmetic.

inf30.5Twins, 2018

In November 2018 a researcher in Shenzhen announced that twin girls had been born from embryos he had edited, to switch off a gene that some forms of HIV use to enter cells. Their father had HIV. A third child was born in 2019. The edits were made in embryos, so where they took, they were in every cell, eggs and sperm included, and could pass to children.

Each cell holds two copies of the gene, one from each parent, and an editor acting in an embryo acts on each copy on its own.

Two-copy technique: if each of the two copies is changed with chance p, independently, then both are changed with chance p², exactly one with 2p(1 − p), and neither with (1 − p)². The branch-product technique (30l2) along the four paths, two of which give exactly one.

p = 0.7:\ \ p^2 = 0.49,\ \ 2p(1 - p) = 0.42,\ \ (1 - p)^2 = 0.09 @@ suppose; the three add to 1
\text{an } \allowbreak \text{edit } \allowbreak \text{after } \allowbreak \text{the } \allowbreak \text{first } \allowbreak \text{division } \allowbreak \text{makes } \allowbreak \text{a } \allowbreak \text{mix } \allowbreak \text{of } \allowbreak \text{cells} @@ each cell of the embryo is its own tree
s = 0.2,\ \ m = 5:\ \ (1 - s)^5 \approx 0.33 @@ a test of 5 cells misses a share 0.2 of unedited cells (30l2, at least one, turned round)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a } \allowbreak \text{third } \allowbreak \text{of } \allowbreak \text{such } \allowbreak \text{tests } \allowbreak \text{see } \allowbreak \text{no } \allowbreak \text{mix } \allowbreak \text{at } \allowbreak \text{all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the test reads 5 cells; the child is all of them

The reported results matched the arithmetic of doubt. One twin was reported to carry changes in both copies and the other in only one. The changes were not the natural loss of 32 letters known to protect against HIV, but new losses and gains of other lengths, whose effects nobody knew, and tests of a few cells could not rule out a mix. The field condemned the work at once, and for reasons beyond the numbers: there were other ways to keep the children safe from their father’s infection; the consent forms described the work as a trial of an AIDS vaccine; approval papers were reported forged; and the work was kept hidden until the births. In December 2019 a court in Shenzhen gave the researcher three years in prison.

inf30.6Edits That Pass Down

An edit in the body’s other cells ends with the patient. An edit in an embryo, or in eggs or sperm, passes down. A parent with the change on one of two copies hands each child one of the two, as if by a coin (30f2).

Half-pass technique: a change on one of a parent’s two copies reaches each child with chance 1/2, independently; with 2 children for each carrier, the expected number of carriers g generations on is 1, and yet the line of carriers dies out with chance 1.

E(\text{carriers, generation } g) = 2^g \cdot 2^{-g} = 1 @@ 2ᵍ descendants, each a carrier with chance 2⁻ᵍ; averages add (30g1)
G(s) = \big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 s\big)^2 @@ each carrier: 2 children, each a carrier with chance 1/2 (30i1)
G(s) = s \iff 4s = 1 + 2s + s^2 \iff (s - 1)^2 = 0 @@ the only root is 1
\htmlClass{new}{q = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the dying-out technique (30i4): the least root in [0, 1]

The average stays at 1 while the chance that any carrier is left falls toward 0, because a few lines hold many carriers and most hold none. An edit that helps its carriers have children could spread instead. Either way, the people who carry the edit, and its risks, are not born when it is made, and cannot agree to it. That is the centre of the argument over edits that pass down: not whether they can be made, but who may decide for people who do not yet exist.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞η.oq.1Is editing that passes down ever acceptable, and who decides? No international body has the power to allow or forbid it. A European treaty of 1997 bars it in the states that ratified it, many countries forbid it by law, and the summits of 2015, 2018 and 2023 called it unacceptable for now while leaving open whether it could become acceptable. What would count as the broad agreement the 2015 summit asked for, and agreement among whom?
∞η.oq.2How safe is safe enough for a change that cannot be undone? The nothing-seen technique bounds only what the reads failed to see, and the effects of a change on a person may show only after decades. When the treated are few and the feared events are rare, what should be measured before a treatment is approved, and for how long should the treated be followed?
∞η.oq.3Can a treatment that costs millions and months of hospital care reach the places where most people with sickle cell disease live? Trials are testing editing done inside the body, with no cells taken out and no marrow emptied. Whether that can be made safe, cheap and simple enough for a district hospital is not known, and nor is who would pay for it.
∞η.oq.4Is there a line between treating and improving? The 2018 edit aimed to protect healthy children from an infection, not to cure a disease, and the natural protective form it imitated is common in parts of northern Europe and rare elsewhere. If a change that protects is allowed, which changes that protect are not, and who draws the line?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

From 2016 to 2019 the editors that change a letter without cutting both strands were reported from Harvard and the Broad Institute: the first kind in 2016, the second in 2017 and the template-carrying kind in 2019. They answered a worry that had grown since 2013, that a cut through both strands is mended in a hurry and often wrongly, losing or adding letters and sometimes long stretches. The claim was a cleaner edit. In 2019 three studies replied that the first kind also changed letters where no guide had sent it, in mouse embryos, in rice and in RNA, and the editors were reworked to do so less. Supporters counted the reworking as progress; critics counted it as a warning that each new tool arrives cleaner in its first report than in its later ones.

In November 2018 the twins were announced days before an international summit in Hong Kong on editing human DNA. The organisers called the work irresponsible, and the condemnation among scientists was close to universal. Some argued that the field shared the blame: since 2015 it had said such editing should wait for broad agreement in society, without saying how that agreement would be reached or who would stop anyone who did not wait. Calls for a binding pause followed in 2019. A commission of national academies set out in 2020 what a first use would have to show; the World Health Organization set up a register of trials in 2019 and in 2021 proposed how governments might oversee editing. China tightened its regulations and in 2020 made the editing of embryos for pregnancy a crime.

The treatments came in the same years. The first patient with sickle cell disease was treated in a trial in Nashville in 2019, with the cutting treatment that was approved four years later: in the United Kingdom in November 2023, in the United States in December and in the European Union in early 2024. A second sickle cell treatment, approved in the United States on the same day, used no editing at all; it adds a working gene by another method, and was priced at $3.1 million. The prices were defended as less than a lifetime of care and attacked as beyond the reach of nearly everyone who has the disease. In 2024 the American agency that pays for health care for people on low incomes agreed prices tied to results with both makers, for states that joined from 2025, and England’s health service agreed to pay for the cutting treatment for sickle cell in January 2025, at a price it did not publish.

Letters were changed in people from 2022. That year a children’s hospital in London treated a girl of 13 with leukaemia using donated immune cells in which letters had been changed without cutting, so that the cells would attack her cancer and not one another. Trials of letter editors working in the liver, to lower cholesterol for life, began in 2022; one was paused in 2024 after abnormal results in a patient’s blood tests, and its maker was bought in 2025. In 2024 a patient in a trial of a letter editor for sickle cell died, and the company attributed the death to the chemotherapy, not the edit. In 2025 a hospital in Philadelphia reported treating a baby with a rare liver disease using a letter editor made for his own faulty letter in about six months. Supporters called it the start of treatments made for one person; others asked how a treatment for one person can be tested, approved and paid for.

What is argued now. On edits that pass down, the positions run from never, through not until safe and agreed, to a right of parents to spare their children a disease. In 2025 several societies of cell and gene therapy called for a ten-year pause, and at least one new company said it would study the editing of embryos. On treatments, the argument has moved from whether editing works to whether it can be given to those who need it, and whether a price set in one country decides who in the world is treated. Neither argument has a court, and neither has a deadline.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The letter editors are base editors: cytosine base editors make the change G·C to A·T, and adenine base editors A·T to G·C. The template-carrying kind is the prime editor. The blunted cutter is a nickase, Cas9 with one of its two cutting sites disabled, or dead Cas9 with both. The larger letters A and G are purines and the smaller C and T pyrimidines; a change that keeps the shape is a transition, one that swaps it a transversion. Changes beside the target are bystander edits, and changes elsewhere are off-target edits; the share of reads showing a change is its frequency, and the number of reads covering a place is the depth. The nothing-seen technique is the rule of three. The lifetime-care technique gives the present value of an annuity. The first approved treatment is exagamglogene autotemcel, exa-cel, sold as Casgevy; the switch is an enhancer of the gene BCL11A, and the unborn baby’s form is fetal haemoglobin, HbF. The second sickle cell treatment of December 2023 is lovotibeglogene autotemcel, sold as Lyfgenia. The gene of the 2018 edit is CCR5, and the natural protective form is CCR5-Δ32. Edits that pass down are germline or heritable edits; the others are somatic. A cell mix is mosaicism. The half-pass is Mendel’s law of segregation, and the line of carriers with on average one carrier child is a critical Galton–Watson branching process. The European treaty is the Oviedo Convention of 1997, Article 13.

Who said it first

Alexis Komor and David Liu reported the first base editor in 2016, Nicole Gaudelli and Liu the adenine editor in 2017, and Andrew Anzalone and Liu the prime editor in 2019. He Jiankui announced the twins in November 2018 and was jailed in December 2019 with two colleagues; he was released in 2022. Victoria Gray was the first patient with sickle cell disease treated in the exa-cel trial, in 2019. The London treatment of 2022 was led by Waseem Qasim at Great Ormond Street Hospital. The Philadelphia baby of 2025 was KJ Muldoon, treated by a team led by Rebecca Ahrens-Nicklas and Kiran Musunuru. The rule of three was set out for medical studies by James Hanley and Abby Lippman-Hand in 1983.

A proof that is not allowed

“The edit was found in each of the five cells tested, so the embryo was edited in every cell.” Not allowed: a test of 5 cells misses a share of 0.2 of unedited cells with chance about 0.33, by the two-copy footing’s line, and misses a share of 0.05 with chance about 0.77. Five cells that agree bound how large an unedited share is likely to be. They do not show that it is none.
