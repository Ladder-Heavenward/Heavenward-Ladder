Getting as Close as You Like Can Be Proved

“The outputs get as close as you like to L” becomes a game: whatever gap is named, an answer keeps the outputs inside it. Once it is a statement of every and some, it can be proved, refuted and computed with.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 10e from the left and from the right is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 10 promised to finish a sentence rung 9 kept starting: “gets as close as you like”. 10a turned it into a statement of every and some, a challenge ε and an answer δ, with the input itself left out of the neighbourhood. It won the first games for lines, for x² and for 1/x. 10b proved that a function cannot head two ways, so the limit is a name, and that limits keep order.

10c proved that limits add, multiply and divide, so substituting into a polynomial is a technique. 10d proved the squeeze. 10e split the game into a left half and a right half, and showed that a limit exists exactly when the two agree. This page states the claim in full, proves its headline parts again, and cites the rest by name.

The claim, in full

“Gets as close as you like” is a statement of every and some, so the techs you already have can prove it, refute it and compute with it.

Let f be a function whose inputs and outputs are reals, defined near a real w, and let L be a real.

lim_{x→w} f(x) = L means: for every positive ε there is a positive δ such that every input x with 0 < |x − w| < δ has |f(x) − L| < ε.

The value of f at w plays no part.

At most one real L passes, so “the limit” names one thing.

Limits add, multiply, and divide when the bottom's limit is not 0. So a polynomial heads to its value at w.

A function trapped near w between two that head to L heads to L.

The limit exists exactly when the limits from the left and from the right exist and agree.

The Proof

The definition is 10a's line. The first part is 10b's one-limit: challenge both claims with half the distance between them.

L \ne M @@ assume, for contradiction (7f): f heads to L and to M at a
\varepsilon = \tfrac12\,|L - M| \gt 0 @@ |L − M| > 0 (8h); half a positive is positive
\text{call } \delta_1 \text{ for } L,\ \ \delta_2 \text{ for } M @@ both lines at this ε; and a reach r on which f is defined near a
\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, r) \gt 0 @@ the least of several (10c), by lesser-below-both
x = a + \tfrac{\delta}{2}:\ \ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta,\ \ x \in \operatorname{dom} f @@ half of δ is positive and less than δ; δ ≤ r
|f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon \;\land\; |f(x) - M| \lt \varepsilon @@ carry each promise through at x; join
\bot @@ two-targets (10b), with y = f(x): 2ε = |L − M|
\htmlClass{new}{L = M} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; not-not-out (7e)

The second part is 10c's limits-add, the pattern for the rest: give each function half the gap, answer with the least reach, and let the triangle inequality add the halves back.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \gt 0 @@ speak of everything; half a positive is positive
\text{call } \delta_1:\ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta_1 \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ lim f = L, at the gap ε/2
\text{call } \delta_2:\ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta_2 \Rightarrow |g(x) - M| \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ lim g = M, at the gap ε/2
\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, r);\ \ \text{assume } 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta @@ the least of several; r a reach on which f and g are both defined
|(f + g)(x) - (L + M)| = |(f(x) - L) + (g(x) - M)| @@ the line for f + g; negative-of-a-sum and middle-swap (8d)
\le |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M| @@ triangle (8h)
\lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} = \htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ both promises (same-direction-add, 8g)

Products and quotients take more care: limits-multiply adds and takes away f(x)M and tames |f(x)| with bounded-near, and limits-divide keeps the bottom away from 0 with away-from-zero. Stacked, they give substitution (10c): lim_{x→w} p(x) = p(w) for every polynomial p.

The third part is 10d's squeeze. The column never uses a value of f: it knows f only through the trap.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \text{call } \delta_1 \text{ for } g,\ \delta_2 \text{ for } h @@ speak of everything; both limits at the gap ε
\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, r);\ \ \text{assume } 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta @@ the least of several: all three windows at once
L - \varepsilon \lt g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x) \lt L + \varepsilon @@ unpack-the-bars (8h) on each promise; the trap, since δ ≤ r
L - \varepsilon \lt f(x) \lt L + \varepsilon @@ at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ: < then ≤ gives <, twice
\htmlClass{new}{|f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ unpack-the-bars, packed; prove an if; speak of everything

Last, the parts join on the example the rung began with, an input that has no output at all. 10a's agreeing-near lets a limit pass over the missing input, and 10e's two-sides-agree checks each side.

\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 \text{ for every } x \ne 1 @@ difference-of-squares (5i) read in ℝ; cancel, since x − 1 ≠ 0
\lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2 @@ substitution (10c)
\htmlClass{new}{\lim_{x \to 1} \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ agreeing-near (10a): the two agree on every neighbourhood of 1, and the game never looks at 1

The same tools refute. |x|/x is 1 just right of 0 and −1 just left of it. So by two-sides-differ (10e) it has no limit at 0, and its value at 0, which it does not have, never entered.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 10f computing limits: The kit of rewrites: substitute, factor and cancel, the conjugate, the squeeze. It stretches the game to inputs that pass every bound and to sequences, where 1, ½, ⅓, ⋯ finally heads to 0.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10g playing the challenge game: Playing the game on purpose: expose the input's distance, tame the leftover with a first promise, read off δ, and write it forwards. Losing on purpose, with the denial worked out by rung 7's techs, refutes a claimed limit.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10·bio how fast can an enzyme go?: An enzyme's rate, V_max · s/(K_m + s), heads to a ceiling as the substrate grows: a limit far out, never reached, and exactly half way at s = K_m.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10·crypt is a huge number of keys enough?: A key is a bijection, and though there are 26! of them, each letter's tally travels unchanged to its image, so the key is read off the tallies, a law whose shares settle as the text grows, without trying any key.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10·logi how many trucks does a route need?: Counting item-hours two ways gives L = λW exactly for a day that starts and ends empty, and limits carry it to the long run. Past the slowest step's capacity the line passes every bound.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10·econ can interest added more often grow without end?: It shows the year's growth rising and capped, so by the least upper bound it heads to a limit, with a shortfall that shrinks like 1/n.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 10·phys can a runner ever catch a tortoise?: It shows endless stages with totals heading to D/(v − u), exactly where the two positions meet, and that shrinking alone is not enough.its line appears when you finish it.

The sentence is finished: “as close as you like” now means something you can prove or refute. The game is yours to play from here, and it has many rounds left.
