1a · proof

Make your case

When guesses clash, who is right? There is always an argument. We don't fear a guess being wrong, because losing an argument is only the next step toward stronger guesses. Not shouting, but method and technique. Every step offers more tools for your toolbox, and the ladder goes somewhere very high.

This rung is about statements joined. Before any joining word gets its mark, this step sets out the one thing every argument is made of: a line, and what satisfies it. An argument starts from a statement whose members seem to clash. It is written as statements in a row, each with its reason: a given, an axiom by name, or a technique by name. A line with such a reason is earned, and nothing else earns one. Supposing, cases, refuting and the rest are each brought in by the joining word that needs them, on the steps after this one; the proof as a whole is built on 4d.

Line up the guesses. Say one thing in a line, and say why you may. Every line leans only on lines before it. When the clash is settled and the last line is the thing you wanted, the argument is done.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0a (names, statements, gaps and their members, the four shapes of a claim, the rule for bringing in a mark) and 0b (the mark =, the axioms self-sameness and swap, turn-about and chain argued in words). Every page above this one argues in the form built here: 1b drops a supposition by denying what it supposed, and the steps after it bring the other ways of dropping one, each with its joining word.

Marks this rung brings in

Rung 1 brings in the joining words and their marks. Each is said here in plain words first, and then shown, so that you know it on sight. None of them is used before the line that brings it in, in the step named beside it.

  • Write out what a word means: unpack, ﹀. Gather the meaning back under the word: pack, ︿ (this step).
  • Suppose something for a while: the word “suppose”, or “assume”, in front of P, kept until the argument drops it (1b).
  • It is not so that: not, written in front, ¬P (1b).
  • A statement that is never true, the totally false: ⊥ (1b).
  • Not the same thing as: x ≠ b (1b).
  • Read this part first: round brackets that group, as in ¬(x = b). Drawn larger, they are the same brackets, made big so the eye can match each pair (1b).
  • Both are true: and, P ∧ Q (1c).
  • At least one is true, maybe both: or, P ∨ Q (1c).
  • If this, then that: the arrow, P ⇒ Q (1d).
  • Exactly when, a promise each way: the double arrow, P ⇔ Q (1d).
  • Two lines of an argument written side by side: the statements with a comma between them, each with its own reason, in order (1d).
  • For every thing: the turned A, ∀x P(x). For some thing: the turned E, ∃x P(x) (1e).
  • True in this row, false in this row: T and F, under a statement in a table (1f).
  • The same column in every row: A ≡ B (1f).
  • Gives, or becomes, in a working: a plain arrow, → (1f). Beside a technique's name, → and ← say which way it is read (1g).
  • Always so, the denial of the totally false: ⊤ (1g).
  • A new name made from an old one: a stroke written high after the letter, e′ (1g).

1a1Ruling guesses out

Stands on 0a3 (what a statement is) and 0a4 (gaps and their members).

Here is an example. The last slice of pie is gone, and it was Ann, or Ben, or the dog. Each guess is a statement that might be so. Then come statements you can lean on. Ann was at work all evening, so it was not Ann. The dog cannot reach the counter, so it was not the dog. Each statement you can lean on rules a guess out, until what is left is the guess that fits: it was Ben.

The pie began with one statement: it was Ann, or Ben, or the dog. Each of the three is a member of it, and two of them seem to clash with what we can lean on. An argument happens when a statement has a member that seems to clash, and it resolves by proving there is no clash, or by proving which part must go. Here Ann and the dog had to go, and Ben was left. The pie argument used a move this rung builds later: ruling out every option of an “or” but one. That move gets its technique in 1c. The shape is already plain, though: a statement whose members seem to clash, then statements that settle the clash, then what is left.

There is more than one kind of argument. A proof is one kind of argument: it rules guesses out with techniques that never fail, so what is left cannot fail either. An equation is another; the ladder reaches it later. Statistics is a third: it rules out the guesses the data make too unlikely, and says how sure it is. A lab experiment is a fourth: it rules out the guesses the world itself refuses. The ladder can give you proofs, equations and statistics. A lab experiment needs a lab, and the ladder cannot give you one.

1a2Statements in a row

Stands on 1a1 (ruling guesses out).

On the ladder an argument has one form. It is plain statements in a row, one claim to a line, each with its reason. Each line carries a statement, in 0a's sense: something so or not so. With it goes its reason, which says why it may be said. Read the argument from the start and each line is justified by what came before it. The last line, its foot, is what the argument leaves standing.

Here is one, so you can see the shape. Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (by what “widow” means). Two lines, each with its reason. The foot is “Ann was married”, and the argument leaves it standing.

A line, as in a line of a poem, is one statement with its reason. A given line is a line whose reason is “given”.

From here on, to parse an argument is to read it from the start, one line at a time, checking each against its reason and the lines before it. Parsing is the ladder's gold standard. An argument that parses all the way to its foot has done its work. One that fails to parse at some line has met one of two things: a mistake, or an edge, the frontier of what anyone yet knows how to argue. Mistakes and edges are both interesting. A mistake shows where your reasoning slipped. An edge shows where everyone's does.

1a3The kinds of reason

Stands on 1a2 (statements in a row), 0b4 and 0b5 (the axioms).

A reason is always one of three kinds: a given, an axiom by name, or a tech by name. Every reason on every page of the ladder is one of the three. Each is tagged below with a line from this argument: Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog. By the swap axiom, on Rex = the dog on the porch, the dog on the porch is a puppy.

  • given: the line was laid down at the start, as the thing we are supposing for the length of this argument. “Rex is a puppy. That is given.”
  • an axiom, by name: the line is an axiom an earlier page laid down, filled with names. Or it is what is so by the axiom, given lines before it. “By the swap axiom, on what we have.”
  • a tech, by name: the line was got from lines before it by a tech, short for technique. The reason names the tech and the lines it used. “So Rex is a dog”, by what “puppy” means: that tech is called unpack, in 1a7. A tech comes built or proved:
    • built: laid down by us, because what the words mean keeps it working, as unpack and pack are in this step;
    • proved: argued once from what came before, and cited by name from then on, as 0b’s turn-about and chain techniques are, argued in words. Citing its name stands in for telling its argument again.

A given is a statement laid down at the start, that the argument supposes for its length. The axioms are givens of another kind: so in every case, laid down with nothing beneath them (0a1), and standing behind every argument from the rung where you learned them (0b4).

Where do givens come from? From observation, common sense, measurements, names, lists, and axioms. For example: “given, bread is made of wheat”, or the list of kids on the soccer team. Techniques already proved are givens of that kind too. More widely, a given is any statement you have enough confidence in to start an argument from, so that you can be confident in where it ends.

A given is said at the start, before the argument takes its first step. You only say the givens that matter to the argument in front of you; the rest stand behind it unsaid.

Using a tech is a move: the step an argument takes. Nothing but a given, an axiom or a tech earns a line. “Obvious”, “everyone knows” and looking out of the window are close to how givens are got. Said at the start, as givens, they are honest. Said in the middle of an argument, each is a given brought in out of order, and it breaks the parse.

The wording of a reason is free. “Unpack ‘puppy’”, “what puppy means”, “a puppy is a dog”: any of these three, written with the line, is a reason. Each tells a reader exactly which move was made and on what. What a reason may not be is absent, or a wish.

1a4More than one given

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 0b5 (swap equals).

The next argument uses two givens. The arguments so far each started from a single given. An argument may start from as many as it needs. Each is said at the start, with the reason “given”. Together the givens are everything the argument supposes, and their order does not matter. A move may then lean on more than one line before it, and when it does, its reason names every line it used. Unpack works on one line at a time. A swap cited to the swap axiom, from 0b, is the earliest move that needs two: a sameness, and a statement about one of its names. A given that nothing later uses is allowed, and harmless: it is supposed, and simply not needed. What an argument proves is always “from these givens, this foot”. So the givens belong to the result as much as the foot does: change them, and you have a different proof.

0b laid down that a fact carried across a sameness is still so. Here is that carrying as an argument, one move to a line, with the swap axiom cited by name. Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat, and Tibbles is grey.

t = m. That is given: Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat. t is grey. That is given too: a statement about t. Call “… is grey” P, so it is P(t). By the swap axiom, on t = m and P(t), P(m), with m said where t stood: m is grey.

1a5Guesses as givens

Stands on 1a1 (ruling guesses out) and 1a3 (the kinds of reason).

Some givens are guesses, and they have kinds of their own. Each is laid down at the start like any given, but it is laid down to be tried, not because you are confident in it. The pie argument of 1a1 had three guesses, each tried in turn. Here are the kinds:

  • a supposition: a guess kept for a while, tried and not claimed, and dropped before the end (1b3);
  • a case: one guess among a few that the givens leave open, each tried in turn. Ann, Ben and the dog were the cases of the pie;
  • a very large group of cases: a guess about every member of a group at once, too many to try one by one. Example: “every dog at the park last Saturday was brown”;
  • a wild conjecture: a guess with nothing behind it yet, laid down to see where it goes. It is honest as long as the argument says so: what it reaches is true only if the conjecture is.

Cases are how a guess is tried in a series: one, then the next, then the next. A few cases can each be tried. A very large group cannot, and that difference matters as soon as you ask what an argument may conclude from cases. 1a6 asks it.

1a6“True”, on the ladder

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 1a5 (guesses as givens).

In life a statement is true when what it says is so, and you check by looking. An argument cannot look, and does not try. In an argument, “true” means earned. A statement counts as true when it stands in an argument with its reason, and the reason is a given, an axiom or a tech. Counting only earned statements as true is strict on purpose. Every true statement can be traced back, line by line, to givens, axioms and named techs. And you can check each step without taking anyone's word.

An argument in which every line is earned this way is induction over the arrangement: if the givens are true, the foot cannot fail to be true. Everyday reasoning has another kind, induction over cases: from cases to a rule. For example, the sun has come up every morning you have seen, so it will come up tomorrow. Induction over cases can be very strong evidence, and statistics and the lab live on it: they rule guesses out too, though never every one. But in an arrangement, a guess from cases never earns a line, however many cases stand behind it. A thousand grey cats do not earn “every cat is grey”. One cat that is not grey would undo “every cat is grey”, and the argument cannot look to see whether that cat exists.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

Both kinds of induction are anexact, yet rigorous. Anexact, because neither one has to touch the world: an arrangement holds whether or not its things exist, and cases never reach every case. Rigorous, because each keeps exact books. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre: measuring says in numbers how wrong it might be.

The books are a ledger, and the argument form already is one. In the Rex argument of 1a3, every line is paid for by its reason, and nothing is claimed that was not paid for. Scientists keep a ledger too: every bit of stuff that goes into a burning candle comes out as smoke, gas and heat, and nothing vanishes.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every statement in it is true.

A candle burns down, and the wax is gone. Where would you look for it, if nothing vanishes?

1a7Unpack, pack and transform

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 0a6 (four shapes of a claim).

0b's axioms say what is so of sameness, and citing them is a move. The techs that make moves were not found in dusty old books. They were built: people noticed which statements kept doing work for them, and made them shorthand. To prove the point, we build three ourselves, here, out of plain English: unpack, pack and transform. Each gets a name, so that a reason can cite it.

  • Unpack: when a line says something of a thing using a word, you may say what that word means, said of the same thing. Example: “puppy” means a young dog, so from “Rex is a puppy” you may say “Rex is a dog”, and you may say “Rex is young”. The reason names the word unpacked.
  • Pack: unpack run the other way. When lines say of a thing everything a word means, you may say the word, said of that thing. Example: from “Rex is a dog” and “Rex is young” you may say “Rex is a puppy”. The reason names the word packed and the lines it used.
  • Transform: when a line says something of a thing in one of 0a's four shapes, you may say the same claim in another shape. Example: from “Rex is spotted” you may say “Rex has spots”. The claim is the same; only the shape has changed. From “spotted Rex is running” you may say “Rex is spotted”, taking the descriptive apart. The reason names the shapes: “transform, predicate to tentative”.

A transform is any move that changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. Changing the shape is the first kind of transform: it says no more and no less of the thing than the line it came from. Transform rests on 0a's possibility axiom: the shapes are ways of writing one claim, so moving between them loses nothing.

“Is” does two jobs in English. In “Mike is the boy in the red cap” it is sameness, =, and the swap axiom and the chain technique carry it. In “Rex is a puppy” it says what kind of thing Rex is. A line of the “is a kind of” shape is unpacked, never chained, and two unpacks in a row carry Rex down a line of kinds.

The signs for unpack and pack are drawn from what unpack and pack do. ﹀ is a wide cup opening downward: the meaning pours out of the word. ︿ is the same cup turned over: the meaning is gathered back under the word. Each sign is a name: the name of a tech, packed into one scribble. Unpack and pack are techs on a word and its meaning, so a reason may cite them as it cites any tech.

No key for ﹀ and ︿? Write unpack and pack.

Marks are shorthand for words, and every mark on the ladder was brought in by a “from here on” line saying what it means. So when a later page unpacks a mark, that is the unpack tech again, with the “from here on” line as the meaning it unpacks.

The shortest argument that moves is the widow argument of 1a2 again, now with the mark as its reason: a given line and a line got from it.

Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (﹀ “widow”).

And here is a longer one, in which the unpack is made again on its own result. Each line makes a move of its own and names the line it used. The ladder argues this finely so that nothing can hide; you will not have to.

Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog (﹀ “puppy”, on the line before). A dog is an animal, so Rex is an animal (﹀ “dog”, on the line before).

This step is in foreclosure, the state 0a1 named: it uses what it has not yet built, and says so. To unpack “Rex is a puppy” to “Rex is a dog” is to use a meaning of the shape “if anything is a puppy, it is a dog”, and that shape needs the if (1d), the “anything” (1e) and, for “puppy” as a collection, rung 2. The argument itself, lines in order, each let in from lines already there, needs collections, order and counting, built on rungs 2 to 4. Until rung 4, every argument runs on meanings and on a form it has not yet built. Rung 2 pays for the meanings. Then 4d builds the proof: there supposing, cases, refute and nesting are justified, the circle closes, and everything is earned. Everything checks out, even though we are using tools before they are built: we are assembling the plane in midair.

1a8Illegal lines

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 1a7 (unpack and pack).

An argument can look like a proof and not be one. Each failing line fails for a reason you can name. A line may have no reason with it. It may be a question or a command still in its own shape. Each carries a statement, but it must say that statement before it stands on a line. Its reason may be “obvious”, a given brought in out of order (1a3). Or its reason may cite a line that comes after it.

That last one is the subtle one. Reasons point back, always, so that tracing a line back through its reasons ends at givens and axioms and does not go round. An argument in which a line cites a line after it has not proved anything; it has only arranged its wishes in order.

The word “wish” has a meaning here. A wish is a line written with no reason behind it: no given, no axiom, no tech earns it. It is the inverse of a guess: not an intuition that something is so, but a desire that it should be so, and it pretends a line already is so, though it was never earned. A wish stays a wish however true its statement is.

Example. Given “p is a hen”. “Hen” means an adult female chicken, so “p is female” is an unpack. “p lays eggs” is true of most hens, but it is not what the word means, so writing it with “﹀ hen” beside it is a wish; it needs its own given. A wish has two repairs. Earn it from lines before it, or move it to the start as a given. A given is honest, but then the argument proves its foot only if the given is true.

\text{Rex is brown.} @@ no reason at all: a line with nothing to its right has not been earned, however true it happens to be
\text{Is Rex a dog?} @@ a question in its own shape: it keeps “Rex is a dog” open. On a line, keeping a statement open is a supposition (1b): suppose Rex is a dog
\text{Shut the gate.} @@ a command in its own shape: it carries “if the gate is open, close it”, and must say so before it stands on a line
\text{Rex is a good dog.} \;\;\text{(obvious)} @@ “obvious” in the middle is a given out of order, and it breaks the parse. Say “given Rex is a good dog” at the start instead
\begin{aligned}&\text{Rex is an animal.} \;\\\ &\quad ;\text{(from the line below)}\end{aligned} @@ a reason may cite only lines above it; a line that leans on a later line is leaning on nothing yet

One more fails in a quieter way: a letter that drifts. Suppose a second naming were let in, before the first argument ended. Then:

\text{Call the grey cat } c. @@ naming: c = the grey cat, until the argument ends
\text{Call the ginger cat } c. @@ not allowed: c already names the grey cat in this argument, and a letter names one thing until the argument ends
c = \text{grey cat} @@ the first naming
c = \text{ginger cat} @@ the second naming
\text{grey cat} = c @@ the turn-about technique, on “c = grey cat”
\text{grey cat} = \text{ginger cat} @@ the chain technique, on the two lines above: every move legal, and the argument proves two cats are one cat

Each move after the two namings is a fair move. That is what makes the drift dangerous: nothing in the chain looks wrong. Ending the first argument before naming again is the whole repair; 4d21 shows two cats on one letter, an argument each.

Who said it first

Pascal described a perfect method: define every word, and prove every claim. Then he admitted it cannot be done. Each definition uses words, and each proof uses claims, so the work never ends unless you stop at words too plain to define and claims too plain to prove.

Pascal, “On the Geometrical Spirit” (c. 1657); paraphrase

Accepted names

A line reached with nothing given is, in the books, a theorem of logic, or a logical truth; checked by tables (1f), it is a tautology. The box is a subproof, and this way of laying out proofs is natural deduction, in the box style; the supposition is an assumption, and dropping it is discharging it.
