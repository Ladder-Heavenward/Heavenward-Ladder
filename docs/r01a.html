1a · proof

Make your case

When guesses clash, who is right? There is always an argument. We don't fear a guess being wrong, because losing an argument is only the next step toward stronger guesses. Not shouting, but method and technique. Every step offers more tools for your toolbox, and the ladder goes somewhere very high.

This rung is about statements joined. Before any joining word gets its mark, this step sets out what an argument is and where it is worked. An argument starts from a statement whose members seem to clash. It is written as statements in a row, each with its reason: a given, an axiom by name, or a technique by name. It closes with the end mark. Then comes the empty row, with nothing given and nothing supposed. On it you place a guess, kept for a while, and work from it. Then you drop the guess and keep what it showed: what it leads to, or that it cannot stand. Guesses go inside guesses. In the end all that stands on the row with nothing under it is the givens and the foot. The joining words in the steps after this one each bring a way of dropping a guess; what every argument shares is here, once.

Line up the guesses. Say one thing in a sentence, and say why you may. Every sentence leans only on sentences before it. A supposition puts down a sentence that stands on nothing, tried and not claimed, and is dropped with one sentence that says what the trying reached. When the clash is settled and the last sentence is the thing you wanted, end the argument.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0a (names, statements, gaps and their members, the four shapes of a claim, the rule for bringing in a mark) and 0b (the mark =, the axioms self-sameness and swap, turn-about and chain argued in words). Every page above this one argues in the form built here: 1b drops a supposition by denying what it supposed, and the steps after it bring the other ways of dropping one, each with its joining word.

Marks this rung brings in

Rung 1 brings in the joining words and their marks. Each is said here in plain words first, and then shown, so that you know it on sight. None of them is used before the line that brings it in, in the step named beside it.

  • The argument is complete: the end mark, ∎, or the word end (this step).
  • Write out what a word means: unpack, ﹀. Gather the meaning back under the word: pack, ︿ (this step).
  • Suppose something for a while: the word “suppose”, or “assume”, in front of P, kept until the argument drops it (this step).
  • It is not so that: not, written in front, ¬P (1b).
  • A statement that is never true, the totally false: ⊥ (1b).
  • Not the same thing as: x ≠ b (1b).
  • Read this part first: round brackets that group, as in ¬(x = b). Drawn larger, they are the same brackets, made big so the eye can match each pair (1b).
  • Both are true: and, P ∧ Q (1c).
  • At least one is true, maybe both: or, P ∨ Q (1c).
  • If this, then that: the arrow, P ⇒ Q (1d).
  • Exactly when, a promise each way: the double arrow, P ⇔ Q (1d).
  • Two sentences of an argument written side by side: the statements with a comma between them, each with its own reason, in order (1d).
  • For every thing: the turned A, ∀x P(x). For some thing: the turned E, ∃x P(x) (1e).
  • True in this row, false in this row: T and F, under a statement in a table (1f).
  • The same column in every row: A ≡ B (1f).
  • Gives, or becomes, in a working: a plain arrow, → (1f). Beside a technique's name, → and ← say which way it is read (1g).
  • Always so, the denial of the totally false: ⊤ (1g).
  • A new name made from an old one: a stroke written high after the letter, e′ (1g).

1a1Ruling guesses out

Stands on 0a3 (what a statement is) and 0a4 (gaps and their members).

Here is an example. The last slice of pie is gone, and it was Ann, or Ben, or the dog. Each guess is a statement that might be so. Then come statements you can lean on. Ann was at work all evening, so it was not Ann. The dog cannot reach the counter, so it was not the dog. Each statement you can lean on rules a guess out, until what is left is the guess that fits: it was Ben.

The pie began with one statement: it was Ann, or Ben, or the dog. Each of the three is a member of it, and two of them seem to clash with what we can lean on. An argument happens when a statement has a member that seems to clash, and it resolves by proving there is no clash, or by proving which part must go. Here Ann and the dog had to go, and Ben was left. The pie argument used a move this rung builds later: ruling out every option of an “or” but one. That move gets its technique in 1c. The shape is already plain, though: a statement whose members seem to clash, then statements that settle the clash, then what is left.

There is more than one kind of argument. A proof is one kind of argument: it rules guesses out with techniques that never fail, so what is left cannot fail either. An equation is another; the ladder reaches it later. Statistics is a third: it rules out the guesses the data make too unlikely, and says how sure it is. A lab experiment is a fourth: it rules out the guesses the world itself refuses. The ladder can give you proofs, equations and statistics. A lab experiment needs a lab, and the ladder cannot give you one.

1a2Statements in a row

Stands on 1a1 (ruling guesses out).

On the ladder an argument has one form. It is plain sentences in a row, one claim each, each with its reason. Each sentence is a statement, in 0a's sense: something so or not so. With it goes its reason, which says why it may be said. Read the argument from the start and each sentence is justified by what came before it. The last sentence, its foot, is what the argument leaves standing.

Here is one, so you can see the shape. Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (by what “widow” means). Two sentences, each with its reason. The foot is “Ann was married”, and the argument leaves it standing.

The ladder also calls each sentence a line, as in a line of a poem. A given line is a given sentence.

From here on, to parse an argument is to read it from the start, one sentence at a time, checking each against its reason and the sentences before it. Parsing is the ladder's gold standard. An argument that parses all the way to its foot has done its work. One that fails to parse at some sentence has met one of two things: a mistake, or an edge, the frontier of what anyone yet knows how to argue. Mistakes and edges are both interesting. A mistake shows where your reasoning slipped. An edge shows where everyone's does.

1a3The kinds of reason

Stands on 1a2 (statements in a row), 0b4 and 0b5 (the axioms).

A reason is always one of three kinds: a given, an axiom by name, or a tech by name. Every reason on every page of the ladder is one of the three. Each is tagged below with a line from this argument: Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog. By the swap axiom, on Rex = the dog on the porch, the dog on the porch is a puppy.

  • given: the sentence was laid down at the start, as the thing we are supposing for the length of this argument. “Rex is a puppy. That is given.”
  • an axiom, by name: the sentence is an axiom an earlier page laid down, filled with names. Or it is what is so by the axiom, given sentences before it. “By the swap axiom, on what we have.”
  • a tech, by name: the sentence was got from sentences before it by a tech, short for technique. The reason names the tech and the sentences it used. “So Rex is a dog”, by what “puppy” means: that tech is called unpack, in 1a8. A tech comes built or proved:
    • built: laid down by us, because what the words mean keeps it working, as unpack and pack are in this step;
    • proved: argued once from what came before, and cited by name from then on, as 0b’s turn-about and chain techniques are, argued in words. Citing its name stands in for telling its argument again.

A given is a statement laid down at the start, that the argument supposes for its length. The axioms are givens of another kind: statements that are so in every case, laid down with nothing beneath them, and earned by what breaks without them, standing behind every argument from the rung where you learned them. A challenge may put an axiom off limits; then you may not cite that axiom, but it is still so.

Where do givens come from? From observation, common sense, measurements, names, lists, and axioms. For example: “given, bread is made of wheat”, or the list of kids on the soccer team. Techniques already proved are givens of that kind too. More widely, a given is any statement you have enough confidence in to start an argument from, so that you can be confident in where it ends.

“It's obvious” is close to how a given is got. But a given is said at the start, before the argument takes its first step. You only say the givens that matter to the argument in front of you; the rest stand behind it unsaid.

Using a tech is a move: the step an argument takes. Nothing but a given, an axiom or a tech earns a sentence. “Obvious”, “everyone knows” and looking out of the window are close to how givens are got. Said at the start, as givens, they are honest. Said in the middle of an argument, each is a given brought in out of order, and it breaks the parse.

1a4More than one given

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 0b5 (swap equals).

The next argument uses two givens. The arguments so far each started from a single given. An argument may start from as many as it needs. Each is said at the start, with the reason “given”. Together the givens are everything the argument supposes, and their order does not matter. A move may then lean on more than one sentence before it, and when it does, its reason names every sentence it used. Unpack works on one sentence at a time. A swap cited to the swap axiom, from 0b, is the earliest move that needs two: a sameness, and a statement about one of its names. A given that nothing later uses is allowed, and harmless: it is supposed, and simply not needed. What an argument proves is always “from these givens, this foot”. So the givens belong to the result as much as the foot does: change them, and you have a different proof.

0b laid down that a fact carried across a sameness is still so. Here is that carrying as an argument, one move to a sentence, with the swap axiom cited by name. Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat, and Tibbles is grey.

t = m. That is given: Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat. t is grey. That is given too: a statement about t. Call “… is grey” P, so it is P(t). By the swap axiom, on t = m and P(t), P(m), with m said where t stood: m is grey. ∎

1a5Guesses as givens

Stands on 1a1 (ruling guesses out) and 1a3 (the kinds of reason).

Some givens are guesses, and they have kinds of their own. Each is laid down at the start like any given, but it is laid down to be tried, not because you are confident in it. The pie argument of 1a1 had three guesses, each tried in turn. Here are the kinds:

  • a supposition: a guess kept for a while, tried and not claimed, and dropped before the end (1a9);
  • a case: one guess among a few that the givens leave open, each tried in turn. Ann, Ben and the dog were the cases of the pie;
  • a very large group of cases: a guess about every member of a group at once, too many to try one by one. Example: “every dog at the park last Saturday was brown”;
  • a wild conjecture: a guess with nothing behind it yet, laid down to see where it goes. It is honest as long as the argument says so: what it reaches is true only if the conjecture is.

Cases are how a guess is tried in a series: one, then the next, then the next. A few cases can each be tried. A very large group cannot, and that difference matters as soon as you ask what an argument may conclude from cases. 1a6 asks it.

1a6“True”, on the ladder

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 1a5 (guesses as givens).

In life a statement is true when what it says is so, and you check by looking. An argument cannot look, and does not try. In an argument, “true” means earned. A statement counts as true when it stands in an argument with its reason, and the reason is a given, an axiom or a tech. Counting only earned statements as true is strict on purpose. Every true statement can be traced back, sentence by sentence, to givens, axioms and named techs. And you can check each step without taking anyone's word.

An argument in which every sentence is earned this way is induction over the arrangement: if the givens are true, the foot cannot fail to be true. Everyday reasoning has another kind, induction over cases: from cases to a rule. For example, the sun has come up every morning you have seen, so it will come up tomorrow. Induction over cases can be very strong evidence, and statistics and the lab live on it: they rule guesses out too, though never every one. But in an arrangement, a guess from cases never earns a line, however many cases stand behind it. A thousand grey cats do not earn “every cat is grey”. One cat that is not grey would undo “every cat is grey”, and the argument cannot look to see whether that cat exists.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

Both kinds of induction are anexact, yet rigorous. Anexact, because neither one has to touch the world: an arrangement holds whether or not its things exist, and cases never reach every case. Rigorous, because each keeps exact books. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre: measuring says in numbers how wrong it might be.

The books are a ledger, and the argument form already is one. In the Rex argument of 1a3, every sentence is paid for by its reason, and nothing is claimed that was not paid for. Scientists keep a ledger too: every bit of stuff that goes into a burning candle comes out as smoke, gas and heat, and nothing vanishes.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every sentence in it is true.

A candle burns down, and the wax is gone. Where would you look for it, if nothing vanishes?

1a7The end mark

Stands on 1a2 (statements in a row) and 0a10 (how a mark is brought in).

An argument needs a way to say it is finished. Here is its mark, brought in by 0a's rule:

The end mark ∎ is a statement, named whole in 0a's way: “the argument is complete”.

Typing an answer, with no key for the end mark? Write the word end in its place. It means the same thing, and the ladder reads it the same way.

The shortest argument of all is one given sentence and the end mark: “Ann is a widow. That is given. ∎” That one-sentence argument proves its given, as given. The foot stands exactly where the given stands, and is exactly as so or not so as the given was when it entered. It looks like nothing, and it is the identity of a statement, as the self-sameness axiom is the identity of a name. (4d earns this claim.)

0a promised that a letter names one thing for the length of an argument, and is free when the argument is over, and it left the ending to this step. The end mark is where an argument is over, and 0a's flag on freeing a letter is closed. A letter named inside an argument keeps its meaning down to the end mark and no further. So the same letter can name different things in different arguments, as long as each argument ends before the next begins. Here are two cats, with a single letter, in an argument each:

Call the grey cat on the mat c. So c is the grey cat, until the end mark. c is asleep; that is given. ∎ The end mark frees c.

Call the ginger cat on the wall c. Now c is the ginger cat, in this argument only. c is hungry; that is given. ∎ And c is free again.

Inside either argument, c names one cat throughout, so every sentence can be checked. What you may not do is let a sentence in the second argument lean on a sentence in the first: across the end mark, c is a different cat.

1a8Unpack, pack and transform

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason), 1a7 (the end mark) and 0a6 (four shapes of a claim).

0b's axioms say what is so of sameness, and citing them is a move. The techs that make moves were not found in dusty old books. They were built: people noticed which statements kept doing work for them, and made them shorthand. To prove the point, we build three ourselves, here, out of plain English: unpack, pack and transform. Each gets a name, so that a reason can cite it.

  • Unpack: when a sentence says something of a thing using a word, you may say what that word means, said of the same thing. Example: “puppy” means a young dog, so from “Rex is a puppy” you may say “Rex is a dog”, and you may say “Rex is young”. The reason names the word unpacked.
  • Pack: unpack run the other way. When sentences say of a thing everything a word means, you may say the word, said of that thing. Example: from “Rex is a dog” and “Rex is young” you may say “Rex is a puppy”. The reason names the word packed and the sentences it used.
  • Transform: when a sentence says something of a thing in one of 0a's four shapes, you may say the same claim in another shape. Example: from “Rex is spotted” you may say “Rex has spots”. The claim is the same; only the shape has changed. From “spotted Rex is running” you may say “Rex is spotted”, taking the descriptive apart. The reason names the shapes: “transform, predicate to tentative”.

A transform is any move that changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. Changing the shape is the first kind of transform: it says no more and no less of the thing than the sentence it came from. Transform rests on 0a's possibility axiom: the shapes are ways of writing one claim, so moving between them loses nothing.

“Is” does two jobs in English. In “Mike is the boy in the red cap” it is sameness, =, and the swap axiom and the chain technique carry it. In “Rex is a puppy” it says what kind of thing Rex is. A sentence of the “is a kind of” shape is unpacked, never chained, and two unpacks in a row carry Rex down a line of kinds.

The signs for unpack and pack are drawn from what unpack and pack do. ﹀ is a wide cup opening downward: the meaning pours out of the word. ︿ is the same cup turned over: the meaning is gathered back under the word. Each sign is a name: the name of a tech, packed into one scribble. Unpack and pack are techs on a word and its meaning, so a reason may cite them as it cites any tech.

Typing an answer, with no key for ﹀ and ︿? Write the words unpack and pack in their place. The ladder reads them the same way.

Marks are shorthand for words, and every mark on the ladder was brought in by a “from here on” line saying what it means. So when a later page unpacks a mark, that is the unpack tech again, with the “from here on” line as the meaning it unpacks.

The shortest argument that moves has a given sentence, a sentence got from it, and the end mark. Here it is, each sentence with its reason:

Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (﹀ “widow”). ∎

And here is a longer one, in which the unpack is made again on its own result. Each sentence makes a move of its own and names the sentence it used. The ladder argues this finely so that nothing can hide; you will not have to.

Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog (﹀ “puppy”, on the sentence before). A dog is an animal, so Rex is an animal (﹀ “dog”, on the sentence before). ∎

1a9Why a sentence can be supposed

Stands on 1a2 (statements in a row) and 1a3 (the kinds of reason).

The arguments so far had two kinds of sentence: givens, said at the start, and sentences got from the ones before them. Everything in such an argument leans, in the end, on the givens. Givens and the sentences got from them are enough to carry facts about. They are not enough to reason the way people do when they say “suppose…”. People try a thing out, see where it goes, and come back with a report, without ever claiming the thing was so.

Try that kind of reasoning without supposing, and see what breaks. Start an argument with nothing given. Say “Rex is a puppy” first, and unpack it: so Rex is a dog. The second sentence is earned from the first. The first stands on nothing: no axiom, no given and no tech let it in. 1a3's rule is that every sentence stands on something. So the argument breaks at its first sentence, and every sentence after it breaks too.

Yet something real happened. That the second sentence was reached from the first is so, whether or not Rex is a puppy. The trying taught something, and a plain argument has no way to keep what it taught. A plain argument needs a way to keep a sentence that stands on nothing, and to mark every sentence that leans on it.

A supposition is a guess, kept for a while on nothing, so that every sentence leaning on it is known to lean. It is made with the word “suppose”, and its reason is supposed:

1a10What a supposition keeps open

Stands on 1a9 (why a sentence can be supposed) and 0a3 (what a statement is).

The sentence “suppose P” is a statement: P, tried and not claimed. It is a question said as a statement. For example, “suppose it is raining” keeps open the same thing as “is it raining?”, and the argument goes looking for what follows.

You may suppose anything you can say, about any name you like. Supposing something does not make it exist. For example, supposing “a is a unicorn” invents no unicorn. The letter a is only a name, as in 0a, and the supposition asks what would follow if a were one.

While a supposition stands, P may be leaned on as if it had been given. Every sentence after it carries the supposition with it. Each sentence after it is earned, but only as far as P. So a sentence under a supposition is never claimed outright. It is claimed only as reached from P.

1a11A supposition's report

Stands on 1a10 (what a supposition keeps open).

A supposition cannot stand for ever. An argument ends with nothing left only supposed, so the supposition is dropped. When it is, every sentence that leaned on it loses what it leaned on, and none of them can be said on its own.

One thing survives the dropping: the fact that the trying reached those sentences from P. That fact never leaned on P being so. Say that fact, and nothing is claimed that was not reached. A supposition's report says what the trying reached, said as reached from the supposition, so it leans on nothing that was only tried.

So dropping a supposition says exactly one sentence: its report. The plainest report is the English “if”. The report does not say Rex is a puppy, and it does not say Rex is a dog. It says what the trying showed:

Suppose Rex is a puppy. That is supposed: tried, not claimed. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog (﹀ “puppy”). That is earned, but only as far as the supposition. Now drop the supposition, and keep what the trying showed: if Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog. That report leans on nothing tried.

Why “if Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog”, and no other statement? The report may lean on whatever the sentences after the supposition leaned on, except the supposition. Givens, axioms and techniques stay in reach, because they never needed it. The supposition is not lost either: it goes into the report itself, as the if's first half. Arguing by suppositions like this is called natural deduction. The supposition is called an assumption, and dropping it is said to discharge it. Each joining word on this rung brings its own way of dropping a supposition, and its own mark; the steps after this one name them one by one. What this step sets down is what every supposition shares.

1a12What a sentence under a supposition may cite

Stands on 1a11 (a supposition's report).

A sentence made under a supposition may cite any sentence before it that is still in reach. Sentences in reach are the givens, the axioms that stand behind every argument, and any technique already proved. Sentences in reach also include any sentence before it made under a supposition still open, its own or one it was made inside. A sentence may never cite a sentence made under a supposition that has been dropped. Sentences made under a dropped supposition leaned on something that has been put away, and dropping it took them with it. What survives a dropping is only the report.

For example: suppose Rex is a puppy; then Rex is a dog; drop the supposition, and report “if Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog”. After the drop, “Rex is a dog” is out of reach. It was said where Rex was supposed to be a puppy. Outside, nobody has said so. The report is in reach, because it leans on nothing that was only tried.

1a13Suppositions inside suppositions

Stands on 1a12 (what a sentence under a supposition may cite).

A supposition may be made while another still stands. The inner one leans on both: its sentences may cite anything the outer one's could, and the outer one's sentences before it too. Suppositions are dropped latest first, each in its own way. Each report is said under the supposition around it, and that supposition carries on, with the report now among its sentences. However many are made, the argument ends with all of them dropped. A foot said while a supposition is still open would still lean on something only tried.

Make suppositions inside suppositions, down and down, and look at the argument from outside. Outside every supposition stand only a few claims: the givens, if any, and the foot. Everything else is machinery, moved by the givens and the foot. Givens and foot outside, machinery inside: that is the shape of nearly every argument on the ladder from here on.

1a14Cases: one statement from every way

Stands on 1a11 (a supposition's report) and 1a12 (what a sentence under a supposition may cite).

Sometimes the givens say that things are one of a few ways, without saying which. A shelter card reads “Rex is a puppy or Rex is a kitten”, and the ink has run on the rest. Neither way can be said outright. Each can be supposed, in turn. Suppose the first: a puppy is a young dog, so Rex is young. Suppose the second: a kitten is a young cat, so Rex is young. Both suppositions reached the same statement: Rex is young.

When every way things could be, each supposed in turn, reaches the same statement, that statement leans on none of them and stands on its own. Whichever way is true, a supposition has shown the statement follows from it. And one of the ways is true, because a given says so. Dropping every case to keep the statement they all reached is called reasoning by cases, and each supposition is a case. Reasoning by cases is guessing done in full: every guess the givens leave open is tried, and what all of them reach is kept, whichever guess is right.

Rex is a puppy or Rex is a kitten. That is given: the card says one of the two, not which. First case: suppose Rex is a puppy. Then Rex is young (﹀ “puppy”). Drop that supposition. Second case: suppose Rex is a kitten. Then Rex is young (﹀ “kitten”). Drop that one too. Every way the card allows reached the same statement, so, by cases, Rex is young. ∎

Each case is a supposition, under the rule for what a sentence under a supposition may cite. Nothing said under one case may be cited in another case, or outside. The common statement is the exception, and it stands on its own once the last case is dropped. The list of ways has to be complete. Leave a way out of the list, and the statement might lean on the one you left out. A card reading “puppy, kitten or parrot” leaves Rex young only if the parrot case reaches it too. It does not: nothing on the card says the parrot is young, so with three ways on the card the argument stops. 1c gives the list its mark, “or”, and names this way of dropping as the way an or is used.

1a15What supposing holds in trust, for now

Stands on 1a14 (cases) and 1a8 (unpack, pack and transform).

This step is in foreclosure, the state 0b named: it uses what it has not yet built, and says so. Its most basic tech, the unpack, leans on things built later. To unpack “Rex is a puppy” to “Rex is a dog” is to use a meaning of the shape “if anything is a puppy, it is a dog”. A meaning of that shape needs the if (1d) and the “anything”, the turn A (1e).

Many meanings need a “some” as well, and “some” is the turn E (1e). Example: “sister” unpacks to “shares a parent with someone else”. The “someone” in that meaning is a “some”.

And “puppy” names a collection, the things that are puppies. Being a puppy is being a member of it. Picking the collection out of all things by a condition is the sift. Membership and the sift both arrive on rung 2.

The unpack is not the only thing held in trust. An argument is itself a collection of sentences, in order, each let in by a tech from sentences already there. Collections, order, counting, and building one step on the last are all built on rungs 2 to 4. So the argument itself, the thing every step writes, is held in trust too.

Until rung 4, every argument runs on meanings and on a form it has not yet built. So every proof on rungs 1 to 4 is partly in foreclosure. That foreclosure completes when 4d builds the proof itself.

Nesting is held in trust too. A supposition is a statement whose truth or falseness is kept open while a smaller argument is put together under it. Its report is the end of that smaller argument, said from outside. The argument around it carries on as if the report were one more of its own sentences. So suppositions inside suppositions are arguments inside arguments, each ending where its supposition is dropped. Nesting is used from here on. Why it always works, that an argument made of arguments is still one argument, waits for rung 4 as well.

So supposing is partly built, so that we can make as much progress as we can. Rung 2 pays for the meanings. Then 4d, once collections, order and counting stand, builds the proof: there supposing, cases, refute and nesting are justified, the circle closes, and everything is made clear and earned. Everything checks out, even though we are using tools before they are built: we are assembling the plane in midair.

1a16When a supposition reaches something totally false

Stands on 1a13 (suppositions inside suppositions).

Sometimes, under a supposition, the sentences in reach include a statement and its denial together. A statement and its denial together are totally false. For example: “Ben is married” and “Ben is not married”, both in reach at once. Reaching both is not a mistake in the argument. It is the argument finding a guess it can say no to.

Who is to blame? The givens are fixed, and the sentences earned along the way were got by sound moves, so neither of them can be at fault. The one thing that was only tried is the one to blame. So when something totally false appears, the blame falls on the latest supposition still open, and it may be dropped by saying it is not so. Dropping a supposition by saying it is not so is the plainest way an argument rules a guess out: the guess led somewhere nothing can stand. Dropping a supposition by denying it is called refute; 1b gives “not” and the totally false their marks and sets refute down as a tech.

The rule of the latest supposition matters when suppositions are nested. Suppose Ben is a bachelor; inside that, suppose Ben is married. A bachelor is an unmarried man, so the two clash. The blame falls on the inner one, “Ben is married”, and it is denied and dropped. The bachelor supposition carries on, with that denial among its sentences. If something totally false is reached again later, the blame falls on the bachelor supposition, now the latest still open.

1a17What an empty row can reach

Stands on 1a11 (a supposition's report) and 1a12 (what a sentence under a supposition may cite).

An argument may start on an empty row, with no givens at all. Its first sentence is a supposition, and nothing stands on its own until that supposition is dropped. Nothing is given, but the argument is not working from nothing. The axioms stand behind it, as 1a3 said, and every move it makes uses a tech the ladder allows.

Seen as a statement, an empty row is a statement with a gap where even the part that makes the claim is a gap: all gaps, nothing left in. It is a little silly to point out. But every argument starts from it, and there is nothing in it to doubt.

What such an argument reaches, once every supposition is dropped, leans on nothing that was only given. The statement it reaches is true however things are, whatever is so of Rex or Ann or the school bus. A statement reached with nothing given is always so. Because an argument reached it with nothing given, you may say it in any argument at all, with nothing before it. Saying the statement with nothing before it uses a tech that needs nothing. The ladder calls that tech a technique from nothing.

For example, “if Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog” is always so. An argument reaches it with nothing given, so a technique from nothing says it anywhere. Its denial is then never so. 1f's tables check every case by hand, and a statement said by a technique from nothing passes every row of the tables. An argument with nothing given is how the ladder earns an always-so statement without checking a single row.

What an empty row reaches may be cited under any supposition at all, because it leaned on nothing that dropping a supposition could take away.

1a18Finding an argument from its foot

Stands on 1a8 (unpack and pack).

Parse an argument from the start and each sentence is justified by what came before it. Read it from the foot and it answers a different question: what would I need to have, to earn this? Reading from the foot is how arguments are found. Wanting “Rex is an animal”, you ask what word unpacks to “animal”, and “dog” does. Wanting “Rex is a dog”, you ask what unpacks to “dog”, and “puppy” does, which is what you were given. Finding runs backward; writing runs forward. Finding backward and writing forward are both worth practising, and the questions ask for both.

The wording of a reason is free. “Unpack ‘puppy’”, “what puppy means”, “a puppy is a dog”: any of these three, written with the sentence, is a reason. Each tells a reader exactly which move was made and on what. What a reason may not be is absent, or a wish.

1a19Reading the foot

Stands on 1a17 (what an empty row can reach) and 1a18 (finding from the foot).

Handed a report, rebuild the supposition that made it. “If Ann is a sister, Ann has a sibling” is an if, and an if is said by dropping a supposition. So the supposition was the if's first half, and the last sentence under it was the if's second half. The foot gave both ends at once; only the middle needs thought, and it is found backward, as 1a18 taught.

That is the plan. Now the work. Found first: the report, which was the foot you were handed. Found second, both at once: what was supposed, “Ann is a sister”, the if's first half; and the last sentence under it, “Ann has a sibling”, the if's second half. Found last: what must come just before that last sentence. Someone who shares a parent with someone else has a sibling, so “Ann shares a parent with someone else” lets it in by pack. And “shares a parent with someone else” is what “sister” unpacks to.

Said forward, it runs like this. Suppose Ann is a sister. Then Ann shares a parent with someone else (﹀ “sister”). So Ann has a sibling (︿ “sibling”). Drop the supposition: if Ann is a sister, Ann has a sibling.

The Ann argument shows the rule. The foot tells you what to suppose. Its outermost joining word names the way of dropping that must say it. A denial is said by refute, an “if” by the dropping that reports an if, and a statement reached from a list of ways by cases. The rung's other joining words each add their own. So you know the last sentence before the foot, and you know what to suppose first. Then work backward under the supposition, as 1a18 taught, asking at each sentence what would have to come before it. When the foot is built from more joining words, the same reading makes a supposition inside a supposition. Found backward, said forward.

Nothing was given in the Ann argument, so it reaches its foot from nothing. Its report is always so, and a technique from nothing may say it anywhere. Read it from the foot and the supposition writes itself, first sentence and last sentence first. Reading the supposition off the foot is the habit every step of this rung trains.

1a20Illegal sentences

Stands on 1a3 (the kinds of reason) and 1a8 (unpack and pack).

An argument can look like a proof and not be one. Each failing sentence fails for a reason you can name. A sentence may have no reason with it. It may be a question or a command still in its own shape. Each carries a statement, but it must say that statement before it stands on a line. Its reason may be “obvious”: a given brought in out of order, after the argument has started. Or its reason may cite a sentence that comes after it.

That last one is the subtle one. Reasons point back, always, so that tracing a sentence back through its reasons ends at givens and axioms and does not go round. An argument in which a sentence cites a sentence after it has not proved anything; it has only arranged its wishes in order.

The word “wish” has a meaning here. A wish is a sentence written with no reason behind it: no given, no axiom, no tech earns it. It is the inverse of a guess: not an intuition that something is so, but a desire that it should be so, and it pretends a line already is so, though it was never earned. A wish stays a wish however true its sentence is.

Example. Given “p is a hen”. “Hen” means an adult female chicken, so “p is female” is an unpack. “p lays eggs” is true of most hens, but it is not what the word means, so writing it with “﹀ hen” beside it is a wish; it needs its own given. A wish has two repairs. Earn it from sentences before it, or move it to the start as a given. A given is honest, but then the argument proves its foot only if the given is true.

\text{Rex is brown.} @@ no reason at all: a line with nothing to its right has not been earned, however true it happens to be
\text{Is Rex a dog?} @@ a question in its own shape: it keeps “Rex is a dog” open. On a line, keeping a statement open is a supposition (1a): suppose Rex is a dog
\text{Shut the gate.} @@ a command in its own shape: it carries “if the gate is open, close it”, and must say so before it stands on a line
\text{Rex is a good dog.} \;\;\text{(obvious)} @@ “obvious” in the middle is a given out of order, and it breaks the parse. Say “given Rex is a good dog” at the start instead
\begin{aligned}&\text{Rex is an animal.} \;\\\ &\quad ;\text{(from the line below)}\end{aligned} @@ a reason may cite only lines above it; a line that leans on a later line is leaning on nothing yet

One more fails in a quieter way: a letter that drifts. Suppose a second naming were let in, before the first argument ended. Then:

\text{Call the grey cat } c. @@ naming: c = the grey cat, until the end mark
\text{Call the ginger cat } c. @@ not allowed: c already names the grey cat in this argument, and a letter names one thing until the end mark
c = \text{grey cat} @@ the first naming
c = \text{ginger cat} @@ the second naming
\text{grey cat} = c @@ the turn-about technique, on “c = grey cat”
\text{grey cat} = \text{ginger cat} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chain technique, on the two lines above: every move legal, and the argument proves two cats are one cat

Each move after the two namings is a fair move. That is what makes the drift dangerous: nothing in the chain looks wrong. Ending the first argument before naming again, as the two cats of 1a7 did, is the whole repair.

1a21Illegal moves

Stands on 1a12, 1a13, 1a16 and 1a17.

Five moves look like uses of this step and are not. One cites a sentence made under a supposition that has been dropped. One drops the outer supposition before the inner one. One leaves the foot under a supposition still open. One lays the blame for something totally false on a sentence that was earned. And one calls a statement always so with no argument behind it.

\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose Rex is a puppy; Rex is a dog; }\\ &\quad \text{drop it.}\\ &\text{Rex is a dog (cited from under it).}\end{aligned} @@ a sentence said under a dropped supposition is cited after the drop. Dropping took it away: what survives is the report, “if Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog”
\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose } P\text{; suppose } Q\text{; drop } P\text{;}\\ &\text{then drop } Q\text{.}\end{aligned} @@ the outer supposition dropped first. Suppositions are dropped latest first; the inner one must report before the outer one can
\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose Rex is a puppy; } \dots\\ &\text{Rex is a dog} \quad \blacksquare \;(\text{still supposed})\end{aligned} @@ a foot under an open supposition. The argument ends with every supposition dropped; a foot said while one is open still leans on it
\begin{aligned}&\text{the totally false reached;}\\ &\text{so the sentence before it is not so.}\end{aligned} @@ the blame laid on an earned sentence. The totally false blames the latest supposition still open, never a sentence that was earned
\begin{aligned}&\text{“this is always so”,}\\ &\quad\text{with no argument behind it}\end{aligned} @@ a wish. A statement is always so only when an argument with nothing given reaches it

The first illegal move is the one that catches people, because the sentence cited was really written, a moment ago, in plain sight. It was written under the supposition, where Rex was supposed to be a puppy. Outside, nobody has said so.

Who said it first

Pascal described a perfect method: define every word, and prove every claim. Then he admitted it cannot be done. Each definition uses words, and each proof uses claims, so the work never ends unless you stop at words too plain to define and claims too plain to prove.

Pascal, “On the Geometrical Spirit” (c. 1657); paraphrase

Accepted names

A line reached with nothing given is, in the books, a theorem of logic, or a logical truth; checked by tables (1f), it is a tautology. The box is a subproof, and this way of laying out proofs is natural deduction, in the box style.
