1a · proof

Line It All Up

When guesses clash, who is right? There is always an argument. We don't fear a guess being wrong, because losing an argument is only the next step toward stronger guesses. Not shouting, but method and technique. Every step offers more tools for your toolbox, and the ladder goes somewhere very high.

This rung is about statements joined. Before any joining word gets its mark, this step sets out the one thing every argument is made of: a line, and what satisfies it. An argument starts from a statement whose members seem to clash. Most of Induction over a series, math, asks you to work a single line, just like this.

Line up the guesses. Say as much as you may. Every piece might affect another. When the clash is settled and the line is true, you have finished the argument.

Marks this rung brings in

Rung 1 brings in the joining words and their marks. Each is said here in plain words first, and then shown, so that you know it on sight. None of them is used before the line that brings it in, in the step named beside it.

  • Write out what a word means: unpack, ﹀. Gather the meaning back under the word: pack, ︿ (this step).
  • Suppose something for a while: the word “suppose”, or “assume”, in front of P, kept until the argument drops it (1b).
  • It is not so that: not, written in front, ¬P (1b).
  • A statement that is never true, the totally false: ⊥ (1b).
  • Not the same thing as: x ≠ b (1b).
  • Read this part first: round brackets that group, as in ¬(x = b). Drawn larger, they are the same brackets, made big so the eye can match each pair (1b).
  • Both are true: and, P ∧ Q (1c).
  • At least one is true, maybe both: or, P ∨ Q (1c).
  • If this, then that: the arrow, P ⇒ Q (1d).
  • Exactly when, a promise each way: the double arrow, P ⇔ Q (1d).
  • Two lines of an argument written side by side: the statements with a comma between them, each with its own reason, in order (1d).
  • True in this row, false in this row: T and F, under a statement in a table (1e).
  • The same column in every row: A ≡ B (1e).
  • Gives, or becomes, in a working: a plain arrow, → (1e). Beside a technique's name, → and ← say which way it is read (1f).
  • Always so, the denial of the totally false: ⊤ (1f).
  • A new name made from an old one: a stroke written high after the letter, e′ (1f).

1a1One Long Line

Here is a line. “The last piece of pie is gone, and if it was eaten then Ann or Ben or the dog ate it, and Ann was at work, and the dog cannot reach the counter, and Ben has no alibi.” All of that is one line, and all of it is true.

A line is one statement, however long, built from smaller statements joined in order. Its pieces come one after another, so a line is a series. We are not checking whether the world agrees with it. We are only keeping the line true while we work on its pieces: changing it so that it stays a true statement, and seeing what else that lets us say. Working a line this way is induction over the series, and most of mathematics is this work.

Everyone who reads the pie line thinks: Ben ate it. Nothing on this rung can yet sift Ben out of the line and set him down on his own; that is earned at 4a. Until then, “Ben ate the pie” is natural induction: a guess the line makes likely, held until it is earned. Once 4a earns it, we can hold the pie eater to it.

The ladder finds out what is true in two ways: equations and proofs. An equation works on a line. A proof sets lines in order and earns each one, and every equation leans on a proof that its moves work. Experiments and gathered data are how our guesswork comes back around: they send a guess out to the world and bring back what the world refuses. The ladder can give you equations, proofs, and statistics to weigh gathered data. An experiment needs a lab, and the ladder cannot give you one.

1a2Reading a Line

From here on, to parse a line is to read it from the start, piece by piece, checking how each piece joins the next. Reading a line for its series is the first kind of parsing; at 4a you will also read rows of lines for their arrangement. Parsing is the ladder's gold standard. A line that parses to its end has done its work. One that fails to parse at some piece has met one of two things: a mistake, or an edge, the frontier of what anyone yet knows how to argue. Mistakes and edges are both interesting. A mistake shows where your reasoning slipped. An edge shows where everyone's does.

1a3Operators Join Statements

The joining words of a line are operators. An operator is a canned statement with gaps, and the gaps take statements: “… and …”, “… or …”, “if …, then …”. 0b's = was the first operator you met, “… = …”, with a name in each gap. Fill an operator's gaps and you have a new statement, true or false as a whole.

Rung 1 builds the operators that bear directly on truth: not, and, or, if, and exactly when. Each is said in plain words first and then given its mark, listed at the top of this page, and each step says when the whole statement its operator makes is true.

1a4What the Operator Tells You

A line is statements joined by operators, and the whole of it is true or false. The form rung 1 teaches you to write is: a statement, an operator, a statement, an operator, and so on, and the whole = true. With letters for the statements, “B and R or c, = true”.

The operator also tells you what kind of work you are doing, and which techniques to reach for. An operator like equals says that you are working on a line, and its two sides name one thing, so either may stand in for the other. “Like equals”, because equal is a type of operator that tells you a condition and later rungs bring new operators that do this job in their own ways (5a sets them side by side).

This is the most important fact on this page: A line's argument is true or false by conforming to its operator's condition. Meeting equal's condition makes an equation.

0b laid down that a fact carried across a sameness is still so. Here is that carrying, worked inside one line. Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat, and Tibbles is grey.

Write it t = m, and t is grey. Call “… is grey” P, so the line is: t = m, and P(t). The = tells you to reach for the swap axiom: say m where t stood, and the line still holds. So P(m): m is grey. The line changed, stayed true, and something new falls out of it.

1a5Givens Fill the Gaps

A line starts from givens: the names and statements that fill the gaps between its operators. In the pie line the givens are “the last piece is gone”, “Ann was at work”, “the dog cannot reach the counter” and the rest; the operators are the “and”, the “if …, then …” and the “or” that join them.

Where do givens come from? From observation, common sense, measurements, names, lists and axioms. For example: “bread is made of wheat”, or the list of kids on the soccer team. More widely, a given is any statement you have enough confidence in to start from, so that you can be confident in where you end.

When you only work a line, you need not ask where its givens came from. A proof, at 4a, does ask: there a line may also be made from the results of other lines and from suppositions.

Some givens are guesses, laid down to be tried rather than because you are sure of them. Ann, Ben and the dog were three cases: one guess among a few, each tried in turn. A guess about every member of a large group at once, such as “every dog at the park last Saturday was brown”, is too many cases to try one by one. A wild conjecture is a guess with nothing behind it yet, honest as long as you say so: what it reaches is true only if it is.

1a6“True”, on the Ladder

In life a statement is true when what it says is so, and you check by looking. A line on the ladder counts as true when it does not contradict itself. Then, if you change it in a way that keeps it true, other true things fall out of it. The ladder makes those changes only with techniques, and every technique is earned: built from what words mean, as in 1a7, or proved once and cited by name from then on. So everything you reach can be traced back, change by change, to the givens, without taking anyone's word.

Lies and untrue things take the form of statements too. “Tibbles is a dog” is a statement, and it is not so. There is also a famous curiosity, the sentence “this sentence is false”. If it is so, then it is not, and if it is not, then it is. Whatever that sentence is, it cannot be judged once and for all. The ladder sets it aside for now, and comes back to it built in wires (4·comp).

Working a line is induction over the series: every change keeps the line true, so what falls out cannot fail if the givens hold. The series works on method, and everyday guesswork usually focuses on the inputs, induction under cases: from cases to a rule. The sun has come up every morning you have seen, so it will come up tomorrow. That can be very strong evidence, and statistics and the lab live on it. But a guess from cases never changes a line. A thousand grey cats do not earn “every cat is grey”. One cat that is not grey would undo it, and a line cannot look to see whether that cat exists.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

Both kinds of induction are anexact, yet rigorous. Anexact, because neither one has to touch the world: a line holds whether or not its things exist, and cases never reach every case. Rigorous, because each keeps exact books. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre: measuring says in numbers how wrong it might be.

The books are a ledger, and the argument form already is one. In the Tibbles line of 1a4, every change is paid for by its technique, and nothing is claimed that was not paid for. Scientists keep a ledger too: every bit of stuff that goes into a burning candle comes out as smoke, gas and heat, and nothing vanishes.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every statement in it is true.

A line that does not contradict itself can still be false: it is false when it does not fit the world. Rung 4 adds one more way, once lines stand in a proof: a line is false when another line of the proof contradicts it.

A candle burns down, and the wax is gone. Where would you look for it, if nothing vanishes?

1a7Unpack and Pack, in the Same Line

Techniques were not found in dusty old books. They were built: people noticed what kept doing work for them, and made them shorthand. To prove the point, we build some ourselves, out of plain English.

  • Unpack: where a line uses a word, you may write out what the word means in its place. “Rex is a puppy” becomes “Rex is a young dog”: the same line, written out.
  • Pack: unpack run the other way. Where a line says everything a word means, you may write the word instead. “Rex is a young dog” becomes “Rex is a puppy”.

The signs for unpack and pack are drawn from what unpack and pack do. ﹀ is a wide cup opening downward: the meaning pours out of the word. ︿ is the same cup turned over: the meaning is gathered back under the word. Each sign is a name: the name of a tech, packed into one scribble. Unpack and pack are techs on a word and its meaning, so a reason may cite them as it cites any tech.

Inside a line, the cup goes right after the word it opens, and the meaning follows in brackets: puppy﹀(a young dog), read “puppy, unpacked: a young dog”. Packing turns the cup over: young dog︿(puppy). A mark unpacks the same way, with its “from here on” line as its meaning: =﹀(the two sides name one thing).

No key for ﹀ and ︿? Write unpack and pack.

Marks are shorthand for words, and every mark on the ladder was brought in by a “from here on” line saying what it means. So when a later page unpacks a mark, that is the unpack tech again, with the “from here on” line as the meaning it unpacks.

“Is” does two jobs in English. In “Mike is the boy in the red cap” it is sameness, =, and the swap axiom and the chain technique carry it. In “Rex is a puppy” it says what kind of thing Rex is. A line of the “is a kind of” shape is unpacked, never chained, and two unpacks in a row carry Rex down a line of kinds.

This step is in foreclosure, the state 0a10 named: it uses what it has not yet built, and says so. To unpack “puppy” is to use a meaning of the shape “if anything is a puppy, it is a young dog”, and that shape needs the if (1d), and it needs rung 2 for the “anything” and for “puppy” as a collection. From 1b on you will often see a line written again and again, one under another, each time changed by a technique named beside it. That is still one line, worked as a series: each is the line before it, changed and kept true, and the rules for lines in this step hold for every one of them. Rows, which are separate lines of fixed arrangements nested together, wait until 4a earns them: there rows, reasons, supposing and cases are justified, the circle closes, and everything is earned. Everything checks out, even though we are using tools before they are built: we are assembling the plane in midair.

1a8Transform

Transform: where a line says something of a thing in one of 0a's four shapes, you may say the same claim in another shape. “Rex is spotted” becomes “Rex has spots”. The claim is the same; only the shape has changed. “Spotted Rex is running” becomes “Rex is spotted, and Rex is running”, taking the descriptive apart.

A transform is any move that changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. Changing the shape is the first kind of transform: it says no more and no less of the thing than the line it came from. Transform rests on 0a's possibility axiom: the shapes are ways of writing one claim, so moving between them loses nothing.

The liar

The sentence “this sentence is false” is the liar paradox, known since the Greeks.

Who said it first

Pascal described a perfect method: define every word, and prove every claim. Then he admitted it cannot be done. Each definition uses words, and each proof uses claims, so the work never ends unless you stop at words too plain to define and claims too plain to prove.

Pascal, “On the Geometrical Spirit” (c. 1657); paraphrase

Accepted names

A line reached with nothing given is, in the books, a theorem of logic, or a logical truth; checked by tables (1e), it is a tautology. The box is a subproof, and this way of laying out proofs is natural deduction, in the box style; the supposition is an assumption, and dropping it is discharging it.
