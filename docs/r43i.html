43i · proof

Modules over a principal ring

Rung twenty seven stretched arrows by numbers from a field. Stretch them instead by members of a ring, and most of the theory survives, with one loss: a space no longer needs a basis. The clock of six, stretched by whole numbers, has no free arrow at all, since six times anything is nothing. What replaces the basis, when the ring is principal, is a list of clocks. Every such space with finitely many makers is some copies of the ring itself, side by side with a few clocks, each clock’s size dividing the next. The proof is elimination, as rung twenty seven solved systems, but with only the moves that can be undone over the ring: swap two rows, add a whole multiple of one row to another. Division with remainder does the rest. Read with the whole numbers as the ring, the technique lists every finite group whose combining turns about. Read with the polynomials of a field as the ring, it gives the next newsflash.

Arrows stretched by a ring are a module. Over a principal ring, row and column moves that can be undone bring any grid to a diagonal, each entry dividing the next, so every module with finitely many makers is copies of the ring beside clocks of those sizes. With ℤ as the ring, every finite group that turns about is one list of clocks.

43i1Arrows stretched by a ring

A module over a ring R that turns about is a set M with an adding that obeys 27a’s four adding rules and a stretching by members of R that obeys 27a’s four stretching rules. Over a field it is a space of arrows. Rⁿ, the lists of n members of R, is a module. Every ideal of R is one, and so is every quotient R/I.

Group-is-module technique: every group whose combining turns about is a module over ℤ, with n · g the sum of n copies of g, and −n · g its negative. The four stretching rules are 19b’s powers technique written with plus.

M = \mathbb Z/6 \text{ over } \mathbb Z @@ a module, by the group-is-module technique
6 \cdot g = 0 \text{ for every } g @@ six copies of any hour on the clock of six
c \cdot g = 0 \text{ with } c = 6 \ne 0 @@ so no list in M is free (27b)
\htmlClass{new}{\mathbb Z/6 \text{ has no basis over } \mathbb Z} \quad \blacksquare @@ a basis would be free

A module that has a basis is free: ℤⁿ is free with the n unit lists as a basis. A module with finitely many makers, every member a mix of them, is finitely made. Over a field every finitely made module is free (27b’s grow-to-basis). Over ℤ the clocks are not.

43i2Grids brought to a diagonal

Over a ring, the row moves of 27j that can be undone are: swap two rows; add a multiple of one row to another; stretch a row by an undoable member. Column moves likewise.

Diagonal-grid technique: by undoable row and column moves, every grid over ℤ, or over F[x] for a field F, becomes diagonal, with entries d₁, d₂, … each dividing the next.

\text{bring an entry of least size to the corner, by swaps} @@ size: |n| on ℤ, the degree on F[x]
\text{divide each entry of its row and column by it: } a = dq + r @@ division with remainder (4j, 43c)
\text{subtract } q \text{ times the corner’s row or column; a smaller } r \ne 0 \text{ goes to the corner} @@ the size falls each time; it cannot fall forever (4h)
\text{the corner divides its row and column, which are cleared to } 0 @@ the falling stops only then
\text{if the corner fails to divide some entry, add that row to the corner’s row, and repeat} @@ the size falls again
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a diagonal, each entry dividing the next}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same moves on the grid that is left, by induction
  
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} @@ row 2 less row 1
\to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} @@ swap; row 2 less twice row 1
\htmlClass{new}{\to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ column 2 plus column 1; 2 divides 10

43i3The structure technique

Structure technique: a finitely made module over ℤ, or over F[x], is a copy of Rʳ × R/(d₁) × ⋯ × R/(dₖ) for some r ≥ 0 and members d₁ ∣ d₂ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ, none undoable.

m_1, \ldots, m_n \text{ make } M;\ \ \pi : R^n \to M,\ \ (c_i) \mapsto \textstyle\sum c_i m_i @@ onto; M is a copy of Rⁿ divided by what π loses (42·cb19, with stretching)
K = \text{what } \pi \text{ loses is made by at most } n \text{ members} @@ induction on n: K’s last entries form an ideal, one maker (43d1)
K = \text{the rows of a grid } A @@ the relations among the makers
A \to \mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_k, 0, \ldots, 0) @@ the diagonal-grid technique; the moves change the makers of Rⁿ and of K, not the quotient
R^n / K = R/(d_1) \times \cdots \times R/(d_k) \times R^{n-k} @@ a diagonal relation says dᵢ times the iᵗʰ maker is 0, and nothing more
\htmlClass{new}{\text{drop the } d_i \text{ that are undoable: } R/(1) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ r = n − k

The list d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ is the module’s only such list, up to partners. Held in trust: the proof counts the members killed by each power of each prime, and is in 43i.6.3. The same lines work over every principal ring, with the one-maker technique in place of division at the corner.

43i4Every finite group that turns about

Clock-list technique: every finite group whose combining turns about is a copy of ℤ/d₁ × ⋯ × ℤ/dₖ for exactly one list d₁ ∣ d₂ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ of whole numbers above 1, and its size is d₁ ⋯ dₖ. It is the structure technique over ℤ, with r = 0 because ℤ is endless and the group is not.

G = \mathbb Z^2 \text{ divided by the rows } (4, 6),\ (6, 4) @@ makers g, h with 4g + 6h = 0 and 6g + 4h = 0
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix} @@ the diagonal-grid technique (43i2)
G = \mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/10,\ \ |G| = 20 @@ the structure technique; |det| = 20
\htmlClass{new}{\mathbb Z/10 = \mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/5,\ \ G = \mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/5} \quad \blacksquare @@ 42c’s remainder technique, 2 and 5 sharing nothing

So the groups of size 8 that turn about are ℤ/8, ℤ/2 × ℤ/4 and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 × ℤ/2: the lists whose product is 8, each dividing the next. Of size 72 = 2³ · 3² there are 3 · 2 = 6: three ways to split the 2s, two ways to split the 3s, joined by 42c.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A module over R is an R-module; a finitely made one is finitely generated, and the structure technique is the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, in its invariant factor form. The diagonal-grid technique is the Smith normal form, and d₁, …, dₖ are the invariant factors. The clock-list technique is the fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups; groups that turn about are abelian groups.

Who said it first

Henry Smith brought whole-number grids to a diagonal in 1861. Leopold Kronecker classified finite groups that turn about in 1870, after Ernst Schering’s work of 1868, and Georg Frobenius and Ludwig Stickelberger proved the list unique in 1878. Emmy Noether and her students, Bartel van der Waerden writing it up in 1930, put the technique in the form of modules over a principal ring.

A proof that is not allowed

“The group ℤ² divided by (4, 6) and (6, 4) has size 4 · 4 − 6 · 6 = −20, so it is ℤ/20.” Not allowed: the size is |det| = 20, but the size does not fix the group. The diagonal is 2 and 10, so it is ℤ/2 × ℤ/10, which has no member of order 20.
