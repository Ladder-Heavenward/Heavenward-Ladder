13i · technique

Products and quotients

13c proved the product rule from 5i's grid of cells, and 13f built the quotient rule from it and the chain rule. This step makes both rules quick in the hands. The product rule is the two one-change cells: each function times the other's slope, added. Three factors give three such cells. The quotient rule is bottom times the top's slope, less top times the bottom's slope, over the bottom squared, and its order and its square both have reasons you can see. Then three roads to one quotient's slope, which check each other; the slips that look like rules; and answers read backwards to the product or quotient that made them.

For a product, each function times the other's slope, added. For a quotient, bottom times top's slope less top times bottom's slope, over the bottom squared. Its sign is the top's.

13i1Each times the other’s slope

Page letters, said out loud. In this step u, v and w name functions whose inputs and outputs are reals, each with a slope at the input in hand. 13e's w was its helper, on that step only; here w is a third factor.

13c split the new product of u and v at x + h into four cells. There is the old product u v, two one-change cells Δu · v and u · Δv, and the leftover cell Δu · Δv. Divided by h, the one-change cells head to u′v and uv′. The leftover cell goes to 0 in the limit, because a slope means no gap. So product for slopes: (uv)′ = u′v + uv′.

One popular calculus book reads the rule as two separate experiments. Keep the first factor fixed while the second changes. Then keep the second fixed while the first changes, and add the two results.

That is the two one-change cells in words: each cell changes one factor and keeps the other at its old value. Here it is on work you can check by expanding.

y = (x^2 + 1)(x^3 - 2) @@ u = x² + 1, v = x³ − 2
u' = 2x,\ \ v' = 3x^2 @@ polynomial-slope (13d)
y' = \htmlClass{new}{2x\,(x^3 - 2)} + \htmlClass{new}{(x^2 + 1)\,3x^2} @@ product for slopes: u′ times v, plus u times v′
= 2x^4 - 4x + 3x^4 + 3x^2 = 5x^4 + 3x^2 - 4x @@ expand by the grid of cells; collect
y = x^5 + x^3 - 2x^2 - 2 \;\Rightarrow\; y' = \htmlClass{keep}{5x^4 + 3x^2 - 4x} @@ the check: multiply out first, then polynomial-slope. They agree
y = \sqrt{x}\,(x + 4),\ \ \text{at } 4 @@ a root as one factor (x > 0)
y'(4) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt4}\cdot 8 + \sqrt4 \cdot 1 = 2 + 2 = \htmlClass{new}{4} @@ product for slopes with slope-of-√ and slope-of-a-line. Check: x^(3/2) + 4x^(1/2) has slope (3/2) · 2 + 2 · ½ = 4 at 4 (fraction-power for slopes)

When you can multiply out, do, and use it as a check. The rule earns its keep when you cannot: when a factor is a root, a fraction, or, on 13j, a chain.

The tempting rule, (uv)′ = u′v′, keeps the one cell that vanishes and throws away both that stay, as 13c said. Test it on 3x: 0 · 1 = 0 instead of 3. On x · x: 1 · 1 = 1 instead of 2x. A rule that fails on 3x is not a rule. Two smaller slips: dropping a cell, and a factor taken twice. Dropping a cell writes u′v alone, which is right only when v is a constant, constant-factor for slopes's case. A factor taken twice writes u′v′ + uv′. Say the gesture as you write: this one, times that one's slope. Each factor appears once plain and once as a slope.

13i2Three factors

Three-factors technique: if u, v and w have slopes at x, then (uvw)′ = u′vw + uv′w + uvw′.

uvw = u\,(vw) @@ regroup for times on ℝ, input by input; equal-functions: the same function
(uvw)' = u'\,(vw) + u\,(vw)' @@ product for slopes, with vw as the second factor (it has a slope, by product for slopes)
= u'vw + u\,(\htmlClass{new}{v'w + vw'}) @@ product for slopes again, inside
= \htmlClass{keep}{u'vw + uv'w + uvw'} \quad \blacksquare @@ spread on ℝ

In the grid of cells, (u + Δu)(v + Δv)(w + Δw) has eight cells, as 13g's cube did. One has no change, three have one change, three have two, and one has three. Divided by h, only the three one-change cells stay: each changes one factor and keeps the other two. With u = v = w = x it is 13g's 3x² again. Worked: x(x + 1)(x + 2) at 1 has slope 1 · 2 · 3 + 1 · 1 · 3 + 1 · 2 · 1 = 6 + 3 + 2 = 11. Multiplied out it is x³ + 3x² + 2x, with slope 3 + 6 + 2 = 11.

13i3The quotient rule

13f's quotient for slopes: where v(x) ≠ 0, (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v².

Said in words, the order runs like this. Multiply the bottom by the slope of the top, then the top by the slope of the bottom. Take the second from the first, and divide by the square of the bottom.

The order matters because taking away cannot be turned about. A check catches a reversed order. 1/x has slope −1/x² (slope-of-one-over-x). With u = 1 and v = x the rule gives (0 · x − 1 · 1)/x² = −1/x². Reverse the order in the top and you get +1/x². Yet every chord of 1/x whose two inputs s and t lie on one side of 0 has the negative slope −1/(st).

y = \dfrac{2x + 3}{3x + 2} \quad (x \ne -\tfrac23) @@ top u = 2x + 3, bottom v = 3x + 2
u' = 2,\ \ v' = 3 @@ slope-of-a-line
y' = \dfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(3x + 2)\cdot 2 - (2x + 3)\cdot 3}}{(3x + 2)^2} @@ quotient for slopes: bottom times top's slope, less top times bottom's slope, over the bottom squared
= \dfrac{6x + 4 - 6x - 9}{(3x + 2)^2} = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{-5}{(3x + 2)^2}} @@ the x's cancel in the top; leave the bottom squared

Quotient-slope-sign technique: the slope of a quotient has the sign of the top of the quotient rule. Precisely: where v(x) ≠ 0, the slope of u/v at x is positive exactly when u′v − uv′ is positive there. It is negative exactly when that is negative, and 0 exactly when it is 0. The bottom v(x)² is positive, by 8f's square-never-negative and 5e's zero-product read in ℝ, since v(x) ≠ 0. And dividing by a positive real keeps the sign (8d's order-and-arithmetic-agree, positive-reciprocal on ℚ). So (2x + 3)/(3x + 2) has a negative slope at every input, on each side of −⅔, without exception. You can say that before drawing anything.

When the top is a constant, the rule shortens to 13f's reciprocal for slopes: (c/v)′ = −c v′/v². So 3/(x² + 1) has slope −3 · 2x/(x² + 1)² = −6x/(x² + 1)². The first product of the full rule, (x² + 1) · 0, was always going to vanish.

13i4Three roads to one slope

Take y = x/(x² + 1). Every road below was proved from the one definition, so they cannot disagree, and that makes each a check on the others.

\begin{aligned}&\text{road 1 (quotient for }\\ &\quad \text{slopes):}\ \ \dfrac{(x^2 + 1)\cdot 1 - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} \\ &\quad = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2}}\end{aligned} @@ bottom times top's slope, less top times bottom's slope, over the bottom squared
\begin{aligned}&\text{road 2 (product for slopes, reciprocal for}\\ &\quad\text{slopes):}\ \ 1\cdot\dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1} + x \cdot \dfrac{-2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2}\end{aligned} @@ y = x · (1/(x² + 1)); reciprocal for slopes for the second factor, as 13e found by naming the inside r
= \dfrac{(x^2 + 1) - 2x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \htmlClass{keep}{\dfrac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2}} @@ one bottom (5h): the same
\text{road 3 (multiply up):}\ \ y\,(x^2 + 1) = x @@ multiplying-keeps-answers: true at every input, since x² + 1 ≠ 0
y'\,(x^2 + 1) + y \cdot 2x = 1 @@ product for slopes on the left (y has a slope: road 1 proved it), slope-of-a-line on the right; slopes-decided-near: equal functions, equal slopes
y' = \dfrac{1 - 2xy}{x^2 + 1} = \dfrac{1 - \frac{2x^2}{x^2 + 1}}{x^2 + 1} = \htmlClass{keep}{\dfrac{1 - x^2}{(x^2 + 1)^2}} @@ solve for y′ (adding-keeps-answers, multiplying-keeps-answers); substitute for the expression y = x/(x² + 1); fractions-of-rationals

At 0 the slope is 1, at 1 it is 0, at 2 it is −3/25. Quotient-slope-sign reads the signs off the top 1 − x² alone: positive between −1 and 1, negative outside.

The third road deserves its caution. Multiplying up and taking slopes of both sides needs y to have a slope first: product for slopes's hypotheses ask for it. Here road 1 had already proved it. The third road recomputes a slope known to exist; it never proves that one exists. 13l makes this same caution the licence for its method.

Every road checks every other. Take x⁵/x², which is x³ for x ≠ 0 (8i's whole-exponent):

\Big(\dfrac{x^5}{x^2}\Big)' = \dfrac{x^2 \cdot 5x^4 - x^5 \cdot 2x}{x^4} = \dfrac{3x^6}{x^4} = \htmlClass{new}{3x^2} @@ quotient for slopes; collect the top; whole-exponent
\big(x^5 \cdot x^{-2}\big)' = 5x^4 \cdot x^{-2} + x^5 \cdot (-2x^{-3}) = 5x^2 - 2x^2 = \htmlClass{keep}{3x^2} @@ product for slopes with negative-powers
(x^3)' = \htmlClass{keep}{3x^2} @@ power for slopes, after whole-exponent. Three roads, one slope

13i5Choosing the road

Before reaching for the quotient rule, ask whether the fraction can be made a sum. If the bottom is a single power of x, split it, as 13h did: (x³ − 5x + 2)/x² = x − 5x⁻¹ + 2x⁻², slope 1 + 5x⁻² − 4x⁻³. If the top is a constant, use reciprocal for slopes. If the bottom is a sum, splitting is illegal (5h: the reciprocal of a sum is not the sum of the reciprocals), and you need the rule. The roads always agree; picking the short one is the manipulating part.

13i6Illegal moves

(uv)' = u'v'\,? @@ 3x: 0 · 1 = 0, not 3. It keeps only the leftover cell, the one that vanishes
\big(x^2(x + 1)\big)' = 2x \cdot 1\,? @@ the same slip. True slope 2x(x + 1) + x² = 3x² + 2x: at 2, 16, not 4
\Big(\dfrac{u}{v}\Big)' = \dfrac{u'}{v'}\,? @@ x²/x is x, slope 1; the slip gives 2x
\dfrac{uv' - u'v}{v^2}\,? @@ the order reversed: right size, wrong sign. 1/x would climb
\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v}\,? @@ the square forgotten. For 1/x it gives −1/x, which is −½ at 2, not −¼
\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x^2} = 1\,? @@ the bottom divides the whole top. Split: 1 + x⁻², slope −2x⁻³

13i7Running it backwards

A product-rule answer has a signature: two terms, each with one factor plain and one factor's slope. Each factor is plain in one term and a slope in the other. Read 2x(x³ + 1) + x² · 3x². 2x is the slope of x², standing beside x³ + 1. 3x² is the slope of x³ + 1, standing beside x². So it is the slope of x²(x³ + 1). A quotient answer's signature is the squared bottom and the minus sign. In (x · 2 − (2x − 1) · 1)/x² the bottom is x and the top 2x − 1, so it came from (2x − 1)/x. But (2x − 1)/x = 2 − 1/x, and −1/x has the same slope: running backwards finds the function only up to a lift. Rung 16 reads product answers backwards on purpose.
