9·phys · physics

Where will a rolling ball be, and when?

Where will a rolling ball be, and when? In 1638 a book on two new sciences was published, and its third day begins the science of motion the way it is still done. Define a kind of motion precisely, deduce what follows, then roll a ball down a board and check. This page takes the book's steps in order, with one change of language. Where the book says the distances are to one another as the squares of the times, the page says that position is a function of time, and names it. And where the book proved its rule for the distance with a figure, the page proves it with rung 4's sums and rung 8's counting-passes-any-real, and nothing else.

At each instant a body is in exactly one place, so position is a function of time. Steady motion is the linear function. Speed gained steadily from rest gives distances growing as the squares of the times, and in successive equal times as 1, 3, 5, 7.

Each instant, one place

Choose a track, a road or a rail, a mark on it, and which way along it counts as positive. The position of a body at an instant is the number of metres from the mark along the track, positive one way and negative the other: a real, in 5i's metres. Choose a starting instant, and measure time t in seconds. A moving body gives pairs (t, x): at this instant, this place. Nothing is in two places at once, so no instant appears in two pairs. That is 9a's definition: position is a function of time, written x(t).

It need not be one-to-one. A bus waiting at a stop has the same position at many instants; a ball thrown up passes each height twice. So “when was it at 3 m?” can have two answers, or many, and running position backwards will need 9d's keeping. The change of position from t₁ to t₂ is 8h's displacement, x(t₂) − x(t₁). Drawn in 9e's plane with time across and position up, the graph of x is the distance–time picture. The column test is the physics: one place at each instant.

From here on, on this page, the average velocity of a motion over the times from t₁ to t₂, with t₁ < t₂, is (x(t₂) − x(t₁))/(t₂ − t₁). It is the displacement divided by the time taken, in metres per second (5i's per). Velocity is speed with a sign: positive moving the positive way along the track, negative the other way. For a trip that never turns back, it is 5i's average speed with a sign.

For any function the same quotient, the change in output over the change in input, is called its average rate of change over the stretch: average velocity is the average rate of change of position.

Uniform motion: the linear function

The old experimenter begins with the simplest motion and is fussy about one word.

The book defines steady, or uniform, motion as one in which the distances covered in any equal intervals of time are equal. It warns that the older definition, equal distances in equal times, lets in a body that covers equal distances in each whole hour while hurrying and dawdling within it. The word “any” rules that out.

It is 1e's quantifier lesson in a physics book: “for any two equal intervals” is much stronger than “for the whole-second intervals”. Said with this page's word, the average velocity is the same number v over every stretch of time. Steady-motion technique: a motion has the same average velocity v over every stretch of time exactly when x(t) = x₀ + vt, where x₀ = x(0). That is 9e's same-ratio, read with t for the input and x for the output. A constant ratio of changes forces the linear function, with step v and start x₀. ∎

\tfrac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = v \text{ for all } t_1 \lt t_2 @@ uniform motion, in this page's words
x(t) = \htmlClass{new}{x_0 + v\,t} @@ same-ratio: the linear function, step v, start x₀ = x(0)

A person walking briskly does about 1.4 m/s, so 100 m takes 100/1.4 ≈ 71 s. Starting 50 m past the mark, after 30 s they are at 50 + 1.4 · 30 = 92 m. A car at 30 m/s is doing 108 km/h, by 5i's chain of ones. In the distance–time picture uniform motion is a linear function with step v. In the velocity–time picture, time across and velocity up, it is the function with one output for all, v.

Uniform acceleration: equal speed gained in equal times

A falling stone speeds up. How? The old experimenter chose the simplest rule he could imagine, and then tested it.

The book calls a motion uniformly accelerated when, starting from rest, it gains equal amounts of speed in equal intervals of time. It argues that nature, which uses the simplest means, makes falling bodies move so.

That is the definition of uniform motion moved up one level: what was steady is now the speed's growth. It needs the speed at an instant, the number a speedometer shows. What that is, exactly, is a question for 13·phys. The old book took it as understood. This page holds it in trust until 13·phys, and uses it only through one plain fact, stated below. With it, the definition says the speed is the linear function in t, starting at 0: v(t) = kt. Here k, the acceleration, is the speed gained per second, in m/s² (5i). Near the ground, ignoring the push of the air, a falling body has k ≈ 9.8 m/s², the value 5i and 7·phys used.

How far? The falling-distance rule, proved by steps

The plain fact about speed is this, the physics premise of the page: when a body's speed only grows, its average velocity over any stretch of time lies between its speed at the start of the stretch and its speed at the end. A car that is always speeding up, doing 20 m/s at one instant and 30 m/s ten seconds later, covered between 200 m and 300 m in those ten seconds. Falling-distance technique: if a body starts from rest at the mark, v(t) = kt, and the premise is true, then x(t) = ½kt² for every t ≥ 0.

Nothing beyond rung 8 was used. The distance is squeezed between two sums of counted steps, and counting-passes-any-real says nothing can sit strictly between them for every n except the one number. The old book proved the same thing with a figure, comparing amounts that rung 15 calls areas, and stated it as below: the mean of the first and last speeds is (0 + kt)/2, and that times t is ½kt². 5i already checked the units: s² against m/s² leaves metres.

The book states that the time in which a body starting from rest and uniformly accelerated covers a distance is the time it would take at a steady speed equal to half its final speed. Its second result is that the distances covered from rest are to one another as the squares of the times.

One, three, five, seven

Now a further technique that gives you something to measure. Look at the distance covered in each successive second rather than the total. With x(n) = ½kn² at whole seconds, the n-th second covers x(n) − x(n − 1) = ½k(n² − (n − 1)²) = ½k(2n − 1), by 5h's difference-of-squares. That is the n-th odd number, times the first second's distance. Run it backwards: if the pieces go 1, 3, 5, 7, the totals are 1, 4, 9, 16. Odd-numbers-make-squares technique: Σ_{i<n} (2i + 1) = n² for every number n. Base: 0 = 0². Step: n² + (2n + 1) = (n + 1)², by sum-one-more and 5h's square-of-a-sum. ∎ Steps and totals are two descriptions of one function.

Real falls fall short of the table after a second or two, because air pushes back. A dense ball over a few metres comes close.

The board

A free fall of 4.9 m takes one second, far too quick to time without a good clock. So the old experimenter slowed it down: a ball rolling down a board raised at one end gains speed the same way, only more gently.

The book describes a wooden board about twelve cubits long (a cubit is an old unit, about half a metre) with a smooth groove cut along it, and a hard polished bronze ball. For a clock there was a large vessel of water whose thin jet was caught in a glass during each run and weighed. Repeating the runs a full hundred times, it reports, the distances were always as the squares of the times, whatever the height the board was raised to.

“Whatever the height” matters. Raising the board changes k, the number in front, but not the square. The ratios 1, 3, 5, 7 do not depend on k at all, since ½k cancels from every ratio. The book's check that a quarter of the board took half the time is the same fact. ½k t_q² = ¼ · ½k t_w² gives t_q = ½t_w (multiplying-keeps-answers, then 9d's kept squaring run backwards), for every k.

Running it backwards: time from distance

A diver or a well-digger asks the inverse question: how long to fall a given distance? On t ≥ 0, x = ½kt² is squaring kept to the non-negative times, times a constant. So 9d runs it backwards.

Physicists often round g to 10 m/s² for estimates. Then x = 5t², and 20 m takes exactly 2 s, 45 m exactly 3 s, arriving at 30 m/s, 108 km/h.

Running it backwards, in general

Handed a table of positions at equal times: if the gaps are equal, the motion is uniform (check “any” with a finer table). If they go as 1, 3, 5, 7, it is uniformly accelerated from rest, with ½k the first gap per square second. Handed a distance: time is √(2x/k). Handed a speed on arrival: v = √(2kx), so x = v²/(2k). Found upward; written downward.

The book on two new sciences is Galileo Galilei’s Two New Sciences (1638), and the old experimenter with the board is Galileo. The ancient philosopher of 9·phys.4.4 is Aristotle.

Galileo defines steady, or uniform, motion as one in which the distances covered in any equal intervals of time are equal.
