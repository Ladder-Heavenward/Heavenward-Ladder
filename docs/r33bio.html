33·bio · biology

How does a leopard get its spots?

How does a leopard get its spots? Every cell of a leopard’s skin carries the same instructions, and none of them can see the whole animal. Yet the skin ends up dark in rosettes and pale between them, with the rosettes about the same size from shoulder to flank, and the tail ringed instead of spotted. Zebras, angelfish and the shells of cone snails along the coast near Monterey do the same kind of thing with stripes and zigzags. What makes the question hard is that spreading, the one process every skin has, does the opposite of making patterns: 33b proved that the heat rule smooths and forgets. So how can a pattern grow out of an even start? The answer this page follows is a guess from 1952 that sounds wrong at first. Two chemicals that react with each other and spread at different speeds can turn an even mix unstable, and spreading itself is what does it. First, though, the page settles a premise an older annex has held in trust since rung 14: the shape of the oxygen inside a sheet of living tissue. And it ends with spreading of another kind, not of chemicals in a skin but of gene shares in a herd.

Oxygen soaking into tissue that uses it settles into a parabola across a sheet and across a ball. Two chemicals that are steady when mixed evenly can be made unsteady by spreading, if the one that holds the other back spreads faster; then wiggles of one size grow, and the size of the animal decides whether they become spots, stripes or nothing. And the share of a gene in a small herd spreads like heat, with a width that grows with time.

33bio1Oxygen inside a sheet and a ball

14·bio took as a premise the oxygen inside a sheet of tissue 2L thick, bathed at c₀ on both faces and using oxygen at q per cubic metre each second: c(x) = c₀ − (q/(2D))(L² − x²). The heat rule with a source (33a1’s ledger) now gives it. Oxygen spreads like heat with diffusivity D and is used up at the rate q, so c_t = DΔc − q, and once settled, DΔc = q.

Uptake-curve technique: settled oxygen in a sheet of half-thickness L is c₀ − (q/(2D))(L² − x²), and in a ball of radius R it is c₀ − (q/(6D))(R² − r²).

D\,c^{\prime\prime} = q,\ \ c(\pm L) = c_0 @@ the sheet: settled, one knob
c = \tfrac{q}{2D}x^2 + Bx + C,\ \ B = 0,\ \ C = c_0 - \tfrac{q}{2D}L^2 @@ same slopes twice (14e); the two faces match
\htmlClass{new}{c = c_0 - \tfrac{q}{2D}\big(L^2 - x^2\big)} @@ 14·bio’s premise, earned
\tfrac{D}{r}\big(r\,c\big)^{\prime\prime} = q,\ \ c(R) = c_0,\ c \text{ finite at } 0 @@ the ball: the round-ball technique (33g4)
\htmlClass{new}{c = c_0 - \tfrac{q}{6D}\big(R^2 - r^2\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ rc = qr³/(6D) + Br + C′; C′ = 0 keeps c finite at 0

That closes the premise 14·bio held in trust. The largest sheet that stays supplied has L² = 2Dc₀/q, as 14·bio found; the largest ball has R² = 6Dc₀/q, so a ball can be √3 times as large as a sheet is thick. The ball’s three directions bring oxygen in from more sides.

33bio2Two chemicals, tested by wiggles

Two chemicals in a skin have shares u and v. Each is made and used at rates that depend on both, f(u, v) and g(u, v), and each spreads, at its own diffusivity:

\(u_t = f(u, v) + D_u\,u_{xx},\qquad v_t = g(u, v) + D_v\,v_{xx}.\)

Let the even mix (u*, v*) be still: f = g = 0 there. Near it, small changes obey the straight rules of the grid A of partial slopes, f_u, f_v, g_u, g_v, at the mix. Try a wiggle that grows or shrinks, (ū, v̄)e^(σt) cos kx.

Wiggle-test technique: the wiggle of size k grows or shrinks at the rates σ that are the stretches of A − k²D, where D is the grid with D_u and D_v down its middle. The even mix is unsteady to that wiggle when det(A − k²D) < 0 or its trace is above 0.

\sigma\,\bar w\,e^{\sigma t}\cos kx = (A - k^2 D)\,\bar w\,e^{\sigma t}\cos kx @@ the second slope of cos kx is −k² cos kx; w̄ = (ū, v̄)
(A - k^2D)\,\bar w = \sigma\,\bar w @@ σ is a stretch of A − k²D (27f)
\sigma^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A - k^2D)\,\sigma + \det(A - k^2D) = 0 @@ the stretch test (27f) for a 2 × 2 grid
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a stretch above } 0 \iff \det(A - k^2D) < 0 \text{ or } \operatorname{tr}(A - k^2D) > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two roots multiply to det and add to tr

With k = 0 the test is the mix without spreading. Suppose it is steady then: tr A < 0 and det A > 0. Spreading only lowers the trace, tr A − k²(D_u + D_v). So the only way for spreading to unsteady the mix is through the determinant.

33bio3Spreading that makes a pattern

Write det(A − k²D) as a function of s = k²:

\(h(s) = D_uD_v\,s^2 - \big(D_v f_u + D_u g_v\big)\,s + \det A.\)

Spreading-breaks-balance technique: if the mix is steady without spreading, it is made unsteady by spreading exactly when D_v f_u + D_u g_v > 2√(D_uD_v det A); the wiggles that grow fastest have k² near (D_v f_u + D_u g_v)/(2D_uD_v).

h \text{ is least at } s^* = \tfrac{D_v f_u + D_u g_v}{2D_uD_v} @@ flat-top (14a) on a parabola that opens upward
h(s^*) = \det A - \tfrac{(D_v f_u + D_u g_v)^2}{4D_uD_v} @@ put s* in
s^* > 0,\ h(s^*) < 0 \iff D_v f_u + D_u g_v > 2\sqrt{D_uD_v\det A} @@ the first for a real k, the second for a negative determinant
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a band of wiggle sizes grows, centred near } k^2 = s^*} \quad \blacksquare @@ the wiggle-test technique

Since tr A = f_u + g_v < 0 and D_v f_u + D_u g_v > 0, one of f_u, g_v is above 0 and the other below, and the spreading must differ. Read u as a chemical that makes more of itself, f_u > 0, and v as one that holds it back. Then v must spread faster: the maker stays local, the holder-back spreads out and stops the maker nearby. Spots appear at the spacing of that reach. One example first. Take f_u = 1, f_v = −1, g_u = 2, g_v = −1.5, D_u = 1, D_v = 20. Then tr A = −0.5 and det A = 0.5: steady without spreading. And 20 − 1.5 = 18.5 > 2√10 ≈ 6.3. The wiggles with k² between 0.028 and 0.897 grow, and the pattern’s spacing is near 2π/√0.4625 ≈ 9.2 of the units D was measured in.

33bio4Spots, stripes, or nothing

A skin is not an endless line. Take a strip of skin of length ℓ whose ends let nothing through, as on 33h1. The only wiggles that fit are cos(nπx/ℓ), so k = nπ/ℓ for n = 0, 1, 2, ….

Room-for-a-pattern technique: a strip of length ℓ grows a pattern only if some k = nπ/ℓ with n ≥ 1 lies in the growing band; if the band’s largest k is below π/ℓ, it grows none. With the numbers above, the band runs from k ≈ 0.167 to 0.947, so a strip shorter than π/0.947 ≈ 3.3 units stays even.

\text{start } = \textstyle\sum c_n\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}{\ell} @@ the sealed-rod technique’s cosines (33h1): every small start is a sum of them
\text{each term grows or shrinks by its own } \sigma(k_n),\ \ k_n = \tfrac{n\pi}{\ell} @@ the wiggle-test technique, term by term; the rules near the mix are straight
k_n \notin \text{band for every } n \ge 1 \Rightarrow \text{every wiggle shrinks} @@ the n = 0 term is the even mix, which is steady
\htmlClass{new}{\text{some } k_n \text{ in the band} \Rightarrow \text{a pattern with } n \text{ half-wiggles grows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ that term outgrows the rest

On a body the same holds both ways at once. A tail is long and thin: round it there is room for no wiggle, along it there is room for many, so the pattern can only be rings. A flank is wide both ways, and wiggles both ways cross into spots. A very small animal, or a very young one when its pattern is laid down, has room for nothing and is plain. That a cheetah’s tail is ringed near its tip and spotted at its root, and that no animal has a spotted tail on a striped body, was argued from this reasoning in the 1980s.

33bio5Gene shares that spread

30·bio drifted the share p of a gene in a herd of N animals: each generation’s 2N copies are drawn at random from the last, so the new share p′ has average p and spread p(1 − p)/(2N). Many herds that start at the same share p₀ drift apart, and their shares spread out like a crowd of walkers.

Drifting-share technique: after t generations the shares of many herds started at p₀ have average p₀ and spread p₀(1 − p₀)(1 − (1 − 1/(2N))ᵗ), which early on grows like p₀(1 − p₀)t/(2N).

\operatorname{E}(p^{\prime} \mid p) = p,\ \ \operatorname{Var}(p^{\prime} \mid p) = \tfrac{p(1 - p)}{2N} @@ 30·bio’s draw of 2N copies
\operatorname{E}\,p_t = p_0 @@ a fair game (30k): the average fortune never changes
\operatorname{E}\big(p^{\prime}(1 - p^{\prime}) \mid p\big) = p - \big(\tfrac{p(1-p)}{2N} + p^2\big) = p(1 - p)\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big) @@ E p′² is the spread plus the square of the average (30g)
\operatorname{E}\big(p_t(1 - p_t)\big) = p_0(1 - p_0)\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big)^t @@ the whole-chance technique, generation by generation
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Var}\,p_t = \operatorname{E}p_t - \operatorname{E}\big(p_t(1 - p_t)\big) - p_0^2 = p_0(1 - p_0)\big(1 - (1 - \tfrac{1}{2N})^t\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ Var = E p² − p₀², and E p² = E p − E p(1 − p)

Early on, (1 − 1/(2N))ᵗ ≈ 1 − t/(2N), and the spread grows in proportion to time, as 33b3’s bell did with spread 2κt. Drift is the heat rule in gene shares, with a diffusivity p(1 − p)/(4N) that shrinks near 0 and 1, where a gene is lost or everywhere. The island foxes of San Miguel Island, off southern California, fell to about fifteen animals in 1999. In a herd of 50, from p₀ = ½, twenty generations give a spread of 0.25(1 − 0.99²⁰) ≈ 0.046, a width near 0.21: a gene at a half can drift to a quarter or three quarters by chance alone.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Two chemicals that react and spread obey a reaction–diffusion system; u is the activator and v the inhibitor. The wiggle-test technique is linear stability analysis about a homogeneous steady state, and the spreading-breaks-balance technique is the condition for a Turing (diffusion-driven) instability; s* gives the critical wavenumber. The room-for-a-pattern argument is domain-size selection. The uptake-curve technique is the steady diffusion equation with zeroth-order consumption. The drifting share is the Wright–Fisher model, its average share kept is the martingale property, E p(1 − p) is half the expected heterozygosity, and the heat rule in gene shares is Kimura’s diffusion approximation. The map of the seam is positional information.

Who said it first

Alan Turing proposed patterns from reaction and diffusion in “The chemical basis of morphogenesis” (1952), two years before his death. Alfred Gierer and Hans Meinhardt gave the activator and inhibitor form in 1972. James Murray argued in 1981 and 1988 that the size and shape of a skin decide spots, stripes or plain, from tails to the coats of whole animals. Shigeru Kondo and Rihito Asai watched stripes move on a growing angelfish in 1995. Lewis Wolpert proposed positional information in 1969. Sewall Wright and Ronald Fisher built the drift model around 1930, and Motoo Kimura wrote its heat rule in 1955. August Krogh measured oxygen in tissue and gave the sheet and cylinder sums in 1919.

Wrong moves, worked

“The mix is steady without spreading, and spreading only takes away differences. So with spreading it is steadier still.” Not allowed: spreading lowers the trace, which does make it steadier in one way, but it also changes the determinant, and when the two chemicals spread at different speeds the determinant can fall below 0 for one band of wiggle sizes. Steadier in each part is not steadier as a pair.
