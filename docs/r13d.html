13d · proof

The power rule

A power is a statement that names the power before it times the input once more: that is how rung 4 defined powers, and 8f read the definition for reals. So the product rule climbs from each power to the next, and induction climbs every counting number at once. The slope of the input to a whole power is the power times the input to the power one less. Then the small rules: a constant has slope 0, slopes add, a constant factor comes out. With them every polynomial has a slope at every real, given by another polynomial, which has a slope of its own: the slope of the slope. Last, two techniques about the graph read in pairs: lifting it changes no slope, and displacing its input displaces its slopes.

Write the next power as the last one times the input. Let the product rule add one more power each climb. Let induction do every climb at once. Then add slopes term by term.

13d1Climbing the powers

Stands on 13c (product for slopes), 4c (induction) and 8f (powers of reals).

8f read rung 4's powers for reals: x⁰ = 1, and x to the next power is x to the power before times x, by power-of-a-next. As functions, x ↦ x^{S(m)} · x is 10c's product of x ↦ x^{S(m)} with x ↦ x. It has the same values as x ↦ x^{S(S(m))}, so by 9a's equal-functions they are one function. That is a product, and 13c has a rule for products.

Power technique for slopes (the power rule): the slope of x to a whole power is that power times x to one less. Precisely: for every number m, x^{S(m)} has a slope at every real w, and it is S(m) · w^m. In its usual form: for every number n ≥ 1, (xⁿ)′ = n x^{n−1}. Here n − 1 names the number m with S(m) = n (4c's zero-or-a-next: a number other than 0 is a next one). And n in front is the real it names by 8d's naming line.

\varphi(m):\ \ \forall w \in \mathbb{R}\ \ (x^{S(m)})'(w) = S(m)\cdot w^m @@ name the condition first. The letter φ is 2d's “any condition”
\text{base: } x^{S(0)} = x^1 = x @@ power-one (4g), read for reals (8f)
(x)'(w) = 1 = 1 \cdot w^0 @@ slope-of-a-line (13a), step 1; power-zero; times-one on ℝ; 1 names S(0) (3b)
\text{step: let } m \in \mathbb{N};\ \ \text{assume } \varphi(m);\ \ \text{let } w \in \mathbb{R} @@ the induction hypothesis
x^{S(S(m))} = \htmlClass{new}{x^{S(m)} \cdot x} @@ power-of-a-next for powers of a real; equal-functions: one function
(x^{S(m)} \cdot x)'(w) = \htmlClass{keep}{S(m)\, w^m} \cdot w + w^{S(m)} \cdot 1 @@ product for slopes (13c), with the hypothesis for the first factor and slope-of-a-line for the second
= S(m)\, w^{S(m)} + w^{S(m)} @@ regroup for times on ℝ, power-of-a-next; times-one on ℝ
= \big(S(m) + 1\big)\, w^{S(m)} @@ spread on ℝ turned about (collect like terms, 5i)
= \htmlClass{new}{S(S(m))\, w^{S(m)}} @@ plus-one (4f): S(m) + 1 = S(S(m)), carried to reals by the naming line
\varphi(S(m));\ \ \text{so } \forall m \in \mathbb{N}\ \varphi(m) \quad \blacksquare @@ speak of everything (a); prove an if; induction (4c)

Look at the step as 13c's cells. x^{S(S(m))} is x^{S(m)} times x. The cell x^{S(m)} · Δx, divided by h, is w^{S(m)}: one more power, contributed by the new factor x. The cell Δ(x^{S(m)}) · x, divided by h, heads to S(m)w^m · w = S(m)w^{S(m)}: all the old ones, carried up. And the leftover cell goes to 0 in the limit, as it always does. So each climb adds one more w^{S(m)}. Two climbs give 13a's slope-of-x², three give slope-of-x³, and the induction gives them all.

(x^2)' = 2x,\ \ (x^3)' = 3x^2 @@ power for slopes with m = 1 and m = 2: agrees with slope-of-x² and slope-of-x³
(x^4)' = 4x^3,\ \ \ (x^5)'(2) = 5 \cdot 2^4 = \htmlClass{new}{80} @@ power for slopes with m = 3 and m = 4
\dfrac{(2.001)^5 - 32}{0.001} \approx 80.08 @@ one chord slope near 2, for comparison: a little above 80

The induction climbs the numbers, so it proves the rule for whole powers only. It says nothing about x^{1/2}: ½ is not a number, there is no rung of ℕ for the induction to stand on, and the shape of the formula is no licence. 13e finds the slope of √x by another road, and 13f the slopes of the negative powers.

13d2Constants, sums, and a constant factor

Stands on 13d1 and 10c (limit techniques).

Three small rules, each a limit technique of 10c read on chord slopes.

Constant-has-slope-0 technique: for a real c, the constant function c has slope 0 at every real. It is slope-of-a-line with step 0: every chord slope is (c − c)/h = 0.

Slopes-add technique: if f and g have slopes at w, then f + g and f − g have slopes at w, and slopes add. Precisely: (f + g)′(w) = f′(w) + g′(w) and (f − g)′(w) = f′(w) − g′(w). Its accepted name is the sum rule (with the difference rule).

\dfrac{(f + g)(w + h) - (f + g)(w)}{h} @@ 10c's line for f + g, on the lesser of the two reaches
= \dfrac{\big(f(w + h) - f(w)\big) + \big(g(w + h) - g(w)\big)}{h} @@ negative-of-a-sum and middle-swap (5b, 5f), read in ℝ: the middle swap
= \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} + \dfrac{g(w + h) - g(w)}{h}} @@ spread on ℝ with h⁻¹
\to f'(w) + g'(w) \quad \blacksquare @@ limits-add (10c); the differences the same way with limits-subtract and negative-of-a-sum

Constant-factor technique for slopes: if f has a slope at w and c is a real, then (cf)′(w) = c f′(w). The chord slope of cf is c times the chord slope of f (spread on ℝ, regroup for times on ℝ), and 10c's constant-factor sends it to c f′(w). 13c's second check reached the same rule by product for slopes and constant-has-slope-0. In particular (−f)′ = −f′, and adding a constant k changes no slope: (f + k)′(w) = f′(w) + 0, by slopes-add and constant-has-slope-0. Call that lifting technique. Its accepted name is the constant multiple rule.

13d3Every polynomial

Stands on 13d1 and 13d2.

Polynomial-slope technique: every polynomial has a slope at every real, found term by term. Precisely: take a number n and reals u₀, ⋯, u_n, and let p(x) = Σ_{k≤n} u_k x^k. Then p has a slope at every real, and p′(x) = Σ_{k<n} S(k) u_{S(k)} x^k, that is u₁ + 2u₂x + ⋯ + n u_n x^{n−1}. The name echoes 10c's substitution, the limit of a polynomial; here it is the slope.

\text{base } n = 0:\ \ p(x) = u_0,\ \ p'(x) = 0 = \textstyle\sum_{k \lt 0} \cdots @@ constant-has-slope-0; sum-of-none (4k, read with real terms on 9g): an empty sum is 0
\text{step: assume it for } n,\ \text{every list } u_0, \cdots, u_n @@ the hypothesis, for every list of n + 1 coefficients
\textstyle\sum_{k \le S(n)} u_k x^k = \sum_{k \le n} u_k x^k + \htmlClass{new}{u_{S(n)}\, x^{S(n)}} @@ sum-one-more: split off the last term
\text{slope} = \textstyle\sum_{k \lt n} S(k)\, u_{S(k)}\, x^k + \htmlClass{new}{u_{S(n)}\, S(n)\, x^n} @@ slopes-add; the hypothesis; constant-factor for slopes and power for slopes
= \textstyle\sum_{k \lt S(n)} S(k)\, u_{S(k)}\, x^k \quad \blacksquare @@ sum-one-more again, with turn-about for times on ℝ; induction (4c)

So the slope function of a polynomial is a polynomial. Suppose n ≥ 1 and u_n ≠ 0. Then its leading coefficient n · u_n is not 0 (zero-product: n names a real other than 0, by 8d's naming line). So p′ has degree n − 1 (9g's words). Every polynomial is differentiable, in 13a's word.

p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1 @@ 12b's and 12c's polynomial
p'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 @@ polynomial-slope: 3 · x² from x³ (power for slopes), −3 from −3x (slope-of-a-line), 0 from 1 (constant-has-slope-0)
p'(1) = 0,\ \ p'(-1) = 0,\ \ p'(2) = \htmlClass{new}{9} @@ substitute for the expression
q(x) = 2x^3 - x + 5,\ \ q'(x) = 6x^2 - 1,\ \ q'(-1) = 5 @@ polynomial-slope
(x - 3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9,\ \ \text{slope } 2x - 6 @@ square-of-a-difference, then polynomial-slope. The next section reaches it another way

The paper of 1684 that 13c5 told of gave the differences of sums, products, quotients and powers, stated with no proofs, in his d-marks. Readers complained the paper was a riddle. This step and the last have proved the ones for sums, constants, products and whole powers from 13a's definition, each once.

13d4The slope of the slope

Stands on 13d3.

A slope function is a function, and it may have slopes of its own.

From here on, for a function f, f″ names (f′)′: the slope of the slope, the derivative of f′, on the inputs at which f′ has a slope. Read it “f double prime”. It is called the second derivative of f, and f″(w) the second derivative of f at w. The two primes are one mark, written as 13a's prime twice. For a function given by a formula, (formula)″ is read the same way.

p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1:\ \ p'(x) = 3x^2 - 3,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{p''(x) = 6x} @@ polynomial-slope twice
p''(1) = 6,\ \ p''(-1) = -6,\ \ p''(0) = 0 @@ substitute for the expression
(x^2)'' = (2x)' = 2 @@ slope-of-x², then slope-of-a-line
(mx + b)'' = (m)' = 0 @@ slope-of-a-line, then constant-has-slope-0: a linear function's slope never changes, so its second slope is 0
(x^n)'' = n(n - 1)\, x^{n - 2}\ \ (n \ge 2) @@ power for slopes twice, with constant-factor for slopes

What the second slope says about a graph is rung 14's question. Here it is only a slope of a slope, computed by the same rules.

13d5Lifting the graph, and displacing the input

Stands on 13d3 and 9e (pairs moved up and across).

9e read two changes of a function in pairs. f(x) + k has the pair (u, v + k) exactly when f has (u, v). f(x − s) has the pair (u + s, v) exactly when f has (u, v). Slopes follow the second and ignore the first.

The lifting technique, above: f(x) + k has the same slope as f at every input where f has one. The chords are lifted along with the graph and keep their slopes: the k cancels in every rise. Adding k to every output is called a vertical shift of the graph.

Displaced-slopes technique: for a fixed real s, let g(x) = f(x − s). If f has a slope at w − s, then g has a slope at w, and g′(w) = f′(w − s). Passing from f(x) to f(x − s) is called a horizontal shift of the graph by s; rung 20 makes both shifts moves of the plane.

f \text{ defined on } (w - s - r,\ w - s + r) \;\Rightarrow\; g \text{ defined on } (w - r, w + r) @@ x − s is in f's interval exactly when x is in g's (adding-keeps-answers, read in ℝ)
\dfrac{g(w + h) - g(w)}{h} = \dfrac{f(\htmlClass{new}{(w - s) + h}) - f(w - s)}{h} @@ substitute for the expression: (w + h) − s = (w − s) + h, by regroup for plus on ℝ and turn-about for plus on ℝ
\text{the chord slope of } g \text{ at } w = \text{the chord slope of } f \text{ at } w - s @@ the same real, at every h with 0 < |h| < r
g'(w) = \htmlClass{keep}{f'(w - s)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same limit

So (x − 3)² has slope 2(w − 3) at w: slope-of-x² at w − 3. That agrees with polynomial-slope's 2w − 6 above, as it must. Read in pairs: the slope function of f(x − s) has the pair (u + s, m) exactly when f′ has (u, m). The displacement pattern x − s rides along unchanged, from the function to its slopes. Rung 20 studies such changes of a whole picture; here they are techniques about pairs.

13d7Running it backwards

Stands on 13d1 to 13d5.

Handed a slope function, find a function that has it. p′(x) = 3x² + 4x − 1 is the slope function of x³ + 2x² − x, by polynomial-slope read backwards. Raise each power by one and divide by the new power. Adding any constant gives another with the same slopes (lifting); whether those are all is rung 14's question. Handed a slope at a displaced input, undo the displacement: (x + 2)³ has slope 3(w + 2)² at w, by displaced-slopes with s = −2. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The paper of 1684 is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s “Nova methodus”. The astronomer of question 13d.4.4 is Johannes Kepler, whose book on wine barrels is the Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum (1615).

Leibniz’s first paper on the calculus gave the rule for powers in a line: the change of x to a power n is n times x to the power one less, times the change of x. He gave almost no proof, and readers complained the paper was a riddle.
