21e · proof

Flips and the complement

Flip the circle over the floor, and a walk anticlockwise becomes the same walk clockwise. The across stays the same, and the up changes sign. Flip it across the diagonal, and east and north trade places, so the cosine of an angle becomes the sine of what is left of the quarter turn. These flips give, almost for free, how cosine and sine behave when an angle is reversed, taken away from a quarter turn or a half turn, or pushed on by a half turn.

Reversing an angle keeps its cosine and changes the sign of its sine. The cosine of an angle is the sine of what is left of the quarter turn. Each rule of this kind is a flip of the circle.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 21d (turning adds arcs), 20g (flips reverse which way round), 20c (flips), 9e (even and odd functions). 21f looks at tiny angles.

21e1Reversing an angle

Stands on 21d1 (paths keep length) and 20g3 (flips reverse which way round).

Reversal technique: cos(−θ) = cos θ and sin(−θ) = −sin θ. The flip over the floor carries the arc of length θ from (1, 0) to an arc of the same length from (1, 0). It reverses which way round, so the image arc runs clockwise: it ends at the point at −θ.

F_0(P_\theta) = P_{-\theta} @@ same length (21d1), the other way round (20g3)
F_0(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = (\cos\theta, -\sin\theta) @@ F₀(x, y) = (x, −y)
\htmlClass{new}{\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta,\ \ \sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta} \quad \blacksquare

In 9e’s words: cos is even and sin is odd. Then tan(−θ) = −tan θ: tan is odd too.

21e2Differences

Stands on 21e1 and 21d3 (the sum technique).

Difference technique: cos(A − B) = cos A cos B + sin A sin B, and sin(A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B. Write A − B as A + (−B) and use the reversal technique on −B.

\cos(A + (-B)) = \cos A\cos(-B) - \sin A\sin(-B) @@ the sum technique (21d3)
\htmlClass{new}{\cos(A - B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B} @@ reversal
\htmlClass{new}{\sin(A - B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same, for sin

21e3The complement

Stands on 21e1, 21b4 (the flip across y = x) and 20g3.

Complement technique: cos(π/2 − θ) = sin θ and sin(π/2 − θ) = cos θ. The flip across y = x swaps (1, 0) and (0, 1), keeps lengths and reverses which way round. So the arc of length θ anticlockwise from (1, 0) goes to the arc of length θ clockwise from (0, 1), which ends at the point at π/2 − θ.

F(x, y) = (y, x),\ \ F(1, 0) = (0, 1) = P_{\pi/2} @@ the flip across y = x (20·cb9)
F(P_\theta) = P_{\pi/2 - \theta} @@ θ back from π/2
F(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = (\sin\theta, \cos\theta) @@ swap
\htmlClass{new}{\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) = \sin\theta,\ \ \sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) = \cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare

The co in cosine is this: the cosine of θ is the sine of the complement, what θ lacks of a quarter turn.

21e4Half turns

Stands on 21e1, 21d2 (turning adds arcs) and 20c (flips and turns).

Half-turn technique: cos(π − θ) = −cos θ and sin(π − θ) = sin θ; cos(θ + π) = −cos θ and sin(θ + π) = −sin θ. The first pair is the flip across the upright axis. The second is the half turn, which by 21d2 adds π to every arc.

F_{\text{up}}(x, y) = (-x, y),\ \ F_{\text{up}}(P_\theta) = P_{\pi - \theta} @@ it swaps (1, 0) and (−1, 0), reversing
R_{(-1, 0)}(x, y) = (-x, -y) = R_\pi,\ \ R_\pi(P_\theta) = P_{\theta + \pi} @@ (cos π, sin π) = (−1, 0); 21d2
\htmlClass{new}{\tan(\theta + \pi) = \frac{-\sin\theta}{-\cos\theta} = \tan\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ tan repeats every half turn

So every arc’s cos and sin can be read from an arc in the first quarter, up to signs that the quarter fixes (21c2). The first-quarter arc is called the reference angle.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The reversal technique says cosine is even and sine odd; π/2 − θ is the complement of θ and π − θ its supplement; the cofunction identities are cos(π/2 − θ) = sin θ and its partners. The first-quarter arc is the reference angle.

Who said it first

The word cosine is Edmund Gunter's co.sinus (1620), the sine of the complement. In 21e.4.5, Regiomontanus's tables (1460s) used a radius of 60 000 and later ones 10 000 000; Leonhard Euler's Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748) set the radius to 1 and made sine and cosine functions.
