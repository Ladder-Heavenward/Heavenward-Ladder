13h · technique

Powers, constants and sums

Most slopes you will ever compute are of sums of terms, each a constant times a power of the input. Three rules cover them, and rungs 13d to 13f proved each. A power brings its count down in front and loses one from it. A constant factor rides along, and an added constant leaves. A sum goes term by term. One gap remains. The power rule has been proved for whole powers, for negative whole powers away from 0, and for a half, but not for a third or two thirds or any other fraction. This step closes the gap from the definition and the chain rule. Then it puts the rules to work. It rewrites roots and fractions so the rule can see the power, and it refuses the moves that look like these rules and are not.

Rewrite every term as a constant times a power of the input. Bring the power down in front and take one off it. Constant factors ride, added constants leave. A sum goes term by term, and nothing else does.

13h1The rule, and how far it is proved

In its usual form the power rule is (xⁿ)′ = n xⁿ⁻¹: bring the power down in front, and take one off the power. On this ladder each case has its licence.

The fraction case needs 8i's lines. Take a positive real w and a fraction p/q, with p an integer and q a positive integer. Then w^(p/q) names (q√w)^p. Fractional-powers-defined showed that the name does not depend on which pair (p, q) is used for the fraction. So the plan is two links: first the slope of the q-th root, then a chain with the outside r ↦ r^p.

13h2The slope of a q-th root

13e found the slope of √ by the conjugate: (√(y + h) − √y)(√(y + h) + √y) = h, by difference-of-squares. For a cube root, or a q-th root, difference-of-squares needs a longer partner, and 5i's grid of cells supplies it.

Difference-of-powers technique: for reals u and v and every number q ≥ 1, u^q − v^q = (u − v) · Σ_{j<q} u^(q−1−j) v^j = (u − v)(u^(q−1) + u^(q−2)v + ⋯ + v^(q−1)).

q = 1:\ \ u - v = (u - v)\cdot u^0 v^0 @@ the sum has one term, u⁰v⁰ = 1 (power-zero); times-one on ℝ
u^{q+1} - v^{q+1} = u\,(u^q - v^q) + \htmlClass{new}{v^q (u - v)} @@ add and take away u · v^q (negative on ℝ, plus-zero on ℝ), then spread on ℝ turned about; power-of-a-next
= u\,(u - v)\textstyle\sum_{j \lt q} u^{q-1-j}v^j + v^q (u - v) @@ the induction hypothesis
= (u - v)\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j \lt q} u^{q-j}v^j + v^q\Big) @@ spread on ℝ turned about; factor-out brings u inside: u · u^(q−1−j) = u^(q−j)
= (u - v)\textstyle\sum_{j \lt q + 1} u^{q-j}v^j \quad \blacksquare @@ sum-one-more: v^q is the term at j = q, since u⁰ = 1; induction

With q = 2 it is difference-of-squares: u² − v² = (u − v)(u + v). With q = 3 it is u³ − v³ = (u − v)(u² + uv + v²). Multiply back by the grid of cells to check: the cells u · uv and −v · u² cancel, and so on along the row, leaving u³ − v³.

Now the root. Fix a number q ≥ 1 and a real w > 0, and keep |h| < w, so that w + h > 0 and the q-th root is defined on the open interval (0, 2w) around w. Write u = q√(w + h) and v = q√w. Both are positive, u^q = w + h and v^q = w (8f's line), and u ≠ v, because their q-th powers differ (powers-keep-order turned back).

Root-continuity technique: a q-th root lands where it is headed. Precisely: for w > 0, |q√(w + h) − q√w| ≤ |h|/v^(q−1) whenever |h| < w. So q√(w + h) → q√w as h → 0. By difference-of-powers, |h| = |u^q − v^q| = |u − v| · S, where S = Σ_{j<q} u^(q−1−j)v^j. S is a sum of positive reals, at least its last term v^(q−1). So |u − v| ≤ |h|/v^(q−1), and 10d's squeezed-by-distance, with C = 1/v^(q−1), sends u to v.

Root-slope technique: for every number q ≥ 1 and every real w > 0, x ↦ q√x has a slope at w, and it is (1/q) · w^(1/q − 1).

\dfrac{\sqrt[q]{w + h} - \sqrt[q]{w}}{h} = \dfrac{u - v}{u^q - v^q} @@ h = (w + h) − w = u^q − v^q: substitute for the expression
= \dfrac{u - v}{(u - v)\,S} @@ difference-of-powers, with S = Σ_{j<q} u^(q−1−j) v^j
= \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{1}{S}} \quad (h \ne 0) @@ cancel u − v (fractions-of-rationals i): licensed because u ≠ v when h ≠ 0
u \to v \text{ as } h \to 0 @@ root-continuity
S \to \textstyle\sum_{j \lt q} v^{q-1-j} v^j = \htmlClass{new}{q\,v^{q-1}} @@ limit-passes-inside (11c): t ↦ Σ t^(q−1−j) v^j is a polynomial, continuous at v (polynomials-are-continuous); each term is v^(q−1) (powers-add-their-counts)
\text{slope} = \dfrac{1}{q\,v^{q-1}} @@ reciprocal-limit (10c): q v^(q−1) ≠ 0
= \tfrac1q\, w^{-(q-1)/q} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac1q\, w^{\frac1q - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ v = w^(1/q) and fraction-exponent (8i): v^(q−1) = w^((q−1)/q)

With q = 2 this is slope-of-√ again: ½ w^(−½) = 1/(2√w). With q = 3 and w = 8: v = 2, S heads to 3 · 2² = 12, and the slope of ∛ at 8 is 1/12. Its chord slope at h = 0.001 is (∛8.001 − 2)/0.001 ≈ 0.0833299, against 1/12 ≈ 0.0833333.

13h3Every fraction

Fraction-power technique for slopes: the power rule is true for every fraction power, at every positive input. Precisely: take a fraction p/q, with p an integer and q a positive integer, and a real w > 0. Then the function x ↦ x^(p/q) on (0, ∞) has slope (p/q) · w^(p/q − 1) at w.

x^{p/q} = \big(\sqrt[q]{x}\big)^p @@ 8i's line for fractional powers: a chain, inside r = q√x, outside r ↦ r^p
r_0 = \sqrt[q]{w} \gt 0,\ \ \text{inside's slope } \tfrac1q w^{\frac1q - 1} @@ root-slope
\text{outside's slope at } r_0:\ \ p\,r_0^{\,p-1} @@ power for slopes if p ≥ 1; negative-powers if p ≤ −1 (r₀ ≠ 0); constant-has-slope-0 if p = 0, where both sides are 0
\text{slope} = p\,r_0^{\,p-1}\cdot \tfrac1q\, w^{\frac1q - 1} @@ chain for slopes (13e): rates through a chain multiply
= \tfrac{p}{q}\, w^{\frac{p-1}{q}}\, w^{\frac1q - 1} @@ r₀^(p−1) = (q√w)^(p−1) = w^((p−1)/q): 8i's line
= \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{p}{q}\, w^{\frac{p}{q} - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ fraction-exponent: (p − 1)/q + 1/q − 1 = p/q − 1

So for every fraction n and every w > 0, the slope of xⁿ at w is n w^(n−1). For a whole n this is so at every real, and for a negative whole n at every real but 0. Fractional-powers-defined made the name x^(p/q) honest whichever pair is used. The answer (p/q)w^(p/q − 1) depends only on the fraction, so the slope is honest too. Worked: x^(2/3) at 8 has slope ⅔ · 8^(−1/3) = ⅔ · ½ = ⅓. x^(3/2) at 4 has slope (3/2) · 4^(1/2) = 3. x^(−1/2) at 4 has slope −½ · 4^(−3/2) = −1/16.

Odd roots of negatives, 8i's other line, follow by one more chain. For odd q and w < 0, q√x = −q√(−x). So the inside is −x, with slope −1, and the outside is −q√r at r₀ = −w > 0. The slope is −(1/q)(−w)^(1/q − 1) · (−1) = (1/q)(−w)^(1/q − 1). So ∛ has slope 1/12 at −8 as at 8. At 0 it has none, by 13e's undoing's-slope turned about.

13h4First, make the power visible

The rule fires only on the shape c · xⁿ. Roots and fractions hide the power, so the first move is always to rewrite, with 8i's whole-exponent and fraction-exponent and 5h's fractions-of-rationals.

\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2},\quad \dfrac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3} @@ 8i's lines: a square root is the power ½; one over a power is the negative power
\sqrt[3]{x^2} = x^{2/3},\quad \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2} @@ the root's order goes on the bottom of the fraction, the power on top (x > 0)
x\sqrt{x} = x^1 \cdot x^{1/2} = \htmlClass{new}{x^{3/2}} @@ fraction-exponent: same base, the powers add
\dfrac{5}{2x^4} = \tfrac52\, x^{-4} @@ the 2 is under the bar but not under the power: (5/2) · (1/x⁴)
\dfrac{5}{(2x)^4} = \dfrac{5}{16x^4} = \tfrac{5}{16}\, x^{-4} @@ power-of-a-product: here the 2 is under the power and is raised with it

Brackets decide. The tempting illegal move here is to take the slope of a bottom where it stands: “the slope of 1/x³ is 1/(3x²)”. That is no rule. Rewrite, x⁻³, and the slope is −3x⁻⁴ = −3/x⁴. A sign check catches the slip. The chord of 1/x³ through 1 and 2 has slope (⅛ − 1)/1 = −⅞, so the graph is not climbing there, and 1/(3x²) is positive.

13h5Constants ride, added constants leave

13d's constant-factor for slopes says a constant factor comes out: (cf)′ = cf′, because c multiplies every chord slope. Lifting says an added constant changes no slope: (f + k)′ = f′, because k is in the old value and the new, and leaves in every subtraction. A constant factor rides along, and an added constant leaves.

Three slips live here. A constant that looks as if it changes: (√2)³ is one real, 2√2 ≈ 2.83, so its slope is 0, not 3(√2)² = 6. Ask of every letter whether it changes when x does. A constant inside the power: (3x)² is 9x², with slope 18x. Writing 2 · 3x = 6x lets the 3 ride as if it were outside. Rewrite first; 13j says what a multiple inside does in general. And a letter in the high place: 2ˣ is not a power of x, since its base stays put and its power moves. The power rule needs the opposite. 8i gave 2ˣ a value only at fractions x, and its slope waits for rung 17.

13h6Sums term by term, and only sums

Slopes-add says slopes add, so a sum or difference goes one term at a time. With constant-factor for slopes and the power rule, every polynomial falls in one pass, which is 13d's polynomial-slope, and so does every sum of powers.

y = 4x^3 - 5x^2 + 3x - 7 @@ four terms, each a constant times a power
y' = 4 \cdot \htmlClass{new}{3x^2} - 5 \cdot \htmlClass{new}{2x} + 3 \cdot \htmlClass{new}{1} - \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ power for slopes on each power; constant-factor for slopes; lifting for the 7; slopes-add
= 12x^2 - 10x + 3 @@ regroup for times on ℝ
y'(1) = 12 - 10 + 3 = \htmlClass{keep}{5} @@ then substitute: the slope first, the input second
y = 3\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{2}{x} + \sqrt[3]{x^2} = 3x^{1/2} - 2x^{-1} + x^{2/3} @@ rewrite into powers (x > 0)
y' = \tfrac32 x^{-1/2} + 2x^{-2} + \tfrac23 x^{-1/3} @@ fraction-power for slopes, negative-powers, constant-factor for slopes, slopes-add
y'(1) = \tfrac32 + 2 + \tfrac23 = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{25}{6}} @@ every power of 1 is 1

Always take the slope first and put in the input second. Put x = 1 in first and you have the constant 4 − 5 + 3 − 7 = −5, whose slope is 0.

Term by term works for sums and nothing else. A product or a quotient is not taken factor by factor, and that is the commonest slip of all. For (x² + 1)/x, “slope of the top over slope of the bottom” gives 2x/1. But no quotient rule is needed: 5h's fractions-of-rationals iii, read backwards, splits a fraction over one bottom into a sum.

y = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x} \quad (x \ne 0) @@ a quotient: no term-by-term rule
= \dfrac{x^2}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x} @@ fractions-of-rationals iii backwards: a sum over one bottom splits
= \htmlClass{new}{x + x^{-1}} @@ cancel; rewrite into powers
y' = 1 - x^{-2} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{x^2} @@ term by term: slope-of-a-line, negative-powers, slopes-add. At 2: ¾, not 2x = 4

13h7Letters for constants

The rules do not care whether a constant is a numeral or a letter, so long as it does not change when x does. That lets one column work for a whole family at once.

y = tx^2 + bx + c \quad (t \ne 0) @@ t, b, c reals kept fixed
y' = \htmlClass{new}{2tx + b} @@ t rides on 2x, b rides on 1, c leaves (constant-factor for slopes, lifting, slopes-add)
2tx + b = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; x = -\dfrac{b}{2t} @@ adding-keeps-answers, multiplying-keeps-answers: where the slope is 0

8i's completing the square found the same input another way: tx² + bx + c = t(x − s)² + k with s = −b/(2t). Here s is 8i's displacement, renamed, since h is a change on this rung. That is the input where the completed form takes its least value (t > 0) or its greatest (t < 0). Two roads, one input. Why a slope of 0 should mark a least or greatest value is rung 14's question; here the completed form already showed it. And c is missing from the slope: lifting the function never moves that input.

13d's displaced-slopes carries the displacement pattern through. (x − 3)² has slope 2(x − 3), (x − 3)³ has slope 3(x − 3)², and √(x − 3) has slope ½(x − 3)^(−1/2) for x > 3. The x − 3 rides along unchanged. A multiple inside is another matter: (2x − 3)² = 4x² − 12x + 9 has slope 8x − 12 = 4(2x − 3), not 2(2x − 3). 13j says why.

13h9Running it backwards

Undo the power rule: given the slope x^m, what had it? Forward, the power dropped by one and the old power came down in front. So go up by one and divide by the new power. x^(m+1)/(m + 1) has slope (m + 1)x^m/(m + 1) = x^m, by fraction-power for slopes and constant-factor for slopes. That is true for every fraction m other than −1, on x > 0; for a whole m ≥ 0 it is true on every real. Any constant may be added, by lifting; whether those are all the answers is rung 14's question, as 13d said. Worked: slope 6x² + 2x comes from 2x³ + x²; slope √x from ⅔x^(3/2); slope 1/x² from −1/x.

One slope has no answer here. m = −1 asks you to divide by 0. And no power of x has slope x⁻¹. Suppose (xⁿ)′ = n x^(n−1) were x⁻¹ at every w > 0. Then n x^(n−1) and x⁻¹ would agree at 1, so n = 1. And then the slope is 1, which is not 1/x at 2. Refuted. Rung 17 builds the function that fills the hole.

Who said it first

Thompson notes that a mere multiplication by a constant reappears as a mere multiplication after the slope is taken, and that a constant added on contributes nothing to the growth and drops out of the result.

Thompson, Calculus Made Easy, 2nd ed. (1914), ch. V

In question 13h.4.4 the book of 1656 is John Wallis’s Arithmetica infinitorum; the rival is Pierre de Fermat; the young reader is Isaac Newton, and his rule is the binomial series.
