Can a Trap Be Escaped by Playing It Again?

Can a trap be escaped by playing it again? 7·econ’s prisoners each did better by telling, whatever the other did, and both ended worse off. Only a promise with teeth got them out. But two shops on one street meet the same choice every week, for years. Next week can punish this week. This page counts a stream of weekly profits as one sum with 22’s geometric sums, finds how much the future must count for a promise to keep itself, shows why a known last week undoes it all, and asks which ways of punishing work best.

Write the weekly table. Let each later week count δ times the week before, so a stream of the same profit p every week is worth p/(1 − δ). Compare keeping the promise forever with breaking it once and being punished ever after. Work back from a known last week as in 4·econ. Look for a punishment that forgives.

23econ1A Trap Every Week

Evita and Joy run the two cafés on a street. Each Monday each sets her prices high or cuts them. Here are the week’s profits, in hundreds of dollars.

Joy highJoy cuts
Evita highEvita 3, Joy 3Evita 0, Joy 5
Evita cutsEvita 5, Joy 0Evita 1, Joy 1

Played once, it is 7·econ’s trap: cutting is better whatever the other does, 5 > 3 and 1 > 0, and both cut and make 1. Both high would give each 3. From here on, on this page, δ is how much a week counts against the week before, between 0 and 1: the chance that the street is still there next week, or plain patience.

23econ2A Stream of Weeks as One Number

A stream of the same profit p every week, this week first, is worth this sum.

p + p\delta + p\delta^2 + \cdots @@ each week counted by δ again
= p/(1 - \delta) @@ the endless-ratio-sum technique (15i), 0 ≤ δ < 1
\delta = 0.9,\ p = 3:\ 3/0.1 = 30 @@ high prices forever
\htmlClass{new}{\text{from next week on: } p\delta/(1 - \delta)} @@ the same stream, a week later

The sum settles because δ < 1, as 22d requires of any endless sum. A week far off counts for almost nothing, but together all the weeks after this one can outweigh it.

23econ3The Promise That Keeps Itself

Each says: I keep prices high as long as you do; if you ever cut, I cut every week after. Should Evita cut this week? She gains 5 now, then 1 every week after, against 3 every week.

\text{keep: } \dfrac{3}{1 - \delta} @@ the stream of 3s
\text{break: } 5 + \dfrac{\delta}{1 - \delta} @@ 5 now, then 1s from next week (23econ2)
3 \ge 5(1 - \delta) + \delta @@ keep is no worse: multiply by 1 − δ > 0
\htmlClass{new}{\delta \ge \tfrac12} @@ the promise holds when next week counts at least half

If Evita keeps it whenever Joy does, and the same for Joy, the pair is stuck in 7·econ’s sense, with no court to enforce it. Repeat technique: when the same trap is met again and again, a promise to keep the good pair, with the threat of the bad pair forever after, keeps itself when the future counts for enough.

23econ4A Known Last Week Undoes It

Say both know the street will be torn down after week 52. Work back from the end, as in 4·econ.

\text{week } 52:\ \text{no week after to punish} @@ it is the one-week trap
\text{so both cut in week } 52, \text{ whatever happened before} @@ cutting is better either way
\text{week } 51:\ \text{week } 52 \text{ is fixed, so } 51 \text{ is a one-week trap too} @@ the threat about week 52 is empty
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both cut every week, from the first}} @@ the last week unravels all the rest

With a last week both know, the promise falls apart from the end backwards; it needs a future with no known end. A chance δ of another week every week is such a future, however long the street in fact lasts.

23econ5Punishments That Forgive

Punishing forever is harsh: one misread price list, and both cut for good. A gentler rule: do what the other did last week. It starts high, answers a cut with a cut, and returns to high when the other does.

\text{week } 1:\ \text{Joy cuts by mistake} @@ Evita high
\text{forever-rule: both cut from week 2 on} @@ the mistake is never undone
\text{copy-rule: Evita cuts, Joy high; then Evita high, Joy cuts; \ldots} @@ the mistake echoes back and forth
\htmlClass{new}{\text{copy, but now and then forgive a cut}} @@ the echo dies out

A rule that is kind first, answers a cut, and forgives soon does well against many others; one that forgives too much invites cuts. In contests where hundreds of rules played each other again and again, the copy-rule and its forgiving cousins came out near the top.

Accepted names

A game met again and again is a repeated game; δ is the discount factor. Punishing forever is the grim trigger, and doing what the other did last week is tit for tat; forgiving now and then is generous tit for tat. The unravelling from a known end is backward induction in a finitely repeated game. That many outcomes can be kept up when δ is near 1 is the folk theorem.

Who said it first

Robert Axelrod ran the contests of rules by computer around 1980, and tit for tat, sent in by Anatol Rapoport, won both rounds. Axelrod wrote them up in The Evolution of Cooperation, 1984.
