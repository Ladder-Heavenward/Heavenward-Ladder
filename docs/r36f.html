36f · proof

Colour Spaces Compared

The eye reaches a region of colours; three chosen lights reach a smaller one. This step draws both as 34’s regions, finds the one straight edge of the eye’s region, which is the purples, and says exactly which colours three lights miss and in which direction. The difference between the two regions is the rung’s known exception rate.

The eye’s region is the set of all triples col(S). Three primaries reach the mixes of their three triples, a triangle. The triangle lies inside the region and does not fill it. A colour outside the triangle is written as the nearest colour inside, and the error is in a known direction.

36f1The Eye’s Region

From here on, the eye’s region G is the set of all triples col(S) for spectra S on W. G is 34’s kind of set: bounded (every coefficient is at most the area of the curve times the greatest amount), connected (col is continuous in S), and convex: a mix of two colours in G is in G, since col(S + T) = col(S) + col(T) and S + T is a spectrum. Its accepted name, drawn at fixed brightness, is the chromaticity diagram, and its shape is a horseshoe.

Convexity technique: G is convex. Proof: for c = col(S), d = col(T) and k in [0, 1], k·c + (1 − k)·d = col(kS + (1 − k)T) by three-numbers, and kS + (1 − k)T is a spectrum by 36a3.

36f2Three Primaries

Choose three colours p, q, r in G, not on one line through (0, 0, 0). From here on, their gamut is the set of mixes u·p + v·q + w·r with u, v, w ≥ 0, and the gamut triangle is the set of those with u + v + w = 1. The triangle is 18a7’s plane through p, q, r cut down to the part between them, 18a5’s dividing of segments done twice; the gamut is that triangle scaled through every brightness.

Gamut technique: the gamut triangle is convex and lies inside G. Proof: a mix of mixes is a mix (5d5, the spreading rule), and G is convex (36f1) and contains p, q and r.

Changing primaries is 27d’s grid: nine numbers that send one triple of primaries to another, and every colour’s coordinates along.

36f3The Straight Edge

G’s boundary (34’s word) is the curve of spikes, bowed, joined by the segment of purples, straight. The straight part is the only straight part, and it is straight because it is a chord: the mixes of the two ends of W, and nothing in between. So the one straight edge of what the eye sees is exactly the set of colours the band does not have.

Straight-edge technique: the boundary of G meets a straight segment in more than two points only along the purples. Proof: the curve of spikes is laid down bowed (36c3), and a bowed curve meets a line in isolated points; the purples are a segment by construction.

36f4Outside the Gamut

A colour in G and outside the gamut cannot be mixed from the three primaries with non-negative amounts. Write it anyway: from here on, the gamut name of a colour c is the point of the gamut triangle nearest to c, by 18a2’s distance, found as 18c4 finds the nearest point on a line and 13·stat finds the line that fits best: the least-squares point. The error, c minus its gamut name, points outward from the triangle, toward G’s boundary: the missed colour is always more saturated than its name, by 36e1, and its hue is kept as nearly as the triangle’s edge allows.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

Three primaries name every colour inside their triangle exactly and every colour outside it by its nearest point, and the error is in a known direction: outward, toward saturation, strongest at the greens and violets where G bows furthest from any chord. The rule holds inside the triangle and fails at its edge, and the failure is tabulated.

The accepted names

The eye’s region drawn at fixed brightness is the chromaticity diagram, and its curved edge the spectral locus. The gamut keeps its accepted name; a screen’s three primaries and their triangle make its colour gamut, as in the standard sRGB triangle. The gamut name is gamut mapping by the nearest point, or clipping toward the triangle; clamping each triple coordinate on its own is the crude form. Changing primaries is a change of basis, a 3 × 3 matrix.

Who said it first

James Clerk Maxwell showed in 1860, by matching with a spinning top and a colour box, that three lights match any colour when one light may be moved to the other side of the match, that is, given a negative amount. William David Wright and John Guild made the matching experiments of the late 1920s from which the International Commission on Illumination (CIE) drew the horseshoe in 1931. The standard sRGB primaries were set in 1996.

A proof that is not allowed

“G is a horseshoe. A horseshoe has no corners. So no three primaries can reach any part of its boundary.” Not allowed: the gamut technique puts the triangle inside G, and its corners p, q and r may be chosen on the boundary itself, as spikes. The triangle then meets the boundary at its corners, and along an edge only when two corners are the two ends of the purples (36f3).
