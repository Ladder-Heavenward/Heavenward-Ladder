7a · proof

The either-way principle

This step lays out the principle the rung is about and studies it before anything rests on it. It states the principle in words and in marks, and says what it adds to rung 1's techs and what it does not. Then it proves three things about it with rung 1's techs alone. No instance of it can ever be refuted. Laid down for every statement, it is the same principle as the missing step from a double not back to the plain statement. And, by building a world, rung 1's techs cannot prove it. The proof of the principle itself is 7c's. Here it is the principle in question: no line in this step cites it as a reason, except as the hypothesis of an if.

An or with no side in hand: the statement, or not the statement. It cannot be refuted, it is the same as “a double not gives back the statement”, and rung 1's techs alone can never reach it.

7a1The principle, said plainly

1f gave the principle a name and left it waiting. Here it is in full.

From here on, the either-way principle, written Either-way as in 1f, is the pattern: for any statement P, . Read it “P or not P”. It is a statement with one gap, P. Until 7c proves it, no line cites either-way as a reason; it may appear only as the hypothesis of an if, as below. From 7c on it may be cited: that is where this flag closes.

The or is 1c's generous or, which would allow both sides. Both cannot be true here: from P and ¬P together, 1f's clash technique writes ⊥. So the principle says that at least one side is true, and therefore exactly one. The old logic books put it this way: of a statement and its denial one is so, and a third possibility, a middle, is excluded. The principle's accepted name is the law of excluded middle.

Read the principle against what it does not say. It does not say which side is true. It does not say anyone can find out. Of “Ann is in Paris”, a phone call settles it. Of “some even number above 2 is not a sum of two primes”, nobody can settle it today, and the principle still says one side is true. 1f had a word for the first kind: a statement is settled when you can find its row. For a settled statement the principle is not news. 7b makes the word exact and proves the principle for every statement whose cases can all be checked. The principle is a claim about all the others.

7a2What an or needs, and what the principle gives

On rung 1 an or is made in one way: add an option. From P, write P ∨ Q; from Q, write P ∨ Q. A technique can also hand you an or, but only because its column made one somewhere. 4h's settled-order, for example, made “one number is less than another, or not” by comparing two numbers. So every or you have had so far came with a side in hand, at least in principle: follow the column back and you find which side it came from.

Either-way is an or with no side in hand. Using it is 1c's reason by cases. Given P ∨ ¬P, suppose P and reach R, suppose ¬P and reach R, and R stands. So the principle adds one thing above all: reasoning by cases on any statement, “either it is so or it is not; either way, R”. 4e listed that shape and allowed it only for settled statements. With the principle it is allowed for every statement. What it costs is the address. Suppose a proof by cases on an unsettled P reaches “there is a thing with this property”. Nobody may be able to say which thing, because nobody knows which case is true. 7d shows such a proof.

7a3No instance can be refuted

Before anything leans on the principle, check that it is safe to lean on. Could a column of rung 1's techs ever reach ¬(P ∨ ¬P)? Either-way-unrefuted technique: the either-way principle at any statement cannot be refuted. In marks: for any statement P, ¬¬(P ∨ ¬P), with rung 1's techs alone.

\text{assume } \neg(P \lor \neg P) @@ aiming to refute
\quad \text{assume } P @@ aiming to refute again, inside
\quad P \lor \neg P @@ add an option (1c)
\quad \bot @@ clash with the outer assumption
\neg P @@ refute: the inner supposition closes
P \lor \neg P @@ add an option, on the other side this time
\bot @@ clash with the outer assumption, still open
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\neg(P \lor \neg P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute

Look at what happened. The column could not reach P ∨ ¬P outright, but it showed that denying it collapses. The denial lets you deny P, and then the denial of P puts the or back in your hand. So any column that ever reached ¬(P ∨ ¬P) would also reach ⊥, by either-way-unrefuted and a clash. Laying the principle down cannot make rung 1's techs contradict themselves unless they already did. That is a reassurance, not a proof of the principle. Either-way-unrefuted ends in a double negative, and removing it is exactly the step rung 1 lacks.

7a4The principle gives not-not out

Either-way-gives-not-not-out technique: if a statement is so or not so, then not not that statement gives the statement. In marks: from P ∨ ¬P, the if ¬¬P ⇒ P follows, for the same statement P.

P \lor \neg P @@ given: the law, for this P
\quad \text{assume } \neg\neg P @@ to prove an if
\quad \htmlClass{new}{P} @@ the remaining-option technique (1c): from P ∨ Q and ¬Q, P, with ¬P in the place of Q
\neg\neg P \Rightarrow P \quad \blacksquare @@ prove an if

The remaining-option technique hides a reasoning by cases. Unfolded: in the case P, P; in the case ¬P, ¬P and ¬¬P clash, and when nothing is true, everything is permitted, so P. Only the common line survives.

7a5Not-not out gives the principle

Now the other way. Not-not-out-gives-either-way technique: if ¬¬Q ⇒ Q is so for every statement Q, then P ∨ ¬P is so for every statement P.

\text{let } P \text{ be any statement} @@ speak of anything
\neg\neg(P \lor \neg P) @@ the either-way-unrefuted technique
\neg\neg(P \lor \neg P) \Rightarrow (P \lor \neg P) @@ the given, used with P ∨ ¬P in the place of Q
\htmlClass{new}{P \lor \neg P} \quad \blacksquare @@ carry the if through

There is a catch worth seeing, and it is why not-not-out-gives-either-way is stated for every statement. Either-way-gives-not-not-out goes from the principle at P to not-not out at P. Not-not-out-gives-either-way goes from not-not out at P ∨ ¬P to the principle at P: a different statement. So the two are the same principle when each is laid down for every statement. That is how 7c will prove either-way and how 7d will spend it. For one statement alone, not-not out at P does not give the principle at P (7a5.1a asks you to build one, and the picture can show it). From here on, “the either-way principle” on the ladder means either form, and 7d names the second form when it reads it off.

7a6Why rung 1 could not prove it: a world of stages

3d showed that no proof of infinity exists by building a world where every earlier principle is true and infinity is false; 6d did the same for choice. The same move shows that rung 1's techs never reach either-way. This time the world is not a world of sets but a way of reading statements. It is the picture of those who refuse the principle, made exact: a statement is true once it has been established, and research goes on.

From here on, in this step, a world of stages is a set of stages together with, for each letter, the stages at which it is established. Each stage comes with the stages that come at or after it: every stage comes at or after itself, and a stage after a later stage is after the first. Once established, a letter stays established at every later stage.

This world is a new use of 3d's word. 3d's worlds, collections of things with a reading of ∈, keep their meaning; a world of stages reads the joining words instead.

A statement built from letters with ¬, ∧, ∨, ⇒ and ⊥ is read at a stage s by these rules:

  • a letter is true at s when it is established at s;
  • A ∧ B is true at s when A and B are both true at s; A ∨ B is true at s when A is true at s or B is true at s;
  • A ⇒ B is true at s when, at every stage at or after s at which A is true, B is true too;
  • ¬A is true at s when A is true at no stage at or after s; and ⊥ is true at no stage.

So ¬A is a strong claim: not merely “A is not established yet” but “A will never be”. It agrees with 1d's not-as-if, which read ¬A as A ⇒ ⊥. Two facts make this reading a world in 3d's sense. First, whatever is true at a stage is true at every later stage, because each rule looks only forward. Second, every tech of rung 1 keeps the reading. If the lines a tech cites are true at a stage, with the open suppositions true there too, then the line it writes is true there. Take refute. If at every stage where P is true, ⊥ follows, then P is true at no stage at or after s, which is what ¬P at s says. The other techs check the same way, one rule at a time; 7a.4.1b and 7a.4.1c ask you to check clash and add an option. So a line that a column of rung 1's techs reaches from nothing is true at every stage of every world.

7a7The two-stage world

Now the world that breaks the principle. It has two stages, now and later, and P is established later but not now.

P \text{ at now} @@ fails: not established now
\neg P \text{ at now} @@ false: P is true at later, which is after now
\htmlClass{new}{P \lor \neg P \text{ at now}} @@ false: neither side is true at now
\neg P \text{ at later} @@ false: P is true at later itself
\neg\neg P \text{ at now} @@ true: ¬P is true at no stage at or after now
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\neg P \Rightarrow P \text{ at now}} @@ false: ¬¬P is true at now, P is not

So no column of rung 1's techs proves P ∨ ¬P from nothing about P. Such a column would work just as well with P read as a letter. Then P ∨ ¬P would be true at now in this world, which it does not. The same world kills not-not out. That is the gap of 1b, seen from outside: it was not an oversight, and no cleverer column of rung 1 would have closed it. Take a statement whose fate is fixed at now: established now, or at no stage at all. It satisfies the principle there; that is what settled means in this picture. The principle fails only for statements still open. The picture lets you build worlds of up to four stages and read each of 1f's withheld directions in them.

7a8Constructive and classical

The rules of logic that the refusing side accepts are exactly rung 1's techs, and worlds of stages are a reading in which exactly those techs work. The ladder now names the two kinds of proof.

From here on, a proof is constructive when every or it uses was earned: given, made by adding an option, or reached by a column that does not cite either-way. A proof that cites either-way is classical.

7a9What the principle adds, and what it costs

Everything rung 7 does follows from these few lines. With either-way you get reasoning by cases on any statement, and not-not out, by either-way-gives-not-not-out. With not-not out come the five withheld directions of 1f's rulebook and the withheld direction of 2b's ¬∀ (7d). Then come proof by contradiction for positive statements (7e), and a least member for every set of numbers with a member (7·cb4). And then the last line of 6e's maximal-member column (7·cb6). What it costs is the address. A constructive proof that something exists hands you the thing; a classical one may only show that its absence is totally false.

7a11Running it backwards

Wanting P from ¬¬P, look for either-way at P, and cite either-way-gives-not-not-out. Wanting either-way at P from not-not out, look for not-not out at P ∨ ¬P, and use either-way-unrefuted. Wanting to show a statement is beyond rung 1's techs, build a world of stages where it fails at some stage. P later and not now is the first thing to try; a fork, with one letter on each branch, is the second. Handed a proof that uses cases, ask where its or came from. If you can follow it back to a side, the proof is constructive. If it came from either-way, it is classical, and its conclusion may have no address. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The principle as a fact about how things are is Aristotle’s. Its refusal is L. E. J. Brouwer’s, from early in the century before this one. Arend Heyting, in 1930, wrote down the rules of logic the refusing side accepts, which are rung 1’s techs. Saul Kripke, in 1965, gave worlds of stages as a reading in which exactly those techs work.

Aristotle put it as a fact about how things are, not about what anyone knows: between a statement and its contradictory there can be nothing intermediate, so of any one subject one must either affirm or deny any one predicate.

Aristotle, Metaphysics IV (Γ).7, 1011b23–24, after Ross's translation (1908)

Hilbert, answering Brouwer, is reported to have said that taking the law of excluded middle away from the mathematician would be like forbidding the astronomer his telescope, or the boxer the use of his fists.

attributed to Hilbert, “Die Grundlagen der Mathematik”, lecture in Hamburg (1927)

Accepted names

The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle; the name says that a middle, a third possibility, is excluded. Those who accept only constructive proofs are called intuitionists, and their logic is intuitionistic logic. Worlds of stages are Kripke models.
