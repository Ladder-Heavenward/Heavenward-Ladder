What Do “Every,” “Most” and “Few” Mean?

What do “every”, “most” and “few” mean? A computer that answers questions in plain English has to settle what “every cat is asleep” claims before it can check it. Each of these small words takes two sets, the cats and the sleepers, and makes a statement about how they sit together. This page reads “every”, “some”, “no” and “not every” as claims about two sets, using 2d’s membership and parts; reads “most” by pairing off members, as 3e read bigness; finds where “most” breaks on an endless collection; and finds one thing all of these words share.

Write the first set A and the second B. “Every A is B” says A is a part of B. “Some A is B” says A and B share a member. “No A is B” denies “some”. “Most A are B” says the members of A inside B outnumber those outside, which needs a way to compare bigness. Each word looks only at A, split into the part inside B and the part outside.

3comp21“Every A Is B”: A Inside B

Evita keeps three cats, Serena, Murphy and a kitten, and a list of who is asleep on the sofa. Let A be the set of her cats and B the set of things asleep on the sofa.

From here on, on this page, a two-set word is a word that takes two sets, A and B, and makes a statement. “Every A is B” is the statement that each member of A is a member of B: A is a part of B (2d).

The claim says nothing about what else is in B. If the dog is asleep on the sofa too, “every cat is asleep on the sofa” is unchanged. And if A has no members at all, A is a part of every set, so “every A is B” is true whatever B is: a claim about the empty set is true by default, as 2d’s empty set showed.

3comp22“Some”: A and B Share a Member

“Some cat is asleep on the sofa” claims that at least one cat is asleep there. That is a claim that A and B have a member in common: their meet has a member.

In everyday speech “some cats are asleep” often hints “but not all”. The two-set reading keeps only the claim: if every cat is asleep, “some cat is asleep” is still true. The hint is the speaker’s, added on top; a computer checking claims checks the claim.

“Some” is the only word on this page that reads A and B the same way round: A and B share a member exactly when B and A do. “Every A is B” and “every B is A” are different claims.

3comp23“No” and “Not Every”

“No cat is asleep on the sofa” denies that some cat is. “Not every cat is asleep on the sofa” denies that every cat is. Each is a not (1b) put on a word from the last two footings.

\text{every } A \text{ is } B:\ A \subseteq B @@ step 1
\text{some } A \text{ is } B:\ A \cap B \ne \varnothing @@ step 2
\text{no } A \text{ is } B:\ A \cap B = \varnothing @@ the denial of “some”
\htmlClass{new}{\text{not every } A \text{ is } B:\ \text{some } A \text{ is not } B} @@ push the not inward, as 2j did

The four words sit at the corners of a square. Each word on one corner is the denial of the word at the opposite corner: “every” faces “not every”, and “some” faces “no”. So a computer needs to check only two of them; the other two are their denials.

3comp24“Most”: More In Than Out

“Most of Evita’s cats are asleep” claims that the cats on the sofa outnumber the cats elsewhere. Rung 3 has no counting yet, but it can compare bigness by pairing (3e): pair each cat elsewhere with a different cat on the sofa, and see whether some cat on the sofa is left over.

A \cap B:\ \text{the members of } A \text{ inside } B @@ the cats asleep on the sofa
A \setminus B:\ \text{the members of } A \text{ outside } B @@ the cats elsewhere
\htmlClass{new}{\text{most } A \text{ are } B:}\ A \setminus B \text{ pairs off into part of } A \cap B, @@ each outside member with its own inside one
\qquad \text{and } A \cap B \text{ cannot be paired off into all of } A \setminus B @@ some inside member is always left over

Now try an endless collection. Let A be the run of numbers of 3c and B the even ones. Pair 1 with 0, 3 with 2, 5 with 4, and so on: every odd number gets its own even one. Pair 0 with 1, 2 with 3, and so on: every even gets its own odd. Each side pairs off into all of the other, so neither is left over, and “most numbers are even” and “most numbers are odd” are both false, though neither half is bigger.

Pairing technique: on a collection whose members can be listed to an end, “most A are B” is settled by pairing the members outside B with those inside; on an endless collection, pairing can match the two sides both ways, and “most” loses its everyday meaning. A program asked “are most numbers even?” has to say which measure it uses, or say the question has no answer.

3comp25Words That Look Only at A

Every word on this page shares a habit. To decide “every cat is asleep”, “some cat is asleep”, or “most cats are asleep”, you never look at anything outside A. The sleeping dog makes no difference.

\text{every } A \text{ is } B \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{every } A \text{ is } (A \cap B) @@ B can be cut down to its part inside A
\text{some } A \text{ is } B \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{some } A \text{ is } (A \cap B) @@ the same
\text{most } A \text{ are } B \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{most } A \text{ are } (A \cap B) @@ the pairing used only A ∩ B and A ∖ B
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only } A \text{ are } B\ \not\Leftrightarrow\ \text{only } A \text{ are } (A \cap B)} @@ “only” looks outside A

Two-set technique: “every”, “some”, “no”, “not every” and “most” each make a claim about A and B that depends only on A split into its part inside B and its part outside. “Only cats are asleep on the sofa” claims that B is a part of A, so it looks at the dog. That is one reason linguists say “only” is a different kind of word.

A linguist checking this across languages lists, for each language, every word that does the job of “every”, “some” or “most”, and tests each one with a pair of sentences like the ones above. In every language checked, each such word looks only at A.

Accepted names

Two-set words are determiners or generalized quantifiers. The square of “every”, “some”, “no” and “not every” is the square of opposition. A word that looks only at A, by the test of replacing B with A ∩ B, is conservative, and the claim that every determiner in every language is conservative is called a universal of language.

Who said it first

Aristotle set out the square of opposition. Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper read determiners as relations between two sets in 1981, in “Generalized quantifiers and natural language”, and showed that “most” cannot be written with “every” and “some” alone.
