Why Does Rubber Snap Back?

Why does rubber snap back? A steel spring pulls back because stretching pulls its atoms apart. Rubber is different: its long molecules are not stretched at all, only straightened. Yet it pulls back hard, and, oddly, a stretched rubber band pulls harder when warmed, while a steel spring weakens. This page treats a rubber molecule as a random walk (30n), finds its size grows as the square root of its length, counts how stretching cuts its number of ways to be, with 4k’s counting and 30·phys’s share of energy, finds the pull grows with the stretch, and finds why warm rubber pulls harder.

A rubber molecule is a chain of thousands of links, each pointing any way: a random walk, whose ends sit about √N links apart. A stretched chain has far fewer ways to lie than a relaxed one, and heat jostles it back toward the many ways. The pull is temperature times the rate at which ways are lost per stretch: 3kT x/(Nb²), a spring whose stiffness grows with the heat.

38chem21A Chain as a Random Walk

Claire develops rubber for car tyres. Rubber is a tangle of very long molecules, each a chain of N short links of length b, joined here and there to its neighbours. Each link can swivel freely, so each points in a direction unrelated to the last.

In 2·chem’s terms a link is a named part with two gaps open, one at each end. R had one gap and could only cap a molecule. A part with two can fill one gap with a copy of itself and still have a gap to offer, and so it chains. The part in natural rubber has five carbons and eight hydrogens. The whole chain is written as 2chem9’s condensed formula with a count outside the brackets: (C₅H₈)ₙ. One short formula stands for a molecule of a hundred thousand atoms.

Small molecules chain in two ways. In the first, each has a double bond. The second pair opens, each carbon has a gap free, and each takes its neighbour (42chem27): nothing is lost, and the chain’s formula is the small molecule’s, n times. Rubber and the plastic of bags are made so. In the second, each small molecule has two different named parts, one at each end, and one end of each joins the other end of the next, with a small molecule such as water let go at every join. Nylon is made so, and so is every protein: the acid part of one building block joins the amine of the next (9chem21).

Anexact, yet rigorous · links and parts

The N of this page counts freely swivelling links, and one such link is a few of the chemist’s parts long. N and n are proportional, not equal. 21chem1 has what one swivel costs.

From here on, on this page, the chain’s size is the distance between its two ends, and a way to be is one arrangement of all its links.

38chem22Its Size Grows as the Square Root of Its Length

\text{size}^2 = N b^2 @@ link arrows independent: squares add (30n)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{size} = b\sqrt N} @@ 
N = 10\,000,\ b = 0.5 \text{ nm}:\ \text{length } 5\,000 \text{ nm},\ \text{size } 50 \text{ nm} @@ a hundredth of its length

Chain-size technique: a chain of N freely turning links of length b curls up to a size of b√N, far less than its full length Nb; it can be pulled out a hundred times further before its links themselves are strained. That is why rubber stretches to several times its length: it is only being uncurled.

38chem23Stretching Cuts the Number of Ways to Be

Count the ways to be with the ends a distance x apart along one direction. Of all the random walks of N steps, the share ending at x falls off as a bell curve (30j), so the number of ways falls the same way.

W(x) \propto e^{-3x^2/(2Nb^2)} @@ a bell curve in x, counted by 4k’s choices
S = k\ln W = S_0 - \dfrac{3k\,x^2}{2Nb^2} @@ the log of the ways (30·phys); k Boltzmann’s number
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each stretch cuts the ways to be}} @@

38chem24The Pull Grows With the Stretch

Heat shakes the chain at random, so it wanders into whichever states have most ways, the curled-up ones. Holding it stretched means fighting that wandering. 30·phys found that, at temperature T, the push toward more ways is T times the slope of S.

F = -T\,\dfrac{dS}{dx} = \dfrac{3kT}{Nb^2}\,x @@ a spring: pull in step with stretch
\htmlClass{new}{T = 300 \text{ K}:\ \dfrac{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300}{10^4 \times (0.5 \times 10^{-9})^2} \approx 5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ N/m per chain}} @@ 
\text{a cubic centimetre holds about } 10^{19} \text{ chains: the pulls add} @@

Rubber pulls back not because its bonds are strained but because heat keeps shaking its chains toward their many curled-up ways.

38chem25Warm Rubber Pulls Harder

F \propto T @@ 
300 \text{ K} \to 330 \text{ K}:\ \text{pull rises by } 10\% @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a steel spring softens when warmed; rubber stiffens}} @@

Because rubber’s pull comes from heat shaking its chains, warming a stretched band makes it pull harder and shorten; and stretching a band quickly warms it, as the lost ways turn into heat. Claire hangs a weight from a rubber band and warms the band with a hair dryer: the weight rises. She stretches a thick band quickly against her lip and feels it warm. Tyre makers allow for this stiffening as tyres heat on the road.

38chem27Cold Steel Snaps

Rubber left in deep cold stops snapping back and shatters like glass: its chains can no longer turn (38chem21). Steel has a switch of its own. Warm, it bends under a blow and soaks it up; below a certain temperature, a crack runs through it before it can bend. Of the 4,700 Liberty cargo ships built in the war, 1,250 had brittle cracks by 1953 and twelve broke in two: their steel turned brittle at the temperature of cold North Atlantic water.

The experiment shown: a heavy hammer swings down and breaks a notched bar of the steel, and how high it swings on afterwards gives the energy the break took. The bar is tested boiling, at room temperature and in ice water. Warm bars bend and take a lot; cold bars break clean and take little.

\text{energy to break a notched bar: high when warm, low when cold} @@ the swinging-hammer test
T_t = \text{the temperature where that energy falls halfway} @@ the transition temperature
\htmlClass{new}{\text{passed for the cold} \iff T_{\min} > T_t + m} @@ m is a margin: a check against a threshold, not a rate
\text{story: hook } T_t = -20,\ m = 10,\ \text{yard at } -35:\ \ -35 > -10 \text{ is false} @@ warm the hook or use one rated colder
\text{Liberty ship steel: } T_t \text{ above the temperature of cold sea water} @@ so the hulls worked below their switch

There is nothing to add up and no rate to work out: one temperature is set against another. The work is knowing which parts carry a load in the cold, what each one is rated for, and how cold the place really gets. Steel made today for ships and bridges is tested so that it stays tough in the cold it will meet.

Transition technique: a steel bends and soaks up a blow above its transition temperature and snaps below it, so a part is passed for the cold only if its lowest working temperature stays above its transition temperature by a margin.

Accepted names

The chain of free links is the freely jointed chain; b is the Kuhn length; the pull from lost ways is entropic elasticity, S being entropy; and the warming on stretching and shortening on heating is the Gough-Joule effect. The switch is the ductile-to-brittle transition, and the swinging-hammer test the Charpy impact test.

Who said it first

John Gough noticed the warming of stretched rubber in 1805, and James Joule measured it in 1859; Werner Kuhn and others explained it by chains in the 1930s (not yet revisited for the dates).
