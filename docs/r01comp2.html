Can Sound Change Be Written as Rewrites?

Can sound change be written as rewrites? The English word “father” and the Latin word “pater” started as one word, spoken long ago, and so did “three” and “tres”, “hundred” and “centum”. Over centuries the sounds shifted, a few at a time, each shift happening everywhere its setting came up. A shift like that is a rewrite: a sound becomes another sound wherever a stated condition holds, which is an if built from 1c’s ands and nots. This page writes sound changes as rewrites, finds that two rewrites run in two orders can give two different words, names the two ways one rewrite can help or block another, and keeps the results in a table of words before and after, as 1e kept columns.

A rewrite says: if a sound is X, and its setting is Z, replace it by Y. Run the rewrites on a word one at a time, in a fixed order, each one over the whole word, and write each word’s form after each rewrite in a row of a table. Swap the order and run them again. Where the rows differ, the order mattered.

1comp21A Rewrite Changes a Sound in a Setting

Many speakers in Canada and the northern United States say the vowel of “write” differently from the vowel of “ride”. In “write” it starts higher in the mouth; in “ride” it starts low, as in “eye”. The difference follows a pattern: the higher vowel comes before t, s, k, p and f, and the low one everywhere else.

From here on, on this page, a rewrite is a statement of the form: if a sound is X, and the sound after it (or before it) meets a condition, the sound becomes Y. Write [ai] for the low vowel and [ʌi] for the high one. The condition is built with 1c’s and and or, and its letters are statements about sounds.

letterthe statement it stands for
Vthis sound is [ai]
Tthe next sound is one of t, s, k, p, f (made without the voice)

The vowel rewrite: when V ∧ T, replace [ai] by [ʌi]. In “write” both letters are T, and the vowel changes. In “ride” the next sound is d, made with the voice, so T is F, the and is F, and nothing changes.

A rewrite is a machine in 1e’s sense with one difference: what it hands back is not 1 or 0 but a sound. Its condition is still a statement with a column, and the rewrite acts in exactly the rows where the column is T.

1comp22Two Rewrites, Two Orders, Two Results

The same speakers have a second rewrite. Between two vowels, t and d are both said as a quick tap of the tongue, written [ɾ]: “butter” and “budder” sound alike. Let B stand for “the sound before is a vowel” and A for “the sound after is a vowel”. The tap rewrite: when (it is t ∨ it is d) ∧ B ∧ A, replace it by [ɾ].

Now run both rewrites on the old forms of “writer” and “rider”, which differed only in t against d. First the vowel rewrite, then the tap.

writerrider
startr-ai-t-err-ai-d-er
vowel rewriter-ʌi-t-err-ai-d-er
tap rewriter-ʌi-ɾ-err-ai-ɾ-er

The words end different: the t is gone from “writer”, but it left its mark on the vowel. Now run the tap first.

writerrider
startr-ai-t-err-ai-d-er
tap rewriter-ai-ɾ-err-ai-ɾ-er
vowel rewriter-ai-ɾ-err-ai-ɾ-er

Order technique: two rewrites run in two orders can give two different results, because each rewrite reads the word as the one before it left it. The tap is made with the voice, so once it has replaced the t, T is F and the vowel rewrite never acts. The speakers say “writer” and “rider” differently, so their rewrites run vowel first.

1comp23One Rewrite Feeds Another

Long before English, the speakers of early Germanic made a chain of changes to the language they inherited. Two of them: p, t, k became f, th, h (“pater” against “father”, “tres” against “three”, “centum” against “hundred”), and b, d, g became p, t, k (Latin “decem” against “ten”, “genu” against “knee”).

Call them the breath rewrite (p becomes f, and so on) and the voice rewrite (b becomes p, and so on). Run the voice rewrite first, on a word with a d.

orderstartafter the firstafter the second
voice, then breathdtth
breath, then voiceddt

Run voice first and the t it makes is fresh food for the breath rewrite, which turns it into th. Run breath first and there is no t yet when the breath rewrite looks, so the new t stays a t. Feeding technique: one rewrite feeds another when it makes new places where the other’s condition is T. Germanic words have t where Latin has d, as in “ten”, so the changes ran breath first: in that order the voice rewrite does not feed the breath rewrite.

1comp24One Rewrite Bleeds Another

Look back at step 2. When the tap ran first, it took away the t that the vowel rewrite needed. That is the opposite of feeding.

Bleeding technique: one rewrite bleeds another when it removes places where the other’s condition would have been T. In the tap-first order, the tap bleeds the vowel rewrite in “writer”.

orderwhat the first does to the secondname
tap, then voweltakes away the t the vowel rewrite needsbleeds
vowel, then tapthe vowel rewrite has already actedneither
voice, then breathmakes the t the breath rewrite needsfeeds
breath, then voicethe new t comes too lateneither

Each of the four is read off by asking one question of the condition’s column: did the first rewrite turn some F into T (feeds), or some T into F (bleeds)? Both orders of a pair can be checked this way without knowing any history.

1comp25A Table of Words Before and After

A linguist working out the order of a language’s rewrites keeps a table, one row per word and one column per stage, as 1e kept one column per statement. Each stage is the column before it with one rewrite run over every word.

wordstartvoweltap
writerr-ai-t-err-ʌi-t-err-ʌi-ɾ-er
riderr-ai-d-err-ai-d-err-ai-ɾ-er
writer-ai-tr-ʌi-tr-ʌi-t
rider-ai-dr-ai-dr-ai-d
butterb-u-t-erb-u-t-erb-u-ɾ-er

Table technique: to test an order of rewrites, run each rewrite over every word in turn and compare the last column with what speakers say; one word that comes out wrong rules the order out. With two rewrites there are two orders to test, with three there are six, and the table settles each one by hand.

A linguist at a desk with the five words above, running both orders, gets exactly the speakers’ forms from vowel-then-tap and a wrong form for “writer” from tap-then-vowel. One table, and the order is fixed.

Accepted names

A rewrite is a phonological rule or sound law, and its setting is its environment. The vowel rewrite is Canadian raising, the tap is flapping, and the breath and voice rewrites are two parts of Grimm’s law (the third part turns bh, dh, gh into b, d, g). Feeding and bleeding are the names linguists use; an order where the first rewrite could have fed or bled the second but does not, because it runs too late, is counterfeeding or counterbleeding.

Who said it first

Rasmus Rask set out the matching consonants in 1818, and Jacob Grimm stated the shifts in his German grammar in 1822. Martin Joos described the writer and rider pair in 1942, in “A phonological dilemma in Canadian English”.
