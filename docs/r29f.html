29f · proof

Total curvature counts holes

Take a soft rubber ball and squeeze it into an egg, or a pear, or a peanut. The surface bend K changes everywhere: it grows where the rubber is pinched and shrinks where it flattens. This step totals K over the whole closed surface and finds that the total does not change at all. For every ball-shaped surface it is 4π. For a doughnut it is 0. For a surface with g holes it is 4π(1 − g). A number made of bends, added over a whole surface, can only count its holes.

Total K over a closed surface is 2π times the number of corners minus edges plus faces of any way of cutting it into triangles, and that number is 2 − 2g for a surface with g holes.

29f1The sphere’s total

Total the surface bend over a whole closed surface S: ∬S K dA, with dA = √(EG − F²) du dv from 29d2. Call it the total bend.

Sphere-total technique: a sphere of any radius has total bend 4π.

K = 1/R^2 \text{ at every point} @@ surface-bend (29e3)
\iint_S K\,dA = \tfrac{1}{R^2} \cdot 4\pi R^2 @@ a constant comes out of the total; skin technique (21o)
\htmlClass{new}{= 4\pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ R cancels

A larger sphere bends less, but there is more of it, and the two balance exactly.

29f2Spun surfaces

Spin a curve about the z-axis. Trace the curve at speed 1 as (ρ(u), z(u)), with ρ the distance from the axis. The surface is r(u, v) = (ρ cos v, ρ sin v, z), and its ruler is du² + ρ² dv²: E = 1, F = 0, G = ρ².

Spun-total technique: a spun surface between u = a and u = b has total bend −2π(ρ′(b) − ρ′(a)).

K = -\rho^{\prime\prime}/\rho,\ \ dA = \rho\,du\,dv @@ straight-out (29e5) with g = ρ; ruler-area (29d2)
\iint K\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_a^b -\rho^{\prime\prime}\,du\,dv @@ ρ cancels; either order (28h)
\htmlClass{new}{= -2\pi\big(\rho^{\prime}(b) - \rho^{\prime}(a)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ρ′ undoes ρ″ (16); the v total is 2π
  
\text{ball-shaped: the curve leaves the axis at right angles and returns at right angles} @@ an egg, a pear, a peanut
\rho^{\prime}(a) = 1,\ \ \rho^{\prime}(b) = -1 \Rightarrow \text{total} = 4\pi @@ the surface closes smoothly over each pole
\text{doughnut-shaped: the curve is a closed loop away from the axis} @@ a ring, a tyre, a bagel
\rho^{\prime}(b) = \rho^{\prime}(a) \Rightarrow \text{total} = 0 @@ the curve comes back to where it started, heading the same way

On a doughnut, the outside is bowl-like and K > 0 there. The inside, round the hole, is saddle-like and K < 0. The two exactly cancel, whatever the doughnut’s proportions.

29f3Cutting into triangles

Cut a closed surface into F triangles whose sides are straightest paths (the lines of the surface, 29g), meeting edge to edge, with E edges and V corners in all. On the sphere, a triangle’s total bend is its area over R², which is its excess (29b3). In general this holds too: triangle-bend, a triangle of straightest paths has total bend equal to its excess. 29g proves it. This step holds it in trust until then.

Excess-count technique: if every triangle’s total bend is its excess, the total bend of the surface is 2π(V − E + F).

\iint_S K\,dA = \sum_{\text{triangles}} (\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi) @@ triangle-bend, held in trust to 29g; totals add over pieces (15a)
= \sum \text{every angle} - \pi F @@ F triangles
\sum \text{every angle} = 2\pi V @@ the angles round each corner fill a whole turn
3F = 2E @@ each triangle has three edges, each edge sits in two triangles
2\pi V - \pi F = 2\pi V - 2\pi E + 2\pi F @@ πF = 2πE − 2πF, from the line above
\htmlClass{new}{\iint_S K\,dA = 2\pi(V - E + F)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

Look at what this says. The left side does not depend on how the surface is cut. So neither does V − E + F. And the right side is a count of corners, edges and faces. It cannot change when the surface is squeezed, as long as the cutting is squeezed along with it.

29f4Each hole takes away two

Hole-count technique: a closed surface with g holes has V − E + F = 2 − 2g for every cutting into triangles, and total bend 4π(1 − g).

\text{ball-shaped: } 2\pi(V - E + F) = 4\pi,\ \ V - E + F = 2 @@ excess-count (29f3) and spun-total (29f2) or sphere-total (29f1)
\text{a tube cut into 6 triangles between two triangular ends: } V = 6,\ E = 12,\ F = 6 @@ 3 + 3 end edges, 3 along, 3 across
\text{remove two triangles, glue the tube's ends to their edges} @@ adds a hole
V \mapsto V,\ \ E \mapsto E + 12 - 6,\ \ F \mapsto F - 2 + 6 @@ the tube's 6 corners and 6 end edges are glued to ones already counted
V - E + F \text{ drops by } 6 - 4 = 2 @@ E gains 6, F gains 4
\htmlClass{new}{V - E + F = 2 - 2g,\ \ \iint_S K\,dA = 4\pi(1 - g)} \quad \blacksquare @@ g handles from a ball; excess-count

So the total bend of a doughnut is 0, as 29f2 found from its ruler, and a two-holed pretzel has total bend −4π. Squeeze, stretch or dent a surface as you like. Unless you tear it or glue it, the total bend stays the same. A number made of slopes counts something that slopes cannot see.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The total bend is the total curvature. V − E + F is the Euler characteristic, written χ, and the number of holes g is the genus. The hole-count technique, total curvature = 2πχ, is the Gauss–Bonnet theorem; triangle-bend is its local form. A cutting into triangles is a triangulation.

Who said it first

René Descartes noticed, around 1630, that the shortfalls of the angles at the corners of a solid add to four right angles twice over; Leonhard Euler found V − E + F = 2 for solids in 1752. Simon Antoine Jean L’Huilier saw in 1813 that solids with holes break the rule. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the triangle form in 1827, and Pierre Ossian Bonnet extended it to triangles with curved sides in 1848. The form for whole surfaces with holes came later in the nineteenth century, with Walther von Dyck in 1888.
