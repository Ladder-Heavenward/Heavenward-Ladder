Is There Only One Way to Measure Surprise?

Is there only one way to measure surprise? Rung 30 defined the average surprise of a chance list as Σ p log₂(1/p) and used it. Why that formula and not another? Ask three things of any measure: that it change continuously with the chances, that more equally likely outcomes mean more surprise, and that the surprise of a choice made in two stages be the first stage’s surprise plus the average surprise of the second. Then it is rung 30’s formula times a constant, and nothing else. The proof goes from equal chances to fractions to all chances, by the same adding-from-multiplying that made the log. Said with arrows, the information lost by a map that keeps chances is the one quantity that adds along maps in a row and mixes straight; that form was found in 2011.

Demands: continuity; A(n) = H(1/n, …, 1/n) grows with n; grouping, H(p) = H(group chances) + Σ (group chance) H(within). Then A(mn) = A(m) + A(n), so A(n) = c log n; for chances kᵢ/N, H = −c Σ pᵢ log pᵢ; by continuity for all chances. Arrows: a map f : (X, p) → (Y, q) that keeps chances loses H(p) − H(q) ≥ 0, losses add along a row, and up to a constant this is the only such loss that mixes straight and changes continuously.

inf14.1Three Demands

30·comp.1’s average surprise H(p₁, …, pₙ) = Σ pᵢ log₂(1/pᵢ) was defined, then used. Now go the other way. Ask of any measure H of the surprise of a chance list three things. It changes continuously with the chances. With n equally likely outcomes, A(n) = H(1/n, …, 1/n) grows as n grows. And a choice made in two stages has surprise equal to the first stage’s plus the second stage’s, averaged over how the first came out.

Grouping technique: rung 30’s formula meets the grouping demand: H(¼, ¼, ½) = H(½, ½) + ½ H(½, ½).

H(\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12) = \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 1.5 @@ the formula, in bits
\text{first stage: the pair or the third, } \tfrac12 \text{ each: } H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = 1 @@ group the two quarters
\text{second stage, only if the pair: } \tfrac12 \cdot H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = \tfrac12 @@ which of the two, weighted by the chance of getting there
\htmlClass{new}{1.5 = 1 + \tfrac12} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two stages add up to the whole

inf14.2The Chain Technique of Surprise

For two choices X and Y made one after the other, write H(Y | X = x) for the surprise of Y once x is known, with 30f2’s given chances.

Surprise-chain technique: H(X, Y) = H(X) + Σx p(x) H(Y | X = x), for rung 30’s formula.

p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x) @@ given chance (30f2)
\log \tfrac{1}{p(x, y)} = \log \tfrac{1}{p(x)} + \log \tfrac{1}{p(y \mid x)} @@ the log of a product (17c1)
H(X, Y) = \textstyle\sum_{x, y} p(x) p(y \mid x) \log \tfrac{1}{p(x)} + \sum_{x, y} p(x) p(y \mid x) \log \tfrac{1}{p(y \mid x)} @@ average both sides
\htmlClass{new}{= H(X) + \textstyle\sum_x p(x)\, H(Y \mid X = x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ Σ over y of p(y | x) is 1

The grouping demand is this technique with X the group and Y the member within it. So the formula meets all three demands; the question is whether anything else does.

inf14.3Equal Chances Force the Log

Equal-log technique: if A(mn) = A(m) + A(n) and A grows with n, then A(n) = c log n for a constant c ≥ 0.

A(n^k) = k\,A(n),\ \ A(1) = 0 @@ adding from multiplying, k times; A(1) = A(1) + A(1)
m^j \le n^k < m^{j+1} \Rightarrow j\,A(m) \le k\,A(n) \le (j + 1)\,A(m) @@ A grows with n
j \log m \le k \log n < (j + 1) \log m @@ log grows too
\left|\tfrac{A(n)}{A(m)} - \tfrac{\log n}{\log m}\right| \le \tfrac1k \text{ for every } k @@ divide both by k A(m) and k log m
\htmlClass{new}{A(n) = c \log n,\ c = A(m)/\log m} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gap is below every 1/k, so 0 (m ≥ 2 with A(m) > 0)

Without “grows with n” the step fails: A could give each prime its own number, as in the Moves to Watch Out For footing. The growth demand is what pins A to the log, as 17c1’s log is pinned by adding from multiplying with order.

inf14.4Then Every Chance

Forced-formula technique: the three demands force H(p) = c Σ pᵢ log(1/pᵢ) for every chance list.

A(N) = H(p_1, \dots, p_n) + \textstyle\sum_i p_i\, A(k_i) @@ grouping: first the block, then the member
H(p) = c \log N - \textstyle\sum_i p_i\, c \log k_i @@ the equal-log technique, twice
= c \textstyle\sum_i p_i (\log N - \log k_i) = c \sum_i p_i \log \tfrac{1}{p_i} @@ Σ pᵢ = 1, and log N − log kᵢ = log(N/kᵢ)
\htmlClass{new}{H(p) = c \textstyle\sum_i p_i \log \tfrac{1}{p_i}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for fractions; every chance is a limit of fractions, and H is continuous

So rung 30’s formula is not a choice. With c = 1 and logs to base 2, surprise is counted in bits; any other c is a change of unit.

inf14.5Said With Arrows

Make a category: things are finite sets with chances (X, p), and an arrow f : (X, p) → (Y, q) is a function that keeps chances, q(y) = Σ of p(x) over the x with f(x) = y. Such an f lumps outcomes together and forgets which. Call H(p) − H(q) the information it loses.

Loss-adds technique: the loss of an arrow is at least 0, and the loss of g ∘ f is the loss of f plus the loss of g.

H(p) = H(q) + \textstyle\sum_y q(y)\, H(p \mid f = y) @@ the surprise-chain technique, with Y = f(X)
H(p) - H(q) \ge 0 @@ each H(p | f = y) is at least 0
L(g \circ f) = H(p) - H(r) = (H(p) - H(q)) + (H(q) - H(r)) @@ f to (Y, q), then g to (Z, r)
\htmlClass{new}{L(g \circ f) = L(f) + L(g)} \quad \blacksquare @@ losses add along maps in a row

The 2011 technique reads the whole page in arrows: a loss that adds along a row, that mixes straight (the loss of a chance mixture of two arrows is the same mixture of their losses), and that changes continuously, is c times H(p) − H(q). That is told here, not proved; the three demands of the first footing become three properties of a map of categories into the numbers.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1948 an engineer set out three demands of this kind and showed that they force the formula, in the paper that founded the theory of communication. He said the demands were meant to make the formula plausible, and that its real justification was in what it does.

In 1956 a mathematician showed that the growth demand could be dropped if the measure does not care about the order of the chances; the proof then needs a harder step about functions that turn products into sums. Many other sets of demands followed.

In 2011 three mathematicians recast the demands as properties of information lost by arrows in a category of chance spaces: adding along a row, mixing straight and changing continuously. A 2014 paper did the same for relative surprise.

The accepted names

Average surprise is Shannon entropy; the three demands are Shannon’s axioms, the third the grouping or recursivity property; the chain technique is the chain rule for entropy. A function that keeps chances is a measure-preserving map in the category FinProb; “mixes straight” is convex linearity; the loss-adds technique says information loss is a functor. The family that changes the mixing technique is the Tsallis entropies; relative surprise is relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence). A function turning products into sums is a completely additive function.

Who said it first

Claude Shannon, “A mathematical theory of communication” (1948). Dmitry Faddeev (1956). John Baez, Tobias Fritz and Tom Leinster, “A characterization of entropy in terms of information loss” (Entropy, 2011). John Baez and Tobias Fritz, “A Bayesian characterization of relative entropy” (2014).
