38i · proof

True and Unprovable

The last step built a statement of arithmetic that is reached as equivalent to “I am not reached”. Call it the unreached statement. Suppose the axioms of arithmetic reached it. Then they would also reach that it is reached, since every proof is a number they can check, and they would reach that it is not, since that is what it says. That is a clash, and the axioms have a world, the numbers themselves, so they do not clash. So the statement is not reached. But that is exactly what it says, so it is true. Here is a statement about numbers, as ordinary in its form as any statement about adding and multiplying, that is true and that the axioms never reach, and its denial is not reached either. Adding it as a new axiom does not help: the longer list has its own unreached statement, by the same argument. No list that a recipe can check, and that is true of the numbers, reaches every truth about them.

The unreached statement is not reached by the axioms of arithmetic, and so it is true, and its denial is not reached either. The same holds for every collection that holds those axioms, that a recipe can check, and that is true of the numbers.

38i2True, and Its Denial Not Reached

Unreached-truth technique: the unreached statement is true in N, and PA reaches neither it nor its denial.

\text{no number codes a proof of } U \text{ from } PA @@ PA ⊬ U (38i1)
N \models \neg\text{Prov}(\ulcorner U \urcorner) @@ Prov says that some number codes such a proof
N \models U \Leftrightarrow \neg\text{Prov}(\ulcorner U \urcorner) @@ PA reaches it, and N is a world of PA (38c2)
N \models U @@ carry an if through, the right-to-left half, in N
PA \vdash \neg U \Rightarrow N \models \neg U @@ PA reaches only statements true in N (38g2)
\htmlClass{new}{N \models U,\ \ PA \nvdash U,\ \ PA \nvdash \neg U} \quad \blacksquare @@ ¬U is false in N, so it is not reached

U is true, and the reason given is a proof. It is a proof from more than PA: it used the fact that N is a world of PA, which PA cannot state about itself. That is the subject of 38j.

Arrangement against series again. A proof answers a question about the arrangement: does this list of givens reach this statement? Truth in N answers a question about the series: does every number behave so? Here the two answers come apart, for a statement about adding and multiplying.

38i3Every List Falls Short

Nothing in 38i1 and 38i2 was special to PA except three facts: PA holds the axioms of arithmetic, so the search-reached technique holds for it; whether a number codes a member of PA is settled by a recipe, so Proof can be written; and PA is true in N.

Every-list technique: if a collection T holds PA, membership in T is settled by a recipe, and every member of T is true in N, then some statement true in N is not reached by T.

\text{Proof}_T(y, x) \text{ and } \text{Prov}_T(x) \text{ written as in 38h1} @@ the recipe for T in place of the one for PA
T \vdash U_T \Leftrightarrow \neg\text{Prov}_T(\ulcorner U_T \urcorner) @@ the self-statement technique; T holds PA
T \nvdash U_T,\ \ N \models U_T @@ 38i1 and 38i2, word for word, with T in place of PA
\htmlClass{new}{T + U_T \text{ is again such a collection, with its own } U_{T + U_T}} \quad \blacksquare @@ adding one true axiom keeps all three facts

So the true statements of arithmetic can never be listed by a recipe. If they could, take T to be all of them: whether φ is in T would be settled by listing until φ or ¬φ turns up, as one of them must. T would hold PA and be true in N, and it would reach every truth, against the technique.

38i4A World Where U Is False

PA ⊬ U, so PA together with ¬U is clash-free, and the full-growth and term-world techniques give it a world M (38d). In M, ¬U is true, so Prov(⌜U⌝) is true: some member of M is, in M’s eyes, the code of a proof of U. No number is. So that member is past every numeral, as in 38f.

PA \cup \{\neg U\} \text{ is clash-free} @@ PA ⊬ ¬¬U, since PA ⊬ U and not-not out would give it (7e)
M \models PA,\ \ M \models \neg U @@ the full-growth and term-world techniques (38d)
M \models \text{Proof}(e, \ulcorner U \urcorner) \text{ for some member } e @@ ¬U is reached as equivalent to Prov(⌜U⌝); Prov is a some
e \text{ is not the value of any numeral } \bar k @@ else Proof(k̄, ⌜U⌝) would be true in N, a true bounded statement in both worlds
\htmlClass{new}{e \text{ is past every numeral: a “proof” in } M \text{ that no list of lines is}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the numerals come first in M (38f2)

So U is not reached because it is true in N and false in M, and both are worlds of PA. When someone says U “is true”, they mean true in N. That meaning is clear, and PA cannot see which world it is in.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

U is true of the numbers and not reached by PA; true in N and false in M. The ladder’s “true” (1a6, earned) and its “necessary” (3b2, every box reaches it) come apart here: U is true in the world the axioms were written for, and not necessary under them. That gap is the whole content of the theorem. A proof reaches what holds in every world; truth in one world, even the intended one, is more than any proof from PA can deliver.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The unreached-truth technique and the every-list technique are Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem: a consistent, recursively axiomatized theory that contains enough arithmetic is incomplete. The unreached statement is the Gödel sentence. A collection whose membership is settled by a recipe is recursive, or recursively axiomatizable. That the truths of arithmetic are not recursively enumerable follows. 38i.6.1’s weaker fact is ω-consistency; John Barkley Rosser’s trick of 1936 replaces it with plain consistency, by a sentence that says “for every proof of me there is a shorter proof of my denial”.

Who said it first

Kurt Gödel announced the result at a meeting in Königsberg in September 1930, where David Hilbert gave his address ending “We must know. We will know.” the next day. The paper appeared in 1931. Gödel assumed ω-consistency; Rosser removed it in 1936. The proof by the diagonal lemma, as on this page, is a later tidying of Gödel’s own argument.
