26e · proof

Integrals round a loop

On the line, an integral runs from one end of an interval to the other. In the plane there are endlessly many roads between two points, and an integral can follow any of them. This step builds the integral along a path, fences its size by the length of the road, and finds that when the function has an undoing, the road does not matter: only the two ends count. Then it goes once round a circle with each power of z. Every power totals zero, except one. One over z totals two pi i, because its undoing, the logarithm, comes back a whole turn out.

An integral along a path adds the function times the path’s velocity. When the function has an undoing, only the ends count. Once round a circle, every power of z totals zero except one over z, which totals two pi i.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 18i (paths, length), 16c (evaluating by antiderivatives), 26d (complex slopes) and 26c (the logarithm). 26f shows when a loop’s total is always zero.

26e1An integral along a path

Stands on 18i1 to 18i4, 16 and 25c.

From here on, a path in ℂ is γ(t) = x(t) + iy(t) for t in [a, b], with x′ and y′ continuous; γ′(t) = x′(t) + iy′(t). For f continuous on the path, ∫_γ f(z) dz = ∫ₐᵇ f(γ(t))γ′(t) dt, where the integral of a complex function of t is the integral of its floor part plus i times the integral of its upright part. A path with γ(a) = γ(b) is a loop. Accepted names: a contour integral; a closed contour.

Path-fence technique: if |f| ≤ M on the path, and the path has length L, then |∫_γ f(z) dz| ≤ ML.

\int_a^b g(t)\,dt = \rho e^{i\varphi} \Rightarrow \rho = \int_a^b e^{-i\varphi}g(t)\,dt @@ out and round (26a3): turn the total onto the positive floor
\rho = \int_a^b \text{floor part of } e^{-i\varphi}g(t)\,dt \le \int_a^b |g(t)|\,dt @@ ρ is real, so the upright part integrates to 0; a part is at most the size (25c2)
\Big|\int_\gamma f\,dz\Big| \le \int_a^b |f(\gamma(t))|\,|\gamma'(t)|\,dt @@ the line above, with g = f(γ)γ′; sizes multiply (25c1)
\htmlClass{new}{\Big|\int_\gamma f\,dz\Big| \le M\int_a^b |\gamma'(t)|\,dt = ML} \quad \blacksquare @@ |γ′| is the speed; its area is the length (18i4)

26e2Only the ends count

Stands on 26e1, 26d1 (the chain rule in ℂ) and 16c.

Path-ends technique: if F has a complex slope F′ = f at every point of an open set holding the path γ, then ∫_γ f(z) dz = F(γ(b)) − F(γ(a)); so round any loop in that set, the total is 0.

\tfrac{d}{dt}F(\gamma(t)) = F'(\gamma(t))\,\gamma'(t) = f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t) @@ the chain rule (13e, in ℂ by 26d1), with a real input t
\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt = F(\gamma(b)) - F(\gamma(a)) @@ change for any integrable (16c), on the floor part and the upright part
\htmlClass{new}{\int_\gamma f\,dz = F(\text{end}) - F(\text{start})} \quad \blacksquare

So zⁿ, for every whole n ≠ −1, has the undoing zⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1), with a complex slope everywhere it is defined. Every loop that misses 0 gives it a total of 0. And e^z, sin z and every polynomial give every loop a total of 0. The one power left out is 1/z. Its undoing would be a logarithm, and 26c showed that no single logarithm can go all the way round 0 without a jump.

26e3Once round a circle

Stands on 26e1, 26e2 and 26a3.

Circle-powers technique: once round the circle |z| = r anticlockwise, ∫ zⁿ dz = 2πi when n = −1, and 0 for every other whole n.

\gamma(t) = re^{it},\ \gamma'(t) = ire^{it},\ 0 \le t \le 2\pi @@ the circle, traced once (26d2: the slope of e^(it))
\int_\gamma z^n\,dz = \int_0^{2\pi} r^n e^{int}\cdot ire^{it}\,dt = ir^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(n+1)t}\,dt @@ 26e1; the plane-adding technique (26b1)
m \ne 0:\ \int_0^{2\pi} e^{imt}\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi}\cos mt\,dt + i\int_0^{2\pi}\sin mt\,dt = 0 @@ whole turns of cos and sin (21)
m = 0:\ \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dt = 2\pi @@ n = −1 gives m = n + 1 = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\oint_{|z|=r} z^n\,dz = \begin{cases} 2\pi i & n = -1 \\ 0 & n \ne -1\end{cases}} \quad \blacksquare

The answer does not depend on r. However large or small the circle, 1/z round it totals 2πi: one whole turn of the logarithm, times i. That single number is the seed of everything left on this rung.

The accepted names

The integral along a path is a contour integral or line integral; ∮ marks a closed contour. The path-fence technique is the ML-inequality (or estimation lemma). The path-ends technique is the fundamental theorem for contour integrals.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss knew in 1811 that an integral between two points in the plane depends on the path only through the points it goes round, and wrote so in a letter to Friedrich Bessel. Augustin-Louis Cauchy developed integrals along paths in ℂ from 1814 to 1825.
