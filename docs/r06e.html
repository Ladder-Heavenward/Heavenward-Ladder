6e · support

Choosing, lining up any set, and the top of every chain

Mathematicians rarely quote the choosing axiom in 6b's form. They quote one of two statements that, given the other principles, say the same thing. One is that every set can be put in an order in which every non-empty part has a least member. The other is that if every chain in a partly ordered set has something above it all, then something has nothing above it. This step builds the words those statements need, each with its line: partial order, chain, upper bound, maximal member, least member, well-order. Then it proves what the ladder can prove, one move per line, and says plainly what it cannot yet. Well-ordering gives choice, with nothing to spare. The maximal-member principle gives choice too, but the column stops one line short, at a double negative. Choice gives well-ordering by choosing from what is left, again and again, past every counting number. The recursion that runs that far is not built here. A finite set, though, is put in order by 4k's counting alone.

An order with a least member in every part picks for you: take the least. A largest partial picking picks from every set, if nothing is missing from it. Choosing from what is left, over and over, lays a set out in order.

6e1Orders that leave some pairs uncompared

4h's ≤ compares any two numbers (any-two-compare). Many useful orders do not. ⊆ between sets is an order of a kind: a set is a part of itself, parts of parts are parts, and two sets each a part of the other are the same set (double-inclusion). But {cat} and {dog} are not parts of each other either way. Divides, on the numbers, is the same: 2 ∣ 6 and 3 ∣ 6, but neither of 2 and 3 divides the other. 2g gave relations the words reflexive and transitive. One more is needed.

From here on, a relation r on a set W is antisymmetric when two things related each way are the same thing:

From here on, a partial order on W is a relation on W that is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. When a partial order is in hand, may be written for x r y; read it “x is below y”, or “y is above x”. The mark is a statement with two gaps, like 2g's ∼, and it names whichever partial order is in hand.

Three partial orders. The first is ≤ on ℕ: reflexive by =, antisymmetric because any-two-compare allows at most one of x < y, x = y, y < x, and transitive by chain for less. The second is ⊆ on any set of sets (self-part, double-inclusion, chain for part of). The third is ∣ on ℕ (n = n · 1, 4j's divides-both-ways, chain for divides). Two relations that are not: < (not reflexive: no-self-membership), and 2g's “has met” (Ann has met Ben and Ben has met Ann, and they are two people).

6e2Chains, upper bounds, maximal members

From here on, for a partial order ≼ on W, these four words mean the lines below, in order. A part C of W is a chain when any two of its members compare. u is an upper bound of a part C when it is in W and above every member of C. m is a maximal member of W when nothing in W is above m but m itself. And m is a least member of a part X when it is in X and below every member of X.

This chain is a set. 4d's chain of samenesses, a = b = c, and 4i's chain of order, 1 ≤ i ≤ k, are pieces of writing and keep their meanings.

Under ∣, {1, 2, 4, 8} is a chain and {2, 3} is not, and 24 is an upper bound of {2, 3, 4}. In {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}, both 4 and 6 are maximal. Nothing in the set is a multiple of either but itself, and neither is above everything. That is the difference between maximal and greatest: a maximal member has nothing above it, a greatest member is above everything. Under ⊆ on {∅, {cat}, {dog}}, {cat} and {dog} are both maximal and nothing is greatest. ∅ is a chain under any order, vacuously, and every member of the set is an upper bound of it. The least-member line agrees with 4h's when ≼ is ≤. And there is never more than one. One-least-member technique: if m and m′ are least members of X, then m = m′: each is below the other, since each is in X; antisymmetric. ∎

6e3Well-orders

From here on, a partial order on W is a well-order when every non-empty part of W has a least member under it; W is then well-ordered by it.

Well-order-compares technique: if ≼ is a well-order on a set, then the whole set is a chain. A partial order under which the whole set is a chain has the accepted name total order: any two members compare.

6e4The two principles

Now the principles, in plain words and then in marks. The well-ordering principle: every set has a well-order.

The maximal-member principle: if a partial order on a set gives every chain an upper bound, the set has a maximal member.

The empty chain must have an upper bound too, so the set has a member.

A caution before anything is proved. Is ℕ well-ordered by ≤? 4h's least-member gives a least member to every non-empty settled part. For a part whose membership cannot be decided number by number, 4h had only no-least-no-members, the double negative. So the ladder has not yet shown that ≤ is a well-order in this step's sense; 7·cb4 comes back to it. The same is true for every order this step builds by hand: they reach the settled parts.

6e5Well-ordering gives choice

Well-ordering-gives-choice technique: if 𝒜 is a collection of non-empty sets and ⋃𝒜 has a well-order, then 𝒜 has a choosing function: take the least. With the well-ordering principle, every ⋃𝒜 has one, so the well-ordering principle gives Choice.

\preccurlyeq \text{ a well-order on } \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A @@ given; use the some
c = \{(A, m) \in \mathcal A \times \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A : m \text{ is the least member of } A\} @@ Separation, 2h's shorthand
\text{let } A \in \mathcal A;\ \ A \subseteq \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A @@ 2e's ⋃ line: a member of a member is in the union
\text{call } m \text{ the least member of } A @@ A is a non-empty part; the well-order; use the some
\htmlClass{new}{(A, m) \in c} @@ m ∈ A ⊆ ⋃𝒜; ︿ the sift: every input has an output
(A, m) \in c \land (A, m') \in c \;\Rightarrow\; m = m' @@ ﹀ the sift; the one-least-member technique: no input has two
c(A) \in A,\quad c \text{ is a choosing function for } \mathcal A \quad \blacksquare @@ the value line; ﹀ least; ︿

This is 6b's least-chooses with any well-order in place of ≤. It splits nothing into cases and calls on no choosing axiom. Picking is easy once everything stands in a line with a first member in every part.

6e6Partial pickings

The idea: look at all the partial pickings, ordered by ⊆. These are the sets of pairs that pick from some of the members of 𝒜 but perhaps not all. The maximal-member principle gives a maximal one. A maximal partial picking cannot be missing any set, since a missing set could be added. That last “since” is where the ladder must be careful.

From here on, a partial choosing function for 𝒜 is a part p of 𝒜 × ⋃𝒜 in which no input has two outputs and every output is in its input:

A is an input of p when .

This is 4d's word “input”, said of a part p that need not be a function. When p is a function from 𝒜, its inputs in this sense are its inputs in 4d's.

A choosing function is a partial one with every member of 𝒜 an input. Write ℱ for the set of all partial choosing functions for 𝒜, sifted from 𝒫(𝒜 × ⋃𝒜), and order it by ⊆. ∅ is in ℱ. Bounded-chains technique: if 𝒞 ⊆ ℱ is a chain under ⊆, then ⋃𝒞 ∈ ℱ, and ⋃𝒞 is an upper bound of 𝒞.

\textstyle\bigcup\mathcal C \subseteq \mathcal A \times \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A,\ \ y \in A @@ p ∈ ℱ, unpacked: every output in its input
(A, y) \in p,\ \ (A, z) \in q,\ \ p, q \in \mathcal C @@ two pairs with one input, each from its member of 𝒞

6e7The maximal-member principle gives choice, almost

So the maximal-member principle applies to ℱ. Chains-give-choice technique: if the maximal-member principle is true, then for each collection 𝒜 of non-empty sets there is a partial choosing function m such that, for every A ∈ 𝒜, it is not so that A fails to be an input of m.

\text{call } m \text{ a maximal member of } \mathcal F @@ the maximal-member principle, with bounded-chains for its hypothesis; use the some
\text{let } A \in \mathcal A @@ speak of everything
\quad \text{assume } \neg(A \text{ is an input of } m) @@ aiming to refute
\quad \text{call } a \in A;\ \ m' = m \cup \{(A, a)\} @@ A is non-empty; use the some; Pairing, union
\quad m' \in \mathcal F @@ outputs in inputs by merge-membership; a second output at A would make A an input of m, against the assumption
\quad m \subseteq m',\ \ \text{so } m' = m @@ 2e; m is maximal: use the every with m′
\quad (A, a) \in m,\ \ A \text{ is an input of } m,\ \ \bot @@ the swap axiom; show an example; clash
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\neg(A \text{ is an input of } m)} @@ refute; the call for a closes on ⊥
\forall A \in \mathcal A\ \neg\neg(A \text{ is an input of } m) \quad \blacksquare @@ speak of everything

The line a choosing function needs is “A is an input of m”, for every A. The column reaches “not not”, and stops. It is 1b's gap, the same place 4h's no-least-no-members stopped and 6b's non-empty stopped. With the step from ¬¬P to P, which rung 7 takes up, m is a choosing function (7·cb6 writes that line), and the maximal-member principle gives Choice. Without it, this is as far as the techs go, and the stop is not the column's fault.

6e8Choice gives well-ordering: choose from what is left

The other direction is an old idea, and it is easy to say. Given a set X, use Choice on the collection of the non-empty parts of X (a sift of 𝒫(X)), and call the choosing function c. The first member of the order is c(X). The next is c's pick from what is left, c(X ∖ {c(X)}). The next is c's pick from what is left after that. Keep going. Put x below y when x was picked no later than y. The least member of a part is its earliest pick. That every set has a well-order was first proved from Choice in 1904.

For a finite X this is 4d's defining by repeating, with start X and step “take away c's pick”. It runs out after as many steps as X has members. For an endless X, it runs through all of ℕ and may still not have used X up. Then it must go on: after all the counting numbers, choose again from what is left, and again, along a run longer than ℕ. Runs that long, and the recursion along them, are not built on this ladder. The first proof, of 1904, avoided the long run. It looked at all the orders that start out “choose from what is left” and glued them together, which also needs more than the ladder has built. And the step “is anything left?” splits on whether a set is empty, which the techs license only for settled sets. So this direction is a sketch here, and 6e says so; 7·cb6 makes it a proof.

Choice gives the maximal-member principle the same way. Climb, choosing at each step something strictly above where you are. At the end of every run, take an upper bound of the chain climbed so far. The climb cannot go on forever along the long runs, so it stops, at a maximal member. Again, the long run is not built. 7·cb6 takes this direction up again.

6e9What can be proved: a finite set is in order

For a finite set no choosing is needed at all: 4k's counting already lays it out. Finite-order technique: if X has n members, there is a partial order on X under which X is a chain and every non-empty settled part of X has a least member. Let f take each member of X to its partner in n, and g take each k ∈ n to its partner in X (the matching, both ways: turn-about for matchings).

x \preccurlyeq y \;\Leftrightarrow\; f(x) \le f(y) @@ a sift of X × X: x below y when x's place is no later
\text{reflexive, transitive} @@ f(x) ≤ f(x); ≤ chains (chain for less, the swap axiom)
f(x) \le f(y) \land f(y) \le f(x) \Rightarrow f(x) = f(y) \Rightarrow x = y @@ any-two-compare: at most one; f(x) has exactly one partner: antisymmetric
x \preccurlyeq y \lor y \preccurlyeq x @@ any-two-compare on f(x), f(y): a chain
T \subseteq X \text{ settled, non-empty};\ \ P = \{k \in \mathbb N : k \lt n \land g(k) \in T\} @@ given; Separation: the places of T
k \in P \lor k \notin P,\ \text{for each } k @@ settled-order: k < n or not; if so, T settled: g(k) ∈ T or not. P is settled
\text{call } t \in T;\ \ f(t) \in P @@ T non-empty; f(t) ∈ n so f(t) < n; g(f(t)) = t
\text{call } k_0 \text{ the least member of } P;\ \ m = g(k_0) \in T @@ least-member (4h), on the settled set P; ﹀ P
t \in T \Rightarrow f(t) \in P \Rightarrow k_0 \le f(t) \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{m \preccurlyeq t} @@ g(f(t)) = t, so f(t) ∈ P; k₀ least; f(m) = k₀
m \text{ is the least member of } T \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿

So {cat, dog, owl}, matched with 3 by cat ↦ 0, dog ↦ 1, owl ↦ 2, is ordered cat ≼ dog ≼ owl, and the least of {owl, dog} is dog. For a part of X that is not settled, the same column reaches only no-least-no-members's double negative: “it is not so that T has no least member”. So, on this ladder, a finite set is well-ordered on its settled parts. 7·cb4 is where every part joins them.

With every statement true or false, and with a recursion along runs longer than ℕ, the three statements are one principle, given the other axioms. That is what it means to call them equivalent. It is why, in practice, mathematicians reach for whichever form fits the job. They use well-ordering when they want to run through a set one member at a time. They use the maximal-member principle when they want something that cannot be made any bigger. These two forms are what follows from the choosing axiom, and they are the working tools.

Who said it first

“Choose from what is left” is Ernst Zermelo’s idea, and his 1904 proof is the first proof that every set can be well-ordered. Max Zorn published the maximal-member principle in 1935, as a remark on method in algebra; Kazimierz Kuratowski had stated a close relative in 1922, and Felix Hausdorff had one earlier still.

John Bell, studying Zorn's lemma in settings that keep rung 1's gap, found that the lemma, suitably stated, survives there in places where the axiom of choice does not. So the double negative above cannot be removed by a cleverer column; it needs a new principle.

after Bell, “Zorn's lemma and complete Boolean algebras in intuitionistic type theories”, Journal of Symbolic Logic 62 (1997)

A joke that mathematicians tell, attributed to Jerry Bona: the axiom of choice is obviously true, the well-ordering principle obviously false, and who can tell about Zorn's lemma? The joke is that, given the other principles, all three are one statement.

Accepted names

The maximal-member principle is Zorn’s lemma. “Every set has a well-order”, proved from Choice, is the well-ordering theorem. A partial order under which the whole set is a chain, as ≤ is on ℕ, is called a total order, or a linear order. The runs longer than ℕ are the ordinals, and the recursion along them is transfinite recursion.
