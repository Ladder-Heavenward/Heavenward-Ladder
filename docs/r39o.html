39o · proof

Remembering Answers

Some programs that cut a job into smaller copies meet the same small copy again and again. The plainest example is the technique that each number of a famous sequence is the sum of the two before it. Followed blindly, the technique asks for the earlier numbers so many times that the fortieth number takes two hundred million calls. Write each answer down the first time it is found, and look it up after that, and the same number takes thirty-nine additions. That one idea, keep a table of the answers to the smaller copies, solves a whole class of problems that look hopeless at first. The step uses it to line up two strings of genetic letters with the fewest changes, the daily work of every laboratory that reads DNA. Then it turns to the opposite habit, taking the best-looking piece at each moment and never looking back. Sometimes that is exactly right, and the step proves when for one case: fitting the most meetings into one room. Sometimes it fails badly, and a table of answers rescues it. The rung on counting and connection carries both ideas onto maps and routes.

A recursion whose calls repeat costs only as many steps as it has different calls, once their answers are kept. Aligning two strings with the fewest changes fills a table one cell for each pair of starting pieces. Taking the meeting that ends first is always best for one room. Taking the biggest coin first can fail, and a table of amounts fixes it.

39o1Calls That Repeat

The sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … has each number the sum of the two before: F(1) = F(2) = 1 and F(n) = F(n − 1) + F(n − 2), a recursion by 4d. A program that follows the technique as written calls itself for n − 1 and for n − 2, and each of those calls itself twice again.

Remembered-answers technique: a recursion whose answer at n needs only answers at smaller sizes can be run with each answer computed once and kept, at a cost of the number of different sizes times the cost of one step.

C(1) = C(2) = 1,\ \ C(n) = 1 + C(n - 1) + C(n - 2) @@ the calls the plain program makes
C(n) = 2F(n) - 1 @@ strong induction (4h): 1 + (2F(n − 1) − 1) + (2F(n − 2) − 1) = 2F(n) − 1
n = 40:\ \ F(40) = 102\,334\,155,\ \ C(40) = 204\,668\,309 \text{ calls} @@ worked by machine
\text{kept: } F(3), F(4), \ldots, F(40) \text{, each one addition} @@ each answer found once, from two kept answers
\htmlClass{new}{38 \text{ additions in place of over two hundred million calls}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the technique: 38 different sizes past the two starts

The calls of the plain program grow in the family of F itself, about 1.618ⁿ, growth by equal factors (17j). The kept table grows like n. Nothing about the technique changed; only the program stopped forgetting. Programs that keep answers this way are said to use dynamic programming, a name chosen in the 1950s to sound impressive.

39o2Lining up Two Strings

Two strings of DNA letters, A, C, G and T, read from two animals, differ by changes: a letter swapped, a letter dropped, a letter added. The distance between them is the least number of such changes that turns one into the other. Let D(i, j) be the distance between the first i letters of the first string and the first j of the second.

Alignment-table technique: D(i, j) is the least of D(i − 1, j) + 1, D(i, j − 1) + 1, and D(i − 1, j − 1) plus 0 or 1 as the last letters agree or differ; so the whole distance is found by filling a table of (n + 1)(m + 1) cells.

\text{the last letters of a best line-up: dropped, added, or set against each other} @@ the three ways a line-up can end
D(i, 0) = i,\ \ D(0, j) = j @@ against nothing, every letter is a change
\text{each case leaves a best line-up of shorter pieces} @@ a better one for the pieces would make a better whole
\text{ACGT against AGT: rows } 0123,\ 1012,\ 2112,\ 3212,\ 4321 @@ the table filled row by row, by machine
\htmlClass{new}{D(4, 3) = 1:\ \text{drop the C}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the corner of the table

Each cell takes three looks at cells already filled, so two strings of a thousand letters need about a million cells and three million looks: a second on a phone. Trying every line-up instead would mean a number of line-ups with hundreds of digits. Laboratories compare a new gene against millions of known ones this way, with changes scored by how often each kind happens, and the table is what makes a family tree of species something that can be computed.

39o3Take What Ends First

A greedy program builds its answer one piece at a time, always taking the piece that looks best now and never undoing a choice. For meetings that each want one room from a start to an end, a greedy program can take, again and again, the meeting that ends earliest among those that do not clash with what it has taken.

Earliest-finish technique: taking, again and again, the meeting that ends first among those that fit fits as many meetings into one room as any schedule can.

\text{greedy takes } g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_k;\ \text{some best schedule takes } b_1, \ldots, b_\ell,\ \text{each in order of ending} @@ suppose ℓ ≥ k
g_1 \text{ ends no later than } b_1 @@ g₁ ends first of all
\text{end}(g_r) \le \text{end}(b_r) \text{ for each } r \le k @@ induction on r: b_r starts after b_{r−1} ends, so after g_{r−1} ends, so it fits beside g₁, …, g_{r−1}, and greedy took one ending no later
\ell > k \Rightarrow b_{k+1} \text{ fits after } g_k @@ it starts after b_k ends, so after g_k ends
\htmlClass{new}{\bot \text{ unless } \ell = k:\ \text{greedy is best}} \quad \blacksquare @@ greedy would have taken b_{k+1}, or one like it

The proof is the shape every proof that greedy works takes: greedy never falls behind a best answer, piece by piece. Taking the meeting that starts first, or the shortest, fails: one long early meeting can block three short ones.

39o4When Greedy Fails, and the Table That Fixes It

Pay an amount with the fewest coins. Greedy takes the largest coin that fits, again and again. With the coins of a till in Santa Cruz, 25, 10, 5 and 1 cents, it is always best: a machine checked every amount up to 500. With coins of 1, 3 and 4, it pays 6 as 4 + 1 + 1, three coins, when 3 + 3 is two.

Coin-table technique: the fewest coins for an amount v is 1 plus the least, over the coins c no larger than v, of the fewest for v − c; so a table of the amounts 0 to v gives the best answer for any coins, in about v times the number of coins turns.

N(0) = 0 @@ nothing to pay
N(1) = 1,\ N(2) = 2,\ N(3) = 1,\ N(4) = 1 @@ each from the cells before it
N(5) = 1 + \min(N(4), N(2), N(1)) = 2 @@ coins 1, 3 and 4 tried
N(6) = 1 + \min(N(5), N(3), N(2)) = 1 + 1 = 2 @@ coin 3, then N(3)
\htmlClass{new}{N(6) = 2:\ 3 + 3,\ \text{where greedy pays three coins}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the table, read back

Greedy commits early and the table waits until it has seen every smaller amount. That is the trade: greedy is fast when a proof like 39o3’s exists, and the table is slower but needs no such proof. 40’s steps on shortest routes and on joining every town with the least cable are this footing and the last, carried onto links: best routes from best pieces, and a greedy choice that a proof shows is safe.

The accepted names

Keeping answers to smaller copies is dynamic programming, and keeping them in a recursive program is memoization. The sequence is the Fibonacci sequence. The distance of two strings is the edit (Levenshtein) distance, and the table with scored changes is the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm; its local form is Smith–Waterman. The earliest-finish rule solves interval scheduling, and its proof is an exchange (“greedy stays ahead”) argument. Coins for which greedy is always best are canonical coin systems. The crosswalk items here are dynamic programming and greedy algorithms of MIT 6.006 and Stanford CS 161, and sequence alignment and phylogenetic trees of MIT 8.592.

Who said it first

Richard Bellman named dynamic programming at RAND in the early 1950s, choosing the words, he wrote, so that no one in government could object to them. Vladimir Levenshtein defined his distance in 1965. Saul Needleman and Christian Wunsch aligned proteins with a table in 1970, and Temple Smith and Michael Waterman gave the local form in 1981. Leonardo of Pisa’s book of 1202 has the sequence as a puzzle about rabbits.

A proof that is not allowed

“Greedy is best for 25, 10, 5 and 1 cents, checked up to 500. So it is best for every amount.” Not allowed as a proof: a check up to 500 is a guess under the small case. It can be made a proof, since past 25 cents every best payment for v + 25 is a best payment for v plus a quarter, which needs its own lines; until then it is a law with no exception found in its range.
