44k · technique

Field Extensions by Hand

Step 44f proved that the fractions with the square root of two put in are a field, and the fractions with the cube root of two put in are another. Proving that a reciprocal exists is not the same as writing it down. This step writes them down. For the square root of two the trick is the one rung twenty five used for complex numbers: multiply top and bottom by the flipped member. For the cube root of two there is no single flip, and the reciprocal comes from a grid, because multiplying by a member is a straight map on the basis, and its determinant is how much that member scales the field. The same grids find least polynomials: write the powers of a member in the basis and look for the first mix that gives nothing. Last, towers by hand: degrees that share no factor multiply, which settles at once how big a field two roots make.

In the fractions with √2 put in, divide by multiplying top and bottom by the flipped member. With ∛2 put in, multiplying by a member is a grid on the basis 1, ∛2, ∛4; its determinant is the member’s size, and solving the grid gives the reciprocal. The powers of a member, written in a basis, give its least polynomial, and degrees with no common factor multiply.

44k1Dividing With √2

Flip-undo technique: in ℚ(√2), the reciprocal of a + b√2, not 0, is (a − b√2)/(a² − 2b²), and a² − 2b² is never 0.

(a + b\sqrt2)(a - b\sqrt2) = a^2 - 2b^2 @@ the difference of squares (5i)
a^2 - 2b^2 = 0,\ b \ne 0 \Rightarrow (a/b)^2 = 2 @@ divide by b²
\bot @@ no fraction squares to 2 (5f); and b = 0 gives a = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt2} = \frac{a - b\sqrt2}{a^2 - 2b^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by the size a² − 2b², a fraction other than 0

So 1/(1 + √2) = (1 − √2)/(1 − 2) = −1 + √2, and 1/(3 + 2√2) = 3 − 2√2. The size a² − 2b² multiplies, as 25c’s sizes did: it is the product of a member and its flip, and the flip keeps products. Members of size 1 or −1 are undoable in ℤ[√2], and 1 + √2 is one whose powers never end.

44k2Dividing With ∛2

Write α = ∛2, with basis 1, α, α² over ℚ and α³ = 2. Multiplying by β = a + bα + cα² is a straight map of ℚ(α) to itself.

Times-grid technique: the grid of multiplying by β = a + bα + cα² on the basis 1, α, α² has determinant a³ + 2b³ + 4c³ − 6abc, which is 0 only for β = 0, and solving the grid for 1 gives β’s reciprocal.

\beta \cdot 1 = a + b\alpha + c\alpha^2,\ \ \beta\alpha = 2c + a\alpha + b\alpha^2,\ \ \beta\alpha^2 = 2b + 2c\alpha + a\alpha^2 @@ α³ = 2, α⁴ = 2α
M_\beta = \begin{pmatrix} a & 2c & 2b \\ b & a & 2c \\ c & b & a \end{pmatrix} @@ the images as columns (27d)
\det M_\beta = a^3 + 2b^3 + 4c^3 - 6abc @@ 27k’s cofactors
\htmlClass{new}{\beta \ne 0 \Rightarrow \det M_\beta \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ ℚ(α) is a field, so multiplying by β ≠ 0 loses nothing (27c, 27e)
  
(1 + \alpha)(x + y\alpha + z\alpha^2) = (x + 2z) + (x + y)\alpha + (y + z)\alpha^2 @@ the grid with a = b = 1, c = 0
x + 2z = 1,\ \ x + y = 0,\ \ y + z = 0 @@ ask for 1
z = \tfrac13,\ y = -\tfrac13,\ x = \tfrac13 @@ elimination (27j): x = −y = z, so 3z = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{1}{1 + \sqrt[3]2} = \frac{1 - \sqrt[3]2 + \sqrt[3]4}{3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: (1 + α)(1 − α + α²) = 1 + α³ = 3

44k3Least Polynomials From Powers

First-mix technique: to find the least polynomial of β over K, write 1, β, β², … in a basis of the field until one is a mix of those before it; that first mix, moved to one side, is the least polynomial. It is the least by 27b’s spare-vector technique: no shorter mix gives 0.

\beta = \sqrt2 + \sqrt3;\ \ \text{basis } 1, \sqrt2, \sqrt3, \sqrt6 @@ the tower ℚ ⊆ ℚ(√2) ⊆ ℚ(√2, √3) (44f3)
\beta^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt6 @@ 2 + 2√6 + 3
\beta^3 = 11\sqrt2 + 9\sqrt3 @@ (5 + 2√6)(√2 + √3) = 5√2 + 5√3 + 4√3 + 6√2
\beta^4 = 49 + 20\sqrt6 = 10\beta^2 - 1 @@ (5 + 2√6)²; 1, β, β², β³ are free, since β³ needs √2 and √3 in the ratio 11 : 9 and β in 1 : 1
\htmlClass{new}{x^4 - 10x^2 + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first mix; so [ℚ(β) : ℚ] = 4 and ℚ(β) is all of ℚ(√2, √3)

So √2 + √3 alone makes the field that √2 and √3 make together. That one member can make a field of two roots is common, and 43 builds on it.

44k4Degrees That Share Nothing

Coprime-degrees technique: if α has degree m and β has degree n over K, and m and n share no factor, then [K(α, β) : K] = mn.

m \mid [K(\alpha, \beta) : K],\ \ n \mid [K(\alpha, \beta) : K] @@ the degree-divides technique, twice
mn \mid [K(\alpha, \beta) : K] @@ m and n share no factor (4j)
[K(\alpha, \beta) : K(\alpha)] \le n @@ β’s least polynomial over K still has β as a zero over K(α)
\htmlClass{new}{[K(\alpha, \beta) : K] \le mn,\ \text{so} = mn} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tower technique

So [ℚ(√2, ∛2) : ℚ] = 6. When the degrees share a factor, the count can fall short: ℚ(√2, √8) is just ℚ(√2), degree 2, not 4.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The flip of a + b√2 is its conjugate, and a² − 2b² and a³ + 2b³ + 4c³ − 6abc are norms, N(β) = det M_β. The grid M_β is the regular representation. A member that makes a whole field is a primitive element; that √2 + √3 makes ℚ(√2, √3) is a case of the primitive element theorem. The undoable members of ℤ[√2] are ±(1 + √2)ⁿ, and a² − 2b² = ±1 is Pell’s equation, 43k’s x² − ny² = 1 with n = 2.

Who said it first

Writing the reciprocal of a sum of roots by clearing the bottom is old school algebra. Richard Dedekind defined the norm as a determinant in 1871. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved in 1768 that x² − ny² = 1 always has whole answers past the obvious one.

A proof that is not allowed

“√2 + √3 is a zero of x² − 5 − 2√6, which has degree 2, so √2 + √3 has degree 2.” Not allowed over ℚ: that polynomial has √6 in its coefficients, so it is a polynomial over ℚ(√6), and there β has degree 2. Over ℚ the degree is 4.
