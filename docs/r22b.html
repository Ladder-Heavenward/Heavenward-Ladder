22b · proof

Every bounded sequence has a settling thread

A list of numbers kept between a floor and a ceiling need not settle. It can bounce between two values forever. But pick the right terms out of it, in order, and you can always find a thread that does settle. This step proves it by the same trick the twelfth rung used to trap a root: cut the room in half, keep a half that still holds endlessly many terms, and cut again. The halves shrink to a single point, and a thread of terms follows them in. Nobody needs to know the point in advance. The halving finds it.

Keeping only some of the terms, in their order, gives a thread. Every list kept between a floor and a ceiling has a thread that settles. Halving finds it, half by half, without ever knowing where it will land.

22b1Threads

Stands on 9·cb4 (sequences) and 9b (composing).

From here on, a thread of a sequence s is s ∘ k, where k : ℕ → ℕ climbs strictly: k(1) < k(2) < k(3) < …. Its terms are s_{k(1)}, s_{k(2)}, s_{k(3)}, …: some of the terms of s, kept in their order. Accepted name: a subsequence.

Thread technique: if a sequence settles at L, every thread of it settles at L. A strict climb of whole numbers has k(j) ≥ j, by induction. So once n ≥ N keeps sₙ within ε of L, every j ≥ N keeps s_{k(j)} within ε too.

Read backwards, the thread technique is a test for wandering. Two threads that settle at different places, or one thread that wanders, show that the whole sequence wanders. The terms of (−1)ⁿ split into the even thread, all 1, and the odd thread, all −1.

22b2The halving technique for sequences

Stands on 22b1, 12e (halving a trap), 3c (endless sets) and 22a2 (the climb technique).

Halving technique for sequences: every sequence with a floor and a ceiling has a thread that settles. Let every term lie in [u, v]. Call an interval crowded when endlessly many n have sₙ in it. [a, b] is crowded: every n does.

[u_j, v_j] \text{ crowded},\ m = \tfrac{u_j + v_j}{2} @@ start with [u₁, v₁] = [u, v]
\text{one of } [u_j, m],\ [m, v_j] \text{ is crowded} @@ if each held only finitely many n, so would their union (3c, 4j)
[u_{j+1}, v_{j+1}] = \text{the left crowded half if it is crowded, else the right} @@ a rule, so no choosing is spent
v_j - u_j = \tfrac{v - u}{2^{j-1}} @@ halved each time, as in 12e
k(j+1) = \text{the least } n > k(j) \text{ with } s_n \in [u_{j+1}, v_{j+1}] @@ exists: endlessly many such n
u_j \uparrow,\ v_j \downarrow,\ u_j \le v_j @@ nested halves
u_j \to c,\ v_j \to c @@ the climb and fall techniques (22a); their gap heads to 0
u_j \le s_{k(j)} \le v_j @@ each thread term lies in its half
\htmlClass{new}{s_{k(j)} \to c} \quad \blacksquare @@ squeezed between uⱼ and vⱼ (10)

Notice the line that says "a rule". At each stage the halving takes the left half if it is crowded. No pick is ever made freely, so 6's choosing axiom is not spent. The thread is built by a recipe from s alone.

22b3Every bounded sequence clusters

Stands on 22b2.

From here on, a real c is a gathering point of a sequence s when some thread of s settles at c. Accepted names: a cluster point, an accumulation point, or a limit point of the sequence.

Gathering technique: a sequence with a floor and a ceiling has a gathering point, and it settles exactly when it has only one. The first half is 22b2. For the second, suppose s has one gathering point c but does not settle at c. Then some ε has endlessly many terms at least ε from c. Those terms make a thread with a floor and a ceiling. By 22b2 it has a thread that settles, at a point at least ε from c. That is a second gathering point.

s \text{ bounded, one gathering point } c,\ s_n \not\to c @@ suppose (7e)
\exists \varepsilon > 0:\ |s_n - c| \ge \varepsilon \text{ for endlessly many } n @@ turn-about for ∀ and ∃ on ﹀ “settles”
\text{those terms form a bounded thread} @@ 22b1
\text{it has a thread settling at some } d,\ |d - c| \ge \varepsilon @@ 22b2; staying ε away survives the limit
\htmlClass{new}{d \ne c \text{ is a second gathering point}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a thread of a thread is a thread: contradiction

22b4Illegal moves

Stands on 22b1 to 22b3.

\text{a thread settles} \Rightarrow \text{the sequence settles}\,? @@ the even thread of (−1)ⁿ settles; the sequence wanders
s_n \text{ has no ceiling} \Rightarrow \text{no thread settles}\,? @@ 1, 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, … has the thread 1, 1, 1, …
s_{2n},\ s_{2n+1} \text{ each settle} \Rightarrow s \text{ settles}\,? @@ only if both settle at the same place
\text{halving picks the half freely}\,? @@ it takes the left half when it can: a rule, so no choice is spent

22b.4.5seamMany textbook proofs of this step say: pick any term in the crowded half, and do it again, endlessly. In 1890 one mathematician refused to let a proof make endlessly many free picks. Others later showed that his critics, and the books of analysis, made such picks everywhere without noticing. A catalogue of the results that rest on them filled a long paper.

The accepted names

A thread is a subsequence. The halving technique for sequences is the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem; a gathering point is a cluster point (or subsequential limit). The largest and smallest gathering points of a bounded sequence are its limit superior and limit inferior, written lim sup and lim inf.

Who said it first

Bernard Bolzano proved the halving argument in 1817, in a paper on the in-between value of rung 12. Karl Weierstrass taught it in his Berlin lectures from the 1860s, and it reached print through his students. In the choosing battle of 6b, the bill came due here: many textbook proofs of this step pick a term from each half freely, which spends countably many free picks; the left-half rule avoids that. In 22b.4.5, the refusal is Giuseppe Peano’s (1890); Ernst Zermelo (1908) showed that his critics used such picks, and Wacław Sierpiński (1918) catalogued the analysis that rests on them.
