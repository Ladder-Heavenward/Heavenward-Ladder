9e · technique

Reading and Drawing Graphs

A function whose inputs and outputs are reals is a set of pairs of reals. This step builds a picture for such pairs, the plane, and then learns to read it. The picture is a picture of two orders at once, the left entry read across and the right entry read up, and nothing of shape is meant. In it, a function is drawn as its own pairs, its graph. 9a's promises become a test you can see. And the standard families each get a reading: the kind whose output climbs the same amount at every step, the powers, one over the input, the square root and the absolute value. A drawing shows a few pairs; reading the formula tells you about all of them.

The plane is every pair of reals, across for the left entry and up for the right. The graph of a function is the function itself, its own pairs. Each column holds exactly one of its pairs over each input, and none elsewhere.

9e1The Plane: Pairs of Reals, and Their Picture

From here on, the plane is ℝ × ℝ, 2h's product of the line with itself: the set of all pairs (x, y) of reals. Its picture sets each pair out by two orders at once. The left entry is read across, less to the left and greater to the right, as on 8a's line. The right entry is read up, less below and greater above. A pair is drawn as a dot. The picture is a picture of the two orders and nothing more. No shape is meant, and distance in the plane, with the rest of its geometry, waits for rung 18.

As on 8a, the picture uses a scale. Across, equal differences of left entries are drawn as equal gaps, and likewise up, so that you can read a pair's entries off it. That is a convenience of drawing, not a fact about the pairs. 2i drew products as grids with the left set down the side. The plane's picture turns that grid on its side. The left entry goes across, so a fixed left entry is now a column, not a row. Keep that in mind; it is the one place the two pictures disagree.

From here on, the across axis is the set {(x, 0) : x ∈ ℝ} and the up axis the set {(0, y) : y ∈ ℝ}. Together they are the axes, and the one pair they share, (0, 0), is the origin. Each axis is drawn as a copy of 8a's picture of the line, marked with its reals. They are called the x-axis and the y-axis, after the letters usually written in the pairs.

From here on, for a real c, the column at c is {(c, y) : y ∈ ℝ}, every pair with left entry c. For a real d, the row at d is {(x, d) : x ∈ ℝ}, every pair with right entry d. In the picture a column is drawn up through c on the x-axis, and a row across through d on the y-axis.

The habit of setting pairs of numbers out across and up is old, and ℝ × ℝ with this picture is called the coordinate plane. For the philosopher the habit is named after, the pairs measured places on a figure. On this ladder they are pairs of reals first, and the figure, with its distances, comes on rung 18. The plane’s accepted name is the Cartesian plane, from the Latin form of that philosopher’s name.

9e2The Graph Is the Function

From here on, for a function f whose inputs and outputs are reals (f : A → B with A, B ⊆ ℝ), the graph of f is f itself: its set of pairs, a part of the plane. To draw f is to draw its graph.

The pairs where a graph meets the axes are called its intercepts. Where it meets the x-axis, at its zeros, are its x-intercepts. Where it meets the y-axis, at f(0), is its y-intercept.

From here on, when a function is given by a formula in x and no domain is named, its domain is the set of reals at which the formula names a real. No bottom is 0, and nothing under an even root is negative. It is called the natural domain. Its codomain is ℝ unless one is named.

9a's promises now read off the picture. Column-test technique: a part G of A × ℝ is a function from A to ℝ exactly when, for every c ∈ A, the column at c holds exactly one member of G. This is 9a's one-output, form (iii), read with “has left entry c” as “lies in the column at c”. And G ⊆ A × ℝ says every other column holds none of G. 9c's words read off the rows. Row-test technique: for f : A → B with A, B ⊆ ℝ, f is one-to-one exactly when no row holds two members of f; and f is onto B exactly when every row at a member of B holds at least one. Both are 9c's lines, with “f(u) = d” read as “(u, d) lies in the row at d”.

The swapped pairs of 9d can be drawn too. If (u, v) is in f, then (v, u) is in f⁻¹ (swapped-pairs). So the graph of f⁻¹ is the graph of f with every pair's coordinates swapped: left entry and right entry exchanged. Where f is one-to-one, the swapped pairs pass the column test. That is swapped-pairs-function seen in the picture: a row of f that held two pairs would be a column of the swapped set holding two.

Drawn, the swapped graph looks like the original seen in a mirror laid along the diagonal, where across and up are equal. Whether swapping really is a mirror is a claim about moving the plane, and rung 20 builds those moves.

9e3A Drawing Shows a Few Pairs; Reading Shows Them All

The usual way to draw a graph is a table: choose some inputs, compute outputs, set out the dots, join them. That shows a handful of the endlessly many pairs, and says nothing about the rest. An input where the formula names nothing can sit between two of your dots. So the job is to learn enough about all the pairs from the formula that the dots you draw only confirm what you already know. Five questions do it, each answered with tools you own.

From here on, f : ℝ → ℝ (or on a domain holding −x whenever it holds x) is an even function when f(−x) = f(x) for every input x. It is an odd function when f(−x) = −f(x). The names come from the powers: x ↦ xⁿ is even exactly when the number n is even (the even-or-odd-powers technique below).

From here on, even and odd are also said of functions. 4j's even and odd, said of numbers, keep their meanings.

For an even function, (u, v) is a pair of the graph exactly when (−u, v) is: what happens at an input happens at its negative. For an odd function, (u, v) exactly when (−u, −v). Run the questions on f(x) = x³ − 4x. It lives everywhere. Factor: x(x − 2)(x + 2), by difference-of-squares, so its zeros are −2, 0 and 2, and f(0) = 0. The sign chart gives − before −2, + between −2 and 0, − between 0 and 2, + after 2 (test 3: 3 · 1 · 5 = 15). For large x, x³ − 4x = x(x² − 4), and x² − 4 > 0 for x > 2. So it is positive, past 3 above 3 · 5 = 15, and past 10 above 960. It passes any real you name. That is held in trust here, until 9g proves it for every polynomial. And f(−x) = −x³ + 4x = −f(x): an odd function. Every one of those readings was a proof; the drawing only puts them together.

9e4The Climbing Kind

From here on, for reals m and b, the function x ↦ mx + b on ℝ is called the climbing kind, with step m and start b. In 5j's word, m is how much the output changes per step of the input. Each step of 1 across adds m to the output: a climb when m is positive, a fall when m is negative, level when m = 0. b = f(0) is where the graph meets the y-axis. The climbing kind is called a linear function, and b its y-intercept. m is also called the slope; slope is built on rung 13, so until then m is the step.

Start and step are 4d's words, said here of a linear function. 4d's start and step of a definition by repeating keep their meanings; what the words belong to says which.

Same-ratio technique: for f(x) = mx + b and reals s ≠ t, (f(s) − f(t))/(s − t) = m. And a function g : ℝ → ℝ for which this ratio is the same m for every s ≠ t is the linear function with step m and start g(0).

f(s) - f(t) = (ms + b) - (mt + b) @@ the rule at s and at t
\phantom{f(s) - f(t)} = ms - mt = \htmlClass{new}{m(s - t)} @@ negative-of-a-sum, collect; spread on ℝ backwards (8d)
\tfrac{f(s) - f(t)}{s - t} = m @@ multiply by (s − t)⁻¹: s − t ≠ 0 (reciprocal on ℝ)
\text{back: } t = 0,\ s \ne 0:\ \ \tfrac{g(s) - g(0)}{s} = m @@ the hypothesis, at the pair s, 0
g(s) = \htmlClass{new}{ms + g(0)} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by s, then adding-keeps-answers by g(0)
s = 0:\ \ g(0) = m \cdot 0 + g(0) \quad \blacksquare @@ anything-times-zero, plus-zero on ℝ; so g(x) = mx + g(0) at every x: equal-functions

So two pairs fix a linear function. Two-points-one-line technique: for (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) with x₁ ≠ x₂, exactly one linear function has both as pairs: step m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), start y₁ − mx₁. Existence is by substituting. Only one, by same-ratio, since any linear function through both has that ratio as its step. Through (1, 3) and (4, 9): m = 6/3 = 2, start 3 − 2 = 1, so x ↦ 2x + 1. Check at 4: 9. And “per” carries units. If x counts seconds and the output metres, then m is in m/s, metres per second. A person walking briskly at about 1.4 m/s, starting 50 m past a mark, is at 50 + 1.4t metres after t seconds (9·phys).

Same-step technique: two linear functions with the same step have graphs that never meet, unless they are the same. Take mx + b and mx + c. They meet where mx + b = mx + c, and adding −mx to both sides gives b = c. So if b ≠ c there is no such x. Every pair (x, mx + b) of the first is carried to (x, mx + c) of the second by the same move: straight up by c − b. Lines related this way are called parallel.

The move does not stretch or turn anything. It only slides the whole line, every point by the same amount. Two figures that one such move carries exactly onto each other have the same shape and size, and are called congruent. Parallel lines are the simplest case: congruent by a slide alone. Rung 20 builds all the moves that keep shape and size, and closes the word there.

9e5Powers, One Over x, the Square Root, the Absolute Value

Even-or-odd-powers technique: x ↦ xⁿ is an even function when n is even and an odd function when n is odd. By induction on n: (−x)⁰ = 1 = x⁰. And (−x)^S(n) = (−x)ⁿ · (−x). That is xⁿ · (−x) = −x^S(n) if (−x)ⁿ = xⁿ, and −xⁿ · (−x) = x^S(n) if (−x)ⁿ = −xⁿ (signs-come-out, two-negatives). ∎ So the even powers give the same output at x and −x. Every output is at least 0 (square-never-negative for squares; even powers are squares). Odd-powers-keep-order technique: for an odd number n and reals s < t, sⁿ < tⁿ. If 0 ≤ s, it is powers-keep-order. If t ≤ 0, apply powers-keep-order to −t < −s, and turn about with even-or-odd-powers and negative-reverses-order. If s < 0 < t, then sⁿ < 0 < tⁿ, by even-or-odd-powers and powers-keep-order. ∎

Near 0 and far out the powers sort themselves by order alone. For 0 < x < 1, multiplying by x lessens (keep-order for times on ℚ read in ℝ, with x < 1). So x > x² > x³ > x⁴: at ½ they are ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16. For x > 1 the order turns round: at 2 they are 2, 4, 8, 16. At 1 they all agree. So x⁴ hugs the x-axis more closely than x² between −1 and 1, and outgrows it past 1. Large inputs: for x > 1 and n ≥ 1, xⁿ ≥ x. So xⁿ passes any real past which x does (8e's counting-passes-any-real).

It is enough to keep you from joining the dots across 0.

9e6Domain and Range, Read Off

The domain comes from the formula: the natural domain. The range comes from running the formula backwards: a real y is an output exactly when the condition y = f(x) has an answer x in the domain (9a's ran f, unpacked). So finding the range is solving for x, as 9d solved for the inverse, and noting which y the solving needs.

f(x) = \tfrac{2x + 1}{x - 1},\ \ x \ne 1 @@ the natural domain: the bottom is 0 only at 1
y(x - 1) = 2x + 1 @@ y is an output: multiplying-keeps-answers by x − 1, not 0 on the domain
x(y - 2) = y + 1 @@ expand, gather the x terms (adding-keeps-answers), factor x out (spread on ℚ backwards)
y \ne 2:\ \ x = \tfrac{y + 1}{y - 2} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by (y − 2)⁻¹; at y = 2 the line reads 0 = 3: no answer
\tfrac{y + 1}{y - 2} \ne 1 @@ that x is in the domain: = 1 would give y + 1 = y − 2, so 1 = −2
\operatorname{ran} f = \htmlClass{new}{\mathbb R \setminus \{2\}} @@ every y ≠ 2 is reached, and 2 is not

Simpler cases: x² + 1 has range [1, ∞). y = x² + 1 needs y − 1 = x² ≥ 0 (square-never-negative), and then x = √(y − 1) works (square-root). √(x + 2) − 1 has domain [−2, ∞) and range [−1, ∞), since y + 1 = √(x + 2) needs y + 1 ≥ 0, and then x = (y + 1)² − 2. One more reading follows from composing. If g(x) = f(x − h), then g(u + h) = f(u). So (u, v) is a pair of f exactly when (u + h, v) is a pair of g: every pair's left entry grows by h. (x − 3)² takes its least output, 0, at 3, where x ↦ x² takes it at 0: 5i's displacement pattern, read in the pairs. And f(x) + k has the pairs (u, v + k). 9f uses both; rung 20 studies such changes of a whole picture in their own right.

9e8Running It Backwards

Handed dots and asked for a formula: two dots of a linear function give it by two-points-one-line. A power with a stretch, x ↦ px², through (2, 12) has 12 = 4p, so p = 3; check a third dot. Handed a set of pairs and asked whether it is a function: hunt for a column with two, or a column over the domain with none. Handed a function and asked for its range: solve y = f(x) for x and see which y survive. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The habit of setting pairs out across and up is named for René Descartes, and the philosopher of 9e.4.3 is Descartes.

Spivak points out that plotting a few points of x ↦ x² gives no assurance about what happens between them, and that some wrong pictures can be ruled out by algebra alone (if 0 < x < y then x² < y²), while others need ideas that come later.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 4

Accepted names: the coordinate plane is the Cartesian plane; the column test is the vertical line test (“vertical” and “horizontal” are geometry words that wait for rung 18; read them as “column” and “row”).
