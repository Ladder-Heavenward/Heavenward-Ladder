30o · technique

Waiting and Arriving

Calls come in to a dispatch centre in Truckee at no set times. Cars roll up to the one open ticket window at a ferry terminal, a few a minute, never on a timetable. Along the coast from Vancouver Island to northern California, the great earthquakes of the subduction zone have come every few centuries, the last on a winter night some three hundred years ago. Each of these is a stream of arrivals, each arrival rare in any short moment, and each independent of the others. Rung twenty two counted such arrivals in a fixed stretch of time. This step asks the other question: how long until the next one? The answer is the wait that forgets, found on the page before. Two streams together make one faster stream, and a race between them is won in a simple proportion. A machine that breaks and is mended at random settles to a steady share of its time working. And a line at one window settles too, so long as the window works faster than people arrive; as the two rates draw close, the line grows without limit.

In a stream of rare arrivals the wait for the next one is exponential, whatever happened before. The first of two independent waits is again exponential, at the sum of the rates. A chain that forgets its past, moving at steady rates, settles to steady chances, and a line at one window averages the busy share over the idle share.

30o1The Wait for a Rare Arrival

From here on, a rare-arrival stream of rate λ is a way of placing arrivals in time such that the count of arrivals in any stretch of length t is a rare count of average λt, P(k arrivals) = e^(−λt)(λt)ᵏ / k!, and the counts in stretches that do not overlap are independent. 22j and 22·stat built such counts from many small chances: each short moment has a small chance of an arrival, apart from every other moment.

First-gap technique: in a rare-arrival stream of rate λ, from any moment s the wait T to the next arrival has P(T > t) = e^(−λt): it is exponential of rate λ, with average 1/λ, and it is independent of the arrivals before s.

\{T > t\} = \{\text{no arrival in } (s, s + t]\} @@ the next arrival comes after s + t exactly when that stretch is empty
P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\tfrac{(\lambda t)^0}{0!} = e^{-\lambda t} @@ the rare count with k = 0 and average λt
\text{the count in } (s, s + t] \text{ is independent of the counts before } s @@ stretches that do not overlap are independent (30f2)
\htmlClass{new}{T \text{ is exponential of rate } \lambda,\ \ E T = \tfrac{1}{\lambda},\ \text{whatever came before}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the exponential density (30m2)

From the moment of an arrival, the gap to the next is exponential of rate λ as well, and the gaps are independent of each other. That needs the stream restarted at a moment that the stream itself chooses, and it is held in trust: its proof waits much higher up the ladder. The steps below use it only for the queue of 30o4.

\lambda \approx \tfrac{1}{500} \text{ per year} @@ a rough rate for the great earthquakes of the subduction zone off the coast, from the layers they left on the sea floor
P(\text{none in the next } 50 \text{ years}) = e^{-50/500} = e^{-0.1} \approx 0.905 @@ the first-gap technique
P(\text{at least one in } 50 \text{ years}) \approx 0.095 @@ the chance-rules technique (30f1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the years since the last, in January 1700, do not change it, in this model}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the forgets-the-wait technique (30m2): the stream has no memory

30o2Two Streams at Once

Two independent waits run side by side: a call for the police and a call for an ambulance, a bulb in the kitchen and a bulb in the hall. Which comes first, and when?

First-of-two technique: if S and T are independent, with P(S > t) = e^(−λt) and P(T > t) = e^(−μt), then the first of them, the lesser of S and T, has P(lesser > t) = e^(−(λ + μ)t); and S comes first with chance λ/(λ + μ).

P(\min(S, T) > t) = P(S > t \text{ and } T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\,e^{-\mu t} @@ the lesser is late exactly when both are; independent events multiply (30f2)
= e^{-(\lambda + \mu)t} @@ the first of the two is exponential of rate λ + μ
P(S < T) = \textstyle\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_s^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda s}\,\mu e^{-\mu t}\,dt\,ds @@ independent: the pair has the product density on the plane (30e1)
= \textstyle\int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda s}\,e^{-\mu s}\,ds @@ the inner integral is P(T > s); the either-order-sized technique (30e3) allows either order
\htmlClass{new}{P(S < T) = \tfrac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the total of the exponential density of rate λ + μ is 1

Two independent rare-arrival streams, of rates λ and μ, together make one rare-arrival stream of rate λ + μ: in any stretch the two counts add to a rare count of average (λ + μ)t, by the pages-add technique of 22·stat. Each arrival of the joined stream is from the first with chance λ/(λ + μ).

\text{police } 6 \text{ an hour},\ \ \text{fire and ambulance } 3 \text{ an hour} @@ two independent rare-arrival streams at a dispatch centre in Truckee
\text{all calls: } 9 \text{ an hour},\ \ \text{average wait } \tfrac{60}{9} \approx 6.7 \text{ minutes} @@ the pages-add technique; the first-gap technique
P(\text{a call within } 5 \text{ minutes}) = 1 - e^{-9\cdot 5/60} = 1 - e^{-0.75} \approx 0.528 @@ the first-of-two technique
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{the next call is for the police}) = \tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac23} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first-of-two technique

30o3Chains That Forget, in Continuous Time

A machine is either working or broken. While it works, its wait to break is exponential of rate α; while broken, its wait to be mended is exponential of rate β. By the forgets-the-wait technique, its future depends only on its state now, not on how long it has been there or what came before: it is a chain that forgets its past. Write p(t) for the chance that it is working at time t.

P(\text{breaks within } h \mid \text{working at } t) = 1 - e^{-\alpha h} = \alpha h + r(h) @@ the forgets-the-wait technique; r(h)/h heads to 0 as h does: (1 − e^(−αh))/h heads to α, the slope of e^(−αx) at 0 (the slope-of-exp technique, 17f)
p(t + h) = p(t)\,(1 - \alpha h) + (1 - p(t))\,\beta h + r_2(h) @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3) over the state at t; two changes within h carry chance at most αh · βh
\tfrac{p(t + h) - p(t)}{h} = \beta - (\alpha + \beta)\,p(t) + \tfrac{r_2(h)}{h} @@ divide by h
p'(t) = \beta - (\alpha + \beta)\,p(t) @@ let h head to 0
\htmlClass{new}{p'(t) = -(\alpha + \beta)\big(p(t) - \tfrac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same rule, written for the shift-to-rest technique

Two-state technique: a chain with two states that leaves the first at rate α and the second at rate β, with α + β > 0, is in the first state at time t with chance p(t) = β/(α + β) + (p(0) − β/(α + β)) e^(−(α + β)t). It settles at β/(α + β) from every start, and the gap shrinks by the factor e^(−(α + β)t).

p' = k\,(p - A),\ \ k = -(\alpha + \beta),\ \ A = \tfrac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} @@ the rate rule above
p(t) = A + (p(0) - A)\,e^{kt} @@ the shift-to-rest technique (24h1): the one solution with this start
\htmlClass{new}{p(t) \to \tfrac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}} \quad \blacksquare @@ k < 0, so e^(kt) heads to 0
  
\alpha = \tfrac{1}{200},\ \ \beta = \tfrac12 \text{ per hour} @@ a lift that breaks on average once in 200 hours of running and takes 2 hours on average to mend
\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta} = \tfrac{0.5}{0.505} \approx 0.990 @@ in the long run, working 99% of the time
p(0) = 0:\ \ p(4) = 0.990\,(1 - e^{-0.505 \times 4}) \approx 0.990 \times 0.867 @@ broken at the start of a morning
\htmlClass{new}{p(4) \approx 0.859} \quad \blacksquare @@ four hours later, still not quite settled

30o4A Line at One Window

People arrive at one window as a rare-arrival stream of rate λ. The window serves one at a time, and each service time is exponential of rate μ, independent of everything else. The number in the system, waiting or being served, is a chain that forgets its past on the states 0, 1, 2, …: it goes up one at rate λ, and down one at rate μ when it is not 0. Its steady chances pₙ are those that, once held, stay held: the rate rule of 30o3, written for every state, gives slope 0.

0 = \mu p_1 - \lambda p_0 @@ state 0: in from 1 at rate μ, out at rate λ (as in 30o3)
0 = \lambda p_{n-1} + \mu p_{n+1} - (\lambda + \mu)\,p_n,\ \ n \ge 1 @@ state n: in from n − 1 and from n + 1, out both ways
\mu p_{n+1} - \lambda p_n = \mu p_n - \lambda p_{n-1} @@ the second line, regrouped
\htmlClass{new}{\mu p_{n+1} = \lambda p_n \text{ for every } n \ge 0} @@ induction on n (4c), from the first line: the flow up across each cut equals the flow down

Steady-line technique: if λ < μ, write ρ = λ/μ. The steady chance of n in the system is (1 − ρ)ρⁿ, the window is busy with chance ρ, and the average number in the system is ρ/(1 − ρ).

p_n = \rho^n p_0 @@ the cut line above, n times
1 = \textstyle\sum_n \rho^n p_0 = \tfrac{p_0}{1 - \rho} @@ the chances add to 1; the endless-ratio-sum technique (15i3), ρ < 1
p_0 = 1 - \rho,\ \ p_n = (1 - \rho)\rho^n @@ busy with chance 1 − p₀ = ρ
\textstyle\sum_n n\rho^n = \rho\,\tfrac{d}{d\rho}\tfrac{1}{1 - \rho} = \tfrac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} @@ the term-by-term technique (23e3), inside the reach |ρ| < 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{average number} = (1 - \rho)\tfrac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} = \tfrac{\rho}{1 - \rho}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average of a count (30g1)

How long does a newcomer spend there? One who finds n ahead waits for n services and then its own. The one being served forgets how long it has been served (30m2), so each of the n + 1 takes 1/μ on average. That arrivals of a rare stream find n ahead with the steady chance pₙ is held in trust: its proof waits much higher up the ladder. With it, the average time is the average of (n + 1)/μ, which is (ρ/(1 − ρ) + 1)/μ = 1/(μ − λ).

\lambda = 50,\ \ \mu = 60 \text{ an hour}:\ \ \rho = \tfrac56 @@ cars arrive at 50 an hour; one ticket window serves 60 an hour
\text{average in the system } \tfrac{5/6}{1/6} = 5,\ \ \text{average time } \tfrac{1}{60 - 50} \text{ h} = 6 \text{ minutes} @@ the steady-line technique, and the average time
\lambda = 55:\ \ 11 \text{ cars},\ 12 \text{ minutes};\ \ \lambda = 58:\ \ 29 \text{ cars},\ 30 \text{ minutes} @@ the same window, a busier afternoon
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a window busy } 97\% \text{ of the time keeps a line of } 29} \quad \blacksquare @@ ρ/(1 − ρ) grows without bound as ρ heads to 1

The accepted names

A rare-arrival stream is a Poisson process, λ its rate or intensity; the first-gap technique says its interarrival times are exponential, and the property held in trust, the stream restarting at an arrival, is its strong Markov property. Joining two streams is superposition, and keeping each arrival with chance p is thinning. The first-of-two technique is the minimum of independent exponentials, or competing exponential clocks. A chain that forgets its past in continuous time is a continuous-time Markov chain, α, b and λ, μ its transition rates, and the rate rules its forward (Kolmogorov) equations. Its steady chances are the stationary distribution; the cut line is detailed balance for a birth–death chain. The line at one window is the M/M/1 queue, ρ its utilization or traffic intensity. That arrivals of a Poisson stream see the steady chances is PASTA, "Poisson arrivals see time averages"; average number equals arrival rate times average time is Little's law. The window with no waiting room is a loss system, and the share of cars turned away is Erlang's loss formula for one line. That a rider arriving at random waits a whole average gap is the waiting-time (or inspection) paradox.

Who said it first

Agner Krarup Erlang, an engineer at the Copenhagen Telephone Company, used the Poisson stream for calls in 1909 and gave the loss formula in 1917; the unit of telephone traffic is named the erlang after him. Andrey Kolmogorov wrote the rate rules for chains in continuous time in 1931. David Kendall gave the notation M/M/1 in 1953. John Little proved his law in 1961, and Ronald Wolff proved PASTA in 1982. The last great earthquake of the Cascadia subduction zone was on 26 January 1700: the drowned forests of the coast give the year, and a tsunami with no felt earthquake recorded along the coast of Japan gives the day and hour, as Kenji Satake and his colleagues showed in 1996. Chris Goldfinger and colleagues dated the layers of mud left on the sea floor by its earlier earthquakes, giving a long record with an average gap of several centuries.
