28m · technique

Integrals by Hand

The proof steps built a total over a rectangle, showed it can be taken in either order, and found the extra factor a change of measures brings in. This step is the workshop. A region with curved edges is cut into strips, and the first job is to read its edges off a sketch. Sometimes the strips one way lead to a function whose area no table holds, while the strips the other way make it easy, so swapping the order is a real tool and not a trick. Round regions are easier in distance and angle, with the extra factor of the distance. In space the same ideas give two more sets of measures: one for cylinders and one for balls. With them the volume of a ball, the water a storm drops on a valley, and the weight of the earth from a guess at how dense its insides are, all come out of one kind of total.

Read the edges of the region off a sketch, and total in strips. If one order is hard, swap it: the region is the same, described the other way. Round regions take distance and angle, with an extra factor of the distance. In space, cylinders take an extra distance, and balls take the distance squared times the sine of the angle down from the top.

28m1Swapping the Order

28i3 totalled a region slanted in x, a ≤ x ≤ b with φ₁(x) ≤ y ≤ φ₂(x), as ∫ from a to b of ∫ from φ₁(x) to φ₂(x) of f dy dx. With the roles of x and y swapped, a region slanted in y, c ≤ y ≤ d with ψ₁(y) ≤ x ≤ ψ₂(y), is totalled the other way. In both the edges are smoothly sloped, and the lower edge is below the upper one inside.

Order-swap technique: if a region D is slanted in x and also slanted in y, and f is continuous on D, the two totals in strips are equal: the total from a to b of the total from φ₁(x) to φ₂(x) of f dy dx is the total from c to d of the total from ψ₁(y) to ψ₂(y) of f dx dy.

\textstyle\int_a^b\!\int_{\varphi_1(x)}^{\varphi_2(x)} f\,dy\,dx = \iint_D f @@ D slanted in x: the slanted regions of 28i3
\textstyle\int_c^d\!\int_{\psi_1(y)}^{\psi_2(y)} f\,dx\,dy = \iint_D f @@ the same lines with x and y swapped: the map T(t, y) = (ψ₁(y) + t(ψ₂(y) − ψ₁(y)), y) has det ψ₂ − ψ₁ up to sign, and 28i3 takes its size
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_a^b\!\int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} f\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d\!\int_{\psi_1}^{\psi_2} f\,dx\,dy} \quad \blacksquare @@ one region, one total
  
\textstyle\int_0^1\!\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx @@ the inner total needs an antiderivative of e^(y²), which no table of rung 16 holds
D:\ 0 \le x \le 1,\ x \le y \le 1 \ \ = \ \ 0 \le y \le 1,\ 0 \le x \le y @@ the triangle with corners (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1), read off a sketch the other way
= \textstyle\int_0^1\!\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,dy @@ the order-swap technique; the inner total is y e^(y²), as y is fixed
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac12\big[e^{y^2}\big]_0^1 = \tfrac{e - 1}{2} \approx 0.859} \quad \blacksquare @@ substitution (16d), u = y²

The hard part is the sketch, not the totals. Draw the region. Draw one strip the first way, and read where it enters and leaves. Then draw one strip the other way, and read again. The numbers at the ends of the outer total are always plain numbers; the ends of the inner total may hold the outer letter.

28m2Round Regions

A region slanted in θ is α ≤ θ ≤ β with r₁(θ) ≤ r ≤ r₂(θ), between two curves of 21r, with β − α at most 2π and 0 ≤ r₁ ≤ r₂.

Round-slab technique: if f is continuous on a region D slanted in θ, then ∬D f = ∫ from α to β of ∫ from r₁(θ) to r₂(θ) of f(r cos θ, r sin θ) r dr dθ. With f = 1, the area of D is ∫ from α to β of ½(r₂² − r₁²) dθ.

T(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta),\ \ |\det DT| = r @@ round measures (28i3)
\textstyle\iint_D f = \iint_{(\theta, r)} f(T)\,r @@ the change-of-variables technique (28i3); T is one-to-one once a thin ring r < ε and a thin wedge at θ = β are cut off, and their totals head to 0, as f is bounded
= \textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta\!\int_{r_1(\theta)}^{r_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta @@ the region in (θ, r) is slanted in θ: 28i3 again
\htmlClass{new}{f = 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta \tfrac12\big(r_2^2 - r_1^2\big)\,d\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the inner total of r is ½r² between the edges: 21r4’s swept area, now with an inner edge too
  
z = 4 - x^2 - y^2 \ge 0 \text{ over the disc } x^2 + y^2 \le 4 @@ a bowl turned upside down, 4 high
= \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^2 (4 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta @@ the round-slab technique: x² + y² = r²
= 2\pi\big[2r^2 - \tfrac14 r^4\big]_0^2 = 2\pi(8 - 4) @@ the inner total does not hold θ
\htmlClass{new}{= 8\pi \approx 25.1} \quad \blacksquare @@ half the cylinder of radius 2 and height 4 that holds it, 16π

In across-and-up measures the same bowl is ∫ from −2 to 2 of ∫ from −√(4 − x²) to √(4 − x²) of (4 − x² − y²) dy dx, with roots at both ends of every strip. The round measures fit the round edge, so the edges become plain numbers.

28m3Cylinders and Balls

28j3 took totals over a box in space in any order, ∭ f. The change-of-variables technique runs in space word for word, with small cubes in place of small squares and the size of the determinant of a 3 × 3 slope grid, the volume scale of 27e, in place of the area scale. Two sets of measures fit round solids. Cylinder measures: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, and z. Ball measures: ρ, the distance from 0; φ, the angle down from the top, from 0 to π; and θ, the turn round the upright, so x = ρ sin φ cos θ, y = ρ sin φ sin θ, z = ρ cos φ.

Round-space technique: in cylinder measures the volume scale is r. In ball measures it is ρ² sin φ. So a total over a round solid is ∭ f r dr dθ dz in the first, and ∭ f ρ² sin φ dρ dφ dθ in the second.

DT = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\varphi\cos\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\cos\theta & -\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta \\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta \\ \cos\varphi & -\rho\sin\varphi & 0 \end{pmatrix} @@ the one-knob technique (28a2), one column for each of ρ, φ, θ
\det DT = \cos\varphi\cdot\rho^2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi - \rho\sin\varphi\cdot(-\rho\sin^2\varphi) @@ the expansion technique (27k2) along the bottom row; sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 (21c) in each stand-in
= \rho^2\sin\varphi\,(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = \rho^2\sin\varphi @@ 21c again
\sin\varphi \ge 0 \text{ for } 0 \le \varphi \le \pi @@ so the size is ρ² sin φ itself
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cylinders: } r;\ \ \text{balls: } \rho^2\sin\varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ for cylinders, expand along the z row: 1 times the round-measure grid of 28i3, det r
  
\text{the ball of radius } a:\ 0 \le \rho \le a,\ 0 \le \varphi \le \pi,\ 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi @@ a box in ball measures
= \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^a \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta @@ the round-space technique, f = 1
= 2\pi\cdot\big[-\cos\varphi\big]_0^\pi\cdot\tfrac{a^3}{3} = 2\pi\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac{a^3}{3} @@ the three totals do not hold each other’s letters
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac43\pi a^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ 21o’s ball technique, reached without slices

Latitude on a globe, as in 21·cb18, is measured up from the equator, not down from the top. With latitude in place of φ the scale is ρ² cos of the latitude. Either way the scale is 0 on the upright axis, where a whole circle of θ shrinks to one point.

The accepted names

Totals in strips are iterated integrals; a region slanted in x is a type I (or vertically simple) region, and one slanted in y a type II region. The order-swap technique is reversing (or switching) the order of integration, a case of Fubini’s theorem. Round measures are polar coordinates, cylinder measures cylindrical coordinates, and ball measures spherical coordinates, with ρ the radial distance, φ the polar (or zenith) angle and θ the azimuth. The volume scale is the Jacobian determinant, and the shorthand dV = ρ² sin φ dρ dφ dθ is the volume element. A triple total is a triple integral, ∭ f dV. The heart-shaped curve r = 1 + cos θ is the cardioid.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler swapped the order of double integrals and changed their variables in 1769. Joseph-Louis Lagrange used spherical coordinates and their volume factor in triple integrals in 1773, working out the pull of an ellipsoid. The density of the earth was first measured by Henry Cavendish in 1798, at about 5.45 times that of water, from the pull between lead balls in a shed in London. The modern figure is about 5.51.
