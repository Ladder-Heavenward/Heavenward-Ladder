8·cb6 · newsflash for 6·phys

Equal temperament: the number tuning needed

6·phys tuned your bass and found that no scale built from pure ratios closes: twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by a comma. It described the way out, equal temperament: twelve equal steps between neighbouring keys, stacking to exactly 2. It noted that no fraction can be that step. Then it gave the open strings as numbers measured off a tuner, because computing them needed rung 8. Here is rung 8. The step is the twelfth root of 2, a real number that 8f proved exists. 8i's fractional powers give every tempered interval. And the tuner's numbers come out of the low A, at 55 vibrations a second, by arithmetic. Then the frets, placed for this number, and the slow beats that a tempered fifth makes against a pure one.

The tempered step is the real whose twelfth power is 2. Every tempered interval is 2 to a power in twelfths, and every fifth gives up the same twelfth share of the comma.

The step exists, and is no fraction

Equal temperament asks for a ratio s with s¹² = 2: twelve equal steps from a note to its octave. 8f's roots-of-every-order says there is exactly one positive real with that property, and 8f's mark names it: s = ¹²√2. By 8i's line for fractional powers it is also 2^(1/12), and the fraction-exponent techniques give s^k = 2^(k/12) for every integer k. No fraction s has s¹² = 2, as 6·phys asked you to prove: count the 2s in the prime lists of p¹² and 2q¹², a multiple of 12 against one more than a multiple of 12. So the step is irrational, and 6·phys's tuning could not be done on the fractions. On the line it can. The digit rule of 8·cb5, run with exact whole-number checks, gives

Where the comma went

In equal temperament the cycle of fifths closes exactly. Twelve tempered fifths make (2^(7/12))¹² = 2⁷, seven octaves on the nose, by the fraction-exponent techniques. The comma 3¹²/2¹⁹ that 6·phys's stacks of pure fifths overshot by has been shared out. How much did each fifth give up?

\tfrac{3/2}{2^{7/12}} \text{ to the power } 12 = \tfrac{3^{12}/2^{12}}{2^{7}} @@ fraction-exponent techniques, and power-of-a-fraction (6·phys) for (3/2)¹²
= \tfrac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} @@ powers-add-their-counts: 2¹² · 2⁷ = 2¹⁹; the comma
\tfrac{3/2}{2^{7/12}} = \htmlClass{new}{\Big(\tfrac{3^{12}}{2^{19}}\Big)^{1/12}} \approx 1.00113 @@ both positive: one-root's method with twelfth powers (roots-of-every-order)

So each tempered fifth is the pure fifth made smaller by the twelfth root of the comma, about a part in 885. That is too little for most ears to hear as out of tune, and enough to make the two beat slowly when played together. The thirds pay more. The tempered major third 1.25992 is larger than the just 5/4 by about a part in 126, which is why equal-tempered thirds sound restless. The price buys something 6·phys's pure tunings never could: every key is the same, because every step is the same ratio.

Your bass, computed

6·phys's note names put E₁ five steps below A₁ (A₁, A♭₁, G₁, G♭₁, F₁, E₁), D₂ five steps above it, and G₂ ten steps above. A₁ = 55 Hz exact, by international agreement through A₄ = 440 Hz. So:

E_1 = 55 \cdot 2^{-5/12}\ \text{Hz} \approx \htmlClass{new}{41.2034}\ \text{Hz} @@ five steps down: 8i's negative and fractional powers
A_1 = 55\ \text{Hz} @@ the agreed starting place
D_2 = 55 \cdot 2^{5/12}\ \text{Hz} \approx \htmlClass{new}{73.4162}\ \text{Hz} @@ five steps up
G_2 = 55 \cdot 2^{10/12}\ \text{Hz} \approx \htmlClass{new}{97.9989}\ \text{Hz} @@ ten steps up

Rounded to two places these are 6·phys's tuner values, 41.20, 73.42 and 98.00 Hz, now computed instead of measured. 6·phys held them in trust as measured data until rung 8, and that flag is closed here. Each string is a tempered fourth, 2^(5/12), above the one below it. And the harmonic tuning of 6·phys can now be read exactly. A string touched lightly a quarter of the way along sounds its 4th harmonic; a third of the way, its 3rd. Tuning D₂ to A₁ compares A₁'s 4th harmonic, 220 Hz, with D₂'s 3rd, 3 · 73.4162 ≈ 220.2486 Hz. That is about 0.249 beats a second, one beat every four seconds, because a tempered fourth is not a pure 4/3. Tuned until the beats stop, the strings would be pure fourths apart and would drift from the tuner, as 6·phys found.

The frets

6·phys took the law of strings: at one tension, a string's frequency is inversely proportional (5j) to its sounding length. A fret that raises the note k steps must therefore shorten the sounding length from the scale length L to L · 2^(−k/12). So it sits at L(1 − 2^(−k/12)) from the nut, the end the string starts sounding from.

L = 34\ \text{in} = 86.36\ \text{cm} @@ a common bass scale length; 1 in = 2.54 cm (5j)
\text{fret 1}:\ 86.36\,(1 - 2^{-1/12}) \approx \htmlClass{new}{4.847}\ \text{cm} @@ one step up
\text{fret 5}:\ 86.36\,(1 - 2^{-5/12}) \approx 21.663\ \text{cm} @@ a fourth up: the next string's open note
\text{fret 7}:\ 86.36\,(1 - 2^{-7/12}) \approx 28.722\ \text{cm} @@ a fifth up
\text{fret 12}:\ 86.36\,(1 - 2^{-1}) = \htmlClass{new}{43.18}\ \text{cm} @@ exactly half: the octave, the only fraction among them

The gaps between frets shrink, each 2^(−1/12) times the one before. Builders long used a rule of eighteen: place each fret one eighteenth of the remaining length along. That makes each step 18/17 ≈ 1.05882 instead of 1.05946. A lute player and teacher of music in the 1500s used that ratio for lute frets. Twelve such steps stack to (18/17)¹² ≈ 1.9856, short of 2. So the twelfth fret lands about 3 mm nearer the nut than half the string, and its octave sounds flat. The exact rule is one 17.817th of the remaining length, since 1/(1 − 2^(−1/12)) ≈ 17.817: a number no fraction with a small bottom quite reaches.

Beats against pure intervals

Two notes a tempered fifth apart, f and f · 2^(7/12), have harmonics that nearly meet: the 3rd of the lower and the 2nd of the upper. A pure fifth would make them meet exactly. The difference is 6·phys's beats.

f = 220\ \text{Hz (A}_3\text{)}:\ \ 3f = 660\ \text{Hz} @@ the 3rd harmonic of the lower note
2 \cdot 220 \cdot 2^{7/12} \approx 659.255\ \text{Hz} @@ the 2nd harmonic of the tempered fifth, E₄
660 - 659.255 \approx \htmlClass{new}{0.745} \text{ beats a second} @@ about three beats every four seconds

The beat rate grows with the pitch: the same fifth on A₁ beats at about 0.186 a second, on A₃ about 0.745. Take a tempered major third on A₃, 220 · 2^(4/12) ≈ 277.18 Hz, against the just 275 Hz. It gives 4 · 277.18 − 5 · 220 ≈ 8.73 beats a second, a fast shimmer: the price of every key being alike. One worker in China (1584) and another in the Netherlands (around 1605) each worked the step out to many places, and a third, in France, printed tables for it. On this ladder it took eight rungs to say what number they were computing.

Running it backwards

Handed a frequency and asked how many steps above A₁ it is, find k with 55 · 2^(k/12) nearest it. Handed two strings' frequencies, divide, and compare with 2^(5/12). Handed a fret's distance from the nut, divide by the scale length and solve 1 − 2^(−k/12) for k by trying whole k. Found upward; written downward.

That completes rung 8. The line has no holes, and every number your bass needed is on it.

Who said it first

The law of strings that 6·phys used was set out by Marin Mersenne in 1636. The lute player who used 18/17 for frets was Vincenzo Galilei, Galileo’s father. The step was worked out to many places by Zhu Zaiyu in China (1584) and Simon Stevin in the Netherlands (around 1605), and Mersenne printed tables for it. The composer of 8·cb6.4.3 is Johann Sebastian Bach, whose set is The Well-Tempered Clavier.

Accepted names: the comma of twelve fifths over seven octaves is the Pythagorean comma.
