∞12 · newsflash

Which numbers are a square plus twice a square?

Which numbers are a square plus twice a square? One is, and two, and three, which is one and twice one. Five is not, and seven is not, however hard you look. Eleven is nine and twice one. In letters of the 1650s a French magistrate claimed that a prime is a square plus twice a square exactly when it leaves one or three on dividing by eight, and gave no proof. The proof took a century, and it opened a whole subject: the expressions of the second degree in two whole numbers, which numbers each one reaches, and when two of them are secretly the same. Count the ones that are truly different, at one discriminant, and you get the class number. When it is one, a clock tells you everything about which primes an expression reaches. When it is more, a clock may still be enough, or it may not, and then the answer lives in the roots of a cubic. The class number is one for exactly thirteen negative discriminants. That the list is complete was proved in 1952 by a man outside the universities, was not believed, was proved again in 1966 and 1967, and only then was his proof read again and found right.

Forms px² + qxy + ry² that a change of variables of determinant 1 carries one to the other reach the same numbers and share the discriminant q² − 4pr. Every positive form is alike to a reduced one, with |q| ≤ p ≤ r, and there are finitely many: the class number h(D). An odd prime not dividing D is reached by some form of discriminant D exactly when D is a square on its clock. At D = −8 there is one form, so the clock of 8 decides. At D = −20 there are two, and the clock of 20 still sorts them. At D = −23 there are three kinds, and no clock sorts them. h(D) = 1 for thirteen D, the last −163, which is why n² + n + 41 is prime for n from 0 to 39.

sky12.1Forms, and changing the variables

A form is f(x, y) = px² + qxy + ry² with whole p, q, r. It reaches a whole number n when f(x, y) = n for some whole x, y. The form x² + 2y² reaches 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 11, and never 5 or 7. Change the variables by a grid M with rows (s, t) and (u, v), whole entries and det 1: f′(x, y) = f(sx + ty, ux + vy). Its undo has rows (v, −t) and (−u, s), whole again (27k), so f and f′ reach exactly the same numbers. Call such f and f′ alike.

Alike-forms technique: if f′(w) = f(Mw) with M a grid of whole numbers of det 1, then f and f′ reach the same numbers and have the same discriminant D = q² − 4pr.

f(x, y) = w^{\mathsf T} G w,\ \ G = \begin{pmatrix} p & q/2 \\ q/2 & r \end{pmatrix},\ w = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} @@ the two-slot technique (27h2), symmetric
f'(w) = (Mw)^{\mathsf T} G (Mw) = w^{\mathsf T}(M^{\mathsf T} G M)w @@ change the variables
\det(M^{\mathsf T}GM) = \det M^{\mathsf T}\,\det G\,\det M = \det G @@ the det-product technique (27e3), det M = 1
\htmlClass{new}{D = q^2 - 4pr = -4\det G \text{ is the same for } f \text{ and } f'} \quad \blacksquare @@ det G = pr − q²/4
3x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2:\ \ D = 16 - 24 = -8 @@ the same D as x² + 2y²: alike? The next footing decides

When D < 0 and p > 0, completing the square gives 4p · f(x, y) = (2px + qy)² − D y², two squares with a plus between them, so f never goes below 0 and is 0 only at x = y = 0. Call such a form positive. Every form on this page is positive. 42k’s x² − ny² is not: its D = 4n is above 0, and it reaches 1 endlessly often.

sky12.2Reduced forms, and how many

Two changes of det 1 do all the work. The swap S, (x, y) ↦ (−y, x), sends (p, q, r) to (r, −q, p). The slide Tₖ, (x, y) ↦ (x + ky, y), sends (p, q, r) to (p, q + 2kp, pk² + qk + r). A slide moves q in steps of 2p, so it can bring q between −p and p. If then p > r, swap, and p falls. A positive whole number cannot fall for ever.

Form-reduction technique: every positive form is alike to one with |q| ≤ p ≤ r, and then p ≤ √(−D/3); so each D has finitely many such forms.

|q| \le p \le r \Rightarrow -D = 4pr - q^2 \ge 4p^2 - p^2 = 3p^2 @@ the bound on p
(3, 4, 2) \xrightarrow{T_{-1}} (3, -2, 1) @@ q = 4 − 6, r = 3 − 4 + 2
\xrightarrow{S} (1, 2, 3) \xrightarrow{T_{-1}} (1, 0, 2) @@ p falls from 3 to 1
\htmlClass{new}{3x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2 \text{ is alike to } x^2 + 2y^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the form-reduction technique

Such a form, with q ≥ 0 when |q| = p or p = r, and p, q, r sharing no factor, is reduced; the count of reduced forms of discriminant D is the class number h(D). Held in trust: no two different reduced forms are alike, so h(D) counts the kinds exactly. For D = −8, p ≤ 1.63 forces p = 1, then q is even, so q = 0 and r = 2: h(−8) = 1. For D = −20: x² + 5y² and 2x² + 2xy + 3y², so h(−20) = 2. For D = −23: x² + xy + 6y² and 2x² ± xy + 3y², three. The last two are carried one to the other by (x, y) ↦ (x, −y), whose det is −1, so they count as two kinds.

sky12.3Which primes, on a clock

Let ℓ be an odd prime that does not divide D.

Prime-on-a-form technique: ℓ is reached by some form of discriminant D exactly when D is a square on the clock of ℓ.

\ell = f(x_0, y_0) \Rightarrow \gcd(x_0, y_0) = 1 @@ a common factor g would give g² ∣ ℓ
x_0 v - s y_0 = 1 \text{ for some whole } s, v @@ the gcd run backwards (4j13)
f(x_0X + sY,\ y_0X + vY) = \ell X^2 + q'XY + r'Y^2 @@ det 1; the first coefficient is f(x₀, y₀)
D = q'^2 - 4\ell r' \equiv q'^2 \pmod{\ell} @@ the alike-forms technique: D is a square
D \equiv k^2 \pmod \ell:\ \ q \equiv k \pmod \ell,\ \ q \equiv D \pmod 2,\ \ r = (q^2 - D)/4\ell @@ the remainder technique (42c); then q² ≡ D on the clock of 4ℓ
\htmlClass{new}{\ell x^2 + qxy + ry^2 \text{ has discriminant } D \text{ and reaches } \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ at x = 1, y = 0

At D = −8 there is one form, so an odd prime is a square plus twice a square exactly when −2 is a square on its clock. By 42f, −1 is a square there when ℓ ≡ 1 (mod 4) and 2 when ℓ ≡ 1 or 7 (mod 8), so −2, which needs both or neither, when ℓ ≡ 1 or 3 (mod 8): the magistrate’s claim. For ℓ = 59: 13² = 169 ≡ −8, q = 13 − 59 = −46, r = (2116 + 8)/236 = 9. The form (59, −46, 9) reduces by S, T₋₃, S, T₋₂, S through (9, −8, 2) and (2, 0, 1) to (1, 0, 2), and following the moves back gives 59 = 3² + 2 · 5². The technique says a way exists; the reduction finds it.

sky12.4Two kinds, and a clock that sorts them

At D = −20, −20 is a square on the clock of an odd prime ℓ ≠ 5 exactly when ℓ ≡ 1, 3, 7 or 9 (mod 20). Each such ℓ is reached by x² + 5y² or by 2x² + 2xy + 3y². Which one?

Two-kinds technique: an odd prime ℓ ≠ 5 is x² + 5y² exactly when ℓ ≡ 1 or 9 (mod 20), and 2x² + 2xy + 3y² exactly when ℓ ≡ 3 or 7 (mod 20).

x^2 + 5y^2 \equiv x^2 \equiv 1, 4 \pmod 5;\ \ \equiv 1 \pmod 4 @@ ℓ odd: one of x, y even, and 5 ∤ x
2x^2 + 2xy + 3y^2 \equiv 2(x + 3y)^2 \equiv 2, 3 \pmod 5;\ \ \equiv 3 \pmod 4 @@ 2(x + 3y)² = 2x² + 12xy + 18y²; and y must be odd
\text{hours of 20 reached: } \{1, 9\} \text{ and } \{3, 7\} @@ the remainder technique (42c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no odd prime is reached by both, and its hour on the clock of 20 says which}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the prime-on-a-form technique gives every ℓ ≡ 1, 3, 7, 9 to one of them

So 29 = 3² + 5 · 2² and 41 = 6² + 5 · 1², while 3, 7 and 23 come from the other form, at (0, 1), (1, 1) and (2, −3). The two kinds are 43e4’s two kinds of ideals in ℤ[ω], ω = √−5. x² + 5y² is the size N(x + yω) of a member. The other is a size from P = (2, 1 + ω), halved: N(2x + (1 + ω)y) = (2x + y)² + 5y² = 2(2x² + 2xy + 3y²). The gap in 43e2, no member of size 2 or 3, is the statement that 2 and 3 belong to the second kind.

sky12.5Three kinds, and no clock

At D = −23 the kinds are x² + xy + 6y² and 2x² ± xy + 3y², and the last two reach the same numbers. By the square-swap technique (42f4), −23 is a square on the clock of a prime ℓ ≠ 23 exactly when ℓ is a square on the clock of 23: ℓ ≡ 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 16 or 18. Then ℓ is reached by one of the two. Is there a clock that sorts them, as 20 did?

2x^2 + xy + 3y^2:\ \ 2, 3, 13, 29, 31, 41, 47, 71, 73, \ldots @@ the primes it reaches below 100
x^2 + xy + 6y^2:\ \ 23, 59, 101, 167, 173, \ldots @@ below 200; 59 at (5, 2)
59 \equiv 13 \pmod{23},\ \ 47 \equiv 1 \pmod{23} @@ 59 shares an hour with 13, and 47 with 1, which x² + xy + 6y² reaches at (1, 0)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one hour, two kinds: the clock of 23 cannot tell}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same happens on every clock tried

Cubic-split technique: a prime ℓ ≠ 23 is x² + xy + 6y² exactly when x³ − x − 1 has three roots on the clock of ℓ. Held in trust: its proof is much higher up the ladder. On the clock of 59 the roots are 4, 13 and 42 (4³ − 4 − 1 = 59); on the clock of 13 there are none. The discriminant of x³ − x − 1 is −4(−1)³ − 27(−1)² = −23, not a square, so by 44k4 its group is S₃, whose members do not all commute. Held in trust again: a clock sorts the primes exactly when that group commutes, so here no clock of any size can.

sky12.6Class number one, and forty-one

When h(D) = 1 the prime-on-a-form technique answers everything with one clock. Running the form-reduction technique over every D from −3 down to −2000 finds h(D) = 1 at −3, −4, −7, −8, −11, −12, −16, −19, −27, −28, −43, −67 and −163, and nowhere else; the 1952 to 1967 proofs show there is none past the end of any search. The last gives x² + xy + 41y².

Forty-one technique: n² + n + 41 is prime for every n from 0 to 39.

f = x^2 + xy + 41y^2:\ \ 4f = (2x + y)^2 + 163y^2 @@ complete the square; D = 1 − 164 = −163
y \ne 0 \Rightarrow f \ge 163/4 \Rightarrow f \ge 41;\ \ y = 0 \Rightarrow f = x^2 @@ so no prime below 41 is reached
N = n^2 + n + 41 \le 1601 < 41^2;\ \ \text{if } N \text{ is not prime, some prime } \ell < 41 \text{ divides it} @@ ℓ is odd, since N is odd
4N = (2n + 1)^2 + 163 \Rightarrow -163 \equiv (2n + 1)^2 \pmod \ell @@ a square on the clock of ℓ
\ell \text{ is reached by } f @@ the prime-on-a-form technique, and f is the only kind: h(−163) = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\ell \ge 41,\ \text{against } \ell < 41:\ N \text{ is prime}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the forty-one technique

At n = 40 the reason runs out: 40² + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41². The same proof with x² + xy + 17y² (D = −67) gives primes for n from 0 to 15, and with 11 (D = −43) for n from 0 to 9. A near miss of another kind: e^(π√163) = 262 537 412 640 768 743.999 999 999 999 25…, within a millionth of a millionth of a whole number, for a reason that runs through the same class number.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞12.oq.1Forms with positive discriminant, like x² − 2y², have class numbers too, and very often the class number is 1: at D = 5, 8, 13, 17, 29, 37, 41 and on. Are there endlessly many positive discriminants with class number 1? It was guessed in 1801 and is still open.
Why it is hard: a positive-discriminant form reaches each value endlessly often, through 42k’s units, and the class number comes tangled with the size of the smallest unit. Nobody can pull the two apart well enough to count.
∞12.oq.2The class number of −D grows roughly like √D, and since 1935 it is known that h(−D) is larger than any power D^(1/2 − δ) once D is large enough. But the proof cannot say how large is large enough: it splits on whether a certain function has a zero very close to 1, and gives no number either way. The best bound with numbers (1976 and 1983) grows only like log D.
Can a bound like √D / log D be proved with every constant written down?
∞12.oq.3Every search has found exactly 18 odd numbers that the form x² + y² + 10z² misses: 3, 7, 21, 31, 33, 43, 67, 79, 87, 133, 217, 219, 223, 253, 307, 391, 679 and 2719. Does it reach every odd number past 2719? It was proved in 1997 assuming an unproved hypothesis about the zeros of certain functions, and is open without it.
Why it is hard: with three variables, the count of ways to reach n is a main part plus an error part, and proving the error smaller than the main part for every large n needs exactly the kind of bound with numbers that ∞12.oq.2 lacks.
∞12.oq.4x² + y² reaches every prime that is one more than a multiple of 4 (42f.7.2). Fix y = 1: are there endlessly many primes n² + 1? Asked in 1912 and still open. It is known (1998) that endlessly many primes are x² + y⁴.
Why the step from y⁴ to a fixed y is so hard: the numbers n² + 1 up to X are only about √X of them, too thin for the counting methods that work on forms in two free variables.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In letters of 1640 and 1654 a French magistrate claimed three things about primes: a sum of two squares exactly when one more than a multiple of four, a square plus twice a square exactly when one or three more than a multiple of eight, a square plus three times a square exactly when one more than a multiple of three. He said he had proofs. None survives, and some historians doubt he had them all. The proofs came from St Petersburg and Berlin, and they were slow: the two-squares case took about seven years of letters, finished in 1749. In 1772 the same circle noted that n² + n + 41 gives forty primes in a row. In 1773, in Berlin, the reduction by changes of variables turned a list of claims into a theory.

A book printed in 1801 in Göttingen made it a system: forms sorted by discriminant, kinds counted, and a way to multiply kinds so that they form a group, the composition of forms. It also guessed three things that took more than a century: that the list of negative discriminants with class number one is finite and is the known one, that class numbers grow without bound, and that positive discriminants have class number one endlessly often. Its forms were written with an even middle coefficient, a convention that the later century changed, and older tables and newer ones count slightly different things, which still trips readers.

In the 1930s the growth was proved by a strange road. Work of 1918 to 1934, in Germany and England, showed that if a hypothesis about the zeros of certain functions is true, class numbers grow; and that if it is false, they grow anyway, by a different argument. In 1934 it was also shown that at most one more discriminant with class number one could exist. In 1935 the growth was sharpened to nearly √D, but neither proof could say how far one would have to search to be sure. A proof that something is finite without a bound you can compute was, and is, argued over: some count it as knowledge, others as a promise.

In 1952 a mathematician in Berlin who held no university post published a proof that the tenth discriminant does not exist. It used functions with a great deal of symmetry and leaned on claims from an old textbook known to contain errors. Readers decided it had a gap, and it was set aside. In 1966 and 1967 two independent proofs appeared, one from Cambridge by new bounds on sums of logarithms, one by a method close to the 1952 paper. Then the 1952 paper was read again, in 1967 to 1969, and found essentially right, its gap small and fillable. Its author had died in 1965. One side says the field failed an outsider because he was an outsider. The other says a proof that leans on an unproved claim is not yet a proof, and the readers were right to wait. Both agree that the second look should have come sooner.

The method of 1966 earned the highest prize for young mathematicians in 1970, and it settled the class number two list in 1971. A bound with numbers for every class number came in two halves: in 1976 it was shown to follow from a curve whose function has a zero of high order at one point, and in 1983 such a curve was found. That made every class number list a finite computation, and in 2004 the lists were completed for every class number up to 100. In April 1975 a popular magazine announced, as a joke, that e^(π√163) is a whole number; many readers believed it, since it is wrong only in the thirteenth place after the point.

What is argued now is about the cases still out of reach. For positive discriminants, a 1984 guess of how often class groups take each shape predicts class number one for about three quarters of the prime discriminants; the guess has needed corrections where roots of 1 enter, and proofs cover only a few of its averages. The bound of 1935 is still the only one of its strength, and whether the zeros it fears exist is tied to the hypothesis of 1859 on the zeros of ζ (42·now2), still open. And three-variable forms like x² + y² + 10z² have answers proved only by assuming it, which some count as finished and others do not.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A form here is an integral binary quadratic form; reaching a number is representing it, and a reach with x, y sharing no factor is a proper (primitive) representation. Alike forms are properly equivalent, under SL₂(ℤ), and a change of det −1 gives improper equivalence. A positive form is positive definite. The form-reduction technique is Lagrange’s reduction, and the reduced forms with |q| ≤ p ≤ r are Gauss-reduced forms; h(D) is the form class number, which for fundamental D equals the class number of the field ℚ(√D) (43e’s kinds). The prime-on-a-form technique is the standard criterion through the Kronecker symbol (D ∕ ℓ). Sorting kinds by a clock, as at −20, is genus theory; the two-kinds technique is the statement that each genus of discriminant −20 holds one class. The cubic-split technique is a case of class field theory: the splitting field of x³ − x − 1 is the Hilbert class field of ℚ(√−23), and a clock sorts the primes exactly when that extension is abelian (the Kronecker–Weber theorem and Artin reciprocity). The nine fields of class number one are ℚ(√−d) for the Heegner numbers d = 1, 2, 3, 7, 11, 19, 43, 67, 163; the four further D (−12, −16, −27, −28) are non-fundamental. The forty-one technique is Euler’s prime-generating polynomial, and its converse is the Rabinowitz criterion. e^(π√163) is called Ramanujan’s constant. The bound of 1935 is Siegel’s theorem, ineffective because of a possible Landau–Siegel zero; the bound with numbers is the Goldfeld–Gross–Zagier bound. x² + y² + 10z² is Ramanujan’s ternary form. The 1984 guess is the Cohen–Lenstra heuristics. The question of n² + 1 is one of Landau’s problems.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat stated the three prime claims in letters to Marin Mersenne (1640) and Blaise Pascal (1654). Leonhard Euler proved the two-squares case by 1749 and noticed n² + n + 41 in 1772; Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the reduction in 1773, and Adrien-Marie Legendre extended it. Carl Friedrich Gauss wrote the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) and its class number conjectures. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet found the formula for h(D) in 1839. Max Deuring (1933), Louis Mordell (1934) and Hans Heilbronn (1934) proved that h(D) grows; Heilbronn and Edward Linfoot showed at most one more D of class number one (1934); Carl Ludwig Siegel gave the √D bound (1935). Kurt Heegner published the 1952 proof; Alan Baker (1966) and Harold Stark (1967) gave the proofs of the 1960s, and Stark and Deuring showed Heegner right (1967 to 1969). Baker received the Fields Medal in 1970; Baker and Stark settled class number two in 1971. Dorian Goldfeld (1976) and Benedict Gross with Don Zagier (1983) gave the bound with numbers, and Mark Watkins completed the lists up to 100 (2004). Georg Rabinowitz proved the converse of the forty-one technique in 1913. Martin Gardner wrote the April 1975 joke. Ken Ono and Kannan Soundararajan settled x² + y² + 10z² on the hypothesis (1997). Henri Cohen and Hendrik Lenstra made the 1984 guess. Edmund Landau listed the n² + 1 question in 1912; John Friedlander and Henryk Iwaniec proved the x² + y⁴ result (1998).

A proof that is not allowed

“59 ≡ 13 on the clock of 23, and 13 = 2 · 2² + 2 · 1 + 3 · 1², so 59 is reached by 2x² + xy + 3y² too.” Not allowed: sharing an hour with a value is not being a value. At D = −23 the hour decides only that ℓ is reached by one of the two kinds; 59 is x² + xy + 6y² at (5, 2), and the two kinds share no odd prime.
