Why Do Two Hydrogen Atoms Stick and Two Helium Atoms Not?

Why do two hydrogen atoms stick and two helium atoms not? A bond is electrons shared between atoms, but sharing is not always a gain. Quantum mechanics answers with a grid: an electron near two atoms can sit in a mix of each atom’s own orbital, and the mixes it may settle into are the square axes of a symmetric grid, as rung 31 found for every symmetric map. This page finds the two mixes for a pair of atoms, fills them with electrons, sees why helium will not pair, lets one atom pull harder, and finds the extra steadiness of a ring of six.

Write the electron as c₁φ₁ + c₂φ₂. Its energies are the stretches of the symmetric grid with α on the diagonal and β between neighbours: α + β with the even mix, lower since β < 0, and α − β with the odd mix. Fill from the bottom, two electrons a level: hydrogen gains 2|β|, helium gains nothing. Unequal atoms lean the low mix toward the lower α. A ring of six has stretches α + 2β cos(2πk/6), and gains 2|β| over three separate pairs.

31chem1An Electron Shared by Two Atoms

From here on, on this page, φ₁ and φ₂ are the orbitals an electron would have around atom 1 or atom 2 alone, and a mix is c₁φ₁ + c₂φ₂ with c₁² + c₂² = 1. The energy of a mix is the two-slot reading cᵀHc of a symmetric grid H.

H = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \beta & \alpha \end{pmatrix} @@ α an electron’s energy on one atom, β the gain from sharing, β < 0
\text{energy of a mix} = c^{\mathsf T} H c @@ a quadratic form (31e)
\text{steady mixes} = \text{square axes of } H @@ symmetric maps have square axes (31d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the lowest energy is the smallest stretch}} @@ at the axis it points along

The numbers α and β come from experiment or from heavier calculation. The shape of the answer comes from the grid alone.

31chem2Bonding and Antibonding

Find the stretches and axes of H.

\det(H - \lambda I) = (\alpha - \lambda)^2 - \beta^2 = 0 @@ the stretch test (27f)
\lambda = \alpha + \beta,\ \ \alpha - \beta @@ β < 0, so α + β is lower
\alpha + \beta:\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2) @@ the even mix: piled up between the atoms
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha - \beta:\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2)} @@ the odd mix: cancelled between them

Pair technique: two equal atoms give two mixes, the even one lower than one atom alone by |β| and the odd one higher by |β|. The even mix puts the electron between the nuclei, where both pull on it.

31chem3Filling the Mixes

Each mix holds at most two electrons, filled from the bottom. Hydrogen brings one electron per atom; helium brings two.

\text{H}_2:\ 2 \text{ electrons in } \alpha + \beta @@ one from each atom
2(\alpha + \beta) - 2\alpha = 2\beta < 0 @@ a gain of 2|β|: a bond
\text{He}_2:\ 2 \text{ in } \alpha + \beta,\ 2 \text{ in } \alpha - \beta @@ the odd mix must be filled too
\htmlClass{new}{2(\alpha + \beta) + 2(\alpha - \beta) - 4\alpha = 0} @@ no gain: no bond

Filling technique: count electrons in the even mix less those in the odd mix, and halve it: that is the bond order, 1 for hydrogen and 0 for helium. With the push between electrons counted, the odd mix rises a little more than the even falls, and helium atoms in fact push apart.

31chem4One Atom Pulls Harder

In a bond between unequal atoms, an electron is lower on one of them: α₂ < α₁. Take α₁ = 0, α₂ = −2, β = −1, in some unit of energy.

H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} @@ α₁, α₂ down the middle
\lambda = -1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 1} \approx -2.41,\ 0.41 @@ the stretch test
\lambda = -2.41:\ c \propto (1,\ 2.41) @@ solve (H − λI)c = 0 (27l)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{share on atom 2: } \tfrac{2.41^2}{1 + 2.41^2} \approx 0.85} @@ the shared pair leans to atom 2

When one atom holds electrons more tightly, the low mix leans toward it, and the bond carries a charge: slightly negative at that end, slightly positive at the other. That lean is why water’s oxygen end is negative and its molecules cling to each other.

31chem5A Ring of Six

Benzene is a ring of six carbon atoms, each sharing with its two neighbours. Its grid has α down the middle and β between neighbours round the ring. Try cj = e2πikj/6, a turning round the ring (26a).

(Hc)_j = \alpha c_j + \beta(c_{j-1} + c_{j+1}) @@ two neighbours each
c_{j-1} + c_{j+1} = (e^{-2\pi i k/6} + e^{2\pi i k/6})\,c_j = 2\cos\tfrac{2\pi k}{6}\,c_j @@ the power-to-sum technique (26k)
\lambda_k = \alpha + 2\beta\cos(2\pi k/6):\ \alpha + 2\beta,\ \alpha + \beta\ (\times 2),\ \alpha - \beta\ (\times 2),\ \alpha - 2\beta @@ k = 0 to 5
6 \text{ electrons: } 2(\alpha + 2\beta) + 4(\alpha + \beta) = 6\alpha + 8\beta @@ the three lowest levels
\htmlClass{new}{\text{three separate pairs: } 6\alpha + 6\beta;\ \text{ring gains } 2|\beta| \text{ more}} @@ β < 0: 8β is lower

Ring technique: electrons free to run round a ring settle into turnings, and a ring of six with six electrons is steadier than three separate pairs by 2|β|. That extra steadiness is why benzene resists the reactions that break ordinary double bonds.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

A mix of atomic orbitals is a molecular orbital, built by LCAO, a linear combination of atomic orbitals. The even mix is bonding, the odd antibonding. α is the Coulomb integral, β the resonance integral, and H the Hamiltonian in this basis. The lean is bond polarity, from a difference in electronegativity. The ring model is Hückel theory, and the extra steadiness aromaticity, from delocalised electrons.

Who said it first

Friedrich Hund and Robert Mulliken built molecular orbitals in 1927 and 1928, and John Lennard-Jones wrote them as mixes of atomic orbitals in 1929. Erich Hückel worked out the ring in 1931.
