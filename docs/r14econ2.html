What Happens When Two Sellers Share a Market?

What happens when two sellers share a market? 14·econ found the best amount for a grower among many, who takes the price as given, and for a seller alone, who sets it. Most markets are neither: a few sellers, each watching the others. This page takes two springs selling bottled water, finds each one’s best amount given the other’s, finds the pair where neither wants to change, and compares the price with one seller and with many. Then the two sell by naming prices instead of amounts, and the answer changes completely.

Write the price as a line falling with the total amount. For each seller, profit is its amount times the price, less its cost; with the other’s amount held fixed, set the slope to 0 for its best amount. The pair where each amount is best against the other is where both best-amount lines cross. Compare with one seller and with many. If sellers name prices, the lower price takes the whole market, and undercutting drives the price to the cost.

14econ21Two Springs, One Price

Sarah and Jean each own a spring and sell bottled water in one town. Pumping costs them nothing worth counting. The more water there is for sale, the lower the price at which it all sells: with x thousand bottles a week from Sarah and y from Jean, the price in dollars is 12 − (x + y).

From here on, on this page, Sarah’s best amount against y is the x that makes S greatest when y is held fixed. Jean’s is the same, with the letters swapped. Neither controls the price alone: each one’s amount moves it for both.

14econ22The Best Amount Against the Other

Hold Jean’s y fixed. Sarah’s profit is a curve in x that rises and then falls, and 14’s greatest-inside technique puts its top where its slope is 0.

S(x) = 12x - x^2 - xy @@ spread (5i)
S\prime(x) = 12 - 2x - y @@ slopes of a sum (13), y held fixed
12 - 2x - y = 0 @@ a top inside needs slope 0 (14)
\htmlClass{new}{x = (12 - y)/2} @@ Sarah’s best amount against y

Best-amount technique: each spring’s best amount is half of what the other leaves of the 12: x = (12 − y)/2, and y = (12 − x)/2. The more Jean sells, the less Sarah should.

14econ23Where Neither Wants to Change

As in 7·econ, call a pair stuck when neither seller can do better by changing their own amount alone. That needs each amount to be the best against the other: both best-amount lines at once.

x = (12 - y)/2,\ \ y = (12 - x)/2 @@ both best at once
2x = 12 - (12 - x)/2 @@ substitution (5g)
4x = 24 - 12 + x @@ clear the fraction (5h)
x = 4,\ y = 4 @@ and the price is 12 − 8
\htmlClass{new}{p = 4,\ \text{each earns } 16} @@ thousands of dollars a week

Two-seller technique: with this price line, the stuck pair sells 8 between the two, at a price of 4. Each has done its best against the other, and neither has looked past that.

14econ24One Seller, Two, or Many

Compare the same town with one spring, which sets the amount alone, and with many, where the price falls until it meets the cost of 0.

sellerswater soldpricesellers’ total profit

M(t) = t\,(12 - t) @@ t the total
M\prime(t) = 12 - 2t = 0 @@ slope 0 at the top (14)
\htmlClass{new}{t = 6,\ p = 6,\ M = 36} @@ more than the two together, 32

If the two springs agreed to sell 3 each, they would share 36, more than 32. Each would then be tempted: against y = 3, Sarah’s best is (12 − 3)/2 = 4.5, not 3. Two sellers sell more and charge less than one, and less and more than many; a deal to act as one is not stuck.

14econ25Naming Prices Instead

Now each spring names a price, and buyers go to whichever is cheaper, sharing equally at a tie. If Sarah names 4 and Jean names 3.99, Jean sells to everyone. Sarah answers with 3.98, and so on.

\text{both at } p > 0:\ \text{each sells half} @@ a tie shares
\text{one at } p - \text{a cent}:\ \text{sells it all} @@ almost twice the profit, for a cent less
\text{so no pair with } p > 0 \text{ is stuck} @@ each would undercut
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both at the cost, } p = 0} @@ neither can gain by changing alone

With prices named, two sellers end where many do. Whether a few sellers act like one or like many depends on what they choose: amounts or prices. Real sellers choose both, and what a firm can change quickly decides which story fits it better.

Accepted names

A market of a few sellers is an oligopoly, and of two a duopoly. Choosing amounts is Cournot competition; a best-amount line is a reaction function or best response, and the stuck pair is the Cournot equilibrium, a Nash equilibrium. Choosing prices is Bertrand competition, and its end at the cost is the Bertrand paradox. A deal to act as one seller is a cartel or collusion.

Who said it first

Antoine Augustin Cournot worked out the two springs, as mineral springs, in 1838. Joseph Bertrand objected in 1883 that sellers name prices, not amounts.
