43b · proof

Ideals and quotients

The multiples of seven are a strange kind of part of the whole numbers. Add two of them and you get another. Multiply one by any whole number at all, not only by another multiple, and you still get a multiple of seven. That second property is what lets a clock work: two numbers that differ by a multiple of seven stay that way after multiplying, so the clock of seven can multiply its hours. Rung forty one used it for the clock. Here it is taken as the whole definition, in any ring. A part that is closed under adding and swallows multiplying by anything is the kind of part a ring can be divided by, and dividing by it gives a new ring. Then two questions about such a part decide what the new ring is like. Does a product land in the part only when a factor does? Then the new ring has no zero products. Is there no room for a bigger part below the whole ring? Then the new ring is a field. The clock of a prime is the first case of both.

A part of a ring that holds 0, holds sums, and holds every multiple of each of its members is what a ring can be divided by. The classes then add and multiply honestly. What a map loses is such a part, and dividing by it gives what the map reaches. A part that holds a product only when it holds a factor gives a ring with no zero products; a largest part below the whole gives a field.

43b1Parts that swallow multiplying

A part I of a ring R that turns about is an ideal when it holds 0, holds w + b whenever it holds w and b, and holds rw for every r in R whenever it holds w. The multiples of 7, written 7ℤ, are an ideal of ℤ. So are the multiples (g) = {rg : r ∈ R} of any member g of any ring. In ℝ[x], the polynomials with p(2) = 0 are an ideal. {0} and R itself are ideals of every ring.

The word comes from the ideal numbers of 43e, which were the first ideals anyone wrote down. One ideal can be the multiples of more than one thing, or of no single thing. In ℤ, the ideal made by 12 and 18, all 12u + 18v, is 6ℤ: 4j’s plus-only combination reaches 6, and every such sum is a multiple of 6.

Undoable-fills technique: an ideal that holds an undoable member is the whole ring.

u \in I,\ \ uv = 1 @@ given: u is undoable
1 = vu \in I @@ I swallows multiplying by v
\htmlClass{new}{r = r \cdot 1 \in I \text{ for every } r} \quad \blacksquare @@ I swallows multiplying by r

So a field has exactly two ideals, {0} and itself: any ideal with a member other than 0 holds an undoable one. The other way round is 43b.6.2.

43b2Dividing by an ideal

Write w ∼ b when w − b is in I. That is a sameness relation (5·cb2), since I holds 0, negatives and sums. Its classes are the copies w + I = {w + i : i ∈ I}, and 42·cb19 added them as a quotient group: (w + I) + (b + I) = (w + b) + I. The quotient R/I is the set of classes. The new thing is the product.

Honest-product technique: if w ∼ w′ and b ∼ b′, then wb ∼ w′b′, so the classes of an ideal multiply honestly, and R/I is a ring.

wb - w'b' = wb - wb' + wb' - w'b' @@ add and take away wb′
\phantom{wb - w'b'} = w(b - b') + (w - w')b' @@ spread, twice
w(b - b') \in I,\ \ (w - w')b' \in I @@ I swallows multiplying by w and by b′
\htmlClass{new}{wb - w'b' \in I} \quad \blacksquare @@ I holds sums; the seven rules carry over class by class

With I = nℤ this is 42a’s clock: ℤ/nℤ is ℤ/n, and 5·cb2’s remainders-multiply technique is this one. The line that needed I to swallow is the third. A part that holds sums and not multiples, such as the even numbers inside ℚ, gives classes that cannot multiply: 0 ∼ 2, and ½ · 0 = 0 while ½ · 2 = 1, and 1 − 0 is not even.

43b3What a map loses

A ring map f : R → S keeps sums, products and 1: f(w + b) = f(w) + f(b), f(wb) = f(w)f(b), f(1) = 1. The clock map ℤ → ℤ/n is one. So is putting a number in: p ↦ p(i) takes ℝ[x] to ℂ. As in 27c, what f loses is the members it sends to 0, and what it reaches is its range.

Lost-ideal technique: what a ring map loses is an ideal I, and the classes of I match what the map reaches, by w + I ↦ f(w), keeping sums and products.

f(w) = f(b) = 0 \Rightarrow f(w + b) = 0,\ \ f(rw) = f(r) \cdot 0 = 0 @@ f keeps sums and products; anything-times-zero
w + I = b + I \iff w - b \in I \iff f(w) = f(b) @@ f(w − b) = f(w) − f(b)
w + I \mapsto f(w) \text{ is one-to-one and onto what } f \text{ reaches} @@ the line above, both ways
\htmlClass{new}{R/I \text{ is a copy of what } f \text{ reaches}} \quad \blacksquare @@ it keeps sums and products because f does (42·cb19’s matching, with times)

One example. p ↦ p(i) reaches every a + bi. It loses exactly the multiples of x² + 1: divide p by x² + 1 (9g), leaving a + bx, and a + bi = 0 only when a = b = 0. So ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) is a copy of ℂ. 9·cb8 built ℂ from pairs with a multiplying rule of its own. Here the rule comes out of dividing: x · x = x² ∼ −1.

43b4Prime parts and largest parts

An ideal P other than R is prime when wb ∈ P only if w ∈ P or b ∈ P. That is 4j’s prime-divides-a-factor technique made a definition: in ℤ, pℤ is prime exactly when p is a prime or 0. An ideal M other than R is largest when no ideal lies strictly between M and R.

Largest-ideal technique: R/P has no zero products exactly when P is prime; R/M is a field exactly when M is largest.

(w + P)(b + P) = 0 + P \iff wb \in P @@ the zero class is P
\text{so: no zero products in } R/P \iff (wb \in P \Rightarrow w \in P \lor b \in P) @@ the definition of prime, read through the line above
w \notin M,\ \ J = \{m + rw : m \in M,\ r \in R\} @@ an ideal holding M and w, so J = R when M is largest
1 = m + rw \Rightarrow (r + M)(w + M) = 1 + M @@ so w + M has a reciprocal
\htmlClass{new}{M \text{ largest} \iff R/M \text{ a field}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other way: an ideal between M and R gives a class with no reciprocal, by the undoable-fills technique in R/M
  
I \supsetneq p\mathbb Z,\ \ w \in I,\ \ p \nmid w @@ an ideal strictly above pℤ
\gcd(w, p) = 1,\ \ uw = vp + 1 @@ p is prime; 4j’s plus-only combination
1 = uw - vp \in I @@ I swallows and holds sums
\htmlClass{new}{I = \mathbb Z:\ p\mathbb Z \text{ is largest, so } \mathbb Z/p \text{ is a field}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the largest-ideal technique

Every largest ideal is prime, since a field has no zero products. Not every prime ideal is largest: in ℤ[x], (x) is prime, because ℤ[x]/(x) is a copy of ℤ by p ↦ p(0), and ℤ has no zero products. But (x) sits strictly inside (2, x), the polynomials with an even constant term, and ℤ is not a field.

The accepted names

An ideal is a two-sided ideal; in a ring that turns about, the multiples (g) form a principal ideal, and the ideal made by 12 and 18 is written (12, 18). R/I is the quotient ring, or factor ring, and the classes w + I are cosets. A ring map is a ring homomorphism; what it loses is its kernel, and the lost-ideal technique is the first isomorphism theorem for rings. A largest ideal is a maximal ideal. The largest-ideal technique is usually stated in two halves: R/P is an integral domain exactly when P is prime, and R/M is a field exactly when M is maximal.

Who said it first

Richard Dedekind defined ideals in 1871, in a supplement to Dirichlet’s lectures on number theory, to rescue factoring in the rings of 43e; he chose the name to honour Ernst Kummer’s ideal numbers of 1847. Leopold Kronecker built fields by dividing polynomials in 1882, the road by which ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) is ℂ. Emmy Noether wrote the theory of ideals for any ring that turns about in 1921, and Wolfgang Krull proved in 1929 that every ideal other than the whole ring lies inside a largest one, spending choice (6).

A proof that is not allowed

“ℤ has no zero products, and ℤ/6 is made from ℤ, so ℤ/6 has none.” Not allowed: dividing can make zero products where there were none. 2 · 3 = 6 lands in 6ℤ, so (2 + 6ℤ)(3 + 6ℤ) is the zero class. Whether R/I has zero products depends on I, not on R: it has none exactly when I is prime.
