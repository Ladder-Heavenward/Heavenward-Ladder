How Small Can a Reactor Be?

How small can a reactor be? A nuclear reactor keeps going because each split atom frees neutrons that go on to split more. But neutrons wander, and some wander out through the surface and are lost. A small core loses too many and the chain dies; a big enough one keeps it alive. Between them is a critical size. This page lets neutrons wander as 33a’s heat equation, balances making against losing, finds the slowest shape on a ball with 33h, gets the critical radius, and finds why a reflector around the core shrinks it.

Neutrons spread like heat, and the core makes new ones in step with how many there are. A steady chain needs the spreading to balance the making: ∇²φ + B²φ = 0, with B² = (k − 1)/L². On a ball, the slowest shape is sin(πr/R)/r, which fits only if B = π/R. So R = πL/√(k − 1): a richer mix or slower leaking gives a smaller core, and a reflector sends escaping neutrons back.

43eng1Neutrons Wander Out Through the Surface

Terry designs research reactors. Inside the core, neutrons bounce off nuclei thousands of times, each bounce in a random direction, before they are absorbed. Like heat, they spread from where they are crowded to where they are not (33a).

From here on, on this page, φ is the crowding of neutrons at each point, L the wander length, roughly how far a neutron spreads before it is absorbed, and k how many new neutrons each absorbed one makes, in a core so large none escape.

\text{spreading: } D\,\nabla^2\phi @@ the heat equation’s term (33a)
\htmlClass{new}{L^2 = \dfrac{D}{\text{absorption rate}}} @@ a longer wander loses more through the surface

43eng2Making Against Losing

Each absorbed neutron is lost but makes k new ones. In a steady chain, what spreads into each bit of the core, plus what is made there, equals what is absorbed there.

D\nabla^2\phi + (k - 1)\,(\text{absorption})\,\phi = 0 @@ spread in + made − absorbed = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla^2\phi + B^2\phi = 0,\ \ B^2 = \dfrac{k - 1}{L^2}} @@ k must exceed 1 for any steady chain

φ must be 0 at the surface, since neutrons that reach it leave and do not come back. So the question is: for what size does this statement have a positive solution vanishing at the surface?

43eng3The Slowest Shape on a Ball

Each shape of a boundary value problem has its own number (33h): only the shape with the smallest number stays positive everywhere, the slowest shape. For a ball of radius R it is:

\phi(r) = \dfrac{\sin(\pi r/R)}{r} @@ positive inside, 0 at r = R
\nabla^2\phi = -\left(\dfrac{\pi}{R}\right)^2\phi @@ its number (33h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a steady chain needs } B = \dfrac{\pi}{R}} @@

43eng4The Critical Radius

R_c = \dfrac{\pi}{B} = \dfrac{\pi L}{\sqrt{k - 1}} @@ 
R_c = \dfrac{\pi \times 10}{\sqrt{0.1}} \approx 99 \text{ cm} @@ a ball about 2 m across
\text{a cube instead: side } \sqrt 3\,\pi L/\sqrt{k - 1} \approx 172 \text{ cm},\ \text{volume } 5.1 \text{ m}^3 \text{ against } 4.1 @@ the ball is the smallest
\htmlClass{new}{\text{smaller than } R_c \text{ the chain dies; larger, it grows}} @@

Critical-size technique: a core of wander length L and multiplying number k sustains a chain only at radius πL/√(k − 1); smaller loses too many neutrons through its surface, and a ball needs the least material of any shape. Reactor crews approach the critical size step by step, adding fuel and counting neutrons each time: the inverse of the count falls in a straight line toward 0, and where it would reach 0 is the critical load, before it is ever reached.

43eng5Why a Reflector Shrinks It

Surround the core with a material that absorbs few neutrons but bounces them well, such as water, graphite or beryllium. Neutrons that wander out are bounced back in, and the surface leaks less.

\phi \text{ no longer 0 at the core’s edge: it falls to 0 further out} @@ 
R_c \approx \dfrac{\pi L}{\sqrt{k - 1}} - \delta @@ δ the reflector’s saving, a good part of L
\htmlClass{new}{\text{less fuel for the same chain}} @@

A reflector sends escaping neutrons back into the core, so a reflected core goes critical smaller, with less fuel. Every reactor has one; and control rods work the other way, absorbing neutrons to push the core below critical whenever the crew needs to slow or stop the chain.

Accepted names

Treating neutrons as spreading like heat is diffusion theory; B² is the buckling, L the diffusion length, k the infinite multiplication factor k∞; δ is the reflector savings; stepping toward critical is an approach to criticality, read on an inverse multiplication or 1/M plot.

Who said it first

The first reactor reached a steady chain in Chicago in December 1942, approached step by step with an inverse-count plot (not yet revisited for the date). The k and L here are round teaching values for a research core.
