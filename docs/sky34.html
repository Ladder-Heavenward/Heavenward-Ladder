Is There an Odd Perfect Number?

Is there an odd perfect number? Six is the sum of its divisors below it, one and two and three, and twenty eight is one and two and four and seven and fourteen. The next two are 496 and 8128. A book of geometry and number written around 300 BC proved that whenever one less than a power of two is prime, half that power times the prime is perfect. In the eighteenth century the converse was proved: every even perfect number is of that kind. So the even ones are exactly as many as the primes that are one less than a power of two, and fifty two of those are known, the largest found in 2024 with over forty one million digits. Whether there are endlessly many is open. Of odd perfect numbers not one is known, and nobody can show there is none. What is known is a portrait of a number that may not exist: it would have to be a prime to a power times a square, both of special shapes, with at least nine different primes, the largest above a hundred million, and the whole number above one followed by fifteen hundred zeros. This page proves the even case both ways, proves the shape of an odd one, builds the ceilings and chains that the searches follow, and shows a number from 1638 that very nearly is an odd perfect number and is not.

σ(n), the sum of all divisors of n, multiplies across numbers sharing no prime, and n is perfect when σ(n) = 2n. If 2ᵖ − 1 is prime, 2ᵖ⁻¹(2ᵖ − 1) is perfect, and every even perfect number is of this form. An odd perfect number is qᵏm² with q prime and q ≡ k ≡ 1 (mod 4). σ(n)/n is below the product of p/(p − 1) over its primes, so an odd perfect number has at least three primes, and at least seven if 3 does not divide it; and every odd prime of σ(pᵉ) divides n, which gives the chains the searches follow.

sky34.1Adding the Divisors

Write σ(n) for the sum of all the divisors of n, n itself included: σ(6) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 6 = 12. A number is perfect when the divisors below it add to it, that is when σ(n) = 2n. The ratio σ(n)/n measures how far off it is: σ(12)/12 = 28/12, too much, and σ(8)/8 = 15/8, too little.

Divisor-product technique: if m and k share no prime, then σ(mk) = σ(m)σ(k); and for a prime p, σ(pᵉ) = 1 + p + ⋯ + pᵉ = (pᵉ⁺¹ − 1)/(p − 1).

d \mid mk \Rightarrow d = d_1 d_2,\ \ d_1 \mid m,\ d_2 \mid k @@ by the one-prime-list technique (4j18), the primes of d split between m and k
d_1 \mid m,\ d_2 \mid k \Rightarrow d_1 d_2 \mid mk,\ \text{and different pairs give different } d @@ m and k share no prime, so the split is the only one
\sigma(mk) = \textstyle\sum_{d_1 \mid m} \sum_{d_2 \mid k} d_1 d_2 = \big(\sum d_1\big)\big(\sum d_2\big) @@ the stages-multiply technique (4k): every pair once
(p - 1)(1 + p + \cdots + p^e) = p^{e+1} - 1 @@ the middle terms cancel in pairs
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma(mk) = \sigma(m)\,\sigma(k),\ \ \sigma(p^e) = \tfrac{p^{e+1} - 1}{p - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the divisor-product technique

So σ(28) = σ(4)σ(7) = 7 · 8 = 56 = 2 · 28, and 28 is perfect. The condition matters: σ(4) = 7, but σ(2)σ(2) = 9, since 2 and 2 share a prime. And σ(105) = σ(3)σ(5)σ(7) = 4 · 6 · 8 = 192, short of 210.

sky34.2Even Ones, Forward

First a filter. If 2ᵏ − 1 is prime, then k is prime: if k = rs with 1 < r < k, then 2ʳ − 1 divides 2ᵏ − 1, since 2^(rs) − 1 = (2ʳ − 1)(2^(r(s−1)) + ⋯ + 2ʳ + 1), and 1 < 2ʳ − 1 < 2ᵏ − 1. So 2⁴ − 1 = 15 = 3 · 5 and 2⁶ − 1 = 63 = 7 · 9. The filter only goes one way: 2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 · 89.

Power-times-prime technique: if 2ᵖ − 1 is prime, then 2ᵖ⁻¹(2ᵖ − 1) is perfect.

M = 2^p - 1 \text{ prime},\ \ n = 2^{p-1} M @@ M is odd, so it shares no prime with 2ᵖ⁻¹
\sigma(n) = \sigma(2^{p-1})\,\sigma(M) @@ the divisor-product technique
\sigma(2^{p-1}) = 2^p - 1 = M,\ \ \sigma(M) = M + 1 = 2^p @@ M is prime
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma(n) = M \cdot 2^p = 2 \cdot 2^{p-1} M = 2n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power-times-prime technique

p = 2, 3, 5, 7 give 6, 28, 496 and 8128. p = 11 fails: 2¹⁰ · 2047 has σ = 2047 · 24 · 90 = 2047 · 2160, more than 2 · 2¹⁰ · 2047 = 2047 · 2048. p = 13 gives 8191, prime, and 33 550 336.

sky34.3Even Ones, Back

The converse waited about two thousand years, and it fits in a few lines.

Every-even technique: every even perfect number is 2ᵖ⁻¹(2ᵖ − 1) with 2ᵖ − 1 prime.

n = 2^k m,\ k \ge 1,\ m \text{ odd}:\ \ (2^{k+1} - 1)\,\sigma(m) = 2^{k+1} m @@ the divisor-product technique and σ(n) = 2n
2^{k+1} - 1 \text{ is odd and divides } 2^{k+1} m,\ \text{so it divides } m:\ \ m = (2^{k+1} - 1)c @@ it shares no prime with 2ᵏ⁺¹ (4j14)
\sigma(m) = 2^{k+1} c = m + c @@ divide both sides by 2ᵏ⁺¹ − 1
c < m,\ \ c \mid m,\ m \mid m:\ \ \sigma(m) \ge m + c,\ \text{with equality only if } m \text{ and } c \text{ are its only divisors} @@ 2ᵏ⁺¹ − 1 ≥ 3
c = 1,\ \ m = 2^{k+1} - 1 \text{ prime} @@ 1 always divides m, so 1 is c; a number whose only divisors are 1 and itself is prime
\htmlClass{new}{n = 2^k(2^{k+1} - 1),\ 2^{k+1} - 1 \text{ prime}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the every-even technique, with p = k + 1

So the even perfect numbers and the primes 2ᵖ − 1 are matched one to one. Checking every n below 10 000 by machine finds 6, 28, 496 and 8128 and nothing else, as the two techniques predict. The proof spends evenness at its first line: for odd n there is no power of 2 to split off, and the argument has nothing to hold.

sky34.4The Shape of an Odd One

Suppose n is odd and perfect, with prime list n = p₁^(e₁) ⋯ pᵣ^(eᵣ). Then σ(n) = 2n is even but not a multiple of 4, and σ(n) is the product of the σ(pᵢ^(eᵢ)). Read each factor on the clocks of 2 and 4 (43a).

Odd-shape technique: an odd perfect number is qᵏm² with q prime, q not dividing m, and q ≡ k ≡ 1 (mod 4).

\sigma(n) = \textstyle\prod_i \sigma(p_i^{e_i}) = 2n \equiv 2 \pmod 4 @@ n odd: exactly one factor 2
\sigma(p^e) = 1 + p + \cdots + p^e:\ e + 1 \text{ odd terms} @@ so σ(pᵉ) is odd exactly when e is even
\text{exactly one } \sigma(q^k) \text{ is even, and it is } \equiv 2 \pmod 4;\ \text{every other } e_i \text{ is even} @@ one factor 2 in all: so n = qᵏm² with k odd
q \equiv 3 \pmod 4:\ \ 1 + q + \cdots + q^k \equiv (1 + 3) + \cdots + (1 + 3) \equiv 0 \pmod 4 @@ k + 1 even terms, paired; so q ≡ 1
q \equiv 1 \pmod 4:\ \ \sigma(q^k) \equiv k + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 4 @@ every term is 1 on the clock of 4
\htmlClass{new}{n = q^k m^2,\ \ q \equiv k \equiv 1 \pmod 4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the odd-shape technique

On the clock of 4, σ(5) = 6 ≡ 2 and σ(5⁵) = 3906 ≡ 2, while σ(5³) = 156 ≡ 0; σ(3ᵏ) and σ(7ᵏ) are 0 or 1 for every k, never 2. So 105 = 3 · 5 · 7, with three odd exponents, is ruled out before any adding: an odd perfect number has one prime to an odd power, of the shape 4j + 1, and the rest to even powers.

sky34.5Ceilings, Chains and Searches

Two tools narrow the hunt. The first is a ceiling on σ(n)/n from the primes alone; it is 43g’s product over the primes, cut to the primes of n.

Abundance-ceiling technique: σ(n)/n is less than the product of p/(p − 1) over the primes p dividing n.

Forced-prime technique: if pᵉ is the exact power of the odd prime p in an odd perfect number n, then every odd prime dividing σ(pᵉ) divides n.

\frac{\sigma(p^e)}{p^e} = 1 + \frac1p + \cdots + \frac1{p^e} = \frac{p - p^{-e}}{p - 1} < \frac{p}{p - 1} @@ the divisor-product technique, divided by pᵉ
\frac{\sigma(n)}{n} = \prod_p \frac{\sigma(p^e)}{p^e} < \prod_p \frac{p}{p-1} @@ the divisor-product technique: the abundance-ceiling technique
\tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac54 = \tfrac{15}{8} < 2 @@ any two odd primes give at most this: an odd perfect number has at least three
\tfrac54 \cdot \tfrac76 \cdots \tfrac{19}{18} < 2 < \tfrac54 \cdot \tfrac76 \cdots \tfrac{23}{22} \approx 2.038 @@ without 3, it needs at least seven primes
\sigma(p^e) \mid \textstyle\prod \sigma = \sigma(n) = 2n @@ one factor of the product
\htmlClass{new}{\text{odd } r \mid \sigma(p^e) \Rightarrow r \mid n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the forced-prime technique

A chain: if 3² is the exact power of 3, then σ(9) = 13 divides n; if 13² is the exact power of 13, then σ(169) = 183 = 3 · 61, so 61 divides n; if 61², then σ(3721) = 3783 = 3 · 13 · 97, so 97 does. Each guess of an exponent forces new primes, and a branch dies when the forced primes push σ(n)/n past 2 or a prime appears to a power the shape forbids. A computer search (41h) runs these branches with every exponent, and its finished tree is a proof. Such trees give, for an odd perfect number: above 10¹⁵⁰⁰ (2012), at least nine different primes (2007), the largest above 10⁸ (2008). A claim of ten different primes has been made since; this page has not revisited it. For the even side, the search is for primes 2ᵖ − 1: fifty two are known as of the sources this page checked, the largest, found in October 2024, 2^136 279 841 − 1, with 41 024 320 digits.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞34.oq.1Is there an odd perfect number?
Why it is hard: every condition proved so far is a condition a number could meet. The shape, the ceilings and the chains shrink the room where one could hide, and the room left is still endless. A proof of none would need a reason that works for every shape at once, and none is known.
∞34.oq.2Are there endlessly many primes of the form 2ᵖ − 1, and so endlessly many even perfect numbers? Fifty two are known (as of the sources this page checked, from late 2024; a later find has not been revisited here).
Why it is hard: no method is known that shows any such form takes prime values endlessly often. The same question for n² + 1 (∞12.oq.4) is open too, and 2ᵖ − 1 is far thinner: below a bound X there are only about log₂ X of them.
∞34.oq.3The number of 1638 is a spoof: it works if one of its factors is treated as a prime when it is not. A 2020 search found 21 spoofs with six or fewer bases, and every one but the 1638 number needed a negative base. Does every spoof other than that one need a negative base?
Why it is asked: an odd perfect number would be a spoof with all bases positive primes, so facts about all spoofs reach it; but it is not known whether such facts would be enough.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The book of 300 BC that proved the power-times-prime technique stated it as a recipe: add 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on until the sum is prime, and multiply the sum by the last term. It says nothing about whether every even perfect number comes this way, and nothing about odd ones. The converse came in the eighteenth century, from Berlin, more than two thousand years later, and with it the odd shape of sky34.4.

In 1638 a French philosopher offered 198 585 576 189 as a number that behaves like an odd perfect number if 22 021 is taken for a prime. It is not one: 22 021 = 19² · 61. The number has been read as a near miss and as a warning, and it is the reason a whole family of near misses now carries the name spoof.

The bounds of the last decades come from search trees: guess the exponent of a prime, follow the chain, and kill the branch when the ceiling or the shape forbids it. A tree that covers every branch is a proof, but not one any person reads line by line; it is checked by running it again, as 41h’s certificates are. The record of 2012, above 10¹⁵⁰⁰, and the bound of 2008 on the largest prime were both such trees. Whether a proof of this kind is as good as one a person can read is argued in the same terms as other proofs by machine: it is as good as the program and the hardware, and they can be checked again by others.

The hunt for primes 2ᵖ − 1 is the opposite kind of search: one giant number at a time, tested by a rule special to that shape, run by volunteers on free software. In October 2024 the fifty second was found by a former engineer of a chip maker, working on graphics processors. A record of this kind teaches nothing new about primes in general: its value is the search itself, and the endless question of ∞34.oq.2, which no single find can settle.

What is argued now is whether the odd question is close or hopeless. Bounds and searches are sometimes read as evidence that no odd perfect number exists, and sometimes only as evidence of how little the methods see: every bound so far says where one cannot be, and a number above 10¹⁵⁰⁰ with many primes is not ruled out by anything known. Since 2020 a team at a university in Utah has studied spoofs, numbers that pass every test if some factor is treated as a prime, to find which facts about odd perfect numbers follow from the arithmetic of the shape alone. If every fact proved so far holds for spoofs too, the tools cannot tell a spoof from the real thing, and something new is needed.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

σ(n) is the sum-of-divisors function, and the divisor-product technique says it is multiplicative. Numbers with σ(n) > 2n are abundant and those with σ(n) < 2n deficient; σ(n)/n is the abundancy index. Primes 2ᵖ − 1 are Mersenne primes. The power-times-prime technique is Euclid’s Elements IX.36, and with the every-even technique it is the Euclid–Euler theorem. The odd-shape technique is Euler’s theorem on odd perfect numbers, qᵏm² being the Eulerian form and q the special or Euler prime. The forced-prime technique drives the factor chains of the search programs. The number of 1638 is the Descartes number, the first known spoof odd perfect number.

Who said it first

Euclid gave the power-times-prime technique (Elements, around 300 BC). Leonhard Euler proved the converse in the 18th century (De numeris amicabilibus) and gave the odd shape. René Descartes wrote of the spoof in a letter of 1638 to Marin Mersenne. Pascal Ochem and Michaël Rao proved the bound of 10¹⁵⁰⁰ (Mathematics of Computation, 2012); Pace Nielsen proved at least nine distinct primes (posted 2006, Mathematics of Computation 2007); Takeshi Goto and Yasuo Ohno the largest prime above 10⁸ (2008). The 2020 spoof search was led by Pace Nielsen and Paul Jenkins at Brigham Young University, with Michael Griffin and Nickolas Andersen. The fifty second Mersenne prime, 2^136279841 − 1, was found in October 2024 through the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search, on graphics processors.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every odd number below 10¹⁵⁰⁰ fails, and the ceilings only get harder to meet as numbers grow, so no odd number is perfect.” Not allowed: the ceiling of a number grows with its count of primes, as 43g’s product over the primes passes every bound, so large numbers with many primes have room to reach 2. Growth makes the search harder, not the answer no.
