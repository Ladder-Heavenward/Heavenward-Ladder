How Does a Wing Hold a Plane Up?

How does a wing hold a plane up? A small plane weighs a tonne, and its wings hold it on nothing but moving air. The air flows a little faster over the top of the wing than under it, and that small difference in speed is enough. This page writes the flow past a wing as a stream plus a swirl, with 28l’s circulation, finds the pressure difference with 28·phys’s speed and pressure rule, gets the lift from density, speed and circulation, treats a propeller blade as a turning wing, and finds why wings stall.

Flow past a tilted wing is the passing stream plus a swirl round the wing. The swirl adds speed on top and takes it away below, so the pressure is lower on top. Lift per metre of span is ρ V Γ, with Γ the circulation round the wing. For a thin wing at a small tilt α, Γ = πcVα, so lift grows in step with the tilt, until the flow tears off the top and the lift collapses.

29eng1Flow Past a Wing as a Stream Plus a Swirl

Terry tests wings in a wind tunnel. Air streams past a wing at speed V. A wing tilted slightly nose-up turns the air: it runs faster over the top and slower underneath. That is the stream plus a swirl round the wing.

From here on, on this page, the circulation Γ is the swirl’s total: the speed along a loop round the wing, added up all the way round (28l). The plain stream alone adds to 0 round any loop.

\text{top: } V + w,\ \ \text{bottom: } V - w @@ w the swirl’s extra speed
\Gamma = \oint \vec v \cdot d\vec s \approx (V + w)\,c - (V - w)\,c @@ along the top one way, back underneath (28l)
\htmlClass{new}{\Gamma \approx 2wc} @@

29eng2Faster on Top, Lower Pressure

Along a stream with no friction, pressure plus ½ρ times speed squared stays the same (28·phys). Faster air on top is at lower pressure.

p + \tfrac12 \rho v^2 = \text{the same for all streams from far ahead} @@ 28·phys
p_{\text{below}} - p_{\text{above}} = \tfrac12\rho\left[(V + w)^2 - (V - w)^2\right] @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{= 2\rho V w} @@ the squares of V cancel

The top air does not race to meet the bottom air at the back edge; it arrives well ahead of it. What matters is only that it is faster.

29eng3Lift From Density, Speed and Circulation

L\prime = (2\rho V w) \times c = \rho\,V\,(2wc) @@ pressure difference times chord
\htmlClass{new}{L\prime = \rho\,V\,\Gamma} @@ 
1\,000 \text{ kg, span } 10 \text{ m}:\ L\prime = 981 \text{ N/m};\ \ V = 50,\ \rho = 1.2:\ \Gamma \approx 16 \text{ m}^2/\text{s} @@ 
c = 1.5 \text{ m}:\ w = \dfrac{\Gamma}{2c} \approx 5.5 \text{ m/s} @@ top 55.5, bottom 44.5 m/s

Circulation-lift technique: a wing’s lift per metre of span is the air’s density times its speed times the circulation round the wing. For a thin wing tilted α, the air leaving smoothly off the sharp back edge sets Γ = πcVα, so lift = ½ρV² c (2πα): a 4° tilt holds Terry’s plane at 50 m/s. In the tunnel, Terry’s measured lift rises in a straight line with tilt, at the slope 2π per radian this gives, up to about 15°.

29eng4A Propeller Blade Is a Turning Wing

A propeller blade is a wing that goes round. Each piece of it, at radius r, meets the air at the plane’s speed V forward plus its own speed ωr round: two arrows square to each other (21·phys).

\omega r \approx 251 \times 0.8 \approx 201 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\text{air meets it at } \arctan\dfrac{50}{201} \approx 14° \text{ to the turning} @@ arrows add (21·phys)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{set at } 18°:\ \text{tilt } 4°,\ \text{the same as the wing}} @@

Further out, ωr is larger and the air comes in flatter, so the blade is twisted, steep near the hub and flat near the tip, to keep every piece at the same small tilt.

29eng5Why Wings Stall

Past a tilt of about 15°, the air can no longer follow the curved top of the wing. It tears away into swirling eddies, the circulation collapses, and the lift falls suddenly.

W = \tfrac12 \rho V^2 S\, C_{\max} @@ S wing area, C_max the most lift per ½ρV²S
V_{\text{stall}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2W}{\rho S C_{\max}}} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{W = 9\,810 \text{ N},\ S = 15 \text{ m}^2,\ C_{\max} = 1.5:\ V_{\text{stall}} \approx 27 \text{ m/s}} @@ about 52 knots

Lift grows with tilt only until the air tears off the top of the wing; slower than the stall speed, no tilt can hold the plane up. Pilots land well above it, and flaps that curve the wing at landing raise C_max to lower it.

Accepted names

L′ = ρVΓ is the Kutta-Joukowski theorem; the air leaving smoothly off the back edge is the Kutta condition, and Γ = πcVα comes from thin airfoil theory. The tilt is the angle of attack, lift per ½ρV²S the lift coefficient, and the swerve of a spinning ball the Magnus effect.

Who said it first

Martin Kutta (1902) and Nikolai Joukowski (1906) found the lift from circulation independently.
