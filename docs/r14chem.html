How Far Apart Do Two Joined Atoms Sit?

How far apart do two joined atoms sit? Two hydrogen atoms far apart do not notice each other. Bring them closer and they pull together; push them too close and they push apart. Somewhere between, the pull and the push balance, and two atoms joined at that distance are a molecule of hydrogen, 74 picometres from centre to centre. Chemists draw this as one curve: the energy of the pair against the distance between them. The force between the atoms is minus the slope of that curve. So the balance point, where the force is 0, is where the slope is 0, and 14a says where to look: at the bottom. The bond's length is the input of the least value, and the bond's strength is how deep the bottom is.

Write the energy as a function of the distance between the atoms. The force pushing them apart is the slope of the energy, with its sign turned. The bond length is where that slope is 0, at the bottom of the energy. The bond energy is the depth of that bottom below the energy far apart.

14chem1The Energy Curve

From here on, on this page, r is the distance between the centres of two atoms, in picometres (pm, 10⁻¹² m). V(r) is the energy the pair has because of that distance, in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol: the energy for 5·chem's mole of such pairs). It is measured from the pair far apart, where V is 0. Its accepted name is the potential energy curve of the pair.

A physical premise, carried over from physics: the force along the line between the atoms, counted positive when it pushes them apart, is F(r) = −V′(r). Where V falls as r grows, F > 0 and the atoms are pushed apart. Where V rises as r grows, F < 0 and they are pulled together. Energy flows downhill on the curve.

Two measured numbers describe the bottom. For H₂ the bottom is at 74 pm, and it lies 436 kJ/mol below the energy far apart. The first is called the bond length, and the second the bond energy, or bond enthalpy: the energy it takes to pull a mole of the molecules apart into atoms.

14chem2A Curve with a Formula

For two atoms of argon, which do not form a chemical bond but do cling weakly, a formula of two terms fits the measured curve well. It was proposed in 1924:

V(r) = A/r¹² − B/r⁶, for positive constants A and B and r > 0.

The first term is the push at close range, which grows very fast as r shrinks. The second is the pull, which fades as r grows. Its accepted name is the twelve-six potential.

In the column below, r* (read “r star”) is a plain name for the distance at the bottom. The star only marks the name. It is not 8a's cut r*, which this page never uses.

For argon the fitted numbers give the bottom at about 382 pm and a depth of about 1.0 kJ/mol. Hydrogen's bond is more than four hundred times deeper. That is the difference between a chemical bond and a cling: argon atoms stay paired only below about 84 K, where argon is already a solid.

14chem3Fitting the Bottom of a Real Bond

Write the same two-term curve with its bottom put where it is measured, at r* = 74 pm and depth 436 kJ/mol. With ε for the depth, the formula becomes V(r) = ε((r*/r)¹² − 2(r*/r)⁶). At r = r* it is ε(1 − 2) = −ε, and its slope there is 0. This is a curve of the right shape near the bottom, not H₂'s own: it was made to pass through two numbers.

F(80) \approx -15.3,\ \ F(100) \approx -7.2 @@ farther: a pull together, fading
F(74) = 0 @@ the balance: the bond length

14chem4The Bend at the Bottom

Near its bottom, the curve is close to its tangent line, which is level, and its bend is V″(r*). For the two-term curve, V″(r*) = 72ε/r*², which is positive: second-test's bottom. 13k's linearization of the force at the bottom is

F(r) ≈ −V″(r*) · (r − r*): a push back toward r*, in proportion to the stretch.

That is the spring law, read off the bottom of the energy curve. 13k's standing question asked whether the spring law is a law of nature or only the linearization of whatever law a spring obeys at rest. For a bond the answer is on the page: it is the linearization, and the constant k = V″(r*) measures how stiff the bond is. Its accepted name is the force constant.

One chemist argued that two atoms are held together by a pair of electrons that belongs to both of them at once. This sharing, he said, is what a chemist's line between two symbols means.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting: a joined thing

4k, 8·stat and 13·stat followed a crowd by its middle and its spread. The same two numbers describe a joined thing. Two atoms in a bond never sit still at 74 pm. The bottom of the curve is a spring, as the last section found, and the atoms shake about it, a little nearer and a little farther, all the time. 74 pm is the average distance, and the shaking is its spread.

To describe a joined thing, give its distances, each as an average with a spread.

The depth is the price of the lock. 11·chem priced the lock of ice at 334 J a gram without watching one molecule freeze. Here the lock of a pair is priced at 436 kJ a mole, and no one has watched a single pair let go either.

Warm a gas of H₂ and its molecules shake harder. Does the average bond length change? Look at how steeply the curve climbs on each side of its bottom, and guess which way.

Two patterns, which chemists use to guess what they cannot measure. Bigger atoms sit farther apart: Cl–Cl is longer than F–F. More shared pairs between the same two atoms, C–C, C=C, C≡C, make the bottom both nearer and deeper. Fluorine breaks the second pattern a little: F–F is weaker than Cl–Cl, though shorter.

Who said it first

Lewis argued that two atoms are held together by a pair of electrons that belongs to both of them at once, and that this sharing is what a chemist's line between two symbols means.

Lewis, “The atom and the molecule”, Journal of the American Chemical Society (1916); paraphrase

The twelve-six curve was proposed by John Lennard-Jones in 1924; its accepted name is the Lennard-Jones potential. The spring law is Hooke’s law, and N_A is Avogadro’s number. In the closer, the chemist of 1916 is Gilbert N. Lewis, and the physicists of 1927 are Walter Heitler and Fritz London.
