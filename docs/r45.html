Shapes Are Read in Their Rings

Rung 44 took plus and times on their own. This rung puts them to work on shapes. A circle, a pair of lines, a curve in space: each is the set of points where some polynomials are 0, and each polynomial ring of rung 44 is a ring of functions on a space. The rung matches shapes with ideals, and finds that the match is exact over the turning numbers: a shape is read off its ideal, its points are the largest ideals, and its whole pieces are the primes. It defines the dimension of a shape by algebra alone, divides with several letters to decide membership by machine, adds the points at infinity, and counts the points two curves share. It ends where a curve crosses itself, and with a count of how far a smooth curve is from a line.

The gesture: polynomials cut out shapes, and the shape is read in the ring. Bigger ideal, smaller shape; prime ideal, whole shape; largest ideal, point; ring map, shape map the other way.

What it means to say it

To say that shapes are read in their rings is to say that every question about a shape cut out by polynomials, whether it is empty, whether it splits, what its points and dimension are, which maps it has, is a question about an ideal or a ring, and is answered there.

A shape is the set of common zeros of polynomials; shapes and ideals answer each other, and the ring of functions on a shape knows the shape: every ideal in finitely many letters is finitely made, so every system says finitely much; over the turning numbers, no common zero means 1 is a combination, points are largest ideals, and shapes match the ideals equal to their roots; whole shapes match prime ideals and every shape splits into finitely many; maps of shapes are maps of rings run backwards; dimension is the longest chain of whole shapes and one equation cuts it by one; good makers decide membership and remove letters; and in the projective plane over ℂ two curves of degrees m and n meet in exactly mn points. The rung proves it, with some steps held in trust where it says so.

Where it comes from

Descartes and Fermat wrote curves as equations in 1637, and for two centuries geometry and algebra traded tools. Hilbert in 1890 and 1893 proved that equations in finitely many letters say finitely much and that a system with no common zero is a contradiction in disguise. Emmy Noether made ideals the centre in the 1920s. Grothendieck rebuilt the subject on rings from 1958, and computers made the good makers practical from 1965. The accepted names: the subjects are commutative algebra and algebraic geometry.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, every ideal of polynomials in finitely many letters is made by finitely many members, so every system of equations says what finitely many of them say.

Second, polynomials cut out shapes, shapes give back ideals, and over the turning numbers the match is exact: no common zero means 1 is a combination; points are largest ideals; a polynomial vanishing on a shape has a power in its ideal.

Third, whole shapes are prime ideals, every shape splits into finitely many whole pieces, maps of shapes are maps of rings run backwards, and dimension is the longest chain of whole shapes, cut by one by each equation.

Fourth, a good set of makers gives one remainder, so it decides membership and removes letters by machine.

Fifth, in the projective plane any two lines meet once and two curves of degrees m and n meet in mn points; crossings are where the slopes vanish, a crossing cubic is traced by one letter, and a smooth curve of degree d has (d − 1)(d − 2)/2 handles.

What follows

The smooth cubic of 45k is the curve of 43l and 43m, whose points form a group. Rung 46 counts what machines can compute, and rung 47 asks which equations in one letter, the simplest shapes of all, can be solved by roots.

What you can do because you know it

You can say when a system of polynomial equations has no answer over ℂ, and write the contradiction. You can split a shape into whole pieces and find its dimension. You can tell when two shapes are the same by their rings. You can decide by machine whether a polynomial lies in an ideal, and find the equation of a traced curve. You can meet parallel lines, count the points two curves share, find where a curve crosses itself, and say why a smooth cubic cannot be traced by one letter.

What it costs

Everything is 44’s rings and ideals, 28’s slopes, 27’s dimension and 34’s surfaces, spent on the zeros of polynomials; four facts are held in trust: the weak zero-place technique, the projection, the cut, and the exact count of shared points with the handle count.

Who said it first

David Hilbert proved the basis theorem in 1890 and the Nullstellensatz in 1893. Emmy Noether wrote the theory of ideals in rings in 1921, and Wolfgang Krull defined dimension by primes in 1928. Étienne Bézout counted shared points in 1779. Bruno Buchberger built Gröbner bases in 1965. Alexander Grothendieck refounded the subject on schemes from 1958.

The accepted names

The study of ideals in polynomial rings is commutative algebra; the study of shapes cut out by polynomials is algebraic geometry. A shape is an affine variety, a whole one irreducible, the ring of functions the coordinate ring.
