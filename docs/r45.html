Many Machines, One Answer

Rung 40 wrote programs for one machine. Most work now runs on many, joined by links that lose and delay messages, with no clock they share. This rung asks what such machines can know and agree on, and proves the limits that shaped every method in use.

A machine knows only what it has been told. From that, prove what can be agreed, what cannot, and what chance and counting buy back.

The class · read it through, then go to 45a

What it means to say it

To say that many machines give one answer is to say this. Machines that share nothing but messages, some lost, some late and some false, still end in the same state, or the rung says exactly why they cannot.

Over a link that loses messages no finite plan guarantees agreement. With no bound on delay and one machine that may crash, no fixed method is sure to decide. With messages that can be forged, f liars need more than 3f machines. Yet any two majorities meet, counters and lists of counters order events without a clock, and merges that are associative, commutative and idempotent make copies agree with no agreement at all. The rung proves it.

Where it comes from

As soon as computers were joined into networks, the old certainties of one machine went: a silent machine might be dead or slow, and a message might never arrive. The methods in use today, from opening a connection to keeping copies of a bank’s records, grew from the proofs of what could not be done.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things.

Before any of them, 45a sets down the protocol every machine on the network speaks: a number of fixed width, a cut in it that names the machine’s network, and a packet passed one hop at a time toward the longest prefix it matches, with no promise but a try.

First, a machine acts on its view alone, so a dead machine and a slow one look the same, and two generals over a lossy link can never be sure (45b, 45c). Handshakes trade certainty for a chance as near 1 as you like (45d).

Second, order comes from messages, not clocks (45e).

Third, agreement among machines that crash rests on one count: any two majorities meet. A fixed method still cannot be sure to finish, which is why commit waits and real methods may stall (45f, 45g).

Fourth, liars need more than a third of the machines honest to spare, and an open network replaces votes with work and certainty with chance (45h, 45i).

Fifth, chance and algebra spread news, find keys, merge edits and split jobs with no one in charge (45j to 45m).

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 45n checks quorums, and 45o stamps a run.

What you can do because you know it

You can say of any network method what model it needs, what it can promise, and why it cannot promise more. You can tell a merge that will converge from one that will not.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 40’s programs, 42’s graphs, 43’s clock and keys, 44’s laws, and 41i’s chance.
