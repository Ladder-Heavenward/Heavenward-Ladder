32b · proof

Energy Is Kept

A sound has an energy, the total of its square over one repeat. Split the sound into its turnings and each turning carries a share of that energy. The step before showed that the closest sum of turnings leaves a miss at right angles to everything it used, so the shares of the turnings can never add up to more than the whole. Rung 31 proved that much for any list at right angles. What it could not say is whether the turnings miss anything. Perhaps some repeating shape has energy that no turning sees, the way a list of vectors along the floor sees nothing of a vector pointing up. This step proves that the turnings miss nothing. Every continuous repeating shape is as close as you like to a finite sum of turnings, and from there every shape with a finite energy is too. So the shares add up to the whole, exactly, and the books balance. Then one shape with a single jump pays a promise made on rung 22: the sum of one over every square has a total, and now the total can be named.

Every continuous repeating function is, everywhere at once, near a finite sum of turnings. So the turnings are a right-angled basis, and a function’s energy is the sum of the energies of its turnings. One wave with a jump gives one plus a quarter plus a ninth and so on, exactly π² over 6.

32b1No Turning Holds More Than Its Share

The energy of f is ‖f‖², the average of |f|² over one repeat; the energy of its nth turning is |cₙ|². The energy-bound technique (31h2) holds for every right-angled list, so it holds for the turnings. Here is what it says about a wave.

Fading-coefficients technique: the energies of a function’s turnings add up to at most its own energy, so its coefficients head to 0 as n grows either way.

\|f\|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{|n| \le N}|c_n|^2 + \|f - S_Nf\|^2 @@ the turning-shadow technique (32a2)
\textstyle\sum_{|n| \le N}|c_n|^2 \le \|f\|^2 \text{ for every } N @@ a size squared is at least 0
\textstyle\sum_n |c_n|^2 \le \|f\|^2,\ \text{ a settling sum} @@ the running totals rise under a ceiling (22a); the energy-bound technique (31h2)
\htmlClass{new}{c_n \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty \text{ and as } n \to -\infty} \quad \blacksquare @@ the terms of a settling sum head to 0 (22d)

Fading is a claim about every function of finite energy, however rough. 32c spends it.

32b2Turnings Come Near Every Continuous Wave

The energy-sum technique (31h3) turns the bound into an equality, but only for a right-angled basis: a list nothing else is at right angles to, whose finite sums come as near as you like to every function. So the question is whether finite sums of turnings come near everything. The average-polynomial technique (31j2) brings polynomials near any continuous function on an interval. A bend of the input turns polynomials into turnings.

Turnings-near-everything technique: for every continuous f that repeats every 2π and every ε > 0, some finite sum of turnings is within ε of f at every x at once.

g \text{ even, continuous}:\ h(t) = g(\arccos t) \text{ is continuous on } [-1, 1] @@ cos runs one-to-one from [0, π] onto [−1, 1] (21), with a continuous undoing (11)
|h(t) - p(t)| \lt \varepsilon \text{ on } [-1, 1] \text{ for some polynomial } p @@ the average-polynomial technique (31j2)
|g(x) - p(\cos x)| \lt \varepsilon \text{ for every } x @@ g even: g(x) = g(|x|) = h(cos x); and p(cos x) is a sum of turnings (the power-to-sum technique, 26i)
g_1(x) = f(x) + f(-x),\ \ g_2(x) = (f(x) - f(-x))\sin x \text{ are even} @@ an odd function times sin is even (9e)
f(x)\sin^2 x = \tfrac12 g_1(x)\sin^2 x + \tfrac12 g_2(x)\sin x @@ multiply out: the f(−x) parts cancel
|f(x)\sin^2 x - Q(x)| \lt \varepsilon,\ \ Q = \tfrac12P_1\sin^2 + \tfrac12P_2\sin @@ P₁, P₂ sums of turnings within ε of g₁, g₂; |sin| ≤ 1
|f(x)\cos^2 x - Q^{*}(x)| \lt \varepsilon @@ the same for f(x + π/2), then x slid back by π/2; sliding multiplies eₙ by a number (26b)
\htmlClass{new}{|f(x) - (Q(x) + Q^{*}(x))| \lt 2\varepsilon \text{ for every } x} \quad \blacksquare @@ sin² + cos² = 1 (21)

So a nearness everywhere at once, the strongest kind on this ladder, comes from rung 31’s polynomials by one bend. A size is an average, so nearness everywhere gives nearness in size: ‖f − P‖ ≤ ε whenever |f − P| ≤ ε at every x.

32b3Energy Is Kept

Energy-kept technique: for every f that repeats every 2π with finite energy, S_N f heads to f in size, and the energy of f is exactly the sum of the energies of its turnings.

f \text{ continuous}:\ \|f - P\| \le 2\varepsilon,\ P \text{ of speeds up to } M @@ the turnings-near-everything technique (32b2)
N \ge M:\ \|f - S_Nf\| \le \|f - P\| \le 2\varepsilon @@ the turning-shadow technique (32a2): S_N f is the closest
f \text{ of finite energy}:\ \|f - g\| \lt \varepsilon \text{ for a continuous } g @@ near a step function (the total-from-below technique, 30c3); ramp each jump steeply
\|f - S_Nf\| \le \|f - S_Ng\| \le \|f - g\| + \|g - S_Ng\| \lt 3\varepsilon \text{ for large } N @@ closest again; the product-triangle technique (31a4)
\text{the turnings are a right-angled basis} @@ their finite sums come as near as you like to every f
\htmlClass{new}{\|f\|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_n|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy-sum technique (31h3)

Two consequences follow at once. If every coefficient of f is 0, its energy is 0, so f is 0 but for a set of size zero (31g). And two functions with the same coefficients are the same, in that sense: a function is fixed by its shadows on the turnings.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

A microphone in a practice room in Portland records one second of a held cello note, and a machine splits it into turnings. The readings are anexact: the microphone hisses, the room adds its own echoes, and each share is known to a few per cent.

Splitting a wave into turnings loses no energy and makes none: the books balance exactly, and every error is the measurement’s.

The books are rigorous all the same. The energy-kept technique says the shares, added, are the energy of the recording, with nothing lost to rounding in the splitting itself and nothing made. Whatever error the shares carry, it was in the recording.

If the shares of a recorded note added to a little less than the energy of the recording, where would you look for the missing part?

32b4One Plus a Quarter Plus a Ninth

22f fenced 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯ below 2 and left its exact total for this rung (22f3). Take the wave that is x on (−π, π) and repeats every 2π: it climbs steadily and drops at every odd multiple of π. Its accepted name is the sawtooth.

c_n = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\,e^{-inx}\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\Big[\tfrac{x\,e^{-inx}}{-in}\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} + \tfrac{1}{2\pi in}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{-inx}\,dx @@ parts (16), for n ≠ 0
= \tfrac{\pi(-1)^n + \pi(-1)^n}{2\pi(-in)} + 0 = \tfrac{i(-1)^n}{n},\ \ c_0 = 0 @@ e^(±inπ) = (−1)ⁿ (26b); the second total is 0 (32a1)
\|f\|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^2\,dx = \tfrac{\pi^2}{3} @@ the energy of the wave
\tfrac{\pi^2}{3} = \textstyle\sum_{n \ne 0}\tfrac{1}{n^2} = 2\big(1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 + \cdots\big) @@ the energy-kept technique (32b3): |cₙ|² = 1/n²
\htmlClass{new}{1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{16} + \cdots = \tfrac{\pi^2}{6} \approx 1.6449} \quad \blacksquare @@ that pays 22f’s promise

The first ten terms give 1.5498, and 22f’s areas put the rest near 1/10. A whole wave’s energy, counted two ways, gave one number about the counting numbers that no counting had reached.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The fading-coefficients technique is the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma (here for square-integrable functions). The energy bound for the turnings is Bessel’s inequality, and the energy-kept technique is Parseval’s identity, or the Plancherel identity for Fourier series; its first half, S_N f → f in L², is the L² convergence of Fourier series. The turnings-near-everything technique is the trigonometric Weierstrass approximation theorem. The sum of one over every square is the Basel problem, and its value ζ(2) = π²/6.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler found π²/6 in 1734 by treating sin x as a polynomial without end with roots at the multiples of π, and gave sounder proofs later. Friedrich Bessel’s inequality dates from 1828, for orbits; Marc-Antoine Parseval stated his identity in 1799, for sums of waves, without proof. Karl Weierstrass proved the approximation theorem in 1885, for polynomials and for sums of turnings both. Frigyes Riesz and Ernst Fischer proved in 1907 that every square-summable list of coefficients belongs to a function, once the integral of rung 30 was in hand.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each turning is at right angles to the others, so a list of all of them must be complete.” Not allowed: a right-angled list can miss a direction. The turnings with even n alone are a right-angled list, and e^(ix) is at right angles to all of them. Completeness needed 32b2.
