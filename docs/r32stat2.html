What Changes When Today Remembers Yesterday?

What changes when today remembers yesterday? Rung 30’s samples were drawn independently. Readings in time are not: a warm day tends to follow a warm day, a busy week a busy week. This page writes the simplest memory, today as a share of yesterday plus something new, finds its spread with 22’s geometric sums, forecasts with it, finds why remembered data look like more evidence than they are, and, with rung 32’s turnings, sees why such data rumble with slow swings.

Model today as φ times yesterday plus a fresh, independent shock of spread σ. Unroll it: today is a geometric sum of past shocks, so its spread² is σ²/(1 − φ²) and its correlation k days apart is φᵏ. Forecast k days ahead by φᵏ times today. n remembered readings carry about n(1 − φ)/(1 + φ) readings’ worth of evidence about the mean. As a filter of shocks, the model strengthens slow turnings by up to ((1 + φ)/(1 − φ))².

32stat21Today Like Yesterday

Jean records how far each day’s temperature is from the season’s usual, in degrees. From here on, on this page, xt is day t’s departure, and the memory model says xt = φ xt−1 + et, where each et is a fresh shock, independent of all before, with average 0 and spread σ, and 0 < φ < 1.

x_t = e_t + \varphi x_{t-1} @@ the model
= e_t + \varphi e_{t-1} + \varphi^2 x_{t-2} @@ put it in again
\htmlClass{new}{= e_t + \varphi e_{t-1} + \varphi^2 e_{t-2} + \cdots} @@ a geometric weighting of past shocks
\varphi = 0.6:\ \text{a shock is } 0.6^5 \approx 0.08 \text{ of itself after 5 days} @@ memory fades by the ratio

Today is all of the past shocks, each faded by φ for every day since. This is 17·logi’s smoothing seen from the other side: there a forecast averaged the past; here the weather itself does.

32stat22Spread and Memory

The shocks are independent, so the spreads squared of the faded shocks add.

\text{spread}^2 = \sigma^2 (1 + \varphi^2 + \varphi^4 + \cdots) @@ spreads² of independent parts add
= \dfrac{\sigma^2}{1 - \varphi^2} @@ the endless-ratio-sum technique (15i), ratio φ²
\text{correlation of } x_t \text{ and } x_{t-k} = \varphi^k @@ they share the shocks before t − k
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 0.6,\ \sigma = 2:\ \text{spread} = 2/\sqrt{0.64} = 2.5} @@ one day apart 0.6, two days 0.36

Memory technique: in the memory model, the spread² is σ²/(1 − φ²), and readings k days apart have correlation φᵏ. Measuring the correlation one day apart estimates φ, and the rest follows.

32stat23Forecasting Ahead

Today’s departure is +5 degrees. The future shocks average 0, so the forecast drops them.

\text{forecast } k \text{ days ahead} = \varphi^k x_t @@ future shocks average 0
\varphi = 0.6:\ 5,\ 3,\ 1.8,\ 1.08,\ \ldots @@ back toward the usual
\text{forecast spread}^2 = \sigma^2(1 + \varphi^2 + \cdots + \varphi^{2(k-1)}) @@ the shocks still to come
\htmlClass{new}{= \sigma^2 \dfrac{1 - \varphi^{2k}}{1 - \varphi^2} \to \dfrac{\sigma^2}{1 - \varphi^2}} @@ the finite-ratio-sum technique (15i)

Forecasts fade toward the average by the factor φ each day, and their spread grows toward the spread of the data itself. Far enough ahead, the forecast is just the usual, and today tells you nothing.

32stat24Remembered Data Count for Less

Average n readings to estimate the season’s usual. With independent readings, rung 30 gives the spread of the average as spread/√n. Remembered readings repeat each other’s shocks.

\text{spread}^2 \text{ of the average} \approx \dfrac{\text{spread}^2}{n} \cdot \dfrac{1 + \varphi}{1 - \varphi} @@ adding all the correlations φᵏ
\text{as if } n\,\dfrac{1 - \varphi}{1 + \varphi} \text{ independent readings} @@ the worth of the data
\varphi = 0.6:\ n \cdot \tfrac{0.4}{1.6} = n/4 @@ a quarter
\htmlClass{new}{100 \text{ days are worth about } 25} @@ a test that ignores this is four times too sure

Worth technique: n readings with memory φ carry about n(1 − φ)/(1 + φ) readings’ worth of evidence about their average. Two series that both drift slowly will often seem to move together by chance; tests built for independent samples call that real far too often.

32stat25Slow Swings Rumble Loudest

Rung 32 breaks a signal into turnings. The memory model is a filter: shocks of every frequency go in equally, and come out shaped.

e_t = e^{2\pi i f t} \text{ in, out } \dfrac{e^{2\pi i f t}}{1 - \varphi e^{-2\pi i f}} @@ try a turning (32j)
\text{power} = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{|1 - \varphi e^{-2\pi i f}|^2} = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{1 - 2\varphi\cos 2\pi f + \varphi^2} @@ size squared
f = 0:\ \dfrac{\sigma^2}{(1 - \varphi)^2};\ \ f = \tfrac12:\ \dfrac{\sigma^2}{(1 + \varphi)^2} @@ slowest and fastest
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 0.6:\ \left(\tfrac{1.6}{0.4}\right)^2 = 16 \text{ times stronger at the slow end}} @@ the ratio of the two

Memory piles the power into slow swings, so remembered data seem to show long cycles that are only the fading of shocks. Before calling a slow swing in a record a cycle with a cause, compare it with what the memory model alone would make.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Readings in time form a time series; the memory model is an autoregressive model of order 1, AR(1); correlation with the past is autocorrelation; a series whose spread and correlations do not change is stationary. The worth of the data is the effective sample size, and series that drift together by chance give spurious regression. Power piled at slow frequencies is red noise, and the power by frequency is the spectrum.

Who said it first

George Udny Yule fitted a memory model to sunspot numbers in 1927, and warned of chance links between drifting series in 1926. George Box and Gwilym Jenkins made such models a standard tool in 1970.
