42·cb37 · newsflash for 37

Last time on the octave and the bar

Rung thirty-seven laid each octave of the pitch line on the next and found the notes closing into a loop (37j4), and it counted three against two in a bar and found the parts meeting once (37i2). Both were clocks, used before the word. Now the clocks have their arithmetic. In twelve equal steps a note is an hour on the clock of 12, and moving a tune is adding. Stack an interval and it reaches every note, or a ring of them, by the divisors it shares with 12. And the 12 itself is not given: it is five tones and two semitones, with a tone counted as two semitones. Count a tone as something else and the clock changes, and with it which intervals exist and where the circle of fifths goes. A bar is a clock too, and the beats of two figures line up again by the same arithmetic.

Notes in twelve equal steps are the hours of the clock of 12, and an interval of k steps stacked reaches 12/gcd(k, 12) of them. A scale of five tones of t steps and two semitones of s steps makes the clock 5t + 2s, with a fifth of 3t + s, and the fifth reaches every note when t and s share no divisor. A figure of a beats over bars of b beats lines up again after the least common multiple.

42·cb37.1The notes are a clock

37g3 made the note A the set of 440 · 2ᵏ, and 37j4 laid the octaves on each other into a loop. In twelve equal steps to the octave, count each pitch by its steps from A: a pitch k steps up is 440 · 2^(k/12). Two pitches are the same note exactly when they are a whole number of octaves apart, that is, when their counts differ by a multiple of 12.

Pitch-clock technique: in twelve equal steps, the notes are the hours of the clock of 12, and moving a tune up by an interval of k steps adds [k] to every note. Proof: by the difference-test technique (42a1) counts differing by a multiple of 12 are one hour, which is one note; and adding k steps multiplies every frequency by 2^(k/12), which adds k to every count, and by the carry-to-the-clock technique (42a2) adding carries to the hours.

E + 7 = [14] = [2] = B @@ a fifth above E: the pitch-clock technique

42·cb37.2Stacking an interval

Stack fifths from A: A, E, B, F♯, … The stack is the hours [0], [7], [14] = [2], [21] = [9], … Stack whole tones instead: [0], [2], [4], … and only six notes come round before A comes back.

Interval-cycle technique: stacking an interval of k steps on the clock of n reaches exactly the hours that are multiples of d = gcd(k, n), n/d of them, and comes back home after n/d stacks. Proof: every stack jk is a multiple of d. By the gcd-as-a-sum technique (42b1) d = kx + ny for some x and y, so x stacks reach [d], and so every multiple of d. And jk is a multiple of n exactly when j(k/d) is a multiple of n/d, which by the clock-cancel technique (42a4) holds exactly when n/d divides j, since k/d and n/d share no divisor.

k = 1, 5, 7, 11:\ \gcd = 1,\ \text{all } 12 \text{ notes} @@ semitones, fourths, fifths: the circle of fifths
k = 2:\ \gcd = 2,\ 6 \text{ notes} @@ whole tones: a whole-tone scale
k = 3:\ 4 \text{ notes};\ \ k = 4:\ 3 \text{ notes} @@ stacked minor thirds; stacked major thirds
\htmlClass{new}{k = 6:\ 2 \text{ notes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tritone comes home after two

So the only intervals that walk through every note are those that share no divisor with 12, the units of 42b. The rings left behind by the others are the chords musicians name: four minor thirds close into one chord, three major thirds into another. And twelve fifths come home after 7 × 12 = 84 steps, seven octaves, in equal steps; with pure fifths they miss, by the comma 42k4 measured.

42·cb37.3Tones against semitones

Where does the 12 come from? The seven-note scale climbs tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. With a tone counted as two semitones that is 5 · 2 + 2 · 1 = 12. Count in tones alone and the octave is a clock of 6, but the semitone has no hour on it and neither has the fifth, three and a half tones: the clock of tones holds only one whole-tone scale, and the other six notes are its copy moved by a semitone. The unit you count in sets the clock, and the clock sets which intervals there are.

Tone-and-semitone technique: if a tuning’s seven-note scale has five tones of t equal steps and two semitones of s steps, its octave is a clock of 5t + 2s and its fifth is 3t + s steps; and the fifths reach every note exactly when t and s share no divisor. Proof: the scale adds to 5t + 2s, and a fifth spans tone, tone, semitone, tone. Any d that divides both 3t + s and 5t + 2s divides 2(3t + s) − (5t + 2s) = t and 3(5t + 2s) − 5(3t + s) = s, and any divisor of t and s divides both; so gcd(3t + s, 5t + 2s) = gcd(t, s), and the interval-cycle technique finishes it.

t = 2,\ s = 1:\ \text{clock } 12,\ \text{fifth } 7 @@ the piano
t = 3,\ s = 2:\ 19,\ 11;\ \ t = 5,\ s = 3:\ 31,\ 18 @@ semitones more than half a tone
t = 9,\ s = 4:\ 53,\ 31 @@ 42k4’s best small tuning
\htmlClass{new}{t = 4,\ s = 2:\ \text{clock } 24,\ \text{fifth } 14,\ \gcd 2:\ \text{only } 12 \text{ of the } 24} \quad \blacksquare @@ quarter tones: fifths never reach them

The ratio of tone to semitone also says how good the fifth is. The fifth takes the share (3t + s)/(5t + 2s) of the octave, and a pure fifth takes log₂(3/2) ≈ 0.585. That share comes at t/s ≈ 2.26. The piano’s 2 falls a little short; 53’s 9/4 = 2.25 is nearly on it, which is why 42k4 found the fifth of 53 so close.

42·cb37.4The bar is a clock

A bar of b beats is the clock of b: beat 9 of a 4-beat bar is beat [1] of the third bar. 37i2 counted p against q meeting only at the start when p and q share no divisor; that is the remainder technique (42c1): in a bar of pq ticks, the readings (k on the clock of p, k on the clock of q) take each pair once. Now let a figure of a beats run over bars of b beats, a 3-beat pattern over a 4-beat bar.

Realign technique: a figure of a beats played over bars of b beats starts with a bar again first after lcm(a, b) = ab/gcd(a, b) beats. Proof: the figure and the bar start together on the beats x with x ≡ 0 (mod a) and x ≡ 0 (mod b); by the shared-divisor technique (42c4) those are one hour of the clock of lcm(a, b), the multiples of it.

3 \text{ over } 4:\ 12 \text{ beats, three bars} @@ the realign technique
6 \text{ over } 4:\ 24/2 = 12 \text{ beats, not } 24 @@ they share 2
7 \text{ over } 12:\ 84 @@ the circle of fifths, a figure of 7 over a bar of 12
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a figure and a bar line up by the lcm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 37i3 sped such a rhythm into an interval

So the octave and the bar obey one arithmetic. Twelve fifths closing into seven octaves and a 7-beat figure lining up with a 12-beat bar after 84 beats are the same fact, read on the pitch line and on the clock face of time.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

An hour of the clock of 12 is a pitch class, and moving by [k] is transposition. The rings left by stacked intervals are interval cycles: the whole-tone scale, the diminished seventh chord (stacked minor thirds) and the augmented triad (stacked major thirds); the walk by fifths is the circle of fifths. A tuning of n equal steps to the octave is n-tone equal temperament; the tunings built from a large step t and a small step s are the regular diatonic tunings. A figure over a bar of another length is a polymeter.

Who said it first

Jing Fang noticed 53 fifths come close to 31 octaves around 40 BCE, and Nicholas Mercator studied the tuning of 53 in the 1600s. Christiaan Huygens wrote on 31 steps in 1691, and Guillaume Costeley wrote music in 19 steps in 1558. Milton Babbitt brought clock arithmetic into music theory in the 1940s and 1950s, and Allen Forte’s The Structure of Atonal Music (1973) set out the pitch classes as sets.

A proof that is not allowed

“Twelve fifths come home on the clock of 12, so twelve pure fifths are seven octaves.” Not allowed: on the clock a fifth is 7 equal steps by the tuning’s own choice; a pure fifth is the ratio 3/2, and (3/2)¹² is not 2⁷, since no fifth is an octave (37g3). The clock is exact about the tuning, not about the pure intervals it stands in for.
