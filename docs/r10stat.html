Why Sort the People Before You Pick Them?

Why sort the people before you pick them? A baker wants to know how many loaves a household buys in a week, and can ask only two households out of six. Three live in town and buy few; three live on farms and buy many. A fair pick of two might land both in town, or both on farms, and miss badly. Picking one from town and one from the farms cannot miss that way. This page lists every sample both ways and measures their spread exactly, finds the weights needed when the groups are not the same size, and shows how picking whole streets at once can make the spread worse.

List every sample a design can draw, and its mean. If the means average to the true mean, the design is right on average; their standard deviation (8·stat) says how far one sample tends to miss. Sort the people into groups that differ from each other and are alike inside, and pick from each group: the spread shrinks. Weight each group by its share of the whole. Picking whole groups at once does the opposite when the groups are alike inside.

10stat1Every Sample of Two

The six households buy these numbers of loaves a week. In town: 2, 3 and 4. On farms: 8, 9 and 10. The true mean is 36/6 = 6. A fair sample of two, in 6·stat’s sense, makes every pair equally likely. There are 6 choose 2 = 15 pairs.

Right on average, and yet a single sample can land at 2.5 or 9.5. The six worst samples are the ones with both households from the same place.

10stat2Sort First, Then Pick

Now the baker sorts first: pick one household fairly from town, and one fairly from the farms. Every sample has one of each, so the design can draw only the 9 pairs of the last row, each equally likely.

\text{means: } 5, 5.5, 5.5, 6, 6, 6, 6.5, 6.5, 7 @@ the 9 pairs, one of each
54/9 = 6 @@ still right on average
\text{squared misses: } 1 + 0.25 \cdot 4 + 1 = 3 @@ from 6
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt{3/9} \approx 0.58} @@ a third of the spread of the fair pick

Sorted-pick technique: when the groups differ from each other and are alike inside, picking from every group keeps the sample right on average and shrinks its spread. The 6 worst samples were removed by the design, not by luck. A pair from one place is no longer possible.

The groups must be made before anyone is asked, from something known about every household in advance, such as where it is. Sorting by the answers would be 6stat5’s mistake again.

10stat3Groups of Different Sizes Need Weights

A different town: four households in town buy 2, 3, 4 and 3 loaves, and two farms buy 8 and 10. The true mean is 30/6 = 5. Pick one from town and one from the farms, and average the two. The town pick is right on average at 3, and the farm pick at 9, so the plain average of the two is right on average at 6, not 5.

The town is two thirds of the households and the farms one third. Weight each pick by its group’s share.

\tfrac46 \cdot \text{town pick} + \tfrac26 \cdot \text{farm pick} @@ each group by its share of the six
\text{right on average at } \tfrac46 \cdot 3 + \tfrac26 \cdot 9 @@ each pick right on average within its group (6stat3)
= 2 + 3 @@ fractions (5h)
\htmlClass{new}{= 5} @@ the true mean

Weights technique: with groups of different sizes, weight each group’s mean by the group’s share of the whole, and the result is right on average. Without the weights, a small group counts as much as a large one.

10stat4Picking Whole Streets

Visiting houses far apart is slow, so a survey often picks whole streets and asks everyone on them. Say the six households live two to a street: one street buys 2 and 3, one buys 4 and 8, one buys 9 and 10. Pick one street fairly and ask both households.

\text{street means: } 2.5,\ 6,\ 9.5 @@ three samples, equally likely
(2.5 + 6 + 9.5)/3 = 6 @@ right on average
\text{squared misses: } 12.25 + 0 + 12.25 = 24.5 @@ from 6
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt{24.5/3} \approx 2.86} @@ more spread than the fair pick of two, 1.97

Two households on the same street are alike, so asking the second tells the baker little the first did not. Picking whole groups that are alike inside spreads the answer more than picking the same number of people one by one. Sorted picks want groups alike inside; whole-group picks want groups as mixed as the whole.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Sorting first and picking from every group is stratified sampling, and the groups are strata; the weights are stratum weights. Picking whole groups is cluster sampling, and a design that picks groups and then people inside them is multistage sampling. The spread of a sample’s mean is its standard error, and how much a design changes it compared with a fair pick of the same size is its design effect.

Who said it first

Jerzy Neyman showed in 1934 that sorting into strata, with the sample shared out by the size and spread of each stratum, gives a more precise answer than picking whole groups chosen to look typical.
