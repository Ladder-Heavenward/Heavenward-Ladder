Rung 17 · theory

Turning multiplying into adding

Rung 16 left one entry out of its table. Every power of the input has a function whose slope it is, except one: the power minus one, which is one over the input. Yet one over the input is continuous for every positive input, so 16b's antiderivative-exists says it has an antiderivative: its own area function, the area under one over the input, from 1 up to the input. Rung 16 found that function and left it unnamed. This rung names it and studies it. Two facts about it change what can be computed. The area from 1 to a product is the area from 1 to the first factor plus the area from 1 to the second. So the function turns multiplying into adding. And the function that runs it backwards is its own slope: its rate of growth is always exactly its size.

You may know the logarithm as a button on a calculator, and its rules as something to memorise. Here you build it from an area, and the rules come out on their own. The button was the way in. This is the climb.

The natural logarithm of a positive number is the area under one over the input, from 1 to that number. Its slope is one over the input, and the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms. It rises forever, slowly, through every real height, so it can be run backwards: the exponential. The exponential is its own slope. Its value at 1 is the number e, and any positive number raised to any real power is the exponential of that power times the number’s logarithm.

What it means to say it

For each x > 0 there is a number: the area under the graph of 1/t from 1 to x, counted negative when x is less than 1. That gives a function of x. It is defined by an area, not by a recipe of powers and roots, and no such recipe gives it.

The claim of this rung is about how it treats multiplying. Stretch the stretch from 1 to x by a factor y, so that it runs from y to xy. The graph of 1/t is lower there by the same factor y. The strip is y times wider and y times lower, so its area is unchanged. So the area from y to xy equals the area from 1 to x. And the area from 1 to xy is the area from 1 to y plus that. Multiplying the inputs adds the outputs.

That one fact makes the function a translator. A product of numbers becomes a sum of their logs, a power becomes a multiple, a root becomes a fraction of the log. Running the function backwards translates sums back into products. The function that runs it backwards has one more property, and it is the one that matters most outside mathematics. Its slope at every point equals its value there. Anything whose rate of change is proportional to its size is described by it: a colony of cells, a sample of radium, money at interest.

Where it comes from

In 1614 a Scottish landowner published tables that turned multiplying into adding. He built them by imagining two points moving along two lines, one at a steady speed and one slowing in proportion to the distance it had left. A Swiss clockmaker had computed similar tables before 1600, and printed them in 1620. A professor in London visited the first maker in 1615. He recomputed the tables so that the log of 1 is 0 and the log of 10 is 1, and his tables were used for three and a half centuries.

In 1647 it was shown that areas under the graph of y = 1/x behave like logarithms, and in 1649 this was said outright. In 1668 the area was given as an endless sum. In 1683 the number e turned up in a question about interest added more and more often. From 1728 a Swiss mathematician named e. He defined the exponential as a limit and as an endless sum, and in 1748 set out the logarithm as its inverse.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things, each from the ones before.

First, the definition. For x > 0, ln x is the area under 1/t from 1 to x (17a2). It is 0 at 1, positive past 1, negative before it (17a3).

Second, its slope. By rung 16, the slope of ln at x is 1/x (17b1). That is the table's missing entry.

Third, multiplying becomes adding. For fixed y, x ↦ ln(xy) has the same slope as ln, so the two differ by a constant, which x = 1 shows is ln y (17c1).

Fourth, ln reaches every height. It always rises, and ln(2ⁿ) = n ln 2 passes every bound both ways (17d1); by rung 12 it takes every value between, so it pairs the positive reals one to one with all the reals (17d2).

Fifth, the function that undoes it. Call it exp. It takes every real to a positive real, and exp(1) is a number named e, a little over 2.7 (17e2). Multiplying becomes adding under ln, so adding becomes multiplying under exp (17e1).

Sixth, exp is its own slope. By rung 13's rule for the slope of a function run backwards, the slope of exp at x is 1 over the slope of ln at exp x, which is exp x (17f1). And any function equal to its own slope is a constant times exp (17f2).

Seventh, any real power. For p > 0 and any real x, pˣ is exp(x ln p). For whole and fractional x it agrees with rung 8's powers (17g1), and its techniques are rung 8's exponent techniques, now for every real exponent (17g2).

What follows

Logs to any base are multiples of ln, and equations with an unknown in the exponent are solved by taking logs. The slopes of eˣ, ln x, pˣ and xʳ for every real r follow by the chain rule. The integrals of 1/x, of g′/g and of eˣ join the table. A quantity whose rate of change is k times its size is Ce^{kt}: growth with a fixed doubling time, or decay with a fixed half-life. Fractions of polynomials split into pieces whose integrals are logs. And in the sciences there is acidity on a scale of logs, and the decay of atoms and the ages of rocks. There is the growth of populations and the limits on it. And there are graphs on which curves of growth become straight lines.

What you can do because you know it

You can say exactly what a logarithm and an exponential are, and prove their techniques. You can raise any positive number to any real power, and say what that means. You can solve equations where the unknown is an exponent. You can find slopes and areas that involve eˣ and ln x. You can model anything that grows or decays in proportion to its size, and find its doubling time or half-life. You can date a rock or a bone from what is left of an atom inside it. You can read a graph with a log scale.

That is a first handful of logs. The ladder has seen only the tip of what you will do with them.

And 17·stat asks why so many measured numbers start with 1, and reads the first digit off the log scale. It carries a box on laws with a known exception rate. And 17·crypt asks how long a password takes to guess, counting the passwords, measuring the count in bits, and turning bits into years at real guessing speeds. It carries a box on two sizes of truth.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down. The rung spends rung 16's two fundamental techniques, and rung 14's rule that same slopes mean the same shape lifted. It spends rung 13's chain rule and its rule for the slope of an inverse, and rung 12's intermediate value technique. And it spends rung 9's inverse functions and rung 8's rational powers. One price is new: ln is defined by an area, so its values come from squeezing or from series, not from a finite recipe. e is a number that rung 15's squeeze can pin down to any number of places. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that an area turns multiplying into adding, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

John Napier, in 1614, published the first tables of logarithms, built from his two moving points. Jost Bürgi had computed similar tables before 1600 and printed them in 1620. Henry Briggs, after visiting Napier in 1615, recomputed the tables so that the log of 1 is 0 and the log of 10 is 1, and Briggs’s tables were used for three and a half centuries.

Grégoire de Saint-Vincent, in 1647, showed that areas under the hyperbola y = 1/x behave like logarithms, and Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa said so outright in 1649. Nicholas Mercator, in 1668, gave the area as an endless sum. Jacob Bernoulli, in 1683, met the number e while asking about interest added more and more often. Leonhard Euler, from 1728, named e, defined the exponential as a limit and as an endless sum, and set out the logarithm as its inverse in 1748.

Accepted names

The log techniques are the laws of logarithms, and the real-exponent techniques are the laws of exponents. ln is the natural logarithm.
