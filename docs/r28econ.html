28·econ · economics

What is the best basket on a budget?

What is the best basket on a budget? A student in Missoula has sixty dollars a week for coffee and meals out. Every extra cup of coffee is a meal not eaten out, and every meal is three cups not drunk. Economists wanted a rule for how a person, or a household, or a whole country’s shoppers, split a budget between goods, and for what a change in a price or an income does to the split. What makes it hard is that the choice has two knobs, how much of each, tied together by the budget, and the answer must be best along the budget line, not best overall. Rung twelve’s and rung thirteen’s tools found the top of a hill with one knob. This page uses rung twenty-eight’s gradients that line up to find the best basket on a budget line. Then it reads the multiplier that comes with it as the worth of one more dollar, and splits the effect of a price rise into two parts.

Draw the curves along which the shopper is equally happy. On a budget, the best basket is where one of those curves just touches the budget line, and there the uphill arrow of satisfaction points the same way as the uphill arrow of spending. For the simplest kind of satisfaction that means spending a fixed share of the budget on each good, whatever the prices. The number that scales one arrow onto the other is the worth of one more dollar: how much the best satisfaction rises when the budget does. When a price rises, what you buy changes for two reasons. The good is dearer next to the others, so you switch away from it. And your budget buys less, so you are a little poorer. The two parts can be separated exactly. When the budget is small, the best basket may leave one good out altogether.

28econ1Where a level curve touches the budget line

Let x be cups of coffee a week at p_x dollars, and y meals out at p_y dollars. The budget m allows every basket with p_x x + p_y y = m: the budget line. Suppose the student’s satisfaction is U = α ln x + (1 − α) ln y, with α between 0 and 1. Each doubling of either good adds a fixed amount, and the first cup counts for more than the tenth.

\nabla U = \big(\tfrac{\alpha}{x},\ \tfrac{1 - \alpha}{y}\big),\ \ \nabla(p_xx + p_yy) = (p_x, p_y) @@ the one-knob technique (28a), on each
\tfrac{\alpha}{x} = \lambda p_x,\ \ \tfrac{1 - \alpha}{y} = \lambda p_y @@ at the best point on the budget line, the gradients line up: the lines-up technique (28g)
p_xx = \tfrac \alpha\lambda,\ \ p_yy = \tfrac{1 - \alpha}{\lambda} \Rightarrow m = \tfrac1\lambda @@ add the two: the budget is spent
\htmlClass{new}{p_xx = \alpha m,\ \ p_yy = (1 - \alpha)m} @@ λ = 1/m
f(t) = \alpha\ln t + (1 - \alpha)\ln\tfrac{m - p_xt}{p_y},\ \ f''(t) = -\tfrac{\alpha}{t^2} - \tfrac{(1 - \alpha)p_x^2}{(m - p_xt)^2} \lt 0 @@ along the budget line, one knob t = x is left
\text{the lined-up point is the top of the budget line} \quad \blacksquare @@ f′ only falls, so its one 0 is the top (14)

From here on, a level curve of satisfaction, along which the shopper is equally happy, is an indifference curve. Spending-share technique: a shopper whose satisfaction is α ln x + (1 − α) ln y spends the share α of the budget on x and the rest on y, whatever the prices. At the best basket the indifference curve just touches the budget line. With m = 60 dollars, coffee at 5 dollars and meals at 15, and α = 0.25, the student spends 15 dollars on 3 cups and 45 dollars on 3 meals.

Lined up is not always best. The gradients line up at every point where a level curve and the budget line touch, and a level curve can touch from the wrong side. The second half of the proof, the one knob left along the budget line, is what makes this point the top.

28econ2The worth of one more dollar

The multiplier λ came out of 28g as a number that scales one arrow onto the other. It has a meaning. Let the budget m move, and let x(m), y(m) be the best basket at each budget, with U*(m) = U(x(m), y(m)) the best satisfaction it buys.

p_x\,x(m) + p_y\,y(m) = m \Rightarrow p_x\,x'(m) + p_y\,y'(m) = 1 @@ the best basket spends the budget, at every m
U^{*\prime}(m) = U_x\,x'(m) + U_y\,y'(m) @@ the roads-add technique (28c)
= \lambda p_x\,x'(m) + \lambda p_y\,y'(m) @@ the gradients line up at every best basket (28g)
\htmlClass{new}{U^{*\prime}(m) = \lambda} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first line

Dollar-worth technique: at the best basket, the multiplier is the worth of one more dollar: the rate at which the best satisfaction rises with the budget. For α ln x + (1 − α) ln y, λ = 1/m. The student’s sixty-first dollar adds about 1/60 to the best satisfaction. A household with 600 dollars a week gets a tenth as much from its next dollar.

Every budget-like limit gets a multiplier of its own. A planner who picks the best plan under a limit on steel, another on hours of work and another on power gets one multiplier for each: what one more tonne, hour or kilowatt hour would add. 27·econ.6.5 asked whether a table could run an economy. These multipliers are the prices a planning office would set, and they come out of the plan without a market.

28econ3What a price rise does

When coffee gets dearer, the student buys less of it, for two reasons. Coffee is now dearer next to meals, so a basket with less coffee and more meals is as good for less money. And the same budget now buys less of everything. To pull the two apart, let e(p, u) be the least spending that reaches satisfaction u at prices p, and h(p, u) the basket that does it: what the shopper would buy if handed just enough to stay as happy as before.

e = p\cdot h,\ \ U(h(p, u)) = u @@ h is the cheapest basket on the level curve u
\nabla U\cdot\tfrac{\partial h}{\partial p_x} = 0,\ \ \nabla U = \mu\,p \Rightarrow p\cdot\tfrac{\partial h}{\partial p_x} = 0 @@ roads add (28c) along the level curve; at the cheapest point the gradients line up (28g)
\tfrac{\partial e}{\partial p_x} = h_x + p\cdot\tfrac{\partial h}{\partial p_x} = h_x @@ the product rule, then the line above
h_x(p, u) = x\big(p, e(p, u)\big) @@ with exactly enough money, the best basket is the cheapest one
\tfrac{\partial h_x}{\partial p_x} = \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} + \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial m}\,\tfrac{\partial e}{\partial p_x} = \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} + \tfrac{\partial x}{\partial m}\,x @@ roads add (28c), and the line two up
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} = \tfrac{\partial h_x}{\partial p_x} - x\,\tfrac{\partial x}{\partial m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the switch, less the shrink of the budget

Price-split technique: what a price rise does to what you buy is the switch away from the dearer good at the same satisfaction, less the amount you buy times what an extra dollar of budget does to it. The switch part is never above 0: a cheapest basket never buys more of what got dearer. For the student, x = αm/p_x, so at 5 dollars a cup ∂x/∂p_x = −am/p_x² = −0.6 cups a week per dollar: −0.45 of switch and −0.15 of shrink.

In 2018 Seattle put a tax of 1.75 cents an ounce on sugary drinks, about 21 cents on a can. Counts of what stores sold found the volume of taxed drinks bought in the city fell by about a fifth, while sales in stores just outside the city changed little. A drink is a small part of a budget, so nearly all of the fall was switch.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

“Raise the price and people buy less” is the law of demand. It is a measured law: shoppers, stores and whole countries have been watched obeying it for centuries. The price-split technique says what it rests on. The switch part always pulls the right way. The shrink part pulls the right way for most goods, those a shopper buys more of when richer.

The law of demand is a measured law with known exceptions; the price-split technique says where to look for them: a staple, a large share of the budget, and a shrink that pulls the other way.

For a staple that fills most of a very poor household’s budget, the shrink can pull the other way, and harder. A rise in the price of rice leaves the family too poor for meat, and it buys more rice to fill the gap. In 2006 a study that cut the price of rice for the poorest households in Hunan, at random, found some of them bought less of it.

A student in Eugene spends half of a small food budget on rice. If rice gets dearer, which part of the price split would you measure first, and what would you expect it to show?

28econ4Leaving one good out

Gradients line up only where both goods are bought. With satisfaction ln(1 + x) + ln(1 + y), a concert ticket at 40 dollars and coffee at 5, the lined-up point asks for x = (m + 5 − 40)/80 tickets. With a budget of 30 dollars that is below 0: no basket lines up. The best basket sits on the edge of what can be bought.

\text{move one dollar from } y \text{ to } x: \ \ \tfrac{d}{dt}U\big(x + \tfrac t{p_x}, y - \tfrac t{p_y}\big)\Big|_{t = 0} = \tfrac{U_x}{p_x} - \tfrac{U_y}{p_y} @@ roads add (28c)
\text{at the best basket with } x = 0: \ \ \tfrac{U_x}{p_x} \le \tfrac{U_y}{p_y} @@ otherwise the move would raise U, and it is allowed
(0, 6):\ \ \tfrac{U_x}{p_x} = \tfrac{1}{40} = 0.025,\ \ \tfrac{U_y}{p_y} = \tfrac{1/7}{5} \approx 0.029 @@ m = 30: all on coffee
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a good is left out when its satisfaction per dollar is the lower one at the edge}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with m = 100 the lined-up basket is 0.81 tickets and 13.5 cups

Edge-basket technique: at a best basket that leaves x out, a dollar spent on x adds no more satisfaction than a dollar spent on y. Most households buy nothing at all of most goods in a store. The edge is not a curiosity but the usual case, and a small rise in the budget can bring a new good into the basket. This is why the lines-up technique of 28g speaks only of points where both goods can move.

The accepted names

The satisfaction U is a utility function, and α ln x + (1 − α) ln y, or x^α y^(1 − α), is Cobb–Douglas utility. An indifference curve’s slope, the rate at which a shopper trades one good for the other, is the marginal rate of substitution, and the lined-up point is where it equals the price ratio. The multiplier λ is the marginal utility of income, and a multiplier on a planner’s limit is a shadow price. e is the expenditure function, h the compensated (Hicksian) demand and x the ordinary (Marshallian) demand. ∂e/∂p_x = h_x is Shephard’s lemma, and the price-split technique is the Slutsky equation, with a substitution effect and an income effect. A good bought more as it gets dearer is a Giffen good. A basket that leaves a good out is a corner solution. That the worth of the best changes with a limit at the rate λ is the envelope theorem.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli (1738) proposed that the worth of a gain is in proportion to its log, so a dollar is worth less to the rich. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the lined-up multiplier in 1788 (in his Mécanique analytique), for mechanics. Eugen Slutsky split the price effect in 1915, and John Hicks and Roy Allen made the split known in 1934. Leonid Kantorovich (1939) found multipliers acting as prices in a planner’s problem. Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller ran the rice study in Hunan (published 2008). The Seattle tax study is by Lisa Powell and colleagues (2020). In 28·econ.6.5, William Stanley Jevons and Francis Edgeworth treated utility as measurable; Vilfredo Pareto (1906) and Hicks and Allen (1934) built demand on the order alone; John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern (1944) brought measured utility back for choices under chance.
