27·chem · chemistry

Can every reaction be balanced?

Can every reaction be balanced? A propane barbecue on a deck in Portland burns its fuel with the air, and out come carbon dioxide and water. Chemists write that change with a number in front of each kind of molecule, so that no atom appears from nowhere and none is lost. For propane the numbers are easy to find by trying. For the water that runs out of the old copper mines at Butte, Montana, turning orange and sour, the reaction holds six kinds of particle and five things to keep, and trying stops working. Worse, some reactions seem to have many right sets of numbers, and some have none at all. What makes the question hard is that balancing looks like a craft, learned by feel, and a craft cannot tell you when to stop looking. This page turns it into rung twenty-seven’s straight samenesses: one sameness for each element, one more for the charge, and the balances are exactly the arrows a grid sends to zero. Then the dimension of that space decides everything: one balance up to stretching, many, or none.

Count each element’s atoms on both sides, and ask that the counts agree. That is one straight sameness for each element, and one more for the charge when charged particles take part. The lists of amounts that keep them all are what one grid sends to zero, so they add and stretch, and form a space of arrows. When that space has dimension one, every balance is a stretch of one, and there is a single balance in least whole numbers. When it has dimension two or more, the reaction is a mix of separate reactions, and counting atoms cannot say in what proportion: the fire or the flask decides. When the grid sends only the zero list to zero, there is no balance, and some species has been left out. A balance with a negative amount is a balance with that species on the other side.

27chem1One sameness for each element

A reaction is written with an unknown amount in front of each species, here x₁ to x₄. Atoms are not made or destroyed in a reaction: that is a law, measured for more than two hundred years. So each element gives a sameness: its atoms on the left equal its atoms on the right.

x_1\,\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + x_2\,\text{O}_2 \to x_3\,\text{CO}_2 + x_4\,\text{H}_2\text{O} @@ propane burning in a barbecue
\text{C}: 3x_1 = x_3,\ \ \text{H}: 8x_1 = 2x_4,\ \ \text{O}: 2x_2 = 2x_3 + x_4 @@ count each element on both sides
A\,x = 0,\ \ A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 8 & 0 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} @@ one row per element, one column per species; the right side moved across with a minus
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the balances are what } A \text{ loses: a space of arrows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nothing-sent technique (27c1): what a straight map loses is a space

Atom-grid technique: a list of amounts balances a reaction exactly when the reaction’s atom grid sends it to zero, so balances add and stretch, and together they form a space of arrows. Two balances added give a balance. A balance stretched gives a balance. The question “can it be balanced?” becomes a question about what one grid loses.

27chem2One balance, up to stretching

Solve the propane samenesses by clearing one unknown at a time (27j). Then count the dimension of what the grid loses.

x_3 = 3x_1,\ \ x_4 = 4x_1 @@ the C and H rows
2x_2 = 6x_1 + 4x_1 \ \Rightarrow\ x_2 = 5x_1 @@ the O row, with x₃ and x₄ cleared
x = x_1\,(1, 5, 3, 4) @@ every balance is a stretch of one list
\dim(\text{lost}) = 4 - 3 = 1 @@ lost-plus-reached (27c2): the three rows of A are free, so A reaches all lists of three
\htmlClass{new}{\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\,\text{O}_2 \to 3\,\text{CO}_2 + 4\,\text{H}_2\text{O}} \quad \blacksquare @@ x₁ = 1 gives whole numbers with no common divisor

One-up-to-scale technique: when the atom grid of n species reaches a space of dimension n − 1, every balance is a stretch of one, and exactly one balance has whole amounts, all positive, with no common divisor above 1. Find any balance, clear its fractions by multiplying by their bottoms, and divide by the greatest common divisor of what is left (4j). Charged particles add one row: the charge is kept too.

x_1\,\text{MnO}_4^- + x_2\,\text{Fe}^{2+} + x_3\,\text{H}^+ \to x_4\,\text{Mn}^{2+} + x_5\,\text{Fe}^{3+} + x_6\,\text{H}_2\text{O} @@ purple permanganate, used to measure the iron in a sample
x_4 = x_1,\ \ x_6 = 4x_1,\ \ x_5 = x_2,\ \ x_3 = 2x_6 = 8x_1 @@ the Mn, O, Fe and H rows
-x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 = 2x_4 + 3x_5 @@ the charge row: charges on the left equal charges on the right
-x_1 + 2x_2 + 8x_1 = 2x_1 + 3x_2 \ \Rightarrow\ x_2 = 5x_1 @@ clear x₃, x₄ and x₅
\htmlClass{new}{\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\,\text{Fe}^{2+} + 8\,\text{H}^+ \to \text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\,\text{Fe}^{3+} + 4\,\text{H}_2\text{O}} \quad \blacksquare @@ six species, five free rows: one balance up to stretching

27chem3Many balances

Wood is mostly carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Follow its carbon. A flaming fire turns nearly all of it into carbon dioxide. A smouldering fire, as in the peat and duff of a forest floor, sends a larger part out as carbon monoxide. Write both products and see what the atoms allow.

x_1\,\text{C} + x_2\,\text{O}_2 \to x_3\,\text{CO} + x_4\,\text{CO}_2 @@ carbon burning to both oxides
\text{C}: x_1 = x_3 + x_4,\ \ \text{O}: 2x_2 = x_3 + 2x_4 @@ two rows, four unknowns
\dim(\text{lost}) = 4 - 2 = 2 @@ lost-plus-reached (27c2): the two rows are free
x = s\,(2, 1, 2, 0) + t\,(1, 1, 0, 1) @@ put x₃ = 2s and x₄ = t; the two lists are free, a basis (27b)
\htmlClass{new}{s:\ 2\,\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\,\text{CO};\ \ t:\ \text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ every balance is a mix of these two

Many-ways technique: when the balances form a space of dimension two or more, every balance is a mix of free ones, and counting atoms cannot fix the proportions of the mix. The amounts are all positive exactly when s and t are both at least 0, not both 0. Which mix happens is set by the fire: how hot it is, and how much air reaches it. If one carbon in five leaves as carbon monoxide, then per 100 carbons s = 10 and t = 80, and the fire takes 90 O₂. The atoms are kept either way.

27chem4No balance, and the wrong side

Hydrogen peroxide poured on a cut fizzes and leaves water. Write only the water, and try to balance.

x_1\,\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \to x_2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}:\ \ 2x_1 = 2x_2,\ \ 2x_1 = x_2 @@ the H and O rows
x_2 = x_1 = 2x_1 \ \Rightarrow\ x_1 = x_2 = 0 @@ the grid loses only 0: no balance
2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \to 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 @@ the fizz is oxygen: with it, a balance
x_1\,\text{CaO} \to x_2\,\text{CaCO}_3 + x_3\,\text{CO}_2:\ \ x = x_1\,(1, 1, -1) @@ the Ca, C and O rows; one balance up to stretching, with a negative amount
\htmlClass{new}{\text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2 \to \text{CaCO}_3} \quad \blacksquare @@ move the negative species across: lime taking up carbon dioxide as mortar sets

Missing-species technique: if the atom grid sends only the zero list to zero, the reaction as written has no balance, and some species has been left out. Wrong-side technique: a balance with a negative amount is a balance with that species moved to the other side. So the question “can it be balanced?” always has an answer. Either the grid loses only 0, or it loses a space of dimension one or more. Then each balance says, by its signs, which side each species belongs on.

The accepted names

The amounts in front are stoichiometric coefficients, and a reaction with them is a balanced equation. The atom grid is the formula matrix (or element matrix), and the space of balances is its null space. Keeping atoms is the law of conservation of mass; keeping charge, conservation of charge. A reaction with many balances is a system of independent reactions, and the dimension of the space of balances is their number. Reactions in which electrons move, like the permanganate one, are redox reactions; the sour water at Butte is acid mine drainage. Carbon burning to both oxides is incomplete combustion; carbon dioxide taking up carbon to make carbon monoxide is the Boudouard reaction.

Who said it first

Antoine Lavoisier weighed reactions in sealed vessels in the 1770s, and in 1789 stated that matter is neither made nor destroyed. Jeremias Richter counted the amounts in which substances combine, and named the study stoichiometry, in 1792. John Dalton’s atoms (1808) explained why the amounts are whole numbers. Chemical engineers set balancing out as a question about the rank of the formula matrix in the twentieth century, and with it the case of many balances. In 27·chem.6.5, Georg Stahl’s phlogiston, the substance released in burning, was defended in the 1770s by some with a negative weight; Lavoisier’s weighing ended it.
