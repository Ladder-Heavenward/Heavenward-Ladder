11d · proof

Every polynomial is continuous

Two bricks and two ways of joining them build every polynomial. The bricks are a constant and the input itself, both continuous by 11a's lines-are-continuous. The joins are adding and multiplying, both of which keep continuity by 11b. What is needed is a plan that builds each polynomial from these in finitely many joins, and an induction that carries continuity along the plan. The plan is to peel: take the constant term off, and what is left is the input times a polynomial of degree one less. So the induction runs on the degree, 9g's measure of a polynomial, and every polynomial, at every real, lands where it was headed.

Peel the constant term off: a polynomial of degree one more is a constant plus the input times a polynomial of degree one less. A product and a sum carry continuity up one degree; induction carries it up every degree.

11d1Two bricks, two joins

Stands on 11a4 (lines-are-continuous) and 11b (sums, products).

A constant c is continuous at every real, and so is x ↦ x (lines-are-continuous). If f and g are continuous at w, so are f + g and fg (continuous-sums, continuous-products). x² is x · x; 3x² is 3 · x²; 3x² + 1 is that plus 1. Every polynomial you can write is built this way, in finitely many joins. But “every polynomial you can write” is not a set the ladder can induct on; 9g's polynomials, with their coefficients and degrees, are. The proof needs a single step that turns a polynomial of one degree into one of the next, built only from bricks and joins.

11d2Peeling a power

Stands on 11d1, 4j (Σ) and 9g (polynomials).

Peel-off-the-constant technique: for every number n and reals a₀, a₁, ⋯, a_S(n), and every real x, Σ_{k≤S(n)} a_k x^k = a₀ + x · Σ_{k≤n} a_S(k) x^k. The sum on the right of x has the coefficients a₁, a₂, ⋯, a_S(n): the old list with its first entry taken off, each moved down one place.

\textstyle\sum_{k \le S(n)} a_k x^k = \sum_{k \lt S(S(n))} a_k x^k @@ 4j's line: Σ over k ≤ m is Σ over k < S(m)
= a_0 x^0 + \textstyle\sum_{k \lt S(n)} a_{S(k)}\, x^{\htmlClass{new}{S(k)}} @@ first-term-off (4j), with real terms (9g): split off the first term
= a_0 + \textstyle\sum_{k \lt S(n)} a_{S(k)}\,(\htmlClass{new}{x^k \cdot x}) @@ power-zero: x⁰ = 1, times-one on ℝ; power-of-a-next: x^S(k) = x^k · x (8f's powers of a real)
= a_0 + \textstyle\sum_{k \lt S(n)} \htmlClass{new}{x} \cdot (a_{S(k)}\, x^k) @@ regroup for times on ℝ, turn-about for times on ℝ: regroup, turn about
= a_0 + \htmlClass{keep}{x} \cdot \textstyle\sum_{k \le n} a_{S(k)}\, x^k \quad \blacksquare @@ factor-out (4j): a factor comes out; 4j's line back

If the old list's last coefficient a_S(n) is not 0, the old sum has degree S(n). The new sum's last coefficient is that same a_S(n), now at the power n: degree n. So peeling lowers the degree by exactly one and keeps the leading coefficient. Done again and again, it writes a polynomial inside out:

2x^3 - 3x^2 + 5x - 7 @@ degree 3, coefficients −7, 5, −3, 2 (9g's order: a₀ first)
= -7 + x\,(5 - 3x + 2x^2) @@ peel-off-the-constant once: degree 2 inside
= -7 + x\,(5 + x\,(-3 + 2x)) @@ peel-off-the-constant again: degree 1 inside
= -7 + x\,(5 + x\,(-3 + x \cdot \htmlClass{keep}{2})) @@ and again: degree 0 inside, the constant 2
\text{at } 2:\ \ 2 \to -3 + 2 \cdot 2 = 1 \to 5 + 2 \cdot 1 = 7 \to -7 + 2 \cdot 7 = \htmlClass{new}{7} @@ evaluated from the inside out: three multiplications and three additions

Evaluating a polynomial from the inside out like this is an old method, written up in a paper of 1819 and much older than that. Here it is the plan of the proof: each line of the peeling is one product and one sum. Its accepted name is Horner’s method, after the author of that paper.

11d3Every polynomial is continuous

Stands on 11d2 and 4b (induction).

Polynomials-are-continuous technique: every polynomial is continuous at every real. The proof is 4b's induction, on the number n that a polynomial's sum runs up to. For a polynomial of degree n that number is its degree. The induction does not need the last coefficient to be nonzero, so it covers every polynomial of degree n, and the zero polynomial too, with no case left over.

\varphi(n):\ \text{for all reals } a_0, \cdots, a_n,\ x \mapsto \textstyle\sum_{k \le n} a_k x^k \text{ is continuous at every real} @@ name the condition first (4b's shape); 2b's φ
\text{base: } \textstyle\sum_{k \le 0} a_k x^k = a_0 x^0 = \htmlClass{new}{a_0} @@ Σ's lines, power-zero: a constant
\text{continuous at every real} @@ lines-are-continuous with step 0. So φ(0)
\text{step: let } n \in \mathbb N;\ \text{assume } \varphi(n);\ \text{let } a_0, \cdots, a_{S(n)} \text{ be reals} @@ the induction hypothesis
p(x) = a_0 + x \cdot \htmlClass{new}{q(x)},\ \ q(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \le n} a_{S(k)}\,x^k @@ peel-off-the-constant, at every real x; equal-functions: p and x ↦ a₀ + x · q(x) are one function
q \text{ continuous at every real} @@ φ(n), used with the reals a₁, ⋯, a_S(n): the hypothesis
x \cdot q(x) \text{ continuous at every real} @@ x by lines-are-continuous; continuous-products
p \text{ continuous at every real} @@ the constant a₀ by lines-are-continuous; continuous-sums. So φ(S(n))
\forall n \in \mathbb N\ \varphi(n) \quad \blacksquare @@ the induction technique (4b)

9g's line says a polynomial is a function p for which there are a number n and reals a₀, ⋯, a_n with p(x) = Σ_{k≤n} a_k x^k at every x. Use a some; φ(n) at those reals; and p is continuous at every real. So every polynomial is continuous, in 11a's sense, on ℝ. Each polynomial of degree S(n) was continuous because one of degree n was: the induction climbs the degrees, one product and one sum per rung.

11d4Another road to the same place

Stands on 11d3 and 10c (substitution).

10c's substitution already said lim_{x→w} p(x) = p(w) for every polynomial p and real w, and read with 11a's line that is this technique. Substitution's road was different: an induction on the number of terms, adding one term a_k x^k at a time. Each term headed to its value by power-limit and constant-factor, and the sum by limits-add. Here the road multiplies by x and adds a constant at each step. Two inductions, two plans of building, one conclusion. When two roads reach the same place, a proof by either is a proof. Seeing both is seeing why the conclusion does not depend on how you write the polynomial.

11d5On every interval, and from every side

Stands on 11d4, 11a5 (continuous-both-ways) and 10a5 (agreeing-near).

Polynomials-are-continuous says more than it seems to. A polynomial is continuous at every real, so by 11a's continuous-both-ways it is continuous from the left and from the right at every real too. So it is continuous on every interval in 11a's sense: at an end that belongs to the interval only the inside side is asked, and that side is given. x³ − 4x is continuous on [0, 2], on [−5, ∞), on (1, 3]: on any interval anyone names. That is the form rung 12 will lean on.

The same is true for every piece of a function given by polynomials on intervals. Inside each piece, 10a's agreeing-near makes the function continuous wherever its piece is, and by polynomials-are-continuous that is everywhere inside. So for a function made of polynomial pieces, the only suspects are where the pieces meet, and 11e decides those. And notice what the proof itself never did: it never chose an answer δ. Every round of the game was won by the techniques of 11b, which won their own rounds once and for all. The induction only says which technique to cite at each degree. If you want a δ for a particular polynomial, input and ε, run the techniques' recipes from the inside of the peeling out, as 11b assembled one for x³.

11d6Rational functions, and chains with polynomials

Stands on 11d5, 11b5 (continuous-quotients) and 11c (chains).

Rational-functions-are-continuous technique: for polynomials p and q with q not the zero polynomial, p/q is continuous at every input; and p/q is continuous. At an input w, q(w) ≠ 0, so continuous-quotients applies to the continuous p and q. Its domain is ℝ without the zeros of q, of which there are at most the degree of q (9g's at-most-n-zeros). Listed in order (three-way-order on ℝ compares any two), they cut ℝ into finitely many intervals, and p/q is continuous on each. ∎

\lim_{x \to 2} (x^3 - 4x + 1) = 8 - 8 + 1 = \htmlClass{keep}{1} @@ polynomials-are-continuous: continuous at 2, so the limit is the value
\lim_{x \to -1} \tfrac{x^2 + 3x}{x - 2} = \tfrac{1 - 3}{-3} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac23} @@ rational-functions-are-continuous: −1 is an input (the bottom is −3 there)
\lim_{x \to 3} \sqrt{x^2 + 7} = \sqrt{16} = \htmlClass{keep}{4} @@ polynomials-are-continuous inside, root-is-continuous outside at 16 > 0: continuous-after-continuous
\sqrt{x^2 - 2x + 2} = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + 1} @@ 8i's completing the square: the inside is at least 1 (square-never-negative)
\text{continuous at every real} @@ polynomials-are-continuous, root-is-continuous, continuous-after-continuous

Where the inside of a root can be 0, the edge form is needed. √(x² − 1) is continuous at every w with |w| > 1 by continuous-after-continuous. At 1 and −1 it is continuous only from the outer side, where x² − 1 ≥ 0 (chain-at-an-edge's round, played on that side). And a fraction is continuous at its inputs only. (x² − 1)/(x − 1) is a rational function with no input 1, and rational-functions-are-continuous says nothing there; 11f mends it.

11d7Where the bricks stop

Stands on 11d6 and 8h (size).

Not every continuous function is built from these bricks. Size-is-not-a-polynomial technique: there is no polynomial p with p(x) = |x| for every real x. Suppose there were, with p(x) = Σ_{k≤n} a_k x^k; padding with zero coefficients, take n ≥ 1. Then p agrees with x ↦ x at the n + 1 reals 1, 2, ⋯, n + 1, since |x| = x there. 9g's same-coefficients gives a₁ = 1 and every other a_k = 0, so p(x) = x for every x. But p(−1) = −1 and |−1| = 1. Clash; refute. ∎ So |x| needs its own proof of continuity: 11c gave one as a chain, and 11e gives the one that matters.

11d9Running it backwards

Stands on 11d1 to 11d7.

Handed a polynomial, peel it to see its plan, or simply cite polynomials-are-continuous. Handed a limit of a polynomial or a rational function at an input, substitute. Handed values, use continuity backwards: a polynomial of degree 2 with limits 4, 1 and 2 at 1, 0 and −1 has those values there, and three values fix its three coefficients. Handed a function that looks like a polynomial, check it is one: the same inputs, and the same values at every one. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The inside-out order is Horner’s rule (or Horner’s method), after William George Horner’s paper of 1819. In 11d.4.4, the textbook of 1748 is Leonhard Euler’s Introductio in analysin infinitorum; q is Dirichlet’s function (1829); the theorem of 1885 is Karl Weierstrass’s approximation theorem.

Accepted names: evaluating a polynomial from the inside out is Horner’s method.
