How Can You Count Distinct Visitors With Almost No Memory?

How can you count distinct visitors with almost no memory? A site sees a billion visits in a day and wants to know how many different visitors made them. Keeping every name to check for repeats takes room for every name. A sketch keeps far less: give each name a stamp, a number between 0 and 1 fixed by the name, and remember only the smallest stamp seen. Repeats cannot change it. The smallest of n scattered stamps sits near 1/(n + 1), so it tells roughly how many names there were, and the average of many such smallest stamps tells it well. The part that makes sketches useful at scale is an arrow fact: the sketch of a joined set is the merge of the sketches. Since 2007 the stamps have been cut to a few bits each, and in 2010 the least room any such count can use was reached.

Stamp h(name) in [0, 1); keep m = the least stamp. For n different names with scattered stamps, P(m > t) = (1 − t)ⁿ and E m = 1/(n + 1). k stamps apart, averaged: width under 1/√k of the mean. s(A ∪ B) = min(s(A), s(B)): s keeps the operation. No function gives s(A ∩ B).

inf80.1A Stamp for Every Name

A stream is a list of names, one for each visit, with repeats. The question is n, the number of different names. Take a fingerprint, as in 22crypt, and read its output as a number h(x) in [0, 1): the stamp of the name x. A name gets the same stamp each time it comes. For the count below, the stamps of different names are treated as n separate draws, each equally likely to land anywhere in [0, 1); a good fingerprint is built to behave so. The sketch is one number, m, the least stamp seen so far.

Repeat-blind technique: the least stamp of a stream is the least stamp of its set of names; repeats and the order of arrival do not change it.

m = \min\,(h(x_1), h(x_2), \dots, h(x_N)) @@ the sketch after N visits
x_i = x_j \Rightarrow h(x_i) = h(x_j) @@ h is a function: one name, one stamp
\min \text{ of a list} = \min \text{ of the set of its members} @@ a least member is least however often, and wherever, it is listed
\htmlClass{new}{m = \min\,\{h(x) : x \in A\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ A the set of different names

A plain counter cannot do this: it adds 1 for a repeat. The sketch forgets exactly what the question does not ask about.

inf80.2The Smallest of n Draws

What does m say about n? Many names crowd [0, 1), and the least stamp is pushed towards 0.

Smallest-draw technique: for n separate draws from [0, 1), each equally likely anywhere, the least one, m, has P(m > t) = (1 − t)ⁿ and average 1/(n + 1).

P(m > t) = P(\text{every draw} > t) = (1 - t)^n @@ independent chances multiply (30l2); each is 1 − t
\text{density of } m:\ n(1 - t)^{n-1} \text{ on } [0, 1] @@ minus the slope of (1 − t)ⁿ (30m)
E\,m = \int_0^1 t \cdot n(1 - t)^{n-1}\,dt = n\int_0^1 (1 - u)\,u^{n-1}\,du @@ substitute u = 1 − t
n\left(\tfrac{1}{n} - \tfrac{1}{n+1}\right) = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{n+1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power technique, run backwards

So 1/m − 1 is a guess at n. It is a poor one alone. The same integral with (1 − u)² gives E m² = 2/((n + 1)(n + 2)), so the spread of m is n/((n + 1)²(n + 2)), a little under 1/(n + 1)²: the width of m is nearly as large as its average.

inf80.3Many Stamps, Averaged

Use k fingerprints h₁, …, h_k, built apart, and keep the least stamp under each: m₁, …, m_k. The sketch is now k numbers, however long the stream. Let M be their average.

Averaged-stamps technique: M has average μ = 1/(n + 1) and width under μ/√k; so for any c > 0, the chance that M misses μ by c·μ/√k or more is at most 1/c².

E\,M = \mu,\quad \operatorname{Var} M = \tfrac{\operatorname{Var} m}{k} @@ the sample-average technique (30q1): k separate copies of m
\operatorname{Var} m = \tfrac{n}{(n+1)^2(n+2)} < \mu^2 @@ the last footing
\text{width of } M < \tfrac{\mu}{\sqrt{k}} @@ root of the spread
\htmlClass{new}{P\!\left(|M - \mu| \ge \tfrac{c\,\mu}{\sqrt{k}}\right) \le \tfrac{1}{c^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spread-bound technique (30h1)

With k = 1024 the width is under μ/32, about 3 parts in 100, and it does not depend on n: a thousand numbers serve for a thousand names or a billion. The guess is 1/M − 1. Halving the error costs four times the room, as with every average.

inf80.4Merging Sketches Is Joining Sets

Write s(A) for the sketch of a set of names A: the row (m₁, …, m_k) of its least stamps, with s(∅) = (1, …, 1). Two servers see sets A and B, and each keeps its own sketch.

Sketch-merge technique: s(A ∪ B) = min(s(A), s(B)), taken place by place, and s(∅) is the do-nothing of min; so s is an arrow from sets under joining to rows under min that keeps the operation.

\min h_i(A \cup B) = \min\,(\min h_i(A),\ \min h_i(B)) @@ the least of a joined set is the lesser of the two leasts
s(A \cup B) = \min(s(A), s(B)) @@ in each of the k places
\min(x, 1) = x \text{ for } x \in [0, 1] @@ s(∅) = (1, …, 1) does nothing
\htmlClass{new}{s : (\text{sets}, \cup, \emptyset) \to ([0,1]^k, \min, \mathbf{1}) \text{ keeps the operation}} \quad \blacksquare @@ join then sketch = sketch then merge

Everything useful about the sketch is in that square closing. Joining does not care about brackets or order, and A ∪ A = A; min is the same, so sketches may be merged in any grouping, in any order, and twice over. The repeat-blind technique was this fact for sets of one name. A day’s sketches from a thousand servers merge into the sketch of the day, and no server sends a name.

No-meet technique: no function gives s(A ∩ B) from s(A) and s(B).

k = 1,\quad h(x) < h(y) @@ two names and one fingerprint are enough
A = \{x, y\},\ B = \{y\}:\quad s(A) = h(x),\ s(B) = h(y),\ s(A \cap B) = h(y) @@ the names in both: y
A' = \{x\},\ B' = \{y\}:\quad s(A') = h(x),\ s(B') = h(y),\ s(A' \cap B') = 1 @@ no name in both
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same sketches, different } s(A \cap B)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a function gives one output for one input

The count of names in both can still be guessed, as |A| + |B| − |A ∪ B| by 42i, from three sketches. The errors of three guesses add, and when few names are shared they swamp the answer.

inf80.5Fewer Bits Still

A stamp kept in full is some tens of bits. Most of them are wasted: what matters about a least stamp is how small it is, and that is told by how many 0s its binary digits begin with. A stamp that begins with j zeros has chance about 2−j of turning up, so a largest run of j zeros points to about 2ʲ names, and j is a number of size log₂ n, written in about log₂ log₂ n bits. The merge is now a largest, not a least; the square closes in the same way.

  • In 1983 (in print 1985) the first counting of this kind was given, keeping which first-1 positions had been seen.
  • In 2007 a sketch of k registers of 5 bits each was analysed: each register keeps the largest first-1 position among the stamps sent to it, one fingerprint serves all registers, and the typical error is about 1.04/√k. With 2048 registers, about 1.5 kilobytes, it counts past a billion to about 2 parts in 100.
  • In 2010 a sketch was given that counts to within a factor 1 ± ε, with chance 2/3, in about 1/ε² + log n bits, and that much had already been shown necessary.

The 1/ε² is the 1/√k of the averaged-stamps technique, turned round: an error of ε needs about 1/ε² registers, whatever is kept in them.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞q.oq.1What is the least room for a sketch that can be merged? A preprint of 2020 gave a sketch using about 1.98 bits for each unit of accuracy, showed that no sketch of one wide family does better, and gave evidence, not proof, that none at all does. Is 1.98 the floor for every mergeable sketch?

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Counting different items by the patterns of their stamps began in 1983, printed in 1985, for the query planners of databases, which need to know how many different values a column holds.

Through the 2000s the registers shrank from whole stamps to a few bits; the sketch analysed in 2007 is the one now built into many databases.

In 2010 the room and the time were both brought down to the least possible, up to a constant factor. A preprint of 2020 asked for the constant itself.

The accepted names

The question is the count-distinct problem or cardinality estimation; the list of visits is a data stream. A stamp is a hash value, and treating different names’ stamps as separate even draws is the random oracle or ideal hash assumption. The least of n even draws is the first order statistic. A summary that can be combined is a mergeable sketch; “keeps the operation” is a homomorphism of monoids, here of semilattices, and A ∪ A = A is idempotence. The 1983 method is probabilistic counting; the 2007 sketch is HyperLogLog; sending each name to one register by its first bits is stochastic averaging. The 2020 sketch is Fishmonger, and its family is the linearizable sketches.

Who said it first

Philippe Flajolet and G. Nigel Martin, “Probabilistic counting algorithms for data base applications” (conference 1983, journal 1985). Philippe Flajolet, Éric Fusy, Olivier Gandouet and Frédéric Meunier, “HyperLogLog: the analysis of a near-optimal cardinality estimation algorithm” (2007). Daniel Kane, Jelani Nelson and David Woodruff, “An optimal algorithm for the distinct elements problem” (2010). Seth Pettie and Dingyu Wang, “Information theoretic limits of cardinality estimation: Fisher meets Shannon” (2020).
