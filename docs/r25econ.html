Why Do Some Countries Grow Richer?

Why do some countries grow richer? Two hundred years ago nearly everyone, nearly everywhere, lived on about what their grandparents had. Since then some countries have grown richer, year after year, until an hour of work buys many times what it once did. Others have stayed close to where they began. A common answer is that rich countries saved and built machines, roads and mills, while poor ones could not. That answer has a puzzle in it. A mill on the Willamette in Oregon makes a worker far more productive, but a second mill beside it adds less, and a tenth adds little. Machines also wear out, and every one must be replaced. What makes the question hard is that saving clearly helps, yet it is not clear that it can help for ever. This page shows, with rung twenty-four’s states that hold and rung twenty-five’s flipped pairs of roots, where an economy built by saving comes to rest, why saving more lifts that resting point but not the growth beyond it, how fast an economy closes on its resting point, and why the road there can rise and fall in waves.

Output comes from workers and machines, and each extra machine per worker adds less than the one before. Saving buys new machines while old ones wear out, and new workers need machines too. So machines per worker climb until what is saved just covers the wear, and there they hold. More saving lifts that resting point once, and then growth stops again. Only better ways of working keep output per worker growing. Spending that answers to last year’s income, and investment that answers to its change, make a rule whose roots can be a flipped pair, and then the road to rest runs in waves.

25econ1Output From Workers and Machines

Let K be the worth of the machines, buildings and roads, L the number of workers, and Y the output in a year. Measured over many countries and decades, the share of income paid to the owners of machines has stayed near one third, and the share paid for work near two thirds. That is a law, with exceptions: the share for machines has drifted up in some countries since about 2000. A rule that keeps those shares is Y = K^α L^(1 − α), with α ≈ ⅓. Here α (little alpha) means the share of the output paid to the owners of machines.

Y = K^{\alpha} L^{1 - \alpha},\ \ 0 \lt \alpha \lt 1 @@ output from machines and workers
(\lambda K)^{\alpha} (\lambda L)^{1 - \alpha} = \lambda\,Y @@ double both, double output
y = \tfrac{Y}{L} = \big(\tfrac{K}{L}\big)^{\alpha} = k^{\alpha} @@ take λ = 1/L: output per worker
(k^{\alpha})' = \alpha k^{\alpha - 1} \gt 0,\ \ (k^{\alpha})'' = \alpha(\alpha - 1)k^{\alpha - 2} \lt 0 @@ rises, more and more slowly
\htmlClass{new}{y = k^{\alpha}:\ \text{each extra machine per worker adds less}} \quad \blacksquare

Per-worker technique: output per worker depends only on machines per worker, and each extra machine per worker adds less than the last. With α = ⅓, eight times the machines per worker gives only twice the output per worker.

25econ2Machines Wear Out

Each year a share s of output is saved and spent on new machines. A share δ of the machines wears out: across whole economies about 5 per cent of their worth a year, while a log truck in Oregon loses more. The workers grow by a share n each year. Machines per worker, k = K/L, grow by the growth of K less the growth of L.

K' = sY - \delta K,\ \ L' = nL @@ saving buys machines; some wear out
\tfrac{k'}{k} = \tfrac{K'}{K} - \tfrac{L'}{L} @@ the log of a ratio is a difference of logs (17c), then slopes
k' = s\,k^{\alpha} - (\delta + n)\,k @@ multiply by k; Y/L = k^α
k^* = \Big(\tfrac{s}{\delta + n}\Big)^{1/(1 - \alpha)} @@ k′ = 0 with k > 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{slope at } k^*:\ \alpha(\delta + n) - (\delta + n) = -(1 - \alpha)(\delta + n) \lt 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ s(k*)^(α−1) = δ + n; so k* holds (24k)

Resting-point technique: machines per worker climb until what is saved just covers the wear and the new workers, and that resting point holds from every start above 0. With s = 0.2, δ = 0.05, n = 0.01 and α = ⅓, k* = (0.2/0.06)^1.5 ≈ 6.09 and y* ≈ 1.83, in the units of the rule.

25econ3Saving Lifts the Level, Not the Growth

Raise s and the resting point moves up. Then compare the two resting points, and ask how fast the economy closes on the new one.

y^* = (k^*)^{\alpha} = \Big(\tfrac{s}{\delta + n}\Big)^{\alpha/(1 - \alpha)} @@ the resting-point technique
s: 0.2 \to 0.3:\ \ \tfrac{y^*_{\text{new}}}{y^*_{\text{old}}} = 1.5^{1/2} \approx 1.22 @@ α/(1 − α) = ½
\text{at } k^*:\ k' = 0,\ \text{so } y' = 0 @@ output per worker stops growing again
\text{near } k^*:\ (k - k^*)' \approx -(1 - \alpha)(\delta + n)\,(k - k^*) @@ the slope at k*, as a straight-line rule
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the gap closes by } 4\% \text{ a year: half of it in } \tfrac{\ln 2}{0.04} \approx 17 \text{ years}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the own-rate technique (24a)

Level technique: saving more lifts output per worker to a higher resting point, once, and growth then fades back to what better ways of working supply. Half again as much saving buys about 22 per cent more output per worker, not faster growth for ever. South Korea saved and invested about a third of its output for decades, grew very fast, and has slowed as it nears the richest countries.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

5e asked of every law its range, and the way it fails past the edge. The level technique gives a law to test: poorer economies, further below their resting points, should grow faster and catch up.

Catching up is a law among economies with like resting points, and fails, in a known direction, beyond them.

Among the states of the United States and the provinces of Canada, over a century, they did: the gap closed by about 2 per cent a year, slower than the 4 the rule gives. Across all the world’s countries they did not: many poor countries grew no faster than rich ones. The edge is where the resting points differ. Economies with different saving, schooling and laws rest at different points, and catch up only to their own.

Oregon’s output per person has stayed below Washington’s for decades. Is that an exception to catching up, or a sign of two different resting points?

25econ4Booms and Busts on the Way

The road to rest is not always smooth. Let households spend a share c of last year’s income, and let firms invest v times the rise in spending, since more spending needs more machines. The gap x_t of this year’s output from its resting value then obeys x_t = c(1 + v)x_(t−1) − cv x_(t−2). Try x_t = z^t.

z^2 - c(1 + v)\,z + cv = 0 @@ divide x_t = z^t through by z^(t−2)
c(1 + v)^2 \lt 4v \Rightarrow z,\ \bar z \text{ a flipped pair} @@ real coefficients, negative under the root (25e)
z\,\bar z = |z|^2 = cv @@ the product of the roots is the last number (25c; 20·cb8)
x_t = C z^t + \bar C\,\bar z^{\,t} = 2|C|\,|z|^t \cos(t\theta + \varphi) @@ the sum of a pair and its flip is real; multiply-and-add (21·cb20)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{waves of } \tfrac{2\pi}{\theta} \text{ years, shrinking when } cv \lt 1,\ \text{growing when } cv \gt 1} \quad \blacksquare

Wave technique: when the roots are a flipped pair, the road to rest runs in waves, one wave each 2π/θ years, and the waves die away exactly when cv < 1. With c = 0.5 and v = 1, z = (1 ± i)/2: size √0.5 ≈ 0.71 and turn π/4, so a wave every 8 years, each year 0.71 times the last. From x₀ = 0 and x₁ = 1 the gaps run 1, 1, 0.5, 0, −0.25, −0.25, −0.125, 0. Here θ (little theta) means the turn of the flipped pair.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Y = K^α L^(1 − α) is the Cobb–Douglas production function, and α capital’s share of income. Diminishing returns to capital is the per-worker technique. The rule for k is the Solow–Swan growth model, k* its steady state, and the level technique the claim that saving has a level effect, not a growth effect. Better ways of working are total factor productivity, or technical progress. Catching up is convergence: among like economies, conditional convergence, and across all countries, absolute convergence, which fails. Its pace of about 2 per cent a year is the iron law of convergence. The saving rate of 25·econ.6.1 is the golden rule. The waves are the multiplier–accelerator model, and v the accelerator.

Who said it first

Charles Cobb and Paul Douglas fitted the production rule to American factories in 1928. Robert Solow and Trevor Swan gave the growth model separately in 1956. Robert Barro and Xavier Sala-i-Martin measured the 2 per cent pace across American states in the early 1990s. Paul Samuelson gave the waves in 1939, building on Alvin Hansen. In 25·econ.6.5, Paul Krugman’s essay The Myth of Asia’s Miracle (1994), after work by Alwyn Young, compared the East Asian growth to the Soviet Union’s; others, among them the World Bank’s East Asian Miracle report (1993), credited learning and policy.
