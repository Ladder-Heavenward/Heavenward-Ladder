27a · proof

What adds and stretches like arrows

Rung 18 added arrows tip to tail and stretched them by numbers. Lists of numbers add place by place. Polynomials add term by term. Two solutions of the swinging rule of rung 24 add to a third. These look like four different things. This step writes down the few rules they all obey, and from then on proves things once for all four.

A space of arrows is any set whose members add and stretch by eight rules. Arrows of the plane, lists of numbers, polynomials and the solutions of a rate rule are all spaces of arrows, so one proof serves them all.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 18a (displacements add and stretch), 5c (the twelve techniques of arithmetic) and 24h (the two model equations). 27b counts the directions a space has.

27a1The rules of arrows

Stands on 18a and 5c.

A space of arrows is a set V with a way to add two members, u + v, and a way to stretch a member by a real number, t·v, so that eight rules hold for all u, v, w in V and all real s, t. Adding is turn-about and grouping-free; there is a zero arrow 0 with v + 0 = v; each v has an opposite −v with v + (−v) = 0. Stretching obeys 1·v = v and s·(t·v) = (st)·v, and it spreads over both sums: t·(u + v) = t·u + t·v and (s + t)·v = s·v + t·v. Accepted name: a vector space, whose members are vectors.

These eight rules are a definition, not axioms of the ladder: a set either obeys them or it does not. Everything proved from them holds in every set that obeys them.

Zero-stretch technique: in every space of arrows, 0·v = 0, t·0 = 0, and (−1)·v = −v.

0\cdot v = (0+0)\cdot v = 0\cdot v + 0\cdot v @@ 0 + 0 = 0, and stretching spreads over a sum of numbers
0\cdot v = 0 @@ add −(0·v) to both sides, then group
t\cdot 0 = t\cdot(0 + 0) = t\cdot 0 + t\cdot 0 \Rightarrow t\cdot 0 = 0 @@ the same, with stretching spread over a sum of arrows
v + (-1)\cdot v = 1\cdot v + (-1)\cdot v = (1 + (-1))\cdot v = 0\cdot v = 0 @@ 1·v = v, then spread, then the first line
\htmlClass{new}{(-1)\cdot v = -v} \quad \blacksquare @@ an arrow has one opposite: the cancel technique of 19b, read for adding

27a2Four spaces, one kind

Stands on 27a1, 18a, 9g and 24h.

Inside-space technique: if a part W of a space of arrows holds 0, and holds u + v and t·v whenever it holds u and v, then W is itself a space of arrows.

u, v \in W \Rightarrow u + v \in W,\ t\cdot v \in W @@ given: adding and stretching stay inside W
v \in W \Rightarrow -v = (-1)\cdot v \in W @@ the zero-stretch technique (27a1)
\text{the eight rules hold in } W @@ each is a claim about members of V, and W’s members are members of V
\htmlClass{new}{W \text{ is a space of arrows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three lines above

So four sets become one kind. The displacements of the plane, and of space, add and stretch by 18a. Lists of n numbers add and stretch place by place, and each rule holds because it holds in every place, by the twelve techniques. The polynomials of degree at most n hold 0 and are kept by adding and stretching. The functions with y″ = −y hold 0, and if y and z obey it, so do y + z and t·y, because slopes add (13d) and stretch (13c). All four are spaces of arrows.

27a.4.5seamThe eight rules add arrows and stretch them. They never multiply two arrows. In 1843 a mathematician found a way to multiply the arrows of space in which ab and ba differ. Through the 1890s his follower held that this product was the true algebra of space. Two physicists, one at Yale and one in England, cut the product in two: a number from two arrows, and an arrow standing upright to both. The follower called their system a hermaphrodite monster. Half a century before, a schoolteacher had written down spaces of any number of directions, with products of his own, and almost nobody read him.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A space of arrows is a vector space over the real numbers, and its arrows are vectors; the numbers that stretch are scalars. A space inside a space is a subspace. Over ℂ the same eight rules, with complex stretches, give a complex vector space.

Who said it first

Hermann Grassmann described spaces of this kind in his Ausdehnungslehre of 1844, which few read. Giuseppe Peano wrote the rules as a list in 1888. They became standard in the twentieth century, through Stefan Banach, Hermann Weyl and others.

In 27a.4.5, the 1843 product is William Rowan Hamilton’s quaternions, and his follower is Peter Guthrie Tait. The two physicists are Josiah Willard Gibbs at Yale, whose notes on vector analysis were printed from 1881, and Oliver Heaviside. Tait’s phrase “a sort of hermaphrodite monster” is from the preface to his treatise on quaternions of 1890. The schoolteacher is Grassmann.
