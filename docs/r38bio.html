Why Don’t We Dissolve?

Why don’t we dissolve? Sarah stirs sugar into tea and it is gone. She drops a cube of jelly into the same cup and that goes too. Then she gets into a bath nearly as hot and stays whole for an hour. She is three parts water in four already, and most of the rest is made of the same kinds of atoms as the sugar and the jelly. This page finds what holds.

To dissolve, a thing must come apart into separate molecules with water between them. A body does not, for three reasons: its structural molecules are long chains, the chains are tied to each other by full bonds, and the gaps are sealed with fat that water will not enter.

38bio1Four Families

Dry a cell and sort what is left. About half is protein and about a quarter is sugar in one form or another. The rest is mostly the nucleic acids of 4·bio and fats.

Three of the four are chains. A protein is amino acids joined end to end by 9chem26’s stiff amide link. A nucleic acid is 43bio’s sugar and phosphate backbone. A sugar chain is rings joined through an oxygen. Each is made by one move repeated: two parts join and a water molecule leaves. Each is unmade by putting the water back.

Fats are the odd family. They are small molecules, a few dozen atoms, nearly all C-C and C-H with no group for water to grasp. They are not chains of repeated parts. They matter in bulk because they gather: 19·bio’s membrane is fats standing side by side with nothing joining them.

Family technique: sort a molecule of life first as chain or not chain. A chain is one repeated link, made by losing water at each join. Fats are small and act by gathering.

So hot water can undo anything held only by weak bonds, one at a time, and nothing held by full ones. Whether a thing dissolves in a bath comes down to which kind holds its pieces to each other.

Two-strengths technique: ask of any structure whether its pieces are held to each other by full bonds or only by weak ones. Warm water undoes the weak ones and leaves the full ones.

38bio3A Chain Coils

A protein chain has, at every link, an N-H that can share its hydrogen and a C=O that can take one. The chain turns so that each C=O takes the hydrogen of the N-H four links on. The result is a spiral.

5.4 Å of rise for each turn, so 1.5 Å a link.

\htmlClass{new}{\text{400 }\times \text{ 1.5 = 600 }\text{Å}} @@ a chain of 400 links, coiled

Stretched side by side instead, chains share hydrogens across from one to the next and make a sheet. Spiral and sheet are held by weak bonds only, and only along the backbone. The side groups stick out and are free for the next step.

38bio4A Protein That Dissolves

Egg white is protein in water, clear and runny. Each of its chains is folded into a ball. The side groups that are all carbon and hydrogen have gone to the middle, away from water, as oil does in a salad dressing. The side groups with charges are on the outside, where water grasps them. Each ball is one molecule with a wet surface, and so it dissolves.

The fold is held by weak bonds. Mike drops the egg in a hot pan. The balls come undone, the oily middles are bared, and they stick to each other in a tangle that water cannot get between. The white sets. Nothing was added and no full bond was broken.

Fold technique: a protein dissolves when its fold puts the charged side groups outside and the oily ones inside. Heat that undoes the fold bares the oily ones, and the chains clump.

38bio5The Rope Outside the Cells

The commonest protein in a body is not folded into a ball at all. It is a quarter of all our protein, and it lies outside the cells, in skin, tendon, bone and gristle.

Three chains, each a spiral of its own kind with no hydrogen shared inside the one chain.

The three wound round each other into a rod 3000 Å long and 15 Å thick.

Every third link is glycine, the amino acid with no side group, since only that fits in the middle of the rope.

The hydrogens are shared between the three chains.

Cells make the rods and push them out, and outside they are laid side by side and tied to each other into fibres. Weight for weight a fibre is stronger than steel.

The rope needs an extra OH on many of its links, put there by an enzyme that uses iron and vitamin C (41·bio). Without it the rope unwinds at 24 °C. With it, at 39 °C. That is two degrees above the body. A sailor with no vitamin C makes rope that will not hold at body heat, and his gums and old scars open.

Thirty-nine degrees is also the answer to Sarah’s jelly. Boil skin and bone for hours and the ropes unwind into separate chains. Cool it and they half-rewind and set. That is jelly, and a cube of it dissolves in tea because its chains are no longer tied.

38bio6The Sulfur Bridge

A rope that unwinds at 39 °C does not explain the bath. The outer skin does, and it is made differently.

One amino acid, cysteine, ends in S-H (43bio2). Two of them side by side, with oxygen about, lose their hydrogens and join S to S. That is a full bond, and it can tie one chain to another.

The cells of the outer skin fill themselves with a protein of long paired spirals, rich in cysteine. Then the cells die, flatten and dry, and the bridges form between spirals throughout. Hair and nail are the same protein with more bridges. A layer of skin, a hair, a fingernail: each is chains tied to chains by full bonds from end to end.

Joy sells the proof in a box. A hair-waving lotion holds a compound that gives hydrogens back to the sulfurs and opens the bridges. The hair, now only weakly held, is wound on a curler. A second lotion takes the hydrogens off again, the bridges re-form where the chains now lie, and the curl stays through any amount of washing.

Bridge technique: two S-H groups join into one S-S bond when hydrogen is taken away and part when it is given back. Chains tied by such bridges cannot be separated by water or by heat short of burning.

38bio7Tied Chains Are One Molecule

38·chem2 tied rubber’s chains together at a few points, with sulfur as it happens, and showed that the whole sheet was then one molecule: it stretches and springs back and no solvent can carry it off.

The same counting applies to skin.

To dissolve is to come apart into single molecules with water between.

Chains tied by full bonds at even a few points each are a single molecule.

A single molecule cannot come apart into single molecules.

It can swell. Water gets between the chains as far as the ties allow, and fingertips wrinkle in the bath. The ties hold.

Network technique: a set of chains tied to each other by full bonds, each to at least two others, is one molecule. It can swell in a liquid and cannot dissolve in it.

38bio8Chains of Sugar

Glucose dissolves at once: C₆H₁₂O₆, with five OH groups for water to hold. Join glucose rings into a chain and the result depends on which way the joining oxygen points.

Joined one way, the chain coils and branches: the store of sugar in a liver, or in a potato. Enzymes take it apart in minutes.

Joined the other way, the chain runs straight, straight chains lie side by side sharing hydrogens along their whole length, and the bundle is a fibre: wood and cotton. We have no enzyme that fits that join, and cannot digest it.

One sugar, one bond, two directions: food or timber. An insect’s shell is the straight kind with a nitrogen group on each ring.

Between our own cells, among the ropes of 38bio5, lies a mesh of sugar chains whose rings carry acid groups, and many carry sulfur with oxygens round it. Every ring is charged. The charges hold water so strongly that the mesh is a stiff gel, and it is what lets gristle in a knee be squeezed and spring back.

38bio9The Seal

One gap is left. Dead, bridged cells stacked like tiles still have joints between them, and water could pass down the joints.

The joints are filled with fat. A fat molecule has a long tail of CH₂ and nothing on it for water to grasp. Fats with one end that does take water line up in sheets two molecules thick with the tails inside (19·bio), and in the outer skin many such sheets are stacked in every joint. Water does not cross a layer it cannot enter. A duck’s feathers and a leaf’s upper face are sealed the same way with wax.

Soap shows what the seal is worth. It carries fat off into water. Hands washed all day lose their seal, and the skin beneath dries and cracks though its bridges are whole.

So the bath finds three things in its way: chains, where sugar is single molecules; full bonds between the chains, where jelly has none; and fat in the joints.

Accepted names

The chain families are polymers; the fats are lipids. Joining with loss of water is condensation, and undoing it hydrolysis. The spiral is the alpha helix, the sheet the beta sheet; together secondary structure. A ball-folded protein is globular; unfolding is denaturation. The oily groups hiding from water is the hydrophobic effect. The rope is collagen, its triple winding the triple helix, the extra OH hydroxyproline, the sailor’s disease scurvy, and boiled collagen gelatin. What lies outside cells is the extracellular matrix. The sulfur bridge is a disulfide bond, joining two cysteines into a cystine; taking hydrogen away is oxidation, giving it back reduction. The outer skin’s protein is keratin, its paired spirals a coiled coil, the dead outer layer the stratum corneum. Tying chains is cross-linking; rubber’s is vulcanisation. The two joins of glucose are alpha and beta glycosidic bonds; the stores are glycogen and starch, the fibre cellulose, the insect’s chitin. The charged sugar chains are glycosaminoglycans, the sulfur groups on them sulfate esters, as in chondroitin sulfate. The skin’s fats are chiefly ceramides.
