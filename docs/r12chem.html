How Much Is Solid in Half-Frozen Metal?

How much is solid in half-frozen metal? Pure tin melts at one temperature, 232 °C, as 11·chem found for ice: the temperature stalls while it changes phase. Solder, a mix of tin and lead, does not. It softens over a range of temperatures, part solid and part liquid, like slush. A metallurgist casting a part needs to know how much is solid at each temperature on the way down. This page reads a mix’s cooling, draws the line that joins the solid and the liquid at one temperature, finds the share of each with 12·phys’s lever balance, and uses 12a to show the mix must pass through every share of solid as it cools.

At a temperature inside the range, the mix is part solid of one make-up and part liquid of another, both read off a chart. Draw a line between them through the mix’s overall make-up. The shares of solid and liquid balance on that line like weights on a lever: each share is the length of the far arm over the whole line.

12chem1A Cooling Curve That Stalls

Melt pure tin and let it cool, reading a thermometer every few seconds. The temperature falls steadily, then stops at 232 °C while the tin freezes, then falls again: the stall 11·chem found for water, where the heat given off goes into the change of phase. Pure lead stalls at 327 °C.

From here on, on this page, an alloy’s make-up is its share of tin by weight. Pure lead is 0% and pure tin is 100%.

metalmake-upfreezes
lead0% tinat 327 °C
tin100% tinat 232 °C
solder40% tinfrom about 240 °C down to 183 °C

12chem2Two Metals Mixed Melt Over a Range

Cool solder with 40% tin. Around 240 °C, small grains of solid start to form, but the temperature keeps falling, slowly, instead of stalling. The first grains are mostly lead, so the liquid left behind gets richer in tin, which lowers the temperature at which it can freeze. Freezing and cooling go on together until, at 183 °C, the last liquid freezes at once.

Metallurgists draw this on a chart with make-up across and temperature up. Above one curve everything is liquid; below another everything is solid; between the curves the alloy is part solid and part liquid. At 183 °C, a mix of 61.9% tin freezes all at once, with no range, like a pure metal.

12chem3The Tie Line

At one temperature between the curves, the solid grains all have one make-up and the liquid has another, both read off the chart where a level line at that temperature meets the two curves. That level line is the tie line: it ties the solid to the liquid it is in balance with.

\text{solid grains: about } 18\% \text{ tin} @@ the left end of the tie line
\text{liquid: about } 56\% \text{ tin} @@ the right end
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the alloy, } 40\% \text{ tin, lies between them}} @@ its overall make-up sits on the line

The overall make-up is fixed, 40%: nothing is added or taken away. Only the split between solid and liquid changes as the temperature falls.

12chem4The Lever on the Tie Line

Take 100 g of solder. Let L grams be liquid and 100 − L solid. Count the tin: the tin in the liquid plus the tin in the solid is the tin in the whole.

0.56\,L + 0.18\,(100 - L) = 0.40 \cdot 100 @@ tin in liquid + tin in solid = all the tin
(0.56 - 0.18)\,L = (0.40 - 0.18) \cdot 100 @@ gather L (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{L}{100} = \dfrac{40 - 18}{56 - 18} = \dfrac{22}{38} \approx 0.58} @@ liquid share: the far arm over the whole

Tie-line lever technique: on the tie line, the share of liquid is the distance from the solid’s end to the alloy over the whole length, and the share of solid is the distance from the alloy to the liquid’s end over the whole length. It is 12·phys’s lever: hang the solid at one end and the liquid at the other, and they balance at the alloy, each share in the reverse ratio of its arm (5j).

12chem5Must It Pass Through Half Solid?

Let s(T) be the share of solid at temperature T. At 240 °C, where freezing starts, s = 0; at 183 °C, where it ends, the alloy is all solid, s = 1. In between, s changes with T without jumping: the tie line’s ends slide smoothly along the curves, so the lever’s arms change smoothly too.

s(240) = 0,\ \ s(183) = 1 @@ start and end of freezing
s \text{ continuous on } [183, 240] @@ the tie line’s ends slide without jumps
0 < \tfrac{1}{2} < 1 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{some } T \text{ in } (183, 240) \text{ has } s(T) = \tfrac{1}{2}} @@ the intermediate value technique (12a)

As the alloy cools through its range, the share of solid passes through every value from 0 to 1, so there is a temperature where it is exactly half solid, and one for any share a caster needs. A plumber wiping a joint works the solder in its slushy range for this reason; a foundry engineer pours a casting knowing at which temperature it will be stiff enough to keep its shape.

Accepted names

The chart is a phase diagram; the curve above which all is liquid is the liquidus, and the one below which all is solid the solidus. The tie line’s share rule is the lever rule, and the mix that freezes at once, 61.9% tin at 183 °C, is the eutectic. The lead-rich grains are the α phase.

Who said it first

Josiah Willard Gibbs set out the conditions for phases in balance in the 1870s. The lead and tin chart is a standard teaching example, with its eutectic at 61.9% tin and 183 °C; the tie line ends at 200 °C on this page are read from the chart and rounded.
