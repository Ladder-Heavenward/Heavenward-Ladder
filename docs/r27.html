Finding your arrows

Rung 13 drew arrows in the plane: you could add two of them tip to tail, and stretch one by a number. Rung 19 found that moves which undo each other form groups. This rung asks what else adds and stretches the same way, and finds that lists of numbers, polynomials and the answers to a rate rule all do. Anything that keeps those rules is a space of arrows. Each such space has a size that no choice can change, its dimension. Then the rung turns to the maps that keep adding and stretching. Every one of them is a grid of numbers, and doing one map after another is multiplying grids. A single number, the determinant, says whether a grid can be undone. Some arrows a grid only stretches, and those arrows make it simple to understand.

You have been adding arrows since rung 13. This rung asks what an arrow is, and finds arrows in places you never thought to look. The answer is short. Getting there is not, and that is the point.

Anything that adds and stretches by the rules of arrows is a space of arrows, and each such space has one size, its dimension. The maps that keep adding and stretching are grids of numbers.

What it means to say it

To say arrows add and stretch is to say there are two ways to make a new arrow: put two together, or scale one by a number. A space of arrows is any collection where those two ways never lead outside it, and where they obey the same rules as arrows in the plane. To say a space has dimension is to say it takes exactly so many arrows to build every other one, with none to spare, however you choose them.

Every space of arrows that some finite list builds has a dimension: every basis of it has the same number of arrows. The rung proves it by trading arrows one at a time, then follows the maps between spaces down to a single number and a polynomial.

Where it comes from

A German schoolteacher described spaces of any number of directions in 1844, and few readers followed him. An Italian mathematician wrote down the rules of a space of arrows in 1888. A German philosopher used determinants in 1693, and a French mathematician gave the expansion along a row in 1772. An English mathematician named the matrix in 1850, and another made matrices things to multiply in 1858, where he stated that a matrix obeys its own polynomial. A German mathematician proved that in full, with the least polynomial a matrix obeys, in 1878. Another German proved the trading of arrows in 1913, and a third gave the word eigen to arrows a map only stretches, around 1904.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, a few rules about adding and stretching are enough (27a1). Lists of numbers, polynomials, and the answers to y″ = −y all keep them, so one set of techniques serves all of them.

Second, a list that builds a space can always be cut down to one with nothing to spare, a basis (27b1), and two bases of one space always have the same number of arrows (27b3). That number is the dimension.

Third, a map that keeps adding and stretching loses some arrows and reaches others. What it loses and what it reaches are spaces too, and their dimensions add up to the dimension it started from (27c2).

Fourth, every such map between lists of numbers is a grid (27d1), and doing one map after another is multiplying grids (27d2). Three rules fix one number for every square grid, the determinant (27e2). It multiplies when grids multiply (27e3), and it is zero exactly when the grid cannot be undone (27e4).

Fifth, an arrow a grid only stretches is found where the stretch polynomial is zero (27f1). Arrows with different stretches are free (27f2), and when there are enough of them the grid is a pure stretch in disguise (27f3). Fed to its own stretch polynomial, every grid gives the zero grid (27g3).

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, the annexes carry green boxes. 27·cb24 carries the border line: a rest is read from its grid of slopes only when no stretch sits on the line where growth turns to decay, and on that line the page says the grid cannot tell. 27·then carries lumping the world into boxes: a box model says which boxes were lumped together, and how far the answer moves when one is split. 27·econ carries a table measured with error: the table’s own error is carried through to the outputs, and stated with them. 27·nav carries the cloud, from the sky: a fix is a fence, and how the satellites stand stretches its width. 27·now carries the cloud, in rounds: the rounds stop when the fence is narrower than the gaps you mean to read, and the fence is reported.

What follows

With those five, the techniques compute. Arrows in space get lengths, angles and a product that stands upright to both. A system of samenesses is solved by clearing one unknown at a time, at a cost of about n³ steps. Determinants are found by hand, powers of a grid come from its stretched arrows, and every turn of space is found to have an axis. The annexes take grids into rate rules that steer each other, a spinning body, an economy that feeds itself, a phone that finds itself, balanced reactions, a cipher, and the ranking of the web.

What you can do because you know it

You can treat anything that adds and stretches as arrows, and count its directions. You can write a straight map as a grid, compose maps by multiplying, and tell from one number whether a map can be undone. You can find the arrows a grid only stretches, and use them to take high powers of the grid, or to see where repeated steps lead.

From here on, every rung that has more than one unknown at a time leans on this one.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 13’s arrows in the plane, rung 19’s groups, rung 24’s rate rules, rung 25’s roots of polynomials and rung 9’s functions and their undoing. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say arrows add and stretch, and have dimension, when it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
