10·bio · biology

How Fast Can an Enzyme Go?

How fast can an enzyme go? Every living cell runs on enzymes. Give an enzyme more of the substance it works on and it works faster, but not without end. The rate climbs fast at first, then levels off toward a ceiling it never quite reaches. In 1913 two researchers in Berlin wrote down the function. Its ceiling is a limit far out in exactly 10f's sense, with a half-way point that biochemists measure in laboratories every day. This page builds the function from a picture of molecules. It proves the ceiling is a limit and is never reached, finds the half-way point, and works it with real enzymes and real numbers.

The rate is the ceiling times the share of enzyme that is busy. That share is the amount of substance over the amount plus a fixed constant of the enzyme: it heads to 1 as the substance grows, never gets there, and is exactly one half when the amount equals the constant.

The class · the words, the experiment, the model, the limit, the numbers

Enzymes, substrates, rates

An enzyme is a large molecule, made by a living cell, that speeds up one particular chemical change (5·chem) without being used up itself. The substance it acts on is its substrate; what it turns the substrate into is the product. The enzymes of digestion, of breathing, of copying DNA are all of this kind, and each is fussy: one enzyme, one change, sometimes one substrate. Two more words, and a page-local line.

The concentration of a dissolved substance is how much of it there is in each litre of solution, counted in 5·chem's moles. That is moles per litre, or, for the small amounts in cells, millimoles per litre, mmol/L (5j's milli, a thousandth). mmol/L is often written mM; this page does not, because 8·phys's M is mega. The rate of a reaction is how fast substrate becomes product: how much, per litre, per unit of time, in mmol/L per minute, say.

From here on, on this page, s names the concentration of the substrate and v the rate at which an enzyme turns substrate into product, both quantities in 5j's sense; v is read as a function of s. Neither is a letter used for anything else here: 4d's function s does not appear on this page.

Invertase, and a sugar that turns light

Table sugar, sucrose, is two smaller sugars joined: glucose and fructose. The enzyme invertase, from yeast, splits it. Chemists could follow that change without stopping it, because of a property of the sugars themselves. Pass light through a special filter, so that it shakes in one direction only. A sucrose solution turns that direction one way. The half-and-half mixture of glucose and fructose that sucrose becomes turns it the other way. An instrument called a polarimeter reads the turn. As the reaction runs, the reading swings across. The product is called invert sugar for that reason, and the enzyme invertase.

In Berlin in 1912 and 1913, a German physician turned chemist worked with a Canadian physician who had crossed the Atlantic to work with him. They mixed a fixed amount of invertase with sucrose at five strengths: 333, 166.7, 83.3, 41.6 and 20.8 mmol/L, each half the one before. They kept the acidity fixed, and read the polarimeter as the minutes passed. From each run they took the starting rate, before the products could pile up and interfere. At the low strengths, doubling the sucrose nearly doubled the rate; at the high strengths, doubling it barely mattered. The rate was heading for a ceiling.

The pair argued that the rate of the splitting is at every moment proportional to the concentration of a compound of enzyme and sugar. That compound, they said, stands in a fixed balance with the free enzyme and the free sugar.

The model: an enzyme must hold its substrate

Their picture: an enzyme molecule grabs a substrate molecule, forming a pair. Then the pair either lets go, or the enzyme does its work and releases the product, ready to grab again. Every enzyme molecule is either free or busy (holding a substrate). The two workers assumed the grabbing and letting go are fast and balanced, so that a certain ratio stays fixed. Write E_free and E_busy for the concentrations of free and busy enzyme and E_total for all of it (4·comp's low marks with words). The two facts are E_total = E_free + E_busy and K · E_busy = E_free · s, for a fixed positive K. Chemists call K a dissociation constant: small K means the pair rarely comes apart. The rest is 5g's solving for one letter.

K\,E_{\text{busy}} = E_{\text{free}}\,s @@ the balance
K\,E_{\text{busy}} = (\htmlClass{new}{E_{\text{total}} - E_{\text{busy}}})\,s @@ substitute for the expression: free is total less busy
K\,E_{\text{busy}} + \htmlClass{new}{E_{\text{busy}}\,s} = E_{\text{total}}\,s @@ spread (spread on ℚ), then add E_busy · s to both sides (adding-keeps-answers)
E_{\text{busy}}\,\htmlClass{new}{(K + s)} = E_{\text{total}}\,s @@ take out the common factor (spread on ℚ backwards)
E_{\text{busy}} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{E_{\text{total}}\,s}{K + s}} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by (K + s)⁻¹: K + s is positive, since K > 0 and s ≥ 0

Each busy enzyme turns substrate into product at a fixed rate, its turnover number k_cat. (The low mark “cat” is for catalysis, the chemists' word for speeding a change.) The turnover number is how many substrate molecules one busy enzyme converts each second. So v = k_cat · E_busy.

From here on, on this page, V_max names k_cat · E_total, the rate if every enzyme were busy at once: the ceiling. And K_m names the constant K. Both are fixed positive quantities for a given enzyme, substrate and amount of enzyme. So v(s) = V_max · s/(K_m + s) for every s ≥ 0, and its graph is a graph in 9e's sense. The accepted name for K_m is the Michaelis constant, after one of the two workers who first wrote down this function.

In 1925 two other workers reached the same function without assuming the grabbing is balanced, only that the amount of busy enzyme stays steady. In their version K_m is a mix of three rates rather than a pure measure of grip. The function is the same either way, so the mathematics below does not care which story you prefer.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change: the small thing not used up

6·chem met a principle of the second kind of care: things pass through a middle when they change, and some changes have two sides. Here is another part of the same principle. A change has a hill to climb on its way through the middle, and something small that is not used up can lower the hill.

Look for the small, unconsumed thing that changed the rate; it is usually where the real explanation is.

The enzyme is that small thing. It grabs, works, lets go, and is ready to grab again. It ends the change as it began it. Yet with it the sugar splits in minutes, and without it the sugar sits. Leave it out of the story and the rates make no sense.

Sucrose sits in a sugar bowl for years without splitting. A pinch of yeast in sugar water splits it within the hour. What did the yeast bring that the bowl never had?

The ceiling is a limit

Top-speed technique: lim_{s→∞} v(s) = V_max. 10f's rational-functions-far-out does it at once: top and bottom both have degree 1 in s, with leading coefficients V_max and 1. Here it is by hand, one move per line, and then by the game, so that you see the threshold.

\tfrac{V_{\max} K_m}{K_m + s} \lt \varepsilon \;\Leftrightarrow\; V_{\max} K_m \lt \varepsilon\,(K_m + s) @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by the positive K_m + s (and back)

And it never gets there. Never-reached technique: v(s) < V_max for every s ≥ 0, by the first line above: V_max − v(s) = V_max K_m/(K_m + s), a positive over a positive. The enzyme is like the game's L: approached as nearly as you like, reached never. Put the challenger's gap as a share of the ceiling, ε = c · V_max with 0 < c < 1: then N = K_m (1 − c)/c. Within 10% of the ceiling needs s > 9 K_m; within 5%, s > 19 K_m; within 1%, s > 99 K_m. Each further step costs about ten times as much substrate: the last few percent are ruinously expensive. The strongest solution of 1913, 333 mmol/L with K_m = 16.7 mmol/L, reached about 95% of their ceiling. Nobody measured the ceiling. It was inferred from the graph, as its limit.

Half the ceiling, at K_m

Half-way technique: for s ≥ 0, v(s) = ½ V_max exactly when s = K_m. If s = K_m, v = V_max K_m/(2K_m) = ½ V_max (cancel K_m, not 0). Back: ½ V_max = V_max s/(K_m + s) gives K_m + s = 2s (multiplying-keeps-answers by 2(K_m + s)/V_max), so s = K_m (adding-keeps-answers). ∎ So K_m is read directly off the graph, as the concentration of half-saturation. Run it backwards for any share c of the ceiling: c = s/(K_m + s) gives s = K_m · c/(1 − c), for 0 ≤ c < 1. At c = ½ that is K_m; at c = 0.9 it is 9 K_m. At c = 1 the line would divide by 0: that is never-reached, the limit refusing to be reached, seen from the other side.

Measure the substrate in units of K_m, t = s/K_m, and the rate in units of V_max. Then v/V_max = t/(1 + t), the same function for every enzyme in the world. K_m is the enzyme's own unit of concentration: an enzyme with a small K_m is half busy at a trace of its substrate; one with a large K_m needs a flood.

At the low end, nearly proportional

Low-end technique: lim_{s→0⁺} v(s)/s = V_max/K_m. For s > 0, v(s)/s = V_max/(K_m + s) (cancel s, agreeing-near side by side). That heads to V_max/K_m by substitution and limits-divide, the bottom heading to K_m ≠ 0. ∎ So when the substrate is scarce, the rate is nearly proportional to it, with 5j's constant of proportion V_max/K_m. Divided by the amount of enzyme, that constant is k_cat/K_m, which biochemists call the specificity constant: how efficient an enzyme is when its substrate is scarce.

Real enzymes, real numbers

The constants K_m below are standard measured values. Invertase with sucrose has the 1913 value, 16.7 mmol/L. The fitted ceiling of 1913, about 0.76 mmol/L per minute, was confirmed by a modern refit of the data. Hexokinase is the enzyme that starts the burning of glucose in the brain. Its textbook pair is 0.05 mmol/L for glucose and 1.5 mmol/L for fructose. Each share of the ceiling is one substitution, s/(K_m + s).

With V_max ≈ 0.76 mmol/L per minute, the invertase rates were about 0.42, 0.54, 0.63, 0.69 and 0.72 mmol/L per minute. Doubling 20.8 to 41.6 raised the rate by a factor of about 1.29; doubling 166.7 to 333, by about 1.05. Hexokinase meets glucose at many times its K_m. Blood carries about 5 mmol/L, a hundred times K_m. Even in brain tissue, where the level is lower, roughly 1 to 2 mmol/L, it is twenty times K_m or more. So it runs at 95 to 99% of its ceiling. Doubling blood glucose from 5 to 10 mmol/L raises its rate by half a percent ((10/10.05)/(5/5.05) ≈ 1.005). A saturated enzyme ignores the supply; its rate is set by how much enzyme there is. The same enzyme offered fructose at 5 mmol/L is at 77%, because its K_m for fructose is thirty times larger.

Reciprocals make a linear function

A graph levelling off toward an unreachable ceiling is hard to read a ceiling off. In 1934 two chemists took reciprocals of both sides. Flip, then split the top, then read the result as 9e's linear function in the new input u = 1/s.

\tfrac1v = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{K_m + s}{V_{\max}\,s}} @@ reciprocals of both sides (5c's reciprocal; both sides positive for s > 0)
= \tfrac{K_m}{V_{\max}\,s} + \tfrac{s}{V_{\max}\,s} @@ split over the two-term top (fractions-of-rationals iii backwards)
= \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{K_m}{V_{\max}}}\cdot\tfrac1s + \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{1}{V_{\max}}} @@ cancel s in the second piece. With u = 1/s: the linear function, step K_m/V_max, start 1/V_max
\lim_{s \to \infty} \tfrac{1}{v(s)} = \lim_{u \to 0^+}\big(\tfrac{K_m}{V_{\max}}\,u + \tfrac{1}{V_{\max}}\big) = \tfrac{1}{V_{\max}} @@ swap-to-one-over-x (10f), then substitution from the right. The ceiling's reciprocal is the start

So the limit far out, which no experiment reaches, has become an ordinary start of a linear function, read where u = 1/s is 0. And the linear function is 0 at u = −1/K_m. Two measured pairs fix it, by 9e's two-points-one-line. (Modern laboratories fit the function itself to the data by computer. Taking reciprocals swells the errors of the slowest, least precise measurements. But the start of the linear function is still the clearest picture of what V_max is.) Note the legal move and the illegal one nearby. Flipping a single fraction is legal (5c). Flipping a sum term by term is not: 1/(K_m + s) is not 1/K_m + 1/s (try K_m = s = 1: one half against two). The column flips first, while the right side is one fraction, and only then splits the top.

Where the model stops

The function is a model, and knowing where it fails is part of using it. The 1913 workers themselves found that the products slow invertase down, which is why they used starting rates. Some enzymes have graphs that rise slowly, then fast, then level off. Glucokinase in the liver is half busy at about 8 mmol/L of glucose. It responds most sharply right around blood-sugar levels, which suits its job of sensing them. No choice of K_m makes this page's function do that. And some enzymes slow down again at very high substrate, so the ceiling is a limit of the model, not of the test tube. Still, the function travels. A 1918 law for a gas sticking to a surface, and a 1949 law for bacteria growing on a scarce sugar, are the same function: a capacity, a half-way constant, and a limit never reached.

Running It Backwards

Handed a measured share of the ceiling at a known s, solve for K_m: c = s/(K_m + s) gives K_m = s(1 − c)/c. Handed two measured pairs (s, v), flip them into (1/s, 1/v) and find the linear function through both by two-points-one-line: its start is 1/V_max and its step K_m/V_max. Handed a wish (an enzyme that follows its supply in proportion), choose K_m well above the concentrations it will meet, and accept that it will run far below its ceiling.

Who said it first

The pair in Berlin were Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten (1913). The 1925 workers were George Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane; the 1934 chemists were Hans Lineweaver and Dean Burk. The chemist in Paris in 10·bio.4.4 is Victor Henri. The 1918 law is Irving Langmuir’s, for a gas on a surface; the 1949 law is Jacques Monod’s, for bacterial growth.

Michaelis and Menten argued that the rate of the splitting is at every moment proportional to the concentration of a compound of enzyme and sugar, and that this compound stands in a fixed balance with the free enzyme and the free sugar.

Michaelis and Menten, “Die Kinetik der Invertinwirkung”, Biochemische Zeitschrift 49 (1913); see Johnson and Goody, Biochemistry 50 (2011), for a translation

Accepted names: v(s) = V_max · s/(K_m + s) is the Michaelis–Menten equation, and its graph the Michaelis–Menten curve; K_m is the Michaelis constant; the double reciprocal graph is the Lineweaver–Burk plot. Far-out-too is the limit laws read far out.
