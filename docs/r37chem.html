How Can Radio Waves Tell Which Atoms Sit Where?

How can radio waves tell which atoms sit where? The nucleus of a hydrogen atom is a tiny magnet. In a strong field it wobbles at a radio frequency, and a pulse of radio sets every nucleus in a sample ringing at once, like a hall full of bells. Each bell’s pitch is shifted a little by the electrons around it and split by its neighbours, so the sound of the sample is a fingerprint of the molecule. This page finds the pitch, the shift, how rung 37’s spectral view pulls the notes out of one ringing signal, how neighbours split each note by counting, and how a slope in the field turns pitch into place, which is how hospital scanners see inside a body.

A nucleus in a field B rings at f = γB/2π. Electrons shield it slightly, lowering f by a few parts per million that depend on its surroundings. A pulse sets all ringing; the fading signal is a sum of turnings, and its spectrum gives each frequency and how much. n equal neighbours split a line into n + 1, with heights from Pascal’s triangle. A field that slopes across the body makes frequency say where.

37chem1A Nucleus Rings in a Field

Claire puts a sample in a strong magnet. Each hydrogen nucleus, a tiny magnet, wobbles round the field’s direction at a frequency set by the field alone.

f = \dfrac{\gamma}{2\pi} B @@ the field sets the pitch
\dfrac{\gamma}{2\pi} = 42.58 \text{ MHz per tesla} @@ for hydrogen, measured
B = 9.4 \text{ T}:\ f \approx 400 \text{ MHz} @@ a laboratory magnet
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a radio pulse at } f \text{ tips them all, and they ring}} @@ like bells struck together

From here on, on this page, a nucleus’s note is the frequency it rings at. A field of a tesla is twenty thousand times the earth’s; the note is in the band of radio, harmless to the sample.

37chem2Electrons Shift the Note

Electrons round the nucleus answer the field and weaken it a little where the nucleus sits. More electrons nearby, more shielding. Atoms that pull electrons away, like oxygen, leave a nucleus less shielded.

f = \dfrac{\gamma}{2\pi} B\,(1 - \sigma) @@ σ, a few millionths, set by the surroundings
\text{shift} = \dfrac{f - f_{\text{ref}}}{f_{\text{ref}}} \times 10^6 \text{ ppm} @@ in parts per million, the same at any field
\text{at 400 MHz: } 1 \text{ ppm} = 400 \text{ Hz} @@ a millionth of the note
\htmlClass{new}{\text{CH}_3 \text{ near } 1 \text{ ppm},\ \text{next to O near } 3.5,\ \text{on a ring near } 7} @@ each kind of place its own note

Shift technique: a nucleus’s note is lowered by the electrons around it, by an amount in parts per million that names the kind of place it sits in. A list of shifts says what kinds of hydrogen a molecule has, and how many of each by the size of each peak.

37chem3One Signal, Many Notes

After the pulse, the sample gives one fading signal: all the notes at once, each fading as the nuclei fall out of step. Rung 37’s spectral view takes it apart.

s(t) = \textstyle\sum_j a_j\, e^{2\pi i f_j t}\, e^{-t/T} @@ a sum of fading turnings
\text{spectrum: a peak at each } f_j \text{ of size } \propto a_j @@ the spectral view (37f)
\text{fading time } T \Rightarrow \text{peak width} \approx \dfrac{1}{\pi T} @@ short in time, wide in frequency (32f)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one pulse, one second of listening, every note at once}} @@ instead of sweeping note by note

Ringing technique: strike every nucleus at once, record the fading sum, and take its spectrum: each peak is a kind of place, its area the number of nuclei there. A longer ring gives sharper peaks, so notes a few hertz apart can be told apart.

37chem4Neighbours Split the Note

Each nucleus is also nudged by the little magnets of hydrogen nuclei on the next carbon. Each neighbour points with the field or against it, equally likely, nudging the note up or down by the same small amount.

n \text{ neighbours: the nudge is set by how many point with the field} @@ 0 to n of them
\text{lines: } n + 1;\ \text{heights} \propto \binom{n}{k} @@ coin patterns, counted (4)
\text{ethanol, CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH: the CH}_3 \text{ sees 2 neighbours} @@ 1 : 2 : 1, three lines
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the CH}_2 \text{ sees 3: four lines, } 1 : 3 : 3 : 1} @@ Pascal’s triangle in a spectrum

Splitting technique: n equal neighbours split a note into n + 1 lines with heights in the ratios of Pascal’s triangle. The splitting says how many hydrogens sit next door, so the spectrum tells not only what kinds of place there are, but which are joined.

37chem5Pitch Becomes Place

A body is mostly water. Add to the big field a small one that slopes across the body, G per metre along x. Now a nucleus’s note says where it is.

f(x) = \dfrac{\gamma}{2\pi}(B_0 + Gx) @@ note rises across the body
G = 10 \text{ mT/m}:\ 1 \text{ mm} \leftrightarrow 42.58 \times 10^6 \cdot 10^{-5} \approx 426 \text{ Hz} @@ γ/2π × G × 1 mm
\text{spectrum of the signal} = \text{how much water at each } x @@ frequency is position
\htmlClass{new}{\text{slopes in two or three directions, many times: a picture}} @@ built from spectra

Scanner technique: a field that slopes across the body makes each note name a place, so the spectrum of the ringing is a map of the water. Tissues fade at different rates, so timing the listening makes some bright and others dark. No X-rays are used: only a magnet and radio.

Accepted names

Ringing nuclei are nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR); the note is the Larmor frequency, γ the gyromagnetic ratio. The shift is the chemical shift, σ the shielding constant, and the reference is usually tetramethylsilane (TMS). The fading signal is the free induction decay, T the relaxation time T₂. Splitting by neighbours is spin-spin coupling, and the n + 1 rule gives multiplets. Imaging by a sloping field is magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the slope a gradient.

Who said it first

Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell detected NMR in bulk matter in 1946. Richard Ernst introduced the pulse and spectrum method in 1966. Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield made images with sloping fields from 1973, and shared the 2003 Nobel prize in medicine.
