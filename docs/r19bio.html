19·bio · biology

Why not just burn the sugar?

Why not just burn the sugar? A spoonful of sugar thrown on a fire burns in a second, and its energy leaves as heat and light. A cell takes the same sugar to the same end, carbon dioxide and water, by more than twenty separate steps. Long ago it was guessed that breathing is a slow fire, since both take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide and heat. What makes it hard is that the energy comes out the same either way, so the difference must be in how it is caught. This page shows, by 6·chem’s electron counting and 17’s logs, that energy can be caught only in coins of a fixed size, that one big step catches almost none of it, and that a membrane can hold coins as a battery holds charge.

Electrons are counted like money: sugar gives up 24 of them, and oxygen takes them. The energy they release depends only on where they start and end, so it can be paid out in any number of steps. A step can bank only whole coins, and a coin across a membrane is worth RT ln(ratio) plus a charge times a voltage.

19bio1Electrons, counted

6·chem gave each atom an oxidation number: hydrogen +1, oxygen −2, and the numbers in a molecule add to its charge. Raising an atom’s number by 1 means it has given up one electron. Electrons are kept like a ledger: every one given up by the sugar is taken by the oxygen.

6c + 12(+1) + 6(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow c = 0 @@ the six carbons, on average; the molecule has no charge
\text{in } \mathrm{CO_2}:\ c + 2(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow c = +4 @@ each carbon ends at +4
6 \cdot (4 - 0) = 24 \text{ electrons given up} @@ the ledger, one carbon at a time
\mathrm{O_2} \to 2\,\mathrm{O}^{-2}:\ \ 4 \text{ electrons taken each} @@ from 0 to −2, twice
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 24 / 4 = 6 oxygen molecules; then count the atoms

A plant runs the same ledger backwards: it takes electrons from water and puts them back on carbon. The cell’s route from sugar to oxygen is a string of carrier steps, and the plant’s route from water to sugar runs its own string uphill. By socks and shoes (19c), the undo of a string of steps does the last one first: the sugar side, built last by the plant, is the first thing the cell takes apart.

19bio2The drop does not depend on the route

Each carrier holds its electrons at a measured voltage, its reduction potential: the higher it is, the harder it pulls on electrons. Moving n moles of electrons from a carrier at E₁ to one at E₂ releases n · F · (E₂ − E₁), where F ≈ 96.5 kJ per mole per volt. These are measured laws. The math is what follows from them.

Route-free technique: the energy released down a chain of carriers depends only on the first and the last. A chain with carriers at E₀, E₁, …, E_k releases the sum of its steps, and the sum collapses.

\sum_{j=1}^{k} nF\,(E_j - E_{j-1}) @@ the steps, one carrier to the next
= nF\,(E_k - E_0) @@ collapsing-sum (15g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every route from } E_0 \text{ to } E_k \text{ releases the same}} \quad \blacksquare @@ as heats add along any route (11·chem)
  
E_{\mathrm{NADH}} \approx -0.32 \text{ V},\ \ E_{\mathrm{O_2}} \approx +0.82 \text{ V} @@ measured, in a cell’s conditions
2 \cdot 96.5 \cdot (0.82 - (-0.32)) \approx 220 \text{ kJ per mole} @@ two electrons per carrier
\htmlClass{new}{\text{whole sugar}: \approx 2870 \text{ kJ per mole}} @@ the same whether burned or breathed

19bio3Coins of a fixed size

The energy is caught in coins. A cell’s commonest coin is a molecule of ATP, worth about 50 kJ per mole when made in a cell. Another coin is a proton pushed across a membrane. A step that releases energy d can bank at most a whole number of coins; what is left over is heat.

Whole-coins technique: if each step can bank at most m coins of size c, a drop D needs at least D/(mc) steps to be banked in full.

\text{banked at step } j \le m\,c @@ at most m coins each
\text{banked in all} \le k\,m\,c @@ k steps, summed
k\,m\,c \ge D \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{k \ge \tfrac{D}{mc}} \quad \blacksquare @@ to bank the whole drop
  
\text{a fire}:\ \ k = 1,\ m = 0 @@ one step, and nothing is coupled to catch a coin
\text{banked} = 0 \text{ of } 2870 \text{ kJ} @@ all of it leaves as heat
\text{the chain from NADH}:\ \ 3 \text{ pumping steps},\ 4 + 4 + 2 = 10 \text{ protons} @@ measured
\htmlClass{new}{10 \cdot 19 = 190 \text{ of } 220 \text{ kJ}:\ \ \text{about } 86\%} @@ a proton coin is worth about 19 kJ per mole: 19·bio.4

So the slow fire is slow on purpose. A drop of 2870 kJ cannot be banked as 50-kJ coins in one step, and a fire banks none of it. Many small steps let almost every drop pay a whole coin.

19bio4A membrane as a battery

Push protons across a membrane, and two things build up: more of them on one side, and a voltage, since each carries a charge. A measured law says that a dissolved substance at concentration C presses like a gas, with pressure C · RT, where R ≈ 8.314 J per mole per kelvin and T is the temperature. The work to squeeze it is an area under that pressure, and 17 turns the area into a log.

\text{volume } V \text{ for one mole}:\ \ C = 1/V,\ \text{pressure } RT/V @@ the measured law
W = \int_{V_2}^{V_1} \frac{RT}{V}\,dV = RT\,(\ln V_1 - \ln V_2) @@ ln as an area (17a)
\htmlClass{new}{W = RT \ln \frac{C_2}{C_1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the log of a quotient (17c); V = 1/C

Ratio-coin technique: a coin across a membrane is worth RT ln(ratio), plus its charge times the voltage. For a mole of protons, add F times the voltage. The ratio of protons is often given in pH units, each a factor of 10.

RT \approx 8.314 \cdot 310 \approx 2.58 \text{ kJ},\ \ RT \ln 10 \approx 5.93 \text{ kJ} @@ T = 310 K; ln 10 ≈ 2.303
5.93 \cdot 0.75 + 96.5 \cdot 0.15 \approx 4.4 + 14.5 @@ 0.75 pH units across; 0.15 V across
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 19 \text{ kJ per mole of protons}} @@ so one ATP coin of 50 kJ needs at least 3

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Losing electrons is oxidation and gaining them reduction; together, redox. The chain of carriers is the electron transport chain, its pumping steps complexes I, III and IV; NADH and FADH₂ are its fuel carriers, and ATP synthase is the ATP maker. The energy available to do work is the Gibbs free energy, ΔG = −nFΔE. A proton coin’s worth is the proton-motive force, and the work to crowd a dissolved substance comes from the van ’t Hoff law of osmotic pressure. The plant’s uphill route is photosynthesis; the sugar’s first breakdown is glycolysis, and the loop that strips its carbons is the citric acid cycle.

Who said it first

Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace (1780) measured a guinea pig’s heat in an ice calorimeter and called breathing a slow combustion. Walther Nernst (1889) gave the log law for a ratio across a boundary. Hans Krebs found the cycle (1937). In 19·bio.6.5, the long search was for a “high-energy intermediate”; Peter Mitchell proposed in 1961 that the membrane itself carries the energy, as a proton gradient, and was mocked for years before winning the Nobel Prize in 1978.
