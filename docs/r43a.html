43a · proof

Clock Arithmetic

Rung five sorted the counting numbers by what is left over when you divide, and found that the leftovers can be added and multiplied as if they were numbers themselves, round a clock face. That page worked only with counting numbers, so every check went through the remainders by hand, six lines at a time. The integers are built now, and taking away always has an answer. So the same sorting can be said in one short line: two integers are alike on a clock when their difference is a whole number of turns. With that line the old checks shrink to one line each, the negatives join in, and every technique of adding and multiplying comes along to the clock at once. One technique does not come along. On some clocks two hours that are not twelve multiply to give twelve, and then cancelling fails. Which clocks those are is the first question the rung has to answer, and the answer is a matter of what the clock size shares with the hour.

Two integers are alike on a clock of n hours when n divides their difference. The classes of that likeness are the hours, and adding and multiplying hours is honest because a difference of products is a sum of multiples of differences. Every technique of plus and times comes along; order does not, and cancelling works only by an hour that shares nothing with the clock.

43a1Alike on a Clock

5·cb2 wrote m ∼ₙ b for counting numbers that leave the same remainder on dividing by n. It could not use a difference, since in ℕ the difference m − b names a number only when b ≤ m. Rung 5 made taking away total. So the remainder can be traded for a difference.

From here on, for integers d and m, d ∣ m means that m = dk for some integer k: 4j1’s line with k an integer. And for a number n ≥ 1 and integers m and b, m ≡ b (mod n) means that n divides m − b. Read it “m is b on the clock of n”. Its accepted name is “m is congruent to b modulo n”, and n is the modulus, from the Latin modulus, a small measure: the length of one turn of the clock.

The mark ≡ has stood until now only in cellars, for two statements whose tables match (1f). From here on, on the page, it is this relation between integers, and it always has (mod n) after it. In a cellar it may still mean what it meant there; there is no clash, since one side is statements and the other integers.

Difference-test technique: two counting numbers leave the same remainder on dividing by n exactly when n divides their difference.

m = nq + r,\ \ b = ns + t,\ \ 0 \le r, t \lt n @@ division with remainder, twice (4j7)
m - b = n(q - s) + (r - t),\ \ -n \lt r - t \lt n @@ take away (5a); spread for times over taking away
n \mid m - b \iff n \mid r - t @@ divides-a-multiple and divides-the-rest (4j2, 4j3), read in ℤ
\text{the only multiple of } n \text{ strictly between } -n \text{ and } n \text{ is } 0 @@ nk with k ≠ 0 has size at least n
\htmlClass{new}{n \mid m - b \iff r = t \iff m \sim_n b} \quad \blacksquare @@ ﹀ ∼ₙ

So ≡ (mod n), on counting numbers, is 5·cb2’s ∼ₙ. 38 ≡ 14 (mod 12), since 38 − 14 = 24 = 12 · 2: thirty-eight hours from midnight the clock shows what it shows fourteen hours from midnight. And now the negatives come too: −1 ≡ 11 (mod 12), since −1 − 11 = −12. An hour before midnight the clock shows eleven.

43a2The Clock of n

From here on, ℤ/n names the quotient set ℤ/≡ (mod n) of 5·cb2, and [m] the class of m in it. 5·cb2 said the slash always has a relation after it. Here the number n stands for its relation, ≡ (mod n), so ℤ/n is short for that quotient, and nothing is divided. Its members are the hours of the clock of n.

Clock-hours technique: ℤ/n has exactly n members, [0], [1], …, [n − 1], and every integer lies in one of them. For m ≥ 0, 4j7 gives m = nq + r with r < n. For m < 0, divide −m: −m = nq + r, so m = n(−q) − r, and m ≡ n − r when r > 0, m ≡ 0 when r = 0. Two of [0], …, [n − 1] are never one, by the difference-test technique. So [−5] = [7] on the clock of 12, and [−12] = [0].

Carry-to-the-clock technique: if m ≡ m′ and b ≡ b′ (mod n), then m + b ≡ m′ + b′ and mb ≡ m′b′ (mod n).

m - m' = nj,\ \ b - b' = nk @@ ﹀ ≡ twice; use the somes
(m + b) - (m' + b') = n(j + k) @@ regroup and turn-about for plus; spread for times
mb - m'b' = m(b - b') + b'(m - m') @@ add and take away mb′ (5a)
\phantom{mb - m'b'} = n(mk + b'j) @@ the two givens; spread for times
\htmlClass{new}{m + b \equiv m' + b',\ \ mb \equiv m'b' \pmod n} \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ ≡

43a3Every Technique Comes Along, but One

Equations-come-along technique: every technique of plus and times that is an equation between integers is true of the hours of every clock. Turn-about and regroup for plus and for times, spread for times over plus, 0 changes nothing in a sum, 1 changes nothing in a product, and −[m] = [−m] = [n − m] undoes [m] in a sum. Each is a sameness between integers, and the class map keeps both operations: [m] + [b] = [m + b] and [m][b] = [mb]. So the class of one side is the class of the other.

[m]([b] + [c]) = [m][b + c] = [m(b + c)] @@ carry-to-the-clock, twice
\phantom{[m]([b] + [c])} = [mb + mc] = [m][b] + [m][c] @@ spread for times over plus (5a); carry-to-the-clock, twice
10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9,\ \ \text{so } 10^k \equiv 1^k = 1 @@ 9 ∣ 10 − 1; powers carry over
d_k 10^k + \cdots + d_1 10 + d_0 \equiv d_k + \cdots + d_1 + d_0 \pmod 9 @@ carry-to-the-clock, term by term
\htmlClass{new}{38 \cdot 47 = 1786:\ \ 2 \cdot 2 \equiv 4,\ \ 1 + 7 + 8 + 6 = 22 \equiv 4 \pmod 9} \quad \blacksquare @@ 3 + 8 = 11 ≡ 2, 4 + 7 = 11 ≡ 2: the product passes the check

That is casting out nines, the check bookkeepers ran on long multiplications for centuries. 5·cb2.9.6 asked for it in many lines, and here it is one line about 10 and 1. A product that fails the check is wrong. A product that passes may still be wrong: 1768 passes too, since the check sees only the hour.

The technique that does not come along is order. 4h and 5d’s “less than” is not a statement about hours. On the clock of 12, [11] + [1] = [0], so a sum of two hours can be “below” both. There is no way to order the hours that keeps adding honest, since adding [1] twelve times comes back to the start.

43a4Where Cancelling Stops

Clock-cancel technique: on the clock of n, [a][x] = [a][y] always gives [x] = [y] exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1.

\gcd(a, n) = 1,\ \ [a][x] = [a][y] @@ the first way: given
n \mid a(x - y) @@ ﹀ ≡; spread for times over taking away
\htmlClass{new}{n \mid x - y,\ \ [x] = [y]} @@ factor-that-shares-nothing (5h), read in ℤ
\gcd(a, n) = d \gt 1:\ \ a = dj,\ \ n = dk,\ \ 0 \lt k \lt n @@ the other way: ﹀ gcd
a \cdot k = djk = j \cdot n \equiv 0 = a \cdot 0 \pmod n @@ regroup and turn-about for times
\htmlClass{new}{[a][k] = [a][0] \text{ while } [k] \ne [0]} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0 < k < n, so n ∤ k: cancelling [a] fails

The accepted names

m ≡ b (mod n) is congruence modulo n, and n is the modulus. The classes are residue classes, and ℤ/n is written ℤ/nℤ, the integers modulo n. The difference-test technique says congruence modulo n is the same as having the same remainder. The equations-come-along technique says the class map is a ring homomorphism, and ℤ/nℤ a commutative ring; rung 44 names rings. Hours [a] with [a][k] = [0] and [k] ≠ [0] are zero divisors. Casting out nines and elevens are the divisibility rules for 9 and 11.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss introduced the mark ≡ and the word modulus in the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), the great book whose opening definition 5·cb2 quotes. Casting out nines came to Europe from Indian and Arabic arithmetic; Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) taught it in the Liber Abaci (1202).

A proof that is not allowed

“[2][3] = [0] on the clock of 6, so 6 divides 2 · 3, so 6 divides 2 or 3, by prime-divides-a-factor.” Not allowed: 4j14’s technique needs a prime, and 6 is not one. Its failure here is exactly the clock-cancel technique’s second half.
