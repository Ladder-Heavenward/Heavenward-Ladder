Why Does a Group Settle on the Middle?

Why does a group settle on the middle? A club of nine members must pick a time for its weekly meeting, and each member has a best time. Two proposals go to a vote, the winner faces a third, and so on. Where does it end? This page puts every member’s best time on 8a’s line, has each member vote for the nearer of two times by 8h’s distance, writes “x beats y” as a statement for every pair, and finds that the median member’s time, 5·stat’s middle, beats every other. Then it finds where the result breaks.

Put each member’s best time on a line. Given two times, each member votes for the one nearer their best. More than half the members lie on one side of the median’s best time, counting the median, so the median’s time beats any time to its left and any time to its right.

8econ21Best Times on a Line

The club has nine members. Each says the evening time that suits them best, in hours after noon: 5, 5.5, 6, 6, 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8 and 9 (so 6.5 is half past six).

From here on, on this page, a member’s best time is a point b on the line of reals (8a), and a proposal is any time x on the same line. Sides X and Y on the committee may each bring one proposal.

Listed in order, the fifth best time is 6.5: four lie below it and four above. That is the median of the nine (5·stat).

8econ22Each Member Prefers the Nearer Choice

Assume each member, given two proposals, votes for the one nearer their best time, by distance on the line (8h).

\text{member with } b = 7,\ \text{proposals } 6 \text{ and } 8.5 @@ 
|7 - 6| = 1,\ \ |7 - 8.5| = 1.5 @@ distance on the line (8h)
\htmlClass{new}{1 < 1.5:\ \text{votes for } 6} @@ the nearer one (8b: any two reals compare)

For two proposals x < y, the members who vote for x are exactly those whose best time lies below the halfway point (x + y)/2, and those above vote for y. The halfway point splits the line.

8econ23The Median’s Time Beats Every Other

For each pair of times, let “x beats y” be the statement that more members vote for x than for y. The claim is that “m beats y” is true for the median’s time m = 6.5 and every other time y.

y > m:\ \text{halfway point } h = \tfrac{m + y}{2} > m @@ average of two, the bigger above
\text{every member with } b \le m \text{ has } b < h @@ below m is below h
\text{those members vote for } m @@ below the halfway point
b \le m:\ 5,\ 5.5,\ 6,\ 6,\ 6.5 \text{, five of nine} @@ the median and the four below it
\htmlClass{new}{m \text{ beats } y} @@ five votes at least, more than half

The same five-of-nine count on the other side (the median and the four above it) shows m beats every y to its left. Median-choice technique: when members vote for the nearer of two times and their best times lie on one line, the median member’s best time beats every other proposal in a two-way vote.

8econ24Two Proposals Drift to the Middle

Sides X and Y each want their proposal to win. Say X proposes 5.5 and Y proposes 8. The halfway point is 6.75: members with best times 5 to 6.5 vote X, five of them, and X wins.

X\ 5.5,\ Y\ 8:\ h = 6.75,\ X \text{ wins } 5 \text{ to } 4 @@ 
Y \text{ moves to } 7:\ h = 6.25,\ Y \text{ wins } 5 \text{ to } 4 @@ members 6.5 to 9 now vote Y
X \text{ moves to } 6.5:\ X \text{ beats any } Y @@ the median’s time
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither side can beat } 6.5} @@ both end there

Each side, trying to win, moves toward the median, and once one side stands on the median’s time, the other cannot beat it. A committee secretary who lists the members’ best times in order can predict where a run of two-way votes will end before the first vote is held.

8econ25Where It Breaks

The technique stood on three things: every member votes, each votes for the nearer time, and the choice is along one line. Change any one and it can fail.

changewhat happens
only members with best times above 7 bother to votethe median of the voters, not the members, wins
a third proposal joins the vote, most votes winstwo proposals near the middle split those votes, and a far one can win
the choice is a time and a day of the weekthere may be no proposal that beats every other: the votes can go round in a circle, as 6·econ found

The median wins only in two-way votes, among members who all vote, about a choice that lies along one line. The middle is a result about a shape of preferences, not about groups in general.

Accepted names

This is the median voter theorem. Preferences where each member likes times less the further they are from a best time are single-peaked, and a proposal that beats every other in two-way votes is a Condorcet winner. Two sides drifting to the middle is Downsian convergence.

Who said it first

Harold Hotelling found the drift to the middle for two shops on a street in 1929. Duncan Black proved the median result for committees in 1948, and Anthony Downs carried it to two competing sides in 1957.
