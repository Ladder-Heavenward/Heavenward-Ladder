43c · proof

Division With Remainder for Polynomials

Rung nine divided one polynomial by another, with real coefficients, and found that a number is a zero exactly when a straight factor splits off. Nothing in that work used the real numbers as numbers. It used adding, multiplying, and one reciprocal at each step: the reciprocal of the top coefficient of the divisor. So the same division runs with coefficients on a prime clock, in the fractions, in any field. What it needs is now easy to say exactly, and so is where it breaks. With whole-number coefficients, dividing by two x gets stuck at once. On a clock with zero products, the top coefficients of a product can cancel to nothing, a degree can fall, and a polynomial of degree two can have four zeros. This step sorts the rules that carry from rung nine to every field from the rules that need more.

In a ring with no zero products the degree of a product is the sum of the degrees. Division with a remainder of smaller degree works over every field, and over any ring when the divisor’s top coefficient has a reciprocal. The remainder on dividing by x − r is the value at r, so a zero is a straight factor, and a polynomial of degree n has at most n zeros, in any ring with no zero products.

43c1Degrees Add, When Nothing Multiplies to 0

The degree of a polynomial other than 0 is the largest k with a coefficient of xᵏ other than 0, and that coefficient is its top coefficient. The zero polynomial has no degree.

Degree-adds technique: in R[x], with R a cancelling ring, the degree of pq is the degree of p plus the degree of q, and R[x] is a cancelling ring.

p = a_m x^m + \cdots,\ \ q = b_n x^n + \cdots,\ \ a_m \ne 0,\ b_n \ne 0 @@ the top coefficients
(pq)_k = 0 \text{ for } k > m + n @@ every product pᵢqⱼ with i + j = k has i > m or j > n
(pq)_{m+n} = a_m b_n @@ the only pair is i = m, j = n
\htmlClass{new}{a_m b_n \ne 0,\ \ \deg pq = m + n} \quad \blacksquare @@ R has no zero products; so pq ≠ 0 too

On ℤ/4, (2x + 1)(2x + 1) = 4x² + 4x + 1 = 1. Two polynomials of degree 1 multiply to one of degree 0, and 2x + 1 is undoable in ℤ/4[x]. In a cancelling ring that cannot happen, and the undoable polynomials are just the undoable constants.

43c2Dividing Over Any Field

9g’s polynomial-division technique took real coefficients. Its proof was an induction on the degree of p: take away (aₘ/dₖ) x^(m − k) d(x) to kill the top term of p, and divide what is left. The one move that is more than adding and multiplying is the reciprocal of dₖ, the top coefficient of d.

Top-undoable technique: in R[x], if the top coefficient of d has a reciprocal in R, then every p is dq + r with r = 0 or of degree less than d’s; over a field this holds for every d ≠ 0, and q and r are the only ones.

p = a_m x^m + \cdots,\ \ d = d_k x^k + \cdots,\ \ m \ge k,\ \ d_k u = 1 @@ given: dₖ is undoable
p_1 = p - a_m u\, x^{m-k} d @@ its xᵐ coefficient is aₘ − aₘ u dₖ = 0
p_1 = dq_1 + r \text{ with } \deg r < k \text{ or } r = 0 @@ strong induction on m (4h); for m < k take q = 0, r = p
p = d\,(q_1 + a_m u\,x^{m-k}) + r @@ put it back
dq + r = dq' + r' \Rightarrow d(q - q') = r' - r @@ two answers, over a field
\htmlClass{new}{q = q',\ \ r = r'} \quad \blacksquare @@ the left side has degree at least k unless q = q′ (degree-adds); the right side has degree less than k
  
p = x^3 + 2x + 4,\ \ d = 3x + 1,\ \ 3 \cdot 2 = 1 @@ on ℤ/5 the reciprocal of 3 is 2
p - 2x^2 d = -2x^2 + 2x + 4 = 3x^2 + 2x + 4 @@ kill x³: 2x² · 3x = 6x³ = x³
3x^2 + 2x + 4 - x\,d = x + 4,\ \ \ x + 4 - 2d = 2 @@ kill x², then x
\htmlClass{new}{x^3 + 2x + 4 = (3x + 1)(2x^2 + x + 2) + 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ on ℤ/5, checked by multiplying out

43c3The Value Is the Remainder

Divide by x − r. Its top coefficient is 1, so the top-undoable technique works over any ring that turns about.

Remainder-is-value technique: the remainder of p on division by x − r is the constant p(r).

p = (x - r)\,q + c @@ the top-undoable technique: the remainder has degree 0, or is 0
p(r) = (r - r)\,q(r) + c @@ putting r in keeps sums and products (43b3)
\htmlClass{new}{p(r) = c} \quad \blacksquare @@ r − r = 0

So 9g’s factor technique holds over every ring that turns about: r is a zero of p exactly when x − r is a factor. And 9g’s at-most-n-zeros technique holds over every cancelling ring: n zeros r₁, …, rₙ split off one at a time, and a zero s of (x − r₁)q other than r₁ is a zero of q, because (s − r₁)q(s) = 0 with s − r₁ ≠ 0. That step needs no zero products, and only that step. On ℤ/8, x² − 1 has the four zeros 1, 3, 5 and 7.

43c4Division Makes One Maker

Over a field F, the top-undoable technique says the ideals of F[x] are as simple as those of ℤ.

One-maker technique: every ideal of F[x] other than {0} is the multiples of one polynomial: any member of least degree.

I \ne \{0\},\ \ g \in I,\ g \ne 0,\ \text{of least degree in } I @@ least member of a set of numbers (4h)
p \in I,\ \ p = gq + r,\ \ \deg r < \deg g \text{ or } r = 0 @@ the top-undoable technique
r = p - gq \in I @@ I holds multiples and sums
r = 0 @@ a member of I other than 0 has degree at least deg g
\htmlClass{new}{I = (g)} \quad \blacksquare @@ every p in I is gq

The same lines, with 4j’s division-with-remainder technique, give every ideal of ℤ as nℤ. In ℤ[x], where 2 has no reciprocal, (2, x) is not the multiples of one member: such a member would divide 2, so be ±1 or ±2, and ±1 is not in (2, x), while x is not a multiple of 2. 43d builds on rings where the one-maker technique holds.

The accepted names

The degree-adds technique says that a polynomial ring over an integral domain is an integral domain, with units the units of R. The top-undoable technique is the division algorithm for monic divisors, and over a field F the statement that F[x] is a Euclidean domain. The remainder-is-value technique is the remainder theorem, and the factor technique is the factor theorem. The one-maker technique says F[x] is a principal ideal domain; ℤ[x] is not one.

Who said it first

Simon Stevin divided polynomials and took their greatest common divisor by repeated division in 1585. Carl Friedrich Gauss worked with polynomials on prime clocks in an unpublished eighth section of his book of 1801. Évariste Galois wrote with them in 1830, and Richard Dedekind made the comparison of polynomials with whole numbers a theme in 1857.

A proof that is not allowed

“p has degree 2, so it has at most two zeros, by 9g.” Not allowed as it stands: 9g’s technique was proved for real coefficients, and its proof spends a step that needs no zero products. Over a cancelling ring the citation is good. Over ℤ/8 the claim is false.
