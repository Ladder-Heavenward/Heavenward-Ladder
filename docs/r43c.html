43c · proof

The symmetries of the roots

The roots of a polynomial with rational coefficients live in a field: the smallest field inside the plane that holds the rationals and every root. Rung 19 found groups wherever something could be moved and put back, and here is something that can be moved. A symmetry of the field is a matching of it with itself that keeps adding and multiplying and leaves every rational number alone. Such a symmetry cannot send a root anywhere but to another root, and once you know where the roots go, you know the whole symmetry. So the symmetries are shuffles of the roots, and they form a group, the group of the equation. This step builds that group and counts it: it has exactly as many members as the degree of the field. Not every shuffle of the roots is a symmetry, and which ones are is the information the rest of the rung lives on.

The field of the roots is the rationals with every root adjoined. A symmetry of it keeps sums and products and fixes every rational number; it sends roots to roots and is fixed by where they go, so the symmetries are a part-group of the shuffles of the roots. There are exactly as many as the degree of the field, and when f cannot be factored they carry any root to any other.

43c1The field the roots make

Let F be a field inside ℂ and f a polynomial with coefficients in F, of degree n ≥ 1. By 25’s splitting, f has roots r₁, …, rₙ in ℂ. The splitting field of f over F is K = F(r₁, …, rₙ), the smallest field inside ℂ holding F and every root, built by adjoining one root at a time as on 42f. Each root adjoined is a root of f over the field before, so each step has degree at most n, and [K : F] is finite, since degrees multiply up a tower (42f).

\text{roots } \sqrt[3]2,\ \omega\sqrt[3]2,\ \omega^2\sqrt[3]2 @@ the even-spacing technique (21·cb20), ω = (−1 + i√3)/2
K = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2, \omega\sqrt[3]2, \omega^2\sqrt[3]2) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2, \omega) @@ ω = (ω∛2)/∛2, and the other way
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2) : \mathbb Q] = 3 @@ x³ − 2 cannot be factored over ℚ (42k)
\omega \notin \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2) \subset \mathbb R,\ \ \omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0 @@ ω is not real
\htmlClass{new}{[K : \mathbb Q] = 3 \cdot 2 = 6} \quad \blacksquare @@ degrees multiply up the tower ℚ ⊂ ℚ(∛2) ⊂ K

So the field the roots of x³ − 2 make has degree 6, not 3: one root brings in a field of degree 3, and the others need one step more.

43c2Symmetries send roots to roots

A symmetry of K over F is a matching σ of K with itself (9c) with σ(a + b) = σ(a) + σ(b) and σ(ab) = σ(a)σ(b) for all a, b in K, and σ(c) = c for every c in F. Its accepted name is an automorphism. The symmetries of K over F, composed, form a group (19a): composing and undoing keep the three conditions. Write it Aut(K/F).

From here on, a shuffle that moves a few things round a loop is written as the loop: (1 2 3) sends 1 to 2, 2 to 3 and 3 to 1, and leaves the rest alone; (1 2)(3 4) does two swaps. 19a kept the list [2 3 1]; the loop is shorter when a shuffle moves few things.

Roots-to-roots technique: a symmetry of the splitting field of f over F sends each root of f to a root of f, and is fixed by what it does to the roots; so Aut(K/F) is a part-group of the shuffles of the roots.

f(x) = c_n x^n + \cdots + c_0,\ \ c_k \in F,\ \ f(r) = 0 @@ r a root
0 = \sigma(f(r)) = \sigma(c_n)\sigma(r)^n + \cdots + \sigma(c_0) = f(\sigma(r)) @@ σ keeps sums and products, and fixes each cₖ
\sigma(r_i) \text{ is a root, and } \sigma \text{ is one-to-one, so it shuffles the roots} @@ a matching
\text{every member of } K \text{ is a sum of products of roots and members of } F, \text{ with quotients} @@ K is built from F by adjoining them
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma \text{ and } \tau \text{ agree on every root} \Rightarrow \sigma = \tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ both keep sums, products and quotients

For x² − 2 over ℚ, a symmetry sends √2 to √2 or to −√2. Both are symmetries: a + b√2 goes to a + b√2 or to a − b√2. The group has two members, the identity and the swap (1 2) of the two roots.

43c3Counting the symmetries

Two facts first. An irreducible g over F inside ℂ has no repeated root: a repeated root would be a root of the slope g′ too, and g′ is not 0 and has lower degree, so the greatest common divisor of g and g′, found by 42c’s division, would be a factor of g strictly between 1 and g. And adjoining one root α of g gives F(α), each member written once as a polynomial in α of degree below deg g (42f), so a symmetry on F(α) is fixed by where α goes.

Count-the-symmetries technique: if K is the splitting field of a polynomial over F, then Aut(K/F) has exactly [K : F] members.

\text{claim: a matching } \tau: F \to F' \text{ keeping sums and products extends to exactly } [K : F] \text{ such matchings } K \to K' @@ K, K′ the splitting fields of f and of f′, f with τ put on its coefficients; induction on [K : F]
[K : F] = 1:\ \ K = F, \text{ one extension, } \tau \text{ itself} @@ the start
g \text{ an irreducible factor of } f,\ \deg g = d > 1,\ \alpha \text{ a root of } g \text{ in } K @@ some factor has degree above 1, or every root is in F
g' = \tau(g) \text{ has } d \text{ different roots } \beta \text{ in } K' @@ irreducible, so no repeated root
\text{each } \beta \text{ gives one extension } F(\alpha) \to F'(\beta),\ \ \alpha \mapsto \beta @@ both are F[x]/(g) by 42b and 42f, and α must go to a root of g′
[K : F(\alpha)] = [K : F]/d @@ degrees multiply up the tower F ⊂ F(α) ⊂ K
\text{each of the } d \text{ extends in } [K : F]/d \text{ ways} @@ K is the splitting field of f over F(α); the claim, by induction
\htmlClass{new}{d \cdot [K : F]/d = [K : F] \text{ extensions; with } F' = F,\ \tau = \text{identity},\ |\mathrm{Aut}(K/F)| = [K : F]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the count

For x³ − 2, Aut(K/ℚ) has 6 members, so every one of the 6 shuffles of the three roots is a symmetry: the group is all of S₃. For x² − 2 the count is 2.

43c4The group of an equation

The group of f over F is Aut(K/F), with K the splitting field, read as shuffles of the roots by the roots-to-roots technique. Write it G_f. It has [K : F] members, a divisor of n! (19d).

Every-root technique: if f is irreducible over F, then for any two roots α and β of f, some member of G_f sends α to β.

\tau = \text{identity of } F,\ \ g = f @@ the claim of the count-the-symmetries proof
F(\alpha) \to F(\beta),\ \ \alpha \mapsto \beta @@ one extension for each root of f
\htmlClass{new}{\text{it extends to a symmetry of } K \text{ sending } \alpha \text{ to } \beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the claim again, from F(α) to K
  
x^4 - 5x^2 + 6 = (x^2 - 2)(x^2 - 3),\ \ \text{roots } \sqrt2, -\sqrt2, \sqrt3, -\sqrt3 @@ it factors
K = \mathbb Q(\sqrt2, \sqrt3),\ \ [K : \mathbb Q] = 4 @@ √3 is not in ℚ(√2): (a + b√2)² = 3 forces ab = 0, and neither a² = 3 nor 2b² = 3 has a rational answer
\htmlClass{new}{G_f = \{\text{e},\ (1\ 2),\ (3\ 4),\ (1\ 2)(3\ 4)\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ √2 goes to ±√2 and √3 to ±√3: four choices, and the count is 4

So G_f is never just “all shuffles”. For x⁴ − 5x² + 6 it keeps the pair {√2, −√2} together, which is how the group remembers that f factors.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A symmetry of K over F is an F-automorphism, and Aut(K/F) is written Gal(K/F) when K is a splitting field: the Galois group. G_f is the Galois group of the polynomial. A splitting field is a normal extension; that an irreducible polynomial has no repeated root is separability, automatic in fields inside ℂ. The claim in the count-the-symmetries proof is the isomorphism extension theorem. Loop notation is cycle notation. The every-root technique says the Galois group of an irreducible polynomial is transitive on its roots.

Who said it first

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1770–71) studied how expressions in the roots change when the roots are shuffled, and Paolo Ruffini and Augustin-Louis Cauchy developed shuffles as objects. Évariste Galois, in a memoir of 1831 printed in 1846, attached a group to an equation: the shuffles of its roots that keep every rational relation among them. The version by symmetries of a field is Richard Dedekind’s, from his lectures of the 1850s, and Emil Artin’s, from lectures of 1938 printed in 1942.

A proof that is not allowed

“x³ − 2 has three roots, so its group has three members, the turns of the three roots.” Not allowed: the count is [K : ℚ], the degree of the splitting field, which is 6. The turns are three of the six; the flip of ℂ, restricted to K, is a swap of ω∛2 and ω²∛2 that fixes ∛2.
