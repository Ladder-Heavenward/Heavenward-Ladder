Multiplying Geometries

Rung 18 built the plane from pairs of numbers, and in that plane, through a point off a line, exactly one line never meets it. A Greek geometer had laid that down as his fifth axiom, and for two thousand years people tried to prove it from his other four. This rung finds out why they failed. First it takes the numbers away and puts the fifth axiom on trial. Then it builds two other worlds: the surface of a sphere, where every two lines meet, and a disc whose lines are arcs, where through a point endlessly many lines miss a given one. Both are as sound as the numbers they are built from. So the other axioms cannot settle the question, and geometry is not one thing but several. Then the rung asks how a creature living in a surface could tell which kind it lives in. The answer is a ruler that changes from place to place, a bend that the ruler alone can read, and a total of that bend that counts the holes in the surface.

For two thousand years people tried to prove one statement. You are about to see why they could not. Nothing on rung 1 prepared you for that, and everything since has.

The parallel axiom can be denied without contradiction, so flat geometry is one among many. A surface carries a ruler, and from the ruler alone its bend can be read; the bend’s total counts its holes.

What it means to say it

To say flat is one geometry among many is to say that the other axioms of geometry do not decide the parallel question. There are worlds that obey all of them in which the parallel axiom holds, and worlds that obey all of them in which it fails. Which world we live in is a question for measurement, not for proof.

The parallel axiom can be neither proved nor refuted from the other axioms, and the bend of a surface, which tells the geometries apart, can be read from its ruler alone. The rung proves both, and what follows from them: triangles that measure their own area, paths that are shortest, and a total that counts holes.

Where it comes from

A Greek geometer stated the fifth postulate around 300 BCE. Scholars in Alexandria, Athens, Cairo and Persia tried to prove it. An Italian priest put it on trial in 1733, a mathematician in Berlin went further in 1766, and a French mathematician proved the angle-sum bound in 1794. A German philosopher held in 1781 that the old geometry is known before any experience. A German mathematician worked out the other geometry privately from about 1813 and kept quiet; a Russian mathematician published it in 1829 and a Hungarian officer in 1832. The same German mathematician gave the ruler and the remarkable technique in 1827, a French mathematician the triangle form in 1848, and a lecture of 1854 made the ruler the starting point of geometry. An Italian mathematician built a model of the other geometry in 1868, closing the question; a French mathematician gave the disc in 1882 and argued in 1902 that the choice of geometry is a convention. A theory of gravity of 1915 made the ruler of spacetime something measured.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, without the parallel axiom a triangle’s angles add to at most a straight angle (29a4), and whether they must add to exactly that is the whole question (29a5).

Second, on a sphere every two lines meet (29b1) and a triangle’s area is its excess (29b3). In the disc plane, through a point, endlessly many lines miss a given line (29c3), and every triangle has angles adding to less (29c4). The disc plane obeys every one of the other axioms, so the parallel axiom is independent of them (29c5).

Third, a surface carries a ruler, three numbers at each point, and every length (29d1), angle and area (29d2) comes from it.

Fourth, the product of a surface’s two main bends (29e3) can be read from the ruler alone (29e4), and its total over a closed surface counts the holes (29f4).

Fifth, a path that makes a total least obeys one equation (29g1); on a surface, the shortest paths are the straightest (29g2), and a triangle of them has total bend equal to its excess (29g4).

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, the annexes carry green boxes. On this rung each one says the same thing in its own field: logic allows more than one geometry or principle, and a measurement, with its error, picks one. 29·then carries a measurement picks the principle: when two principles fit every angle, look for the quantity on which they disagree, and measure it with its error (light timed slower in water, 1850). 29·phys carries a measurement picks the geometry: logic allows the old change of observer and a plus-sign ruler as well as the minus-sign one, and light timing, muon counts and flown clocks chose c²dt² − dx². 29·nav carries a measurement picks the geometry: whether the ground is flat or curved is found by measuring a triangle’s angles against its area; the excess of a triangle of three Cascade volcanoes is about 10″, there to be found but only just. 29·now carries a measurement picks the geometry: flat and bent rulers differ in what clocks at different heights read, and measurements chose the bent one.

What follows

With those five, the techniques compute. Spherical triangles are solved by hand and flight paths are planned. Distances and areas in the disc and in the half-plane are found. The bends of circles, helices, saddles and doughnuts are worked out. The fastest slide, the hanging chain and the soap film come from the best-path equation, and a painter’s perspective gives a geometry with points at infinity. The annexes take these into mechanics by least action, the counting of endless runs, a single rule for every boundary, time in relativity, routes over the pole, mechanics in steps of time, and the waves of bending space.

What you can do because you know it

You can tell whether a statement of geometry follows from the other axioms or needs the parallel one. You can compute in round and saddle geometry as well as flat. You can measure lengths, angles and areas on any surface from its ruler, find how it bends without leaving it, and find the shortest path across it.

From here on, a geometry is chosen and then measured, not assumed.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down for the ladder. 29a lists the other axioms only to study what they prove. The rung spends rung 18’s plane and spheres, rung 20’s moves, rung 21’s angles and π, rung 25’s maps of the plane, rung 27’s dot and cross products and determinants, and rung 28’s slope grids and totals. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say flat is one geometry among many, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Euclid stated the fifth postulate around 300 BCE. Ptolemy, Proclus, Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi tried to prove it. Girolamo Saccheri put it on trial in 1733, Johann Heinrich Lambert went further in 1766, and Adrien-Marie Legendre proved the angle-sum bound in 1794. Immanuel Kant held in 1781 that Euclid’s geometry is known before any experience.

Carl Friedrich Gauss worked out the other geometry privately from about 1813 and kept quiet; Nikolai Lobachevsky published it in 1829 and János Bolyai in 1832. Gauss gave the ruler and the remarkable theorem in 1827, Pierre Ossian Bonnet the triangle form in 1848, and Bernhard Riemann’s lecture of 1854 made the ruler the starting point of geometry. Eugenio Beltrami built a model of the other geometry in 1868; Henri Poincaré gave the disc in 1882 and argued in 1902 that the choice of geometry is a convention. Albert Einstein’s theory of gravity of 1915 made the ruler of spacetime something measured.

Accepted names: the flat geometry is Euclidean geometry, and the saddle geometry hyperbolic or Lobachevskian geometry; the remarkable theorem is Gauss’s Theorema Egregium; the product of the two main bends is the Gaussian curvature; the triangle form and the hole count are the Gauss–Bonnet theorem; the ruler is the Riemannian metric; the disc plane is the Poincaré disc.
