Why Does a Ship Roll Back Upright?

Why does a ship roll back upright? A wave tips it, and it swings back; but in 1628 the Swedish warship Vasa heeled over in a light breeze less than a mile into its first voyage and sank in minutes, with guns too heavy and too high. A ship’s weight pulls down at one point and the water’s lift, 16·cb12’s displaced weight, pushes up at another. When the ship tips, the lift moves sideways, because the shape under water changes. Whether it moves far enough to turn the ship back is a matter of strip sums and rung 21’s sine. This page finds where the lift moves to, prices a barge’s stability by hand, says what too much and too little feel like, and why loose water on a deck is so dangerous.

Weight acts at G, lift at B, the middle of the water pushed aside. Tip by a small angle θ: a wedge of water moves from the high side to the low, and B moves sideways by θ·I/V, I the waterline area’s second moment. The upward line from the new B crosses the centreline at M, a height I/V above B. If M is above G, the pair turns the ship back with arm GM sin θ; if below, it turns it over.

21eng21Weight Down, Lift Up

Evita’s barge floats upright. Its weight pulls down through G, the balance point of the barge and its load. The water pushes up through B, the balance point of the water it pushes aside (16·cb12).

\text{weight} = \text{lift} @@ it floats
G \text{ and } B \text{ on one upright line} @@ so they do not turn it
B \text{ lies below } G \text{ in most ships} @@ cargo and decks above the water
\htmlClass{new}{\text{yet ships stand: } B \text{ must move when it tips}} @@ or every ship would turn over

From here on, on this page, to heel is to tip sideways by an angle θ.

21eng22The Lift Moves Over

Heel the barge by a small angle θ. The side going down sinks deeper, the side rising comes up: a wedge of water is pushed aside on the low side and given back on the high.

\text{a strip at } y \text{ from the centre sinks by about } y\theta @@ sin θ ≈ θ (21f)
\text{its turning effect: } y \cdot (y\theta) \cdot L\,dy @@ distance times volume
\text{total} = \theta \int y^2 L\,dy = \theta I @@ strip sums (15c)
\htmlClass{new}{B \text{ moves sideways by } \dfrac{\theta I}{V}} @@ V the volume under water

Metacentre technique: a small heel moves the lift sideways by θ times the waterline’s second moment over the volume under water; the lift’s line then crosses the centreline at M, a height I/V above B. The ship is stable when M is above G.

21eng23A Barge by Hand

V = 50 \cdot 10 \cdot 4 = 2000 \text{ m}^3 @@ volume under water
I = \int_{-5}^{5} 50\,y^2\,dy = \dfrac{50 \cdot 10^3}{12} \approx 4167 \text{ m}^4 @@ strip sums (16c)
BM = \dfrac{I}{V} \approx 2.08 \text{ m};\ B \text{ is } 2 \text{ m up: } M \text{ is } 4.08 \text{ m up} @@ from the bottom
\htmlClass{new}{G \text{ at } 3.5 \text{ m: } GM = 0.58 \text{ m, stable};\ G \text{ at } 4.5 \text{ m: } GM < 0, \text{ it capsizes}} @@ stacking cargo high

The righting turn at a small heel is the weight times GM sin θ (21b). A wider hull raises M fast, since I grows as the cube of the width; heavy loads up high raise G. Vasa’s high gun decks put G above M.

21eng24Stiff and Tender

\text{large } GM:\ \text{a strong turn back} @@ a quick, jerky roll
\text{small } GM:\ \text{a weak turn back} @@ a slow, lazy roll
\text{large: cargo shifts and crew are thrown about} @@ too stiff
\htmlClass{new}{\text{small: a gust or a turn tips it far}} @@ too tender

Designers aim for a GM large enough to come back from wind and waves, small enough for a gentle roll: often under a metre for big ships. Sailors judge it by the time each roll takes.

21eng25Loose Water on the Deck

A tank half full, or water sloshing on a car deck, runs to the low side when the ship heels. Its own surface acts like a small waterline inside the ship.

\text{the loose water’s middle moves by } \theta\,i / v @@ the same strip sum, inside
\text{as if } G \text{ rose by } \dfrac{i}{V} @@ i that surface’s second moment
\text{10 cm of water across a car deck 25 m wide} @@ i grows as width cubed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a little water spread wide can wipe out } GM} @@ depth hardly matters

In 1987 the ferry Herald of Free Enterprise left Zeebrugge with its bow doors open; water spread over the car deck and it capsized in minutes, with 193 lost. Loose liquid with a wide surface raises the ship’s effective G, however shallow it is; tanks are divided lengthwise and car decks now carry barriers.

21eng27The Righting Curve, and the Inclining Test

Upright, buoyancy and weight act on one line. Heeled, they part, and the turn back is the weight times GZ, the sideways gap between them; at small heel GZ = GM sin θ (21eng23). Drawn against heel, GZ rises, tops out, and falls to zero where the ship would capsize.

GZ \approx GM \sin\theta:\ \theta = 10^{\circ}:\ 0.58 \times 0.174 \approx 0.10 \text{ m} @@ the start of the curve
\text{move } w = 10 \text{ t across } d = 8 \text{ m}:\ \tan\theta = \dfrac{w\,d}{\Delta \cdot GM} @@ the turn of the moved weight, balanced
\tan\theta = \dfrac{80}{2\,000 \times 0.58} \approx 0.069 @@ a 5 m plumb line swings about 0.35 m
\htmlClass{new}{\text{read backwards: } GM = \dfrac{w\,d}{\Delta \tan\theta}} @@ G found by a measured swing, not by guessing

Inclining technique: move a known weight across the deck and read the heel; GM is the weight times the distance over the ship’s weight times the tangent of the heel. Shown at a quay: weights are shifted across a new ship’s deck and a long plumb line read at each shift; the swings give a GM within a few centimetres of what the drawings predicted, and the result is recorded before the ship may sail.

Accepted names

M is the metacentre, GM the metacentric height, the arm GM sin θ the righting arm GZ. I is the second moment of area of the waterplane. A ship with large GM is stiff, small GM tender; the loose-water loss is the free surface effect.

Who said it first

Pierre Bouguer named the metacentre in his Traité du navire of 1746, and Leonhard Euler worked out ship stability in the same years. Vasa sank on 10 August 1628 and was raised in 1961; the Herald of Free Enterprise capsized on 6 March 1987.
