8·cb5 · newsflash for 5f

The Fractions' Holes, Filled

5f left three foreclosures. It found a hole in the fractions and said rung 8 would fill it. It found fractions that come nearer and nearer the hole, seven fifths, seventeen twelfths, and on, and said that how near is a question of size, and sizes wait for rung 8. And 5h, dividing 1 by 7 place by place, watched the remainders come round again and said that what an endless run of digits could name is a question for rung 8. This page pays all three. Every splitting of the fractions has exactly one real at it. So does every splitting of the line itself: the rung's title, as a technique. The near misses are measured exactly. And endless decimals become names of reals, with the technique that a decimal repeats exactly when the real it names is a fraction.

Wherever the fractions split with nothing at the split, the lower part is itself a real, and it sits there. An endless decimal names the least real above its cut-offs; it repeats exactly when the remainders do, which they must when the real is a fraction.

8·cb5.1Every Splitting Has a Real at It

5f said “hole” in words. Here is the word it leaned on, made exact.

From here on, a splitting of a set X ordered by < (ℚ or ℝ) is a pair (L, U) of parts of X, each with a member. Every member of X is in exactly one of them, and every member of L is below every member of U. L is its lower part and U its upper part. A real ρ is at the splitting when x ≤ ρ for every x ∈ L and ρ ≤ y for every y ∈ U (fractions read as reals by 8d's naming line).

From here on, ρ (the Greek letter rho, in its small form) names a real at a splitting. It is a name like any other letter.

Holes-filled technique: every splitting of ℚ has exactly one real at it.

\text{case: } L \text{ has no greatest member} @@ split on it (7e's split on any statement)
L \text{ is a cut} @@ (i), (ii) from the two members; (iii): s < r ∈ L with s ∈ U would put r below s; (iv) the case
\rho = L:\ \ r \in L \Rightarrow r \lt \rho;\ \ u \in U \Rightarrow \rho \le u @@ members-are-the-fractions-below (8a); everything of L is below u, so L ⊆ u*
\text{case: } L \text{ has a greatest member } g @@ the other case
\rho = g^{*} = g:\ \ r \le g;\ \ g \lt u @@ fractions-keep-order; and g ∈ L, u ∈ U
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a real at the splitting}} @@ reason by cases
  
\rho, \rho' \text{ both at it},\ \rho \lt \rho' @@ suppose two (three-way-order on ℝ: else they are the same)
\text{call } t \in \mathbb Q \text{ with } \rho \lt t \lt \rho' @@ fraction-between (8e)
t \in L:\ t \le \rho,\ \bot;\ \ t \in U:\ \rho' \le t,\ \bot @@ the two parts; clash each time
\htmlClass{new}{\rho = \rho'} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; three-way-order on ℝ

For 5f's hole, L is 8a9's heap, with no greatest member, so the real at it is L itself: √2, by 8f's hole's-cut-is-√2. For the splitting at 3/4 with L = {r : r < 3/4}, the real at it is L = (3/4)*. For the splitting with L = {r : r ≤ 3/4}, it is the greatest member, 3/4 again. A fraction can sit at a splitting from either side. A hole is a splitting no fraction sits at. And every one of them now has a real.

The inventor of cuts took this as the essence of a line without gaps. Whenever its places fall into two classes, every place of the first to the left of every place of the second, exactly one place produces this division. He then showed that the system of real numbers, built from cuts, has exactly this property.

Its second step is the line's own: split the reals themselves. Decimal-line-has-no-holes technique: every splitting of ℝ has exactly one real at it. L has a member, and every member of U is an upper bound of it, so σ = sup L exists (no-holes, 8c). Every x ∈ L has x ≤ σ. Every y ∈ U is an upper bound of L, so σ ≤ y, since σ is least. Only one: suppose σ < σ′ were both at it. The real m = (σ + σ′)/2 lies strictly between (8d's techniques and ½). Then m ∈ L gives m ≤ σ, while m ∈ U gives σ′ ≤ m, a clash either way. ∎ That is the rung's title, as a technique. Splitting the fractions can leave nothing at the split; splitting the line never does.

8·cb5.2How Near the Near Misses Come

5f found fractions p/q with p² − 2q² = ±1: 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, 99/70. 5f left how near they come as a question of size for rung 8. Now distance on the line (8h) says how near they come to √2, and that flag is closed. How-near technique: for positive integers p, q with p² − 2q² = 1 or −1, |p/q − √2| = 1/(q(p + q√2)) < 1/(2q²).

\big|\tfrac pq - \sqrt2\big| = \tfrac{|p - q\sqrt2|}{q} @@ size-of-a-product (8h); q positive
= \tfrac{|p - q\sqrt2|\,(p + q\sqrt2)}{q\,(p + q\sqrt2)} @@ multiply by 1; p + q√2 is positive
= \tfrac{|p^2 - 2q^2|}{q\,(p + q\sqrt2)} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{q\,(p + q\sqrt2)}} @@ difference-of-squares; size-of-a-product; (q√2)² = 2q²; the given
p \ge q,\ \ q\sqrt2 \gt q:\ \ p + q\sqrt2 \gt 2q @@ p² ≥ 2q² − 1 ≥ q² (powers-keep-order backwards); 1 < √2 (powers-keep-order)
\lt \tfrac{1}{2q^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ positive-reciprocal on ℚ read in ℝ

So 7/5 is within 1/50 of √2 (in fact about 0.0142), and 99/70 within 1/9800 (about 0.0000722). Each step of 7f's climb (p, q) ↦ (p + 2q, p + q) brings the next pair nearer by a factor of about 6. They never arrive: by no-fraction-squares-to-2 none is √2, and how-near's distance is never 0. 8e's second road found the fraction 19601/13860 between √2 and 99/70 with n = 13860. How-near says why the gap is so small: it is 1/(70(99 + 70√2)), whose bottom is just under 13860.

8·cb5.3An Endless Decimal Names a Real

From here on, an endless decimal is a number N and a function d from the numbers 1, 2, 3, … to the digits (4i's ten). It is written N.d₁d₂d₃⋯, the dots standing for the function in hand, as 4i's did for a list. Its cut-offs are the finite decimals c_k = N.d₁⋯d_k of 5h (c₀ = N). The real it names, its value, is sup{c_k : k ∈ ℕ}. A bar over a block of digits means the block repeats for ever: is 0.1666⋯.

Endless-decimal technique: the cut-offs of N.d₁d₂⋯ have a least upper bound, at most N + 1. A list of k digits read by 4i's technique names at most 10^k − 1 (by induction: w(i + 1) = 10 · w(i) + d ≤ 10(10^i − 1) + 9). So c_k ≤ N + (10^k − 1)/10^k < N + 1, and c₀ is a member. No-holes does the rest. ∎ Two techniques about least upper bounds help with decimals. Scale-and-shift-bounds technique: for a set S of reals with a member and an upper bound, a positive real c and any real t: sup{cs : s ∈ S} = c · sup S and sup{t + s : s ∈ S} = t + sup S. c · sup S is an upper bound of the first set (order-and-arithmetic-agree). Any upper bound β of it makes β/c an upper bound of S, so sup S ≤ β/c. Likewise with t. ∎

Every-real-has-a-decimal technique: every real x ≥ 0 is the value of an endless decimal, given by the digit technique below, and that decimal never ends in endless 9s. Let N be the greatest number at most x (least-member-for-any-set on the numbers above x, by counting-passes-any-real). The digit technique, by 4i's counted-recursion: the next digit d_k is the greatest digit d with c_{k−1} + d/10^k ≤ x. By induction, c_k ≤ x < c_k + 10^(−k). The digit 0 always qualifies, and if d_k = 9 the hypothesis gives the strict end. So x is an upper bound of the cut-offs. Now take y < x. Everyday-counting-passes gives n with 1/n < x − y, and 10^(−n) < 1/n, so c_n > x − 10^(−n) > y. Hence x = sup. ∎ For x < 0, write − before the decimal of −x.

Some reals have two decimals. The cut-offs of are 1 − 10^(−k), and their least upper bound is 1, by the same two lines: so 0.999⋯ = 1. The digit technique picks 1.000⋯ for 1, never the 9s. This is no paradox: a decimal is a name, and a real may have two names, as 1/2 and 2/4 are two names of one fraction.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud instead of the raindrop

1a's second kind of care has a principle for things that are there but can't be pinned to a dot. √2 is exact: a cut, fixed for ever by which fractions square to less than 2. Yet no decimal ever writes it. 1.414 is a cut-off, and the digit technique says how far off: ck ≤ x < ck + 10−k. Every decimal of √2 you will ever write carries a width, and you choose it when you choose where to stop.

When you can't pin a thing down, give the stretch it stays inside, and say how wide.

This is not ignorance waiting for a sharper look. No list of digits, however long, is √2. For √2 written in digits, the width is part of the writing. 8·stat gave a list its spread as part of its answer; a single number written in decimals gets one too.

7/5 is within 1/50 of √2, and 99/70 within 1/9800. A tiler cuts the diagonal of a square tile 1 m on a side. Which name of √2 should she cut to?

8·cb5.4A Decimal Repeats Exactly When Its Real Is a Fraction

From here on, an endless decimal repeats when there are numbers m and p ≠ 0 with d_{i + p} = d_i for every i > m: after the m-th place, a block of p digits comes round for ever.

One counting technique first, used for the remainders. Pigeonhole technique: for every number q, a function from S(q) to q has two different inputs with the same output. By induction on q. Base: there is no function from 1 = {0} to 0 = ∅, since 0 would need an output in ∅. So the statement is true, because when nothing is true, everything is permitted. Step: let f go from S(S(q)) to S(q). Suppose the last input, S(q), has output q. Then either another input also has output q (done), or f on S(q) goes into q and the hypothesis finishes. Suppose instead f(S(q)) = t ≠ q. Let g on S(q) be f, except that an input with output q gets t instead. g goes into q, so two inputs j ≠ k share an output under g. If neither was changed, they share it under f. If both were changed, they share q under f. If only j was, then k shares t with the input S(q) under f. ∎ (Every split on outputs is between numbers, decidable by settled-order.) The pigeonhole technique's accepted name is the pigeonhole principle: with more pigeons than holes, some hole holds two.

Repeating-decimal technique: for x ≥ 0, the decimal of the digit technique repeats exactly when x is r* for a fraction r. Rightward, run 5h's division place by place. Take x = v/b, with v and b numbers and b ≠ 0. N and r₀ are the quotient and remainder of v by b (4j's division-with-remainder). Then each next digit d_k and remainder r_k are the quotient and remainder of 10 r_{k−1} by b. By induction x = c_k + r_k/(b · 10^k), with 0 ≤ r_k < b. That is the digit technique's invariant, so these are its digits. The remainders r₀, r₁, …, r_b are b + 1 values in b, so pigeonhole gives i < j with r_i = r_j. Each digit and remainder depends only on the remainder before. So everything from place i + 1 on comes round with period j − i. Leftward, shift the point. Shift-the-point technique: if x = N.d₁d₂⋯, then 10^k x = M_k + t_k, where M_k is the number N d₁⋯d_k read by 4i's technique and t_k is the value of 0.d_{k+1}d_{k+2}⋯. Here 10^k times the cut-off c_{k+j} is M_k plus the tail's j-th cut-off (4i's reading technique and 5h's decimal point). Scale-and-shift-bounds carries that to the least upper bounds. If the decimal repeats with m and p, the decimals after place m and after place m + p are the same function. So t_m = t_{m+p}, and

10^{m+p} x - 10^m x = M_{m+p} - M_m @@ shift-the-point twice; the tails are the same real
x = \dfrac{M_{m+p} - M_m}{10^m\,(10^p - 1)} @@ multiply by the reciprocal; 10^p − 1 ≠ 0 since p ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{x \text{ is a fraction}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a number over a number other than 0 (naming lines)

Going the other way, 1/7 gives the remainders 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, …, and the decimal , the block 5h watched come round. A finite decimal repeats too, in a block of 0s: 5/8 = . And √2 = 1.41421356⋯ never repeats, by repeating-decimal and hole's-cut-is-√2 with hole-is-no-fraction's-cut. Its digits never come round, however far you go. That is 5h's question answered, and the flag 5h left for rung 8 is closed. An endless run of digits names one real, the least above its cut-offs, and it names a fraction exactly when, past some place, it repeats.

8·cb5.6Running It Backwards

Handed a repeating decimal, shift the point by the length of the block, take away, and divide: the fraction. Handed a fraction, divide place by place and watch for a remainder you have seen. Handed a splitting of the fractions, ask whether the lower part has a greatest member. If so, that fraction is at it. If not, the lower part is the real at it. Handed a near miss p/q, compute p² − 2q², and how-near measures it. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The inventor of cuts is Richard Dedekind.

Dedekind took as the essence of a line without gaps that whenever its places fall into two classes, every place of the first to the left of every place of the second, exactly one place produces this division. He then showed that the system of real numbers, built from cuts, has exactly this property.
