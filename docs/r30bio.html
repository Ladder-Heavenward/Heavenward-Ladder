30·bio · biology

How does a gene spread through a population?

How does a gene spread through a population? The threespine stickleback is a small fish of the coast, from the Salish Sea down into California. In the sea it wears a row of bony plates along each side. In the lakes of British Columbia, cut off from the sea when the glaciers melted, the same fish wears only a few. The form of the gene for few plates is rare in the sea, perhaps one copy in a hundred, and in lake after lake it has become nearly the only form. How does a form of a gene go from rare to everywhere? And when a form is lost, as nearly every variant has been lost from the island foxes off the coast of southern California, what took it away? What makes the question hard is that two forces work at once. Selection favours the form that leaves more young, and pushes steadily. Chance decides which parents happen to leave young at all, and it pushes every which way, hardest in small populations. Rung five counted one cross of peas. This page follows a whole population, generation after generation, with rung thirty’s chance as size, averages and fair games, and asks which force did the work.

Mix the eggs and pollen of a whole population at random, and the three kinds of plant come out in shares fixed by the share of each form of the gene, and those shares then stay put. Give one form an edge in leaving young, and its odds against the other grow by a fixed factor each generation, so it spreads along a rising curve that is slow at both ends. In general the share of a form moves uphill on the population’s average fitness, and rests at the tops: when the mixed kind does best, both forms are kept, in a share fixed by how much worse each pure kind does. And in a population of fixed size, chance alone makes the share of a form a fair game. Its average stays put, its spread grows, and the mixing of forms drains away by a fixed share each generation, until one form is everywhere. The chance that a form ends up everywhere is exactly its share at the start. Genes on one strand travel together and are sent out mixed at a share that never reaches a half, and a gene on the X shows in men at its share and in women at its square.

30bio1Allele shares at rest

Take one gene with two alleles, A and a, in a large population. Write p for the share of A among all the copies, and q = 1 − p. 5·bio crossed two plants. Now cross a whole population: every new plant gets one copy from an egg and one from a pollen grain, each drawn from the whole pool of copies, apart from each other (30f).

P(AA) = p\cdot p,\ \ P(Aa) = 2pq,\ \ P(aa) = q^2 @@ independent draws (30f); Aa can come two ways, A from the egg or A from the pollen
p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = (p + q)^2 = 1 @@ the three kinds take up every plant
p' = \tfrac{2p^2 + 2pq}{2} = p(p + q) = p @@ the new share of A: two copies in each AA, one in each Aa
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after one round: } p^2,\ 2pq,\ q^2,\ \text{ and they stay}} \quad \blacksquare @@ p′ = p, so the next round gives the same shares

One-round-rest technique: if copies join at random, one round of mixing sets the shares of AA, Aa and aa at p², 2pq and q², and they stay there while nothing else acts. Mixing alone changes nothing. A gene spreads only when something breaks one of the conditions: copies that do not join at random, an edge in leaving young, a population too small to be a whole pool, or copies arriving from outside.

A classic test: a survey of 1000 people in England typed for the MN blood groups found 298 MM, 489 MN and 213 NN. So p = (2 · 298 + 489)/2000 = 0.5425. The technique predicts 294.3, 496.4 and 209.3. The mixed kind misses by 7.4, against a spread width of √(1000 · 0.4964 · 0.5036) = 15.8, so Z = −0.47 (30·then’s scaled miss). The population looks like one at rest.

30bio2Selection and allele shares

Now let the three kinds leave young at different rates: on average w₁₁ for AA, w₁₂ for Aa and w₂₂ for aa, counted as copies passed on into the next round. Its accepted name is fitness. Each kind’s share of the copies is weighted by its fitness, and the weights are shared out over their total, the average fitness w̄ = p²w₁₁ + 2pqw₁₂ + q²w₂₂.

p' = \tfrac{p^2w_{11} + pqw_{12}}{\bar w} = \tfrac{p\,(pw_{11} + qw_{12})}{\bar w} @@ the copies of A after selection, over all copies
w_{11} = (1 + s)^2,\ \ w_{12} = 1 + s,\ \ w_{22} = 1 @@ each copy of A adds the same edge s
p' = \tfrac{p\,(1 + s)(1 + ps)}{\bar w} @@ pw₁₁ + qw₁₂ = (1 + s)(p(1 + s) + q) = (1 + s)(1 + ps)
q' = \tfrac{q\,(1 + ps)}{\bar w} @@ the same for a: pw₁₂ + qw₂₂ = p(1 + s) + q = 1 + ps
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{p'}{q'} = (1 + s)\,\tfrac{p}{q}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide: the factor 1 + ps and w̄ cancel

Odds-growth technique: when each copy of A adds the same edge s, the odds p/q of A against a grow by the factor 1 + s every generation. Equal steps, equal factors (17j): the odds grow like an exponential, and p itself follows 17·bio’s logistic curve, slow when A is rare, fastest at a half, slow again near the end. From 1% to 99% the odds go from 1/99 to 99, a factor of 9801, which takes ln 9801/ln(1 + s) generations: 188 for s = 0.05, and 464 for s = 0.02.

Not every edge works this way. If only AA gets the edge, so A helps only in a double dose, then a rare A sits almost always in Aa, where it shows no edge. At p = 0.01 and s = 0.02, the share of A rises by 0.0002 a generation when each copy counts, and by 0.000002 when only a double dose counts: a hundred times slower. The stickleback’s few-plates form behaves much like this second kind, which is part of why a lake fish keeps a stock of it hidden in the sea.

30bio3Fitness landscapes

Draw w̄ as a function of p, over [0, 1]. Its accepted name is a fitness landscape, though here it has only one direction to walk in. Which way does selection move p on it?

w_1 = pw_{11} + qw_{12},\ \ w_2 = pw_{12} + qw_{22},\ \ \bar w = pw_1 + qw_2 @@ the average fitness of a copy of A, of a copy of a, and of all
\tfrac{d\bar w}{dp} = 2(pw_{11} + qw_{12}) - 2(pw_{12} + qw_{22}) = 2(w_1 - w_2) @@ the slope of p²w₁₁ + 2p(1 − p)w₁₂ + (1 − p)²w₂₂ (13)
p' - p = \tfrac{pw_1}{\bar w} - p = \tfrac{p\,(w_1 - \bar w)}{\bar w} = \tfrac{pq\,(w_1 - w_2)}{\bar w} @@ w₁ − w̄ = w₁ − pw₁ − qw₂ = q(w₁ − w₂)
\htmlClass{new}{p' - p = \tfrac{pq}{2\bar w}\,\tfrac{d\bar w}{dp}} \quad \blacksquare @@ put in the slope

Uphill-share technique: selection moves the share p in the direction in which the average fitness w̄ rises, at a pace set by pq and the slope of w̄, so p rests only where that slope is 0 or at an end. When the mixed kind does best, w₁₁ = 1 − s, w₁₂ = 1, w₂₂ = 1 − t, the slope is 0 inside [0, 1], at a top.

w_1 - w_2 = p(1 - s) + q - p - q(1 - t) = -ps + qt @@ put the fitnesses in
-ps + qt = 0 \iff p^* = \tfrac{t}{s + t} @@ the flat-top technique (14a): the slope of w̄ is 0
\htmlClass{new}{p \lt p^* \Rightarrow p' \gt p,\ \ p \gt p^* \Rightarrow p' \lt p} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uphill-share technique: −ps + qt falls as p rises

Balanced-share technique: when the mixed kind leaves the most young, both alleles are kept, and the share of A rests at t/(s + t), where s and t are the shortfalls of AA and aa. In the lowlands of West Africa where malaria is common, carrying one copy of the sickle allele guards against malaria, two copies cause sickle cell disease, and none leaves a child open to malaria. With shortfalls near 0.15 for no copies and 0.85 for two, the technique rests the sickle allele at 0.15/(0.15 + 0.85) = 0.15, near the shares found in such regions. Where malaria is gone, the edge is gone, and the uphill-share technique says the allele falls.

When the mixed kind does worst, the landscape has its tops at the ends and a valley between. Selection pushes p to whichever end it starts nearer. A population on one top cannot cross the valley by selection alone, even toward a higher top.

30bio4Drift in a finite population

Now take a population of N plants, so 2N copies, and no edge for either allele. Each generation draws its 2N copies at random from the last one’s, each copy A with chance p. The count of A is a sum of 2N independent parts, so it has average 2Np and spread 2Npq (30g). The share p′ has average p and spread pq/(2N). Its accepted name is drift.

\operatorname{E}[p' \mid p] = p @@ a fair game (30k): the share’s next average is its present value
\operatorname{E}[p'^2 \mid p] = p^2 + \tfrac{pq}{2N} @@ an average square is the square of the average plus the spread (30g)
\operatorname{E}[p'q' \mid p] = p - p^2 - \tfrac{pq}{2N} = pq\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big) @@ q′ = 1 − p′
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}[2p_nq_n] = 2p_0q_0\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big)^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ average once more over p, n times

Shrinking-mix technique: with drift alone, the average share of mixed plants, 2pq, shrinks by the factor 1 − 1/(2N) every generation. With 50 plants, a hundred generations keep only 37% of it. The mixing drains away because chance, sooner or later, carries every population to one end, where it stays. Which end?

\operatorname{E}p_n = p_0 @@ the fair-stays-fair technique (30k)
p_n \notin \{0, 1\} \Rightarrow p_nq_n \ge \tfrac{1}{2N}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{2N}\big) = c @@ at least one copy of each allele
P(p_n \notin \{0, 1\}) \le \tfrac{\operatorname{E}[p_nq_n]}{c} \to 0 @@ the stray-bound technique (30h), and the shrinking-mix technique
p_n \to p_\infty \in \{0, 1\} \text{ with chance } 1 @@ an end, once reached, is kept: no copies of the other allele are left to draw
\operatorname{E}p_\infty = \lim \operatorname{E}p_n = p_0 @@ the fenced-limit technique (30d): 0 ≤ pₙ ≤ 1
\htmlClass{new}{P(A \text{ ends up everywhere}) = p_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ E p_∞ = 1 · P(p_∞ = 1) + 0

Fix-chance technique: with drift alone, an allele ends up in every copy with chance equal to its share at the start, and otherwise it is lost. A single new copy, in a population of N plants, starts at 1/(2N): in 50 plants it ends up everywhere one time in 100, however good or bad it is, as long as it gives no edge. The island foxes of the Channel Islands off southern California have lived for thousands of years in populations of a few hundred to a few thousand. Surveys of their genes found almost no variation left on San Nicolas Island, the drift these techniques predict for a small population kept small for long.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

An allele’s share in a real population is never still. Drift alone moves it each generation by a spread width √(pq/2N). So a reported share is a cloud, and a change in it is evidence of selection only when it stands out of the cloud.

A change in an allele’s share is called selection only when it stands outside the width drift alone gives it, and the chance of a false call is stated.

Call a one-generation change selection only when it is more than two spread widths of drift. By the bell (30j), drift alone passes that line about one time in twenty, so the call is wrong at a rate stated in advance. A smaller change is not proof of no selection. An edge of 1% moves p by about 0.002 a generation near a half, far inside the cloud of a population of 500. Selection that weak is real, and slow, and seen only over many generations.

In a lake on Vancouver Island with 100 breeding sticklebacks, the few-plates allele goes from 0.40 to 0.46 in one generation. Is that drift or selection? How sure could you be either way?

30bio6Genes on one strand travel together

5·bio’s peas sorted two genes apart from each other. That holds for genes on different strands of DNA. Two genes on one strand are passed on together, unless, while eggs and pollen are made, the strand is cut and swapped with its partner somewhere between them. Take a plant with A and B on one strand and a and b on the other. One swap between the genes sends out Ab or aB; no swap, or two, sends out AB or ab. Write r for the share of copies sent out mixed.

A law, measured along the strands of many living things, with exceptions near some places that block swaps: along a stretch of strand the swaps are many small independent chances, so their count in the stretch is 22j’s rare count, with some average m. Lay down m as the stretch’s length on the map, counted in swaps per copy.

Odd-crossing technique: if the swaps between two genes are a rare count with average m, the share sent out mixed is r = (1 − e^(−2m))/2, which never reaches ½; and m = −½ ln(1 − 2r).

r = P(\text{an }\allowbreak \text{odd }\allowbreak \text{number }\allowbreak \text{of }\allowbreak \text{swaps}) @@ an even number puts each gene back with its old partner
= e^{-m}\big(m + \tfrac{m^3}{3!} + \tfrac{m^5}{5!} + \cdots\big) @@ the rare-events technique (22j2): chance e^(−m) mᵏ/k! of k swaps
= e^{-m}\cdot\tfrac{e^{m} - e^{-m}}{2} @@ the exp-as-a-sum technique (22j3) for e^m and e^(−m): the odd terms are half the difference
\htmlClass{new}{r = \tfrac{1 - e^{-2m}}{2} \lt \tfrac12,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \ m = -\tfrac12\ln(1 - 2r)} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^(−2m) is above 0; undo the exponential (17)

Count: AB/ab crossed with ab/ab gives 410 AB, 400 ab, 95 Ab and 95 aB young, so r = 190/1000 = 0.19 and m = −½ ln 0.62 ≈ 0.24, 24 units on the map, where genes on different strands would give r = ½. Map lengths add along a strand, since the counts in two stretches that do not overlap are independent and add (30g). Genes A, B, C with r = 0.1 between each neighbouring pair are 11.2 and 11.2 units apart, 22.3 from A to C, and r from A to C is 0.18, not 0.2. Shares sent out mixed do not add; map lengths do; and over short stretches, where r ≈ m, the two agree.

30bio7A gene on the X

A woman carries two X strands, one from each parent; a man carries one X, from his mother, and a Y from his father. A gene on the X with no partner on the Y comes in two copies in women and one in men. Let q be the share of its copies, in the whole pool, that are the form a, and let a show only in a double dose in women.

X-share technique: if copies join at random, a form a of a gene on the X shows in men at its share q and in women at q², so men show it 1/q times as often; a mother with one copy passes it to half her sons, who show it, and to half her daughters, who carry it.

P(\text{a }\allowbreak \text{man }\allowbreak \text{shows } a) = q @@ his one X is one draw from the pool (30f)
P(\text{a }\allowbreak \text{woman }\allowbreak \text{shows } a) = q \cdot q = q^2 @@ two independent draws, as in the one-round-rest technique (30·bio.1)
X^AX^a \times X^AY:\ \ \text{sons } \tfrac12\,X^aY,\ \allowbreak \ \text{daughters } \tfrac12\,X^AX^a @@ 5·bio’s square; a son’s X is his mother’s
\text{red-green }\allowbreak \text{colour }\allowbreak \text{blindness}:\ \ q \approx 0.08,\ \allowbreak \ q^2 \approx 0.0064 @@ about 8 men in 100 of northern European descent
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{q}{q^2} = \tfrac1q = 12.5} \quad \blacksquare @@ the X-share technique: twelve men for each woman

Measured, about 0.5 women in 100 have red-green colour blindness, near the technique’s 0.64; the gap is in part that the form is several forms at two genes side by side, and a woman with one of each sees normally. A father never passes such a form to his son, whose X came from the mother, and he passes it to every daughter, who carry it unseen to half their sons. So a form that hides in women shows a generation later in their sons, as haemophilia did in the royal families of Europe. Rung 36 finds what a missing cone does to the colours such a man sees.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The one-round-rest technique is the Hardy–Weinberg principle, and a population with shares p², 2pq, q² is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. w₁₁, w₁₂, w₂₂ are relative fitnesses, s and t selection coefficients, and the mixed kind’s edge is heterozygote advantage (overdominance); its shortfall is underdominance. The uphill-share technique is Wright’s formula for the change in allele frequency, and w̄ against p his adaptive landscape. The drawing of 2N copies is the Wright–Fisher model; 2pq is the heterozygosity, and N the effective population size. The fix-chance technique gives the probability of fixation of a neutral allele. The view in the seam’s first side is the neutral theory of molecular evolution, and its steady pace the molecular clock. Genes on one strand are linked, the strand is a chromosome, and the swap is crossing over; the young sent out mixed are recombinants, and r the recombination frequency. The map length is the genetic map distance, in morgans or centimorgans, and m = −½ ln(1 − 2r) is Haldane’s map function. A gene on the X is X-linked, or sex-linked, and a woman with one hidden copy is a carrier.

Who said it first

G. H. Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg found the resting shares independently in 1908; Udny Yule’s question about why dominant traits do not take over prompted Hardy’s letter. The MN survey is from the textbook literature of the twentieth century and should be checked against its source. Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright built the mathematics of selection and drift between 1918 and 1932; Haldane estimated the edge of the dark peppered moth near Manchester in 1924. Anthony Allison tied the sickle allele to malaria in 1954. Motoo Kimura gave the fixation chances in 1962 and proposed the neutral theory in 1968, with Jack King and Thomas Jukes (1969); Tomoko Ohta extended it to nearly neutral changes in 1973. David Kingsley’s group (Colosimo and colleagues, 2005) found the few-plates allele of the stickleback gene Eda at low shares in the sea and nearly fixed in fresh water. Robert Wayne and colleagues (1991) found almost no genetic variation among the foxes of San Nicolas Island. William Bateson, Edith Saunders and Reginald Punnett saw two genes of the sweet pea travel together in 1905. Thomas Hunt Morgan found a gene on the X of the fruit fly in 1910, and his student Alfred Sturtevant drew the first map of a strand from shares sent out mixed in 1913. J. B. S. Haldane gave the map function in 1919. Queen Victoria carried haemophilia into several royal houses of Europe.

In 30·bio.8.5, the neutral side is Kimura’s; the selectionist side runs from the critics of the 1970s to John Gillespie and later work finding selection in genome-wide data. The quarrel between Fisher (selection in large populations) and Wright (drift in small, divided ones, his shifting balance) is its older form.
