9g · technique

Polynomials and Rational Functions

Polynomials are the functions you can compute with adding and multiplying alone; rational functions are one polynomial over another. This step gives their anatomy. Polynomials divide like whole numbers, with a remainder, and 4j's division is the model. Each zero is a factor. A polynomial has no more zeros than its degree, and from that one count come two licences. Two polynomials agreeing at enough inputs are the same, coefficient by coefficient. And an equation between fractions may be checked at inputs where the fractions themselves name nothing. For large inputs the highest power decides everything, said in order words. A fraction of polynomials is undefined where its bottom is 0, and it splits into simple pieces that add back to the whole.

Each zero gives a factor: the input less that zero. Divide one leading term at a time, and multiply back to check. The leading term owns large inputs. A factored bottom splits a fraction into pieces.

9g1The Words

From here on, 4k's Σ is read with real terms as well. For a rule i ↦ t(i) giving reals, Σ_{i<n} t(i) names s(n), where s : ℕ → ℝ is fixed by counted-recursion with start s(0) = 0 and step s(S(i)) = s(i) + t(i). sum-of-none, sum-one-more, termwise, factor-out and first-term-off are techniques for real terms too, by the same inductions, with 8d's rules for ℝ in place of rung 4's.

From here on, a polynomial is a statement that names a function p : ℝ → ℝ for which there are a number n and reals u₀, u₁, ⋯, u_n with p(x) = Σ_{k≤n} u_k x^k = u₀ + u₁x + ⋯ + u_n xⁿ for every real x. The reals u_k are coefficients of p: a list indexed from 0, a function from S(n) to ℝ, written with 4i's low marks. When u_n ≠ 0, the sum has degree n, u_n is its leading coefficient and u_n xⁿ its leading term. A real r with p(r) = 0 is a zero of p. The function with every value 0 is the zero polynomial; it has no degree.

Three words gain jobs here. Degree is said of a polynomial: 5j's degree Celsius, a unit of temperature, keeps its meaning. A zero of p is an input. 3c's zero, the name of 0, keeps its meaning, and p(r) = 0 says the output is that number. And this list of coefficients starts at 0, where 4i's lists start at 1; 4i's line stands for its lists.

The degree-1 polynomials are 9e's linear function with step not 0. The degree-0 ones are the functions with one output for all. 5i's x² + px + q is degree 2. The line speaks of “a sum” having a degree, because a function might, for all you know yet, be written two ways. The same-coefficients technique below shows it cannot: the coefficients are fixed by the function. Until then, “degree” is a fact about a written sum.

9g2Dividing, as 4j Divided

Polynomial-division technique: for polynomials p and d, with d of degree m ≥ 1, there are polynomials q and r with p(x) = d(x)q(x) + r(x) for every real x, where r is the zero polynomial or has degree less than m. q is the quotient, r the remainder: 4j's words, for the same gesture. The proof is strong induction (4h's strong induction) on the degree n of p's sum. If n < m, take q = 0 and r = p. If n ≥ m, let u_n and v_m be the leading coefficients of p and d. Subtract (u_n/v_m)x^(n−m) d(x) from p. The xⁿ terms cancel, so what is left has degree less than n (or is zero), and the induction hypothesis divides it, d q₁ + r. Then p = d(q₁ + (u_n/v_m)x^(n−m)) + r. ∎ That proof is the method: long division removes the leading term, one line at a time.

\tfrac{2x^3}{x} = \htmlClass{new}{2x^2} @@ how many times does x go into the leading term 2x³?
2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6 - 2x^2(x - 3) @@ subtract 2x²(x − 3) = 2x³ − 6x²
\phantom{2x^3} = 3x^2 - 11x + 6 @@ the leading term is gone
\tfrac{3x^2}{x} = \htmlClass{new}{3x} @@ the next quotient term
3x^2 - 11x + 6 - 3x(x - 3) = -2x + 6 @@ subtract 3x² − 9x
\tfrac{-2x}{x} = \htmlClass{new}{-2},\ \ -2x + 6 - (-2)(x - 3) = \htmlClass{keep}{0} @@ subtract −2x + 6: remainder 0
2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6 = (x - 3)(2x^2 + 3x - 2) @@ the quotient read off the orange terms
\phantom{2x^3 - 3x^2 - 11x + 6} = (x - 3)(2x - 1)(x + 2) @@ split the middle term (5i): zeros 3, ½, −2

Run it backwards to check: multiply the factors out with 5i's grid of cells, and you must land on the polynomial you started with. (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2) = 2x³ + 3x² − 2x − 6x² − 9x + 6, which collects to 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6. Dividing and multiplying undo each other, as every tech on 9f's undo list has its undo.

9g3Zeros Are Factors

Factor technique: r is a zero of p exactly when p(x) = (x − r)q(x) for a polynomial q; and if p has degree n ≥ 1, q has degree n − 1 and the same leading coefficient. Its accepted name is the factor theorem.

p(x) = (x - r)\,q(x) + c @@ polynomial-division with d(x) = x − r, of degree 1: the remainder has degree 0 or is zero, a real c
p(\htmlClass{new}{r}) = (r - r)\,q(r) + c @@ true at every real, so at r (use an every)
p(r) = \htmlClass{new}{0} \cdot q(r) + c = c @@ negative on ℝ; anything-times-zero read in ℝ: the remainder is the value at r
p(r) = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; c = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{keep}{p(x) = (x - r)\,q(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap axiom each way; plus-zero on ℝ

So the remainder on dividing by x − r is p(r), with no division needed. The accepted name for this is the remainder theorem: the remainder is the value. Dividing x³ + 2x² − x + 5 by x + 2, which is x − (−2), leaves p(−2) = −8 + 8 + 2 + 5 = 7. And x − r is 5i's displacement pattern, without its square. Factoring a polynomial into such pieces writes it as a product of displacements from its zeros.

An old geometry book observed that when a value is a root of an equation, the equation can be divided exactly by the unknown less that value. The division lowers the degree by one. And an equation can have as many roots as its degree, and no more. (A root is the old word for a zero, still in use.)

At-most-n-zeros technique: a polynomial whose sum has degree n has at most n zeros: its set of zeros has k members for some number k ≤ n. By induction on n. Degree 0: p is u₀ ≠ 0 everywhere, no zeros. Step: if p has no zero, 0 members. If r is a zero, factor gives p(x) = (x − r)q(x) with q of degree n − 1. By zero-product read in ℝ, the zeros of p are r and the zeros of q. The hypothesis gives q at most n − 1. Bringing in r (4k's bring-one-in, when r is not already a zero of q) gives at most n. ∎ The case split “p has a zero or not” is rung 7's either-way principle at work; the column needs no address for the zero.

Same-coefficients technique: if Σ_{k≤n} u_k x^k = Σ_{k≤n} v_k x^k at n + 1 different reals, then u_k = v_k for every k ≤ n. Suppose not. Take the greatest k with u_k ≠ v_k (7·cb4's least member, run from the top on a finite set: 4k's greatest-member). The difference Σ_{j≤k} (u_j − v_j)x^j has degree k ≤ n and at least n + 1 zeros, against at-most-n-zeros. Refute; not-not-out. ∎ So the coefficients of a polynomial are fixed by the function, and with them its degree: “degree” is now a fact about p. A polynomial that is 0 at every real is the zero polynomial. And same-coefficients licenses matching coefficients: two written polynomials that agree everywhere agree term by term.

Where does a first zero come from? Guess, but guess well. Extend 4j's mark to the integers first.

From here on, for integers d and m, d ∣ m means m = dk for some integer k. Read it, as on 4j, “d divides m”. For numbers it agrees with 4j's line. If m and d are numbers with m = dk, k is a number unless m = 0, when k = 0 serves.

Fraction-zeros technique: if a polynomial has integer coefficients, u_n ≠ 0, and the fraction m/d in lowest terms is a zero of it, then m ∣ u₀ and d ∣ u_n. Its accepted name is the rational root theorem. Multiply u_n(m/d)ⁿ + ⋯ + u₀ = 0 through by dⁿ: u_n mⁿ + u_(n−1)m^(n−1)d + ⋯ + u₀dⁿ = 0. Every term but u₀dⁿ has a factor m, so m ∣ u₀dⁿ. Every term but u_n mⁿ has a factor d, so d ∣ u_n mⁿ. m and d share no divisor but 1 (lowest terms). So factor-that-shares-nothing (5h), read through 5b's member-with-a-0, cancels the powers. ∎ It is 5i's whole-answers, grown up. For x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6 the only candidates are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, and p(1) = 1 − 2 − 5 + 6 = 0 at the first try. For 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 they include ±½ and ±3/2, and ½ works.

9g4The Leading Term Owns Large Inputs

9e asked what large inputs do. For polynomials the answer is one technique, said with order alone. Leading-term technique: let p have degree n ≥ 1 and leading coefficient u_n, and let C = |u₀| + |u₁| + ⋯ + |u_(n−1)| and M = max(1, 2C/|u_n|). Then for every real x with |x| ≥ M, |p(x) − u_n xⁿ| ≤ ½|u_n xⁿ|. How a polynomial behaves far out is called its end behaviour, and the leading term settles it.

|p(x) - u_n x^n| = \big|\textstyle\sum_{k\lt n} u_k x^k\big| @@ the leading term taken off (sum-one-more)
\le \textstyle\sum_{k\lt n} |u_k|\,|x|^k @@ triangle (8h) term by term, by induction; size-of-a-product
\le \textstyle\sum_{k\lt n} |u_k|\,|x|^{n-1} = C\,|x|^{n-1} @@ |x| ≥ 1, so |x|^k ≤ |x|^(n−1) for k ≤ n − 1 (multiplying by |x| ≥ 1 does not lessen: keep-order for times on ℚ read in ℝ); factor-out
\le \tfrac{|u_n|}{2}\,|x| \cdot |x|^{n-1} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12\,|u_n x^n|} \quad \blacksquare @@ |x| ≥ 2C/|u_n|, multiplied by the positive |u_n||x|^(n−1)/2 (order-and-arithmetic-agree)

Leading-term settles how p behaves far out on either side. Unpack it with 8h's unpack-the-bars: for |x| ≥ M, p(x) lies between ½u_n xⁿ and 3/2 u_n xⁿ. So p(x) has the sign of its leading term there, and is at least half its size. Four kinds follow, said in order words. Even degree, u_n > 0: past M on either side, p(x) is positive, and passes any real you name (since ½u_n xⁿ ≥ ½u_n|x| for |x| ≥ 1, and counting-passes-any-real). Even, u_n < 0: negative on both sides, below any real you name. Odd, u_n > 0: negative far left, positive far right. Odd, u_n < 0: the reverse. An odd-degree polynomial takes both signs, and passes any real you name on the side where its leading term is positive. So x³ − 4x passes any real you name, and the flag 9e left on it is closed. Must it then have a zero somewhere between? Rung 12 answers that. Take p(x) = x³ − 4x: C = 4, M = 8, and at 8, p(8) = 480, between 256 and 768.

9g5Rational Functions, and Where They Are Undefined

From here on, a rational function is a function x ↦ p(x)/q(x), for polynomials p and q with q not the zero polynomial. Its domain is its natural domain, {x ∈ ℝ : q(x) ≠ 0}. That is every real but the zeros of q, of which there are at most the degree of q (at-most-n-zeros).

Factor the top and the bottom. A zero of the bottom is an input the function lacks. A factor shared by top and bottom may be cancelled, but cancelling is a rewrite with small print (9a): the function still lacks that input. (x² − 4)/(x² − x − 2) = ((x − 2)(x + 2))/((x − 2)(x + 1)) agrees with (x + 2)/(x + 1) at every input it has, and it has neither 2 nor −1. (x + 2)/(x + 1) has 2, with output 4/3. One pair's difference, and equal-functions is unforgiving about single pairs.

Near an input the function lacks, the bottom is small and the top is not, so the outputs are large. In order words, for f(x) = 1/(x − 1): if 0 < |x − 1| < 1/n then |f(x)| > n (positive-reciprocal on ℚ read in ℝ). Rung 10 has the words for saying more. For large inputs, divide first. Polynomial-division gives p = dq + r with r of lower degree, so p/d = q + r/d, and r/d is small far out. With (2x + 1)/(x − 1) = 2 + 3/(x − 1): for x > 1 + 3n, the output is within 1/n of 2. When the top's degree is lower than the bottom's, the whole fraction is small far out. Leading-term on top and bottom gives |p(x)/q(x)| ≤ 3|u|/|v| · |x|^(k − l) for large |x|, where k is the degree of p and l the degree of q, and a negative power passes below any 1/n.

9g6Partial Fractions: Splitting, and Putting Back

Adding fractions is 5h's forward tech: 3/(x − 1) + 2/(x + 1) = (5x + 1)/(x² − 1). The finished fraction hides its pieces, because multiplying out the bottom erased the factors. Splitting runs the same tech backwards.

\tfrac{5x + 1}{x^2 - 1} = \tfrac{5x + 1}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} @@ factor the bottom: difference-of-squares (5i)
\phantom{\tfrac{5x + 1}{x^2 - 1}} = \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{A}}{x - 1} + \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{B}}{x + 1} @@ one unknown real over each factor (the top's degree is below the bottom's)
5x + 1 = A(x + 1) + B(x - 1) @@ add the pieces over the common bottom (fractions-of-rationals iii); equal bottoms, equal tops (multiplying-keeps-answers)
x = 1:\ \ 6 = 2A,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{A = 3} @@ the B term is 0 at x = 1 (licensed by same-coefficients: see below)
x = -1:\ \ -4 = -2B,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{B = 2} @@ the A term is 0 at x = −1
\tfrac{5x + 1}{x^2 - 1} = \tfrac{3}{x - 1} + \tfrac{2}{x + 1} @@ the split; recombine to check

Why may you put x = 1 into an equation that came from fractions that name nothing at x = 1? The equation of the tops, 5x + 1 = A(x + 1) + B(x − 1), was derived at every real but ±1. Both sides are polynomials of degree at most 1, and they agree at far more than 2 reals. So by same-coefficients they have the same coefficients, and so they agree at ±1 too. The move is licensed by the zero count.

Splitting a fraction this way is called partial fractions, and putting in the bottom's zeros so the unknowns drop out one at a time is the cover-up method, as the old calculus primers taught it.

Two-factors technique: for reals r ≠ s, u and v, (ux + v)/((x − r)(x − s)) = A/(x − r) + B/(x − s) at every x ≠ r, s exactly when A = (ur + v)/(r − s) and B = (us + v)/(s − r). The column above is the proof, with letters. The tops' equation is true everywhere by same-coefficients. At x = r it gives A(r − s) = ur + v, and at x = s it gives B(s − r) = us + v. And those values do satisfy it, by recombining. So the split exists and there is only one. Two rules finish the method. If the top's degree is not below the bottom's, divide first (polynomial-division) and split only the remainder's fraction: (x² + 4x − 2)/(x² + x − 6) = 1 + (3x + 4)/((x − 2)(x + 3)) = 1 + 2/(x − 2) + 1/(x + 3). And a repeated factor needs a piece for each power: (x + 1)/(x − 1)² = A/(x − 1) + B/(x − 1)², since a lone A/(x − 1)² can only put a constant on top. Here x + 1 = A(x − 1) + B. x = 1 gives B = 2, and matching the x terms (same-coefficients) gives A = 1.

9g8Running It Backwards

Wanting a polynomial with given zeros: multiply the displacements, then fix the leading coefficient with one more value. A polynomial with zeros −1, 2, 4 and p(0) = 4 is u(x + 1)(x − 2)(x − 4) with u · 8 = 4, so u = ½. Wanting a factor: find a zero by fraction-zeros, divide by factor. Wanting a fraction with given pieces: add them over a common bottom. Wanting the pieces: factor, with one unknown per factor and per power. Clear bottoms, and choose the x that kills all but one term. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The old geometry book is Descartes’s La Géométrie, and the root of 9g.4.4 is his word. The calculus primer is Silvanus Thompson’s Calculus Made Easy.

Descartes observed that when a value is a root of an equation, the equation can be divided exactly by the unknown less that value, and that the division lowers the degree by one; and that an equation can have as many roots as its degree, and no more.

Descartes, La Géométrie (1637), Book III

Thompson, splitting fractions in the same way, says the equation of the tops must be true for every value of x, and therefore also for the values that make one factor of the bottom vanish; putting those in, the unknowns drop out one at a time.

Thompson, Calculus Made Easy, 2nd ed. (1914), ch. XIII

Accepted names: polynomial-division is the division algorithm, and the column is polynomial long division. “The remainder is the value at r” is the remainder theorem, and factor is the factor theorem. Fraction-zeros is the rational root theorem. How p behaves far out, which leading-term settles, is its end behaviour.
