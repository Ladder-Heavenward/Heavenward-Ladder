What Shape Is an Electron’s Cloud?

A molecule of water is bent. Its two hydrogens do not sit on opposite sides of the oxygen, in a line, but 104.5° apart; methane’s four hydrogens sit 109.5° apart, at the corners of a pyramid of four faces. Something in the atom has directions.

35·phys found hydrogen’s lowest orbital, the same in every direction, and counted n² shapes at level n without drawing them. 38·phys filled them two to a shape. This page draws them: each is a polynomial times a fade. Their still surfaces are counted, the distance where each cloud is thickest is found, the order in which atoms fill them is read off the measured table, and four of them are mixed into the arms of carbon.

An orbital’s shape is a polynomial with no second slope, times a fade outward from the centre.

38chem1A Polynomial Times a Fade

A drum struck off-centre can ring with a line across it that never moves (33g2): one side rises while the other falls. An atom has the same kind of shapes, with planes in place of lines.

From here on, a still polynomial of degree ℓ is a polynomial in x, y, z whose every term has degree ℓ and whose Δ is 0. The numbers 1, then x, y and z, then xy and x² − y², are still polynomials; x² is not, since Δx² = 2.

Times-a-fade technique: if p is a still polynomial of degree ℓ, then φ = p · e−r/a is a shape of hydrogen exactly when a = (ℓ + 1) r₀, and its energy is −13.6 eV/(ℓ + 1)².

\Delta(p\,g) = g\,\Delta p + 2\,\nabla p\cdot\nabla g + p\,\Delta g @@ two slopes of a product (13, 17), on each of x, y, z
\nabla g = \tfrac{g^{\prime}}{r}(x, y, z),\quad x p_x + y p_y + z p_z = \ell\,p @@ g depends on r alone; each term of degree ℓ is multiplied by ℓ
\Delta g = g^{\prime\prime} + \tfrac{2}{r}g^{\prime} @@ the round-ball technique (33g)
\Delta \varphi = p\,\big(g^{\prime\prime} + \tfrac{2(\ell + 1)}{r}g^{\prime}\big) @@ Δp = 0
\Delta \varphi = \big(\tfrac{1}{a^2} - \tfrac{2(\ell + 1)}{a\,r}\big)\varphi @@ g = e to the −r/a
\tfrac{\hbar^2(\ell + 1)}{m\,a} = ke^2,\quad a = (\ell + 1)\,r_0 @@ the 1/r terms of the statement must cancel (35·phys6)
\htmlClass{new}{E = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m a^2} = -\tfrac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{(\ell + 1)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ what is left
ℓnamestill polynomialslevelenergy
0s11−13.6 eV
1px, y, z2−3.40 eV
2dxy, yz, zx, x² − y², 2z² − x² − y²3−1.51 eV

ℓ = 0 gives back 35·phys6’s ground orbital. The three p shapes are one shape turned to face along x, y and z.

38chem2Counting the Shapes

Still-count technique: the still polynomials of degree ℓ form a space of dimension 2ℓ + 1.

\text{terms of degree }\ell\text{ in three letters: }\tfrac{(\ell + 1)(\ell + 2)}{2} @@ xⁱ yʲ zᵏ with i + j + k = ℓ: ℓ − i + 1 ways for each i; the sum of the first numbers (4k)
\Delta\text{ sends them to degree }\ell - 2:\ \tfrac{(\ell - 1)\ell}{2}\text{ terms} @@ each second slope drops the degree by 2
\Delta\text{ reaches every term of degree }\ell - 2 @@ for ℓ ≤ 3 by hand: Δx² = 2, Δx³ = 6x, Δx²y = 2y, Δx²z = 2z
\tfrac{(\ell + 1)(\ell + 2)}{2} - \tfrac{(\ell - 1)\ell}{2} = \tfrac{(\ell + 2)(\ell + 1) - \ell(\ell - 1)}{2} @@ the lost-plus-reached technique (27c)
\htmlClass{new}{= 2\ell + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1, 3, 5, 7

That Δ reaches every term in every degree is true and is stated here, not proved past ℓ = 3. ∞39’s bracket gives the same 2ℓ + 1 by another road.

\varphi_{2s} = \big(2 - \tfrac{r}{r_0}\big)\,e^{-r/2r_0} @@ a shape of level 2 with ℓ = 0: put it in the statement, E = −3.40 eV
\varphi_{3p} = z\,\big(6 - \tfrac{r}{r_0}\big)\,e^{-r/3r_0} @@ level 3, ℓ = 1: E = −1.51 eV

38chem3Lobes and Still Surfaces

The drum of 33g2 has two kinds of still line: diameters, one for each winding, and rings. An orbital has the same two kinds, now as surfaces.

Still-surface technique: a shape of level n and type ℓ is 0 on ℓ surfaces through the centre and on n − ℓ − 1 spheres round it, n − 1 in all; it changes sign across each, and near the centre it grows like rℓ.

2p_z:\ z = 0 @@ one plane through the centre; two lobes, + above and − below
3d_{xy}:\ x = 0\ \text{or}\ y = 0 @@ two planes; four lobes
3d_{z^2}:\ 2z^2 = x^2 + y^2 @@ a double cone at 54.7° to the z-axis; two lobes and a ring
2s:\ r = 2r_0 @@ one sphere
3s:\ 2\rho^2 - 18\rho + 27 = 0,\ \ \rho = \tfrac{9 \mp 3\sqrt3}{2} @@ two spheres, at 1.90 r₀ and 7.10 r₀; ρ = r/r₀
3p_z:\ z = 0\ \text{and}\ r = 6r_0 @@ one plane and one sphere: 1 + 1 = 3 − 1
\htmlClass{new}{p\ \text{of degree}\ \ell:\ |\varphi| \le C\,r^{\ell}\ \text{near}\ r = 0} @@ every term of p has degree ℓ

So only the s shapes are not 0 at the nucleus. Every other shape keeps its electron off the centre, and the more so as ℓ grows. The general count is stated from the cases shown; the standing questions build more of them.

38chem4Where the Cloud Is Thickest

|φ|² is largest at the centre for 1s. But the chance of finding the electron at distance r counts a whole thin shell, of area 4πr², and the shell is tiny near the centre.

Thickest-distance technique: for the shape p · e−r/(ℓ + 1)r₀ of level n = ℓ + 1, the chance per unit distance is largest at r = n² r₀.

P(r) = 4\pi r^2 \!\int |\varphi|^2\,d\Omega \;\propto\; r^{2\ell + 2}\,e^{-2r/(\ell + 1)r_0} @@ the shell at r; |p|² grows like r to the 2ℓ over the sphere
\tfrac{d}{dr}\ln P = \tfrac{2\ell + 2}{r} - \tfrac{2}{(\ell + 1)\,r_0} = 0 @@ the flat-top technique (14a)
r = (\ell + 1)^2\,r_0 @@ solve
\htmlClass{new}{r = n^2 r_0:\ \ r_0,\ 4r_0,\ 9r_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1s, 2p, 3d

These are the radii of the circles in 35·phys.8.4’s reroute, where the electron went round like a planet. The circles are not paths, but their sizes return as the distances where the clouds are thickest.

38chem5The Order of Filling

In hydrogen, 3s, 3p and 3d share one energy. In an atom with more electrons they do not. The inner electrons screen the nucleus (38·phys7), and a shape that keeps its electron off the centre, like rℓ, is screened more. So s sits below p, and p below d, inside each level; and the split can be larger than the gap between levels.

Potassium has 19 electrons. Eighteen fill 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p. The nineteenth goes, as measured, to 4s, not to 3d. No technique on this page predicts that. It is read off the measured table of the elements, and the reading has a short form.

n-plus-ℓ technique: atoms fill their shapes in order of n + ℓ, lowest first; where two have the same n + ℓ, the one with smaller n fills first.

1s\,(1)\ \ 2s\,(2)\ \ 2p\,(3)\ \ 3s\,(3)\ \ 3p\,(4)\ \ 4s\,(4) @@ n + ℓ in brackets
3d\,(5)\ \ 4p\,(5)\ \ 5s\,(5)\ \ 4d\,(6)\ \ 5p\,(6)\ \ 6s\,(6) @@ ties go to the smaller n
4f\,(7)\ \ 5d\,(7)\ \ 6p\,(7)\ \ 7s\,(7) @@ 
\text{potassium: }[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1 @@ 4s has n + ℓ = 4, 3d has 5: as measured
\text{chromium, by the rule: }[\text{Ar}]\,3d^4 4s^2 @@ n + ℓ order
\text{chromium, measured: }[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1 @@ the rule misses
\text{copper, by the rule: }[\text{Ar}]\,3d^9 4s^2 @@ n + ℓ order
\text{copper, measured: }[\text{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1 @@ the rule misses

Each row of the table opens on an s shape and closes on a p shape, and in this order the rows come out 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 long: the 2n² of 38·phys, each twice over after the first. The rule is a summary of measurements, good enough to place nearly every element, and it is wrong for chromium and copper. Where it is wrong, the only judge is the atom.

38chem6The Four Arms of Carbon

Carbon has 2s² 2p², yet methane’s four bonds are alike and 109.5° apart. Shapes of one level can be added: a sum of shapes of the same energy is again a shape of that energy. The four arms are four sums of 2s and the three 2p’s.

From here on, for a direction u of length 1, pu = uxpx + uypy + uzpz is the p shape facing along u. With each shape scaled to size 1, ⟨s, pu⟩ = 0 and ⟨pu, pv⟩ = u · v.

Four-arm technique: the shapes hu = ½(s + √3 pu) and hv = ½(s + √3 pv) have size 1, and are at right angles exactly when u · v = −1/3: four of them face the corners of a pyramid of four faces, 109.47° apart.

\langle h_u, h_v\rangle = \tfrac14\big(\langle s, s\rangle + 3\,\langle p_u, p_v\rangle\big) @@ the cross terms are 0: s is even, a p shape is odd
= \tfrac14\,(1 + 3\,u\cdot v) @@ the shapes are scaled to size 1 (31a)
\langle h_u, h_u\rangle = \tfrac14\,(1 + 3) = 1 @@ u · u = 1
\langle h_u, h_v\rangle = 0 \iff u\cdot v = -\tfrac13 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\cos\theta = -\tfrac13,\ \ \theta = 109.47^\circ} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-spread technique (27chem): four directions do it

27chem found 109.47° from the bonds alone, by spreading four directions evenly. Here the same angle comes from inside the atom: an s shape and three p shapes can be remixed into four alike, at right angles, only in those directions. Measured, methane’s angle is 109.5°. Water and ammonia, with the same four arms but some holding a pair of electrons that bonds to nothing, come out smaller: 104.5° and 107°.

The accepted names

A still polynomial is a homogeneous harmonic polynomial; on the sphere these are the spherical harmonics, and ℓ is the angular momentum (azimuthal) quantum number, n the principal quantum number. The still surfaces are nodes: ℓ angular nodes and n − ℓ − 1 radial nodes. The chance per unit distance is the radial distribution function, and r₀ the Bohr radius. That s shapes reach the nucleus and others do not is called penetration; the inner electrons’ cut of the pull is shielding. Filling from the bottom is the aufbau principle, and the n-plus-ℓ technique is the Madelung rule (also the Klechkowski rule). The four arms are sp³ hybrid orbitals, and the remixing is hybridization.

What was measured

The fillings of potassium, chromium and copper, and the angles of methane (109.5°), ammonia (107°) and water (104.5°), are measured values, each checked in two sources. Everything else on the page is computed from 35·phys’s statement.
