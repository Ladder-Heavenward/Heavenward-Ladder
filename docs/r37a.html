37a · proof

The push that travels

Clap your hands on a beach at Tofino and the person a hundred metres down the sand hears it about a third of a second later. Nothing came down the beach to them. The air near your hands was squeezed, it squeezed the air beside it, and that squeezed the air beside that; each bit of air moved a little and came back, and only the squeeze travelled. Rung 33 found the rule a string obeys, and this step finds that the air obeys the same rule, with the speed set by how stiff and how heavy the air is. A steady note is a turning of rung 25 and rung 32 travelling at that speed, and three numbers belong to it: how often it repeats, how far apart its crests are, and how fast it goes. Any two of them fix the third.

A sound is a change in pressure that travels. The air stays where it is and the change runs through it. How often it repeats times how far apart its crests are is how fast it runs.

37a1Sound is a change in pressure

Still air presses on everything it touches with a steady pressure, about 101 000 newtons on each square metre at sea level. 6·phys said a plucked string pushes the air in tiny swings of pressure, and that how often they repeat is the pitch. This step gives the swings their own name and their own function.

From here on, the sound pressure at the place x and time t is p(x, t), the pressure there minus the steady pressure. It is positive where the air is squeezed and negative where it is thinned. A sound is a sound pressure that is not 0 everywhere. A conversation at a metre has p swinging about 0.02 newtons a square metre either side of 0: two parts in ten million of the steady pressure.

So a sound is a function of place and time, as 33c1’s string height y(x, t) was. The difference is what moves. The string moved up and down across its length. The air moves back and forth along the way the sound goes, and what the ear meets is the pressure, not the motion.

Sound-pressure technique: a sound is a function p(x, t) whose value is how far the pressure is from still air, and two sounds at a place are the same exactly when their pressures agree at every moment. Proof: 9a3, same pairs, same function.

37a2The wave runs, the air stays

Take a pipe of air along the x-axis. Let u(x, t) be how far the air that rests at x has been pushed along the pipe. Where u changes along the pipe the air is squeezed or thinned, and the pressure follows.

Two laws held in trust, as 33c1 held its two. First, for small pushes, the sound pressure is p = −K u_x, where K is the air’s stiffness: how much pressure it takes to squeeze it by a given share. Second, mass times acceleration is force, and the force on a slice of the pipe is the drop in pressure across it. With ρ the air’s mass per cubic metre, the second law reads ρ u_tt = −p_x.

Air-wave technique: for small pushes, the air in a pipe obeys the wave equation u_tt = c² u_xx with c = √(K/ρ), and so does its sound pressure.

p = -K u_x @@ the stiffness law, held in trust
\rho\, u_{tt} = -p_x = K u_{xx} @@ the law of motion, then the slope of the first line
u_{tt} = \tfrac{K}{\rho}\, u_{xx} @@ divide by ρ
p_{tt} = -K\,(u_{tt})_x @@ the first line, slopes in time taken inside: their order does not matter when continuous (28d2)
= -K c^2 u_{xxx} = c^2 (-K u_x)_{xx} = c^2 p_{xx} @@ the line above, then the first line again
\htmlClass{new}{u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx},\ \ p_{tt} = c^2 p_{xx},\ \ c = \sqrt{K/\rho}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the string-rule technique’s equation, with air for string

So everything 33c2 proved about the string holds for the air in a pipe. A squeeze splits into two halves running opposite ways at speed c, and neither changes shape. The air at each place only swings about its rest, and the shape is what travels: the two-waves technique, read for air.

37a3How fast sound goes

The speed c = √(K/ρ) is set by the medium, not by the sound. For air the stiffness that counts is the one for a squeeze too quick for heat to leave, and it is K = γP₀, with γ = 1.4 for air (a measured law, held in trust; 37·phys says why). At 20 °C, with P₀ = 101 325 N/m² and ρ = 1.204 kg/m³, that gives c ≈ 343 m/s.

Water is far heavier than air but far stiffer still: K ≈ 2.2 × 10⁹ N/m² and ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³, so c ≈ 1480 m/s. Steel carries sound at about 5900 m/s. A sound does not choose its speed; it is told it by what it runs through.

Medium-speed technique: every sound in one still medium runs at that medium’s one speed c, whatever its shape, loudness or pitch. Proof: the air-wave technique gives one c for the medium, and the two-waves technique (33c2) moves every shape at it.

c = \sqrt{\gamma P_0 / \rho} @@ the air-wave technique with K = γP₀
= \sqrt{1.4 \times 101\,325 / 1.204} @@ air at 20 °C
\htmlClass{new}{c \approx 343 \text{ m/s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a clap crosses 100 m of beach in about 0.29 s

37a4Speed, frequency and wavelength

A steady note is a turning that travels: p(x, t) = p₀ cos(2πf(t − x/c)), with p₀ its size and f its frequency in hertz (6·phys). At each place it repeats f times a second, which is 24·phys’s frequency; at each moment it repeats along x, and the gap between crests is its wavelength, λ.

Wavelength technique: for a steady note in a medium of speed c, frequency times wavelength is the speed: f λ = c. Proof: the cosine repeats when its input grows by 2π (21b3). At a fixed time, that is when x grows by c/f; so λ = c/f.

\lambda = c / f @@ the wavelength technique
= 343 / 440 @@ A₄ in air
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda \approx 0.78 \text{ m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ in water, 1480/440 ≈ 3.4 m: the same note, a wavelength four times as long

The notes a person hears run from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz (37b), so their wavelengths in air run from about 17 m down to about 17 mm. That span, a thousand to one, is why a low note bends round a building and a high one casts a shadow behind it (37e).

The accepted names

A wave in which the medium moves along the way the wave goes is a longitudinal wave; a string’s wave, moving across, is transverse. The stiffness K is the bulk modulus. The squeeze too quick for heat to leave is adiabatic, and γ is the heat-capacity ratio. Sound pressure is measured in pascals, Pa, one newton a square metre.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton worked out the speed of sound in the Principia of 1687 and got about 298 m/s, too slow by a sixth, because he took the stiffness of air at a fixed temperature. Pierre-Simon Laplace found the fix in 1816: the squeeze is too quick for heat to leave, and the stiffness is γ times greater.

A proof that is not allowed

“A thunderclap is louder than a hand clap, so its squeeze pushes harder and runs faster. So the thunder arrives sooner than the flash suggests.” Not allowed: the medium-speed technique gives one c for every small sound in still air, whatever its size; the claim mistakes the push for the speed of the shape.
