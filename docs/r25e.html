25e · technique

Roots in Pairs

The proof steps of this rung found every root of every polynomial. Most polynomials you have met have real numbers in front, and for them the roots off the floor are never alone. Each one comes with its mirror image across the floor, because the flip keeps sums and products. Put a root and its mirror together and their two line factors multiply to a quadratic with real numbers in front. So every polynomial with real numbers in front is a product of real lines and real quadratics, and no more is ever needed. That finishes a job left open on rung seventeen, where a quotient could be split into simple pieces only when its bottom split into different straight factors. One warning comes with the new numbers. The old rule that the root of a product is the product of the roots holds for numbers that are not negative. Off the floor it breaks, because every number has two square roots and no choice between them keeps products.

A polynomial with real numbers in front has its roots off the floor in mirrored pairs. Each pair makes a real quadratic, so the polynomial splits into real lines and real quadratics, and every quotient of two such polynomials splits into pieces whose areas you can find. The root of a product is the product of the roots only for numbers that are not negative.

25e1Roots Come in Mirrored Pairs

From here on, a real polynomial is one with real numbers in front: p(z) = u₀ + u₁z + ⋯ + uₙzⁿ with every uₖ on the floor. Its inputs may be any complex numbers.

The standing question 25d.4.1 met one case: with 1 + 2i a root of a real polynomial, the flip technique forced 1 − 2i to be a root too. The same lines work for every root of every real polynomial.

Flipped-root technique: if p is a real polynomial and p(r) = 0, then p(r̄) = 0. The roots of a real polynomial that lie off the floor come in pairs, each the mirror image of the other.

\overline{u_k} = u_k \text{ for every } k @@ each uₖ is on the floor (25a3: z̄ = z exactly on the floor)
\overline{r^k} = \overline{r}^{\,k} @@ the flip of a product, k − 1 times (25a3); induction on k (4c)
\overline{p(r)} = \textstyle\sum \overline{u_k}\,\overline{r^k} = \sum u_k\,\overline{r}^{\,k} = p(\overline{r}) @@ the flip of a sum and of a product (25a3)
p(r) = 0 \Rightarrow p(\overline{r}) = \overline{0} = 0 @@ 0 is on the floor
\htmlClass{new}{p \text{ real},\ p(r) = 0 \Rightarrow p(\overline{r}) = 0} \quad \blacksquare
  
p(1) = 1 - 3 + 4 - 2 = 0 @@ a root on the floor
(1+i)^2 = 2i,\ \ (1+i)^3 = -2 + 2i @@ the pair product
p(1+i) = (-2+2i) - 6i + (4+4i) - 2 = 0 @@ a root off the floor
\htmlClass{new}{p(1-i) = \overline{p(1+i)} = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the flipped-root technique: no work at all

The technique needs the real numbers in front. z − i has the root i and not −i. A real polynomial of odd degree has its roots off the floor in pairs, an even number of them, so at least one root is left over on the floor. 12e proved that by in-between values; here it comes from counting.

25e2Real Lines and Real Quadratics

A root r = s + ti off the floor and its flip make the line factors z − r and z − r̄. Neither has real numbers in front. Their product does.

Real-pieces technique: every real polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 is its top coefficient times a product of real lines x − c and real quadratics x² + bx + c with b² − 4c < 0. No piece of higher degree is ever needed.

r = s + ti,\ t \ne 0,\ p(r) = 0 @@ a root off the floor (if every root is on the floor, the root technique, 25d4, gives real lines and we stop)
(z - r)(z - \overline{r}) = z^2 - (r + \overline{r})z + r\overline{r} = z^2 - 2s\,z + (s^2 + t^2) @@ share out; r + r̄ = 2s and r r̄ = |r|² (25a3)
b = -2s,\ c = s^2 + t^2:\ \ b^2 - 4c = -4t^2 < 0 @@ real; and (x + b/2)² = (b² − 4c)/4 < 0 has no real answer (8f)
p(x) = (x^2 + bx + c)\,q(x) + (ux + v),\ \ q, u, v \text{ real} @@ polynomial division (9g), on the floor alone
0 = p(r) = ur + v = (us + v) + ut\,i @@ r is a root of p and of the quadratic
u = 0,\ v = 0 @@ equal pairs have equal parts; t ≠ 0
p = (x^2 + bx + c)\,q,\ \ q \text{ real, of degree } n - 2 @@ no remainder
\htmlClass{new}{p = u_n \textstyle\prod (x - c_j) \prod (x^2 + b_k x + c_k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ repeat on q; a root on the floor gives a real line (9g); induction on the degree (4c)
  
x^4 = -4:\ x = 1 + i,\ 1 - i,\ -1 + i,\ -1 - i @@ equal parts of a turn (21·cb20.3), size √2, turns of an eighth plus quarters
(x - (1+i))(x - (1-i)) = x^2 - 2x + 2 @@ the first flipped pair
(x - (-1+i))(x - (-1-i)) = x^2 + 2x + 2 @@ the second
\htmlClass{new}{x^4 + 4 = (x^2 - 2x + 2)(x^2 + 2x + 2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: share out, the x³ and x terms cancel

Repeated roots pair up too. If 1 + i is a root twice, the quotient q in the proof is real and still has 1 + i as a root, so it also has 1 − i. A repeated root off the floor and its flip are repeated the same number of times.

25e3Partial Fractions, Finished

17k split a quotient of polynomials only when its bottom split into different straight factors on the floor. It left two cases open. A bottom such as x² + 1 has no straight factor on the floor at all (17k4, the last illegal line). A bottom such as (x − 1)² repeats a factor (17k4, the second line). 17k3 told the story of 1702, when the claim that x⁴ + c⁴ has no real quadratic factors seemed to call for a new kind of function. The real-pieces technique settles the first case for every bottom, and with it the 1702 question: real lines and real quadratics are always enough.

Paired-fractions technique: let q be a real polynomial with top coefficient 1 and n different roots in ℂ, and p a real polynomial of degree less than n. Then p/q is a sum of pieces A/(x − c), one for each root c on the floor, and (Bx + C)/(x² + bx + c), one for each flipped pair, with every A, B and C real.

\tfrac{p(z)}{q(z)} = \textstyle\sum_j \tfrac{A_j}{z - r_j},\ \ A_j = \tfrac{p(r_j)}{q_j(r_j)} @@ straight-factors (17k1): its proof uses only field facts and that a polynomial of degree below n with n roots is 0, which hold in ℂ (25d4)
q_j(r_j) = \textstyle\prod_{k \ne j} (r_j - r_k) @@ q_j is q with the factor z − r_j left out
\overline{q_j(r_j)} = \textstyle\prod_{k \ne j} (\overline{r_j} - \overline{r_k}) @@ the flip of a product; the roots other than r_j, flipped, are the roots other than r̄_j (25e1)
\text{the } A \text{ at } \overline{r} \text{ is } \overline{A} @@ p(r̄) is the flip of p(r) (25e1); A · q_j(r_j) = p(r_j), flipped (25a3)
\tfrac{A}{x - r} + \tfrac{\overline{A}}{x - \overline{r}} = \tfrac{(A + \overline{A})x - (A\overline{r} + \overline{A}r)}{x^2 + bx + c} @@ one bottom; (x − r)(x − r̄) is real (25e2)
\htmlClass{new}{B = A + \overline{A},\ \ C = -(A\overline{r} + \overline{A\overline{r}})\ \text{ are real}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each is a number plus its flip, twice a floor part (25a3); and A = Ā when r is on the floor

Then every piece has an area function on the floor. Write x² + bx + c = (x + b/2)² + k², with k = √(c − b²/4) > 0, and split the top as Bx + C = (B/2)(2x + b) + (C − Bb/2). The first part has its bottom’s slope on top, so its area is a log (17i). The second is a stretched and slid 1/(1 + x²), so its area is an arctan (21m, 16d):

∫ (Bx + C)/(x² + bx + c) dx = (B/2) ln(x² + bx + c) + ((C − Bb/2)/k) arctan((x + b/2)/k) + const.

x^4 - 1:\ \text{roots } 1,\ -1,\ i,\ -i @@ x⁴ = 1: equal parts of a turn (21·cb20.3)
A_1 = \tfrac{4}{(1+1)(1+i)(1-i)} = 1,\ \ A_{-1} = \tfrac{4}{(-2)(-1-i)(-1+i)} = -1 @@ cover-up (17k1)
A_i = \tfrac{4}{(i-1)(i+1)(2j)} = \tfrac{4}{-4i} = i,\ \ A_{-i} = -i @@ cover-up; the flip, for free
\tfrac{i}{x - i} - \tfrac{i}{x + i} = \tfrac{i\,(2j)}{x^2 + 1} = -\tfrac{2}{x^2 + 1} @@ the flipped pair, joined
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int \tfrac{4\,dx}{x^4 - 1} = \ln\Big|\tfrac{x - 1}{x + 1}\Big| - 2\arctan x + C} \quad \blacksquare @@ 17k2 for the lines; arctan′ x = 1/(1 + x²) (21m); on an interval holding neither 1 nor −1

The second open case, a repeated factor, needs pieces with powers on the bottom: A/(x − c)², and (Bx + C)/(x² + bx + c)² for a repeated pair. The shape is the same, one piece for each power up to the repeat. The first standing question builds one such split and proves it exists.

25e4Square Roots off the Floor

On the floor, √u names the one root that is not negative, and 8i proved √u √v = √(uv) for u, v ≥ 0. Off the floor there is no “not negative” to choose by.

Root-choice technique: every w ≠ 0 in ℂ has exactly two square roots, s and −s; and no choice of one root for each w makes √z √w = √(zw) hold for all z and w. What always holds is √z √w = ±√(zw).

s^2 = w \text{ for some } s @@ equal parts of a turn (21·cb20.3), with n = 2
z^2 = w \iff (z - s)(z + s) = 0 \iff z = s \text{ or } z = -s @@ share out; a product is 0 only if a factor is (5e, in the field ℂ)
\text{suppose a choice with } \sqrt{z}\sqrt{w} = \sqrt{zw} \text{ always} @@ for contradiction
\sqrt{1} = \sqrt{1}\sqrt{1} \Rightarrow \sqrt{1} = 1 @@ √1 ≠ 0; divide by it
\sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1 @@ the rule, with z = w = −1
\sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1} = -1 @@ √−1 is a square root of −1
\htmlClass{new}{1 = -1:\ \text{no choice keeps products}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and (√z √w)² = zw, so √z √w is one of the two roots of zw

So off the floor, √ is a choice, and every rule about it must name its choice. The usual one takes √(−u) = i√u for u > 0. With it, √(−4)√9 = 2i · 3 = 6i = √(−36), but √(−4)√(−9) = 2i · 3i = −6, while √36 = 6. A root is safe in an answer. It is not safe inside a product rule unless both numbers are on the floor and not negative.

The accepted names

The flipped-root technique is the complex conjugate root theorem. The real-pieces technique says every real polynomial factors over ℝ into linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors. The paired-fractions technique is partial fraction decomposition over ℝ, and its pieces are the partial fractions. The choice √(−u) = i√u is the principal square root; a choice of one root for each number is a branch.

Who said it first

Gottfried Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli set out partial fractions in 1702, and Leibniz claimed that x⁴ + a⁴ has no real quadratic factors; Nicolaus I Bernoulli refuted him in 1719. Leonhard Euler stated in 1742, in a letter to Nicolaus I Bernoulli, that every real polynomial is a product of real factors of degree one and two, and gave a proof in 1749. The textbook in 25e.4.5 is Euler’s Vollständige Anleitung zur Algebra (1770), whose rule for multiplying roots of negatives was written for roots of positives.
