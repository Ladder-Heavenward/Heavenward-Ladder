What Flows in a Wire, and What Pushes It?

What flows in a wire, and what pushes it? People use electrons at three levels. At the electrical level a circuit moves energy: a battery lifts charge, a wire carries it, a kettle turns it into heat. At the electronic level, parts control the flow: one-way valves, and switches worked by other currents. At the logic level the flow is read as true or false, and circuits compute, as 4·comp showed. This page is the electrical level, done properly. It says what charge, current, energy and voltage are, each with its unit. It gives the proportion law, and how resistances combine on one path and on side-by-side paths. It gives the two circuit laws, which say nothing is lost and turn every circuit into equations to solve. And it gives power. It ends with a switch as a statement that is true or false, and with where the ladder takes each level.

Current is charge per second; voltage is energy per charge; a resistor makes them proportional. Nothing piles up at a junction, and around any loop the energy per charge comes back to where it started. Solve the equations; multiply current by voltage for power.

7phys1Charge, and Current as Charge Per Second

Matter carries electric charge, of two kinds. Two things with the same kind push each other apart; two with different kinds pull together. The kinds were named positive and negative, plus and minus, in 1747. The charge that moves in a metal wire belongs to the electron, a particle far smaller than an atom, found in every atom (5·chem's word), and found in 1897. An electron's charge is negative.

From here on, the coulomb, written C, is the unit of charge. Since 2019 one electron's charge is fixed, by agreement, at exactly 1.602176634/10¹⁹ C, so 1 C is the charge of about 6.24 · 10¹⁸ electrons.

A current is charge on the move: how much charge passes a point each second.

Here a point of a circuit is a place on it, the electricians' plain word; 5h's decimal point keeps its meaning.

From here on, the ampere, written A, is the unit of current: 1 A = 1 C/s, one coulomb per second (5j's per). A milliampere, mA, is 1/1000 A (5j's milli).

A phone charger delivers 1 to 3 A; a kettle draws about 12 A; the light on a power strip, a milliampere or less. The man who named plus and minus thought one electric fluid flowed from plus to minus, and engineers kept his arrow. Conventional current runs from the + end of a battery, round the circuit, to its − end. The electrons in a wire go the other way. Nothing breaks, because electrons moving left carry the same charge per second as positive charge moving right. It is a choice of which way counts forward, like 5d's choice of sign. Every equation on this page uses that old arrow.

The electrons themselves crawl. In a copper lamp cord carrying 1 A, they drift at about a tenth of a millimetre per second, some thirty-odd centimetres in an hour. The lamp still lights at once, because the wire is already full of electrons. It is like a hose already full of water: push at one end and water leaves the other immediately, though no drop has gone far.

7phys2Energy, and Voltage as Energy Per Charge

Energy is what it takes to make something happen: to lift a thing, warm it, set it moving, make it glow. It is never made or destroyed, only moved from place to place and changed from one form into another.

From here on, the joule, written J, is the unit of energy: 1 J = 1 kg · m²/s² (5j's unit arithmetic). Lifting a thing of mass m by a height h near the ground takes m · (9.8 m/s²) · h. So lifting a 100 g apple by 1 m takes 0.1 kg · 9.8 m/s² · 1 m = 0.98 J, about a joule.

In 1800 a letter to the Royal Society described a pile of zinc and copper discs separated by card or cloth soaked in salt water. It gave a steady current for the first time. The pile was the first battery. What a battery does is lift charge. Each coulomb that arrives at its − end leaves its + end carrying extra energy. It spends that energy on the way round the circuit, as heat, light or motion, and arrives back with none to spare.

From here on, the volt, written V, is the unit of voltage: 1 V = 1 J/C. The voltage between two points is the energy each coulomb gives up, or gains, in going from one to the other.

A 9 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it 9 J. Think of voltage as height. The battery is a pump lifting water up a hill; the circuit is the channel it runs down; each part along the way is a stretch where it loses height. Height is measured from a level you choose; in a circuit you call the battery's − end 0 V and measure every other point from it.

From here on, on this page, as is usual, slanted V, I, R and P are quantities in 5j's sense (a rational followed by a unit). V is a voltage, I a current, R a resistance, P a power. The upright V, A, Ω, W, J and C are unit marks. Low marks tell the parts apart: R₁, R₂, I₁. Here I is not 0b's self-sameness axiom, and P is not a statement.

7phys3Resistance, and the Proportion Law

In 5j's words: for a given wire, the voltage across it is directly proportional to the current through it. Double the push, double the flow. So V = kI with k fixed for that wire, and k is called its resistance. This is the proportion law. A resistor is a part made to have a fixed resistance. The proportion law's accepted name is Ohm's law.

From here on, the ohm, written Ω, is the unit of resistance: 1 Ω = 1 V/A. Ω is the Greek letter omega in its capital form. 3a·f used it as a name for a set; on this page it is only this unit mark.

V = \htmlClass{new}{I\,R} @@ the proportion law: 5j's direct proportion, with R its constant
I = \tfrac{V}{R} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers (5g): multiply both sides by R⁻¹, R ≠ 0
R = \tfrac{V}{I} @@ the same, by I⁻¹, when I ≠ 0
\Omega \cdot \text{A} = \tfrac{\text{V}}{\text{A}} \cdot \text{A} = \text{V} @@ the units check (5j): the right side is a voltage

Here is a circuit you can build from parts in a drawer. It has a 9 V battery, a red LED (a light-emitting diode, a small light that lets current through one way only), and a resistor to keep the current safe.

9\ \text{V} - 2\ \text{V} = 7\ \text{V} @@ a red LED takes about 2 V across it; the rest must drop across the resistor
I = 20\ \text{mA} = 0.02\ \text{A} @@ the current the LED is made for
R = \tfrac{7\ \text{V}}{0.02\ \text{A}} = \htmlClass{new}{350\ \Omega} @@ the proportion law, solved for R
R = 390\ \Omega:\ \ I = \tfrac{7}{390}\ \text{A} \approx 0.018\ \text{A} @@ resistors come in stock sizes; take the next one up: about 18 mA, safely under

The first move to check lives here. The proportion law is not a law of nature, like the two laws below; it is a fact about some materials. An LED is not a resistor: below about 1.8 V it passes almost nothing, and a little above 2 V it passes as much as you let it. Dividing 9 V by “the LED's resistance” gives nonsense. That is why it needs a real resistor beside it to set the current. A lamp's filament fails the law more gently. Its resistance rises many times over as it heats from cold to white, which is why lamps tend to fail at the moment they are switched on.

7phys4One Path, and Side-By-Side Paths

From here on, on this page, two parts are in series when they sit one after the other on a single path with no branch between. They are in parallel when they sit side by side, joined at both ends, so that the path splits between them and joins again. These are the electricians' words, and here they name only these two arrangements.

In series, the same current goes through both parts, because charge has nowhere else to go. Each coulomb gives up energy in the first and then in the second, so the drops add up to the battery's voltage.

V = V_1 + V_2 @@ the drops share out the battery's lift
V = I R_1 + I R_2 @@ the proportion law on each part, with the same I
V = I\,(\htmlClass{new}{R_1 + R_2}) @@ spread on ℚ turned about (5i's factoring): the pair acts as one resistor

And each drop is its share in proportion: V₁ = V · R₁/(R₁ + R₂). Put 12 V across 4 Ω and 8 Ω in series. That is 12 Ω in all, 1 A, and drops of 4 V and 8 V. It is a voltage divider, and it is how a volume knob turns a turn of the hand into a change of voltage.

In parallel, both parts have the same voltage across them, since their ends are joined: the same two heights. The current splits, the larger share taking the easier path, and the shares add up to the whole.

I = I_1 + I_2 = \tfrac{V}{R_1} + \tfrac{V}{R_2} @@ the proportion law on each branch, the same V across both
\tfrac{I}{V} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{R_1} + \tfrac{1}{R_2}} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by V⁻¹: for the pair, the reciprocal of its resistance is the sum of the reciprocals (5c)
\tfrac{1}{R_1} + \tfrac{1}{R_2} = \tfrac{R_2 + R_1}{R_1 R_2} @@ adding over one bottom (5h)
R = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}} @@ reciprocals of both sides (5c's reciprocal, 5h's reciprocal-of-a-product): product over sum

6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel: 18/9 = 2 Ω, less than either. That is always so: a second path can only make it easier for current to get through. Three tempting mistakes live here. Adding the resistances, 6 + 3 = 9, treats them as in series. Adding the reciprocals and stopping, 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, gives the reciprocal of the answer, not the answer. And product over sum is for two resistors only. For three, add three reciprocals and take the reciprocal: 2, 3 and 6 Ω in parallel give 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1, so 1 Ω. Every circuit in a house is wired in parallel. That is why switching off the lamp does not switch off the fridge. Each has the full voltage across it, and the currents add at the panel, until a breaker trips.

7phys5The Two Circuit Laws: Nothing Is Lost

The junction law: at any meeting point of wires, the current flowing in equals the current flowing out. Charge is never made or destroyed, and in a steady circuit it cannot pile up at a point. The loop law: along any loop, a path through the circuit that comes back to where it started, the rises and drops of voltage add to 0. Walk over the hill and back to your door and you are at the same height. These are the two circuit laws. One path and side-by-side paths were these laws in disguise: in series is the loop law, in parallel is the junction law. Here is a circuit that is both: a 12 V battery, a 4 Ω resistor, then 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel.

\tfrac{6 \cdot 3}{6 + 3} = 2\ \Omega @@ the parallel pair, product over sum
4 + 2 = \htmlClass{new}{6\ \Omega};\ \ I = \tfrac{12}{6} = 2\ \text{A} @@ in series with the 4 Ω; the proportion law on the whole
2 \cdot 4 = 8\ \text{V},\ \ 2 \cdot 2 = 4\ \text{V},\ \ 8 + 4 = 12 @@ the drops; the loop law checks
\tfrac{4}{6} = \tfrac{2}{3}\ \text{A},\ \ \tfrac{4}{3}\ \text{A},\ \ \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{4}{3} = \htmlClass{keep}{2}\ \text{A} @@ each branch has 4 V across it; the junction law checks

Some circuits are neither, and two batteries feeding one load is the standard case. Then name the unknown currents, write one equation per loop by the loop law, and solve the two together, by 5g's substitution and elimination. Left: a 9 V battery and 3 Ω. Right: a 6 V battery and 3 Ω. Shared middle: 1 Ω. Let I₁ be the current up through the left battery and I₂ up through the right; by the junction law, I₁ + I₂ goes down the middle.

9 = 3I_1 + 1\,(I_1 + I_2) @@ left loop: the battery's lift equals the drops
6 = 3I_2 + 1\,(I_1 + I_2) @@ right loop, the same way
9 = 4I_1 + I_2,\ \ 6 = I_1 + 4I_2 @@ collect like terms (5i)
3 = 3I_1 - 3I_2 @@ add-a-sameness (5g): the second taken from the first
I_1 = I_2 + 1 @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by 1/3, then adding-keeps-answers
6 = (I_2 + 1) + 4I_2 @@ substitution (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{I_2 = 1\ \text{A},\ \ I_1 = 2\ \text{A}},\ \ I_1 + I_2 = 3\ \text{A} @@ solve; check the left loop: 3 · 2 + 1 · 3 = 9

Now make the left battery 12 V, the side resistors 1 Ω each and the middle 4 Ω, with 6 V on the right. The same steps give I₁ = 4 A and I₂ = −2 A. A negative current is not an error: it says the arrow you guessed points the wrong way. Two amperes are being pushed down through the 6 V battery, against its own lift. It is being charged, as a car's battery is when the engine runs. You never need to guess directions right. Guess, solve, and let the signs tell you. Running backwards is as mechanical. Knowing I₁ = 2 A and I₂ = 1 A in the first circuit, the right loop says the right battery is 3 · 1 + 1 · 3 = 6 V.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, again

1a's ledger has more than one column. 5·chem closed the stuff column atom by atom, and 6·chem closed charge alongside it in a reaction. The junction law is the charge column closed again, at every joint of a wire, every second: charge in equals charge out. The loop law is the push column. The lift a battery gives each coulomb is spent, exactly, on the way round.

You never prove the event happened; you show the books balance.

When the books gave −2 A, the ledger did not break. The guess about direction did. The books close whichever way you guessed, and they tell you which guess was wrong.

7phys6Power: Current Times Voltage

From here on, the watt, written W, is the unit of power: 1 W = 1 J/s. Power is energy moved per second. A kilowatt-hour, kWh, is the energy of 1000 W for an hour: 1000 W · 3600 s = 3 600 000 J.

From here on, power is also said of energy moved per second, as the physicists say it. 4g's power and 2g's power set keep their meanings. The quantity letter P below says which.

P = I\,V @@ (C/s) · (J/C) = J/s: the coulombs cancel (5j's unit chain)
P = I\,(\htmlClass{new}{I R}) = I^2 R @@ substitution, with V = IR
P = \tfrac{V}{R}\,V = \tfrac{V^2}{R} @@ or with I = V/R

A 1500 W kettle on a 120 V outlet draws I = 1500/120 = 12.5 A. Its element has resistance 120²/1500 = 9.6 Ω. (Element is the electricians' word for the part that heats; 5·chem's element, a substance of one kind of atom, keeps its meaning.) Run it four minutes, 240 s, and it uses 1500 · 240 = 360 000 J, which is 0.1 kWh. (The current in a house changes direction 60 times a second, 6·phys's 60 Hz. 120 V is the steady voltage that would warm the kettle at the same rate; the cellar says more.) The LED circuit: the resistor turns 7 V · 7/390 A ≈ 0.13 W into heat, so an ordinary quarter-watt resistor is fine. The LED takes 2 V · 7/390 A ≈ 0.036 W. The tempting mistake is to put the battery's voltage into V²/R for one resistor among several. The V in each formula is the voltage across that part.

7phys7A Switch Is a Statement

A switch is closed, and current can pass, or open, and it cannot. “The switch is closed” is a statement. A switch is built so that the statement is decidable, in 7c's word, at every moment it is read. 4·comp made switches and gates into logic. Two switches in series pass current only if both are closed, an and; in parallel, if either is, an or. 4·comp's point was that these are not analogies but samenesses: a network of switches computes a table.

Now the electrical facts. An ideal switch, open, carries no current, and the whole voltage of the battery appears across its gap, since its two sides are at the top and foot of the hill. Closed, it carries the full current with no voltage across it. Split on “the switch is closed” (7e's split, and here a decidable one). Open, I = 0 and P = I · V = 0; closed, V = 0 and P = 0. Either way an ideal switch controls the flow and spends no energy. That is why switching is the way to compute.

Real voltages pass through every value in between as a switch goes from one state to the other. So engineers forbid the middle on purpose. Take the classic 5 V logic chips of the 1960s and 1970s. An input below 0.8 V is read as false, one above 2.0 V as true, and a circuit is designed never to be read while it sits between. Rung 7 proved the either-way principle for every statement. Electronics builds it into the hardware, by declaring a strip of voltages where no reading is made and staying out of it.

7phys8Three Levels, and Where the Ladder Takes Each

electrical · moving energy

Current, voltage, resistance, power; the proportion law and the two circuit laws. This page.

electronic · controlling the flow

Parts whose current is not proportional to their voltage. One is the diode, a one-way valve, of which the LED is one that glows. Another is the transistor, a switch opened and shut by another, smaller current. They are why the LED needs its resistor.

logic · the flow as true or false

Switches as statements, gates, the adder: 4·comp. Much higher up, the gates are built from transistors, and the three levels meet.

Why a changing voltage has a steady stand-in

A house voltage swings up and down 60 times a second. The 120 V on the outlet is the steady voltage that would warm the kettle at the same rate. Saying exactly what “the same rate” means for something that never stays still needs an average over a whole swing, which a later rung builds. Nothing on this page leans on more than the number 120.

Who said it first

Plus and minus, and the arrow of conventional current, are Benjamin Franklin’s (1747). The electron was found by J. J. Thomson (1897). The pile is Alessandro Volta’s, described in his letter of 1800 to Joseph Banks. The proportion law is Georg Ohm’s (1827), and the junction and loop laws are Gustav Kirchhoff’s (1845). That a network of switches computes a table is Claude Shannon’s point.

Franklin, describing two people who had rubbed glass tubes and given each other sparks, said that one could be called electrified positively and the other negatively, or rather plus and minus, and that the electric fire passes from the one with more to the one with less.

Franklin, letter to Peter Collinson, 25 May 1747, printed in Experiments and Observations on Electricity (1751)

Ohm, a schoolteacher in Cologne, measured currents through wires of different lengths and treated the flow of electricity along a wire as Fourier had treated the flow of heat along a bar. He concluded that the current is directly proportional to the driving force and inversely proportional to the wire's resistance, which grows in proportion to its length.

Ohm, Die galvanische Kette, mathematisch bearbeitet (Berlin, 1827)

Kirchhoff, then a student of twenty-one in Königsberg, stated two rules from which the currents in any network of wires can be found: at a meeting of wires the currents arriving and leaving balance, and around any closed path the driving forces balance the drops.

Kirchhoff, Annalen der Physik und Chemie 64 (1845)

Accepted names

The proportion law is Ohm’s law. The junction law and the loop law are Kirchhoff’s current law and Kirchhoff’s voltage law. The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
