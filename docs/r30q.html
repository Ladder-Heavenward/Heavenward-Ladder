Estimating From Samples

Nobody weighs every salmon in a river or asks every voter in a state. A few are measured, and the rest are guessed at. The guess is the average of the sample, and the honest question is how far it may be from the average of the whole. This step answers it in three moves. The average of a sample wanders less than one measurement does, and by a known amount: halving its wander takes four times as many measurements. The spread of the whole can itself be estimated from the sample, with one small correction that keeps the estimate honest on average. And the bell curve turns the wander into an interval that catches the true average with a stated chance. When the sample is small and the spread unknown, the bell is replaced by a wider curve with a table of its own, and this step says which parts of that are proved and which are held in trust.

The average of a sample has the same average as one measurement, and its spread width is the measurement’s divided by the root of the sample size. The sample’s own spread, with one less than the count in the bottom, has the true spread as its average. The bell turns the average and its width into an interval that covers the true average with a stated chance; for small samples a wider table takes the bell’s place.

30q1The Average of a Sample, and Its Width

A sample of size n is n chance variables X₁, …, Xₙ, independent, each with the same chance law as one measurement X. Write μ = E X for the true average and σ² = Var X for the true spread. The sample average is X̄ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ)/n. It is itself a chance variable: another sample gives another X̄.

Sample-average technique: E X̄ = μ and Var X̄ = σ²/n, so the spread width of X̄ is σ/√n.

E\bar X = \tfrac1n\,(EX_1 + \cdots + EX_n) = \tfrac1n\,n\mu @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
E\bar X = \mu @@ the sample average is right on average
\mathrm{Var}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) = n\sigma^2 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3), as in the root-n technique (30n1)
\mathrm{Var}(cY) = c^2\,\mathrm{Var}\,Y @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2)
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{Var}\,\bar X = \tfrac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n},\ \ \text{width } \tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ c = 1/n
  
\sigma = 0.05\ \text{s} @@ one timing of the swing of 28p’s pendulum, by hand with a stopwatch
\text{width of the average of } 16 \text{ timings} = 0.05/4 = 0.0125\ \text{s} @@ the sample-average technique
\text{the total of } 16 \text{ timings has width } 0.05 \cdot 4 = 0.2\ \text{s, not } 16 \cdot 0.05 = 0.8\ \text{s} @@ independent spreads add as squares; 28p’s worst case adds the widths
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four times the timings halve the width}} \quad \blacksquare @@ √(4n) = 2√n

28p ended with doubts that fall at random and partly cancel. Here is how much they cancel: n independent doubts of width σ add to a doubt of width σ√n, not nσ. The worst-case bound of 28p is never wrong; this one is the usual size.

30q2The Spread of a Sample

σ is rarely known. The sample has its own spread. 8·stat met two versions of it, one with n in the bottom and one with n − 1, and left the choice to the question. Now there is a reason for n − 1.

Sample-spread technique: let S² = ((X₁ − X̄)² + ⋯ + (Xₙ − X̄)²)/(n − 1), for n ≥ 2. Then E S² = σ².

\textstyle\sum_i (X_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2 + n(\bar X - \mu)^2 @@ the squared-misses technique (8·stat), with c = μ
E\textstyle\sum_i (X_i - \mu)^2 = n\sigma^2 @@ average-adds (30g1); each term has average σ²
E\big[n(\bar X - \mu)^2\big] = n\,\mathrm{Var}\,\bar X = \sigma^2 @@ the sample-average technique (30q1)
E\textstyle\sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2 = n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n - 1)\sigma^2 @@ average-adds on the first line
\htmlClass{new}{E S^2 = \sigma^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by n − 1

With n in the bottom the average would be (n − 1)σ²/n, a little short every time. The misses are measured from X̄, which sits closer to the sample than μ does, by the squared-misses technique; dividing by n − 1 puts back exactly what that costs.

5.1,\ 6.3,\ 4.8,\ 5.9,\ 6.6,\ 5.4,\ 4.9,\ 6.0 @@ eight readings of water clarity in metres: how deep a white disc stays in sight
\bar x = 45.0/8 = 5.625 @@ the sample average
\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = 3.155 @@ the misses from 5.625, squared and added
\htmlClass{new}{s^2 = 3.155/7 \approx 0.4507,\ \ s \approx 0.671} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sample-spread technique; with 8 in the bottom, 0.394

30q3An Interval That Covers

X̄ will miss μ. The bell says by how much, and how often. Write Zₙ = (X̄ − μ)/(σ/√n), the sample average measured from μ in widths. The central limit technique (30j3) says the chance that Zₙ lands in an interval heads, as n grows, to the standard bell’s area over it. That area between −1.96 and 1.96 is 0.950, by the bell-series technique (30m3).

Bell-cover technique: the interval from X̄ − 1.96σ/√n to X̄ + 1.96σ/√n covers μ with a chance that heads to 0.95 as n grows.

\mu \text{ is in } \bar X \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n \iff |\bar X - \mu| \le 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n @@ the same event, read from either end
\iff |Z_n| \le 1.96 @@ divide by σ/√n, which is above 0
P(|Z_n| \le 1.96) \to \text{the bell’s area from } -1.96 \text{ to } 1.96 @@ the central limit technique (30j3)
\htmlClass{new}{P(\mu \in \bar X \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n) \to 0.950} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell-series technique (30m3), to three places

The chance belongs to the interval, before the sample is drawn. Once the numbers are in, the interval either covers μ or it does not. What 95% describes is the method: used again and again on fresh samples, it covers μ about 95 times in 100. Its accepted name is in the cellar.

For a share, X is 1 for yes and 0 for no, μ is the true share p, and σ² = p(1 − p). The interval uses p̂(1 − p̂) in place of the unknown p(1 − p). That the swap changes nothing in the limit is held in trust: its proof waits much higher up the ladder.

n = 1000,\ \ \hat p = 540/1000 = 0.54 @@ a sample of voters asked one yes-or-no question
\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n} = \sqrt{0.54 \cdot 0.46/1000} \approx 0.01576 @@ the sample-average technique, with p̂ for p
1.96 \times 0.01576 \approx 0.031 @@ the half-width
\htmlClass{new}{0.509 \text{ to } 0.571,\ \text{at } 95\%} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell-cover technique: “54% give or take 3 points”

30q4Small Samples: A Wider Table

With σ unknown, replace it by the sample’s S. For a large sample S is close to σ, and the bell-cover technique still holds. For eight readings of a lake it is not close enough: S wanders too, and the ratio wanders more than the bell allows.

Held in trust: if every Xᵢ is bell-shaped, then T = (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n) has a chance law that depends only on n − 1, and not on μ or σ. Its accepted name is the t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. Its proof waits much higher up the ladder. What it gives: a table. For each n − 1 the table gives the t with P(|T| ≤ t) = 0.95.

n - 1 = 1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 7,\ 9,\ 19,\ 29,\ 99 @@ degrees
t = 12.706,\ 4.303,\ 2.776,\ 2.365,\ 2.262,\ 2.093,\ 2.045,\ 1.984 @@ the 95% values, held in trust with the law
S^2 \to \sigma^2 \text{ in chance as } n \text{ grows} @@ the averages-settle technique (30h2), run on the squares
\htmlClass{new}{t \to 1.960,\ \text{the bell’s value}} @@ for large n, S may stand in for σ

Small-sample-cover technique: if every Xᵢ is bell-shaped, the interval X̄ ± t S/√n, with t from the table for n − 1, covers μ with chance 0.95, for every n ≥ 2. The proof is the bell-cover proof with T for Zₙ, and the trusted law in place of the limit: so the 0.95 here is exact, not a limit.

\bar x = 5.625,\ \ s \approx 0.6714,\ \ n = 8 @@ 30q2
s/\sqrt 8 \approx 0.2374 @@ the estimated width of the average
2.365 \times 0.2374 \approx 0.561 @@ t for 7 degrees
\htmlClass{new}{5.06 \text{ to } 6.19\ \text{m, at } 95\%} \quad \blacksquare @@ the small-sample-cover technique; 1.96 would give 5.16 to 6.09, too narrow

The accepted names

The sample average X̄ is the sample mean, an estimator of the population mean μ; its spread width σ/√n is the standard error of the mean. An estimator whose average is the thing it estimates is unbiased: the sample-average technique says X̄ is unbiased, and the sample-spread technique says S², the sample variance, is. Dividing by n − 1 in place of n is Bessel’s correction. The interval of the bell-cover technique is a 95% confidence interval, and 95% is its confidence level; 1.96 is the critical value of the standard normal. The law held in trust is Student’s t distribution, and the small-sample-cover interval is the t interval. The swap of p̂ for p in the poll’s interval is justified by Slutsky’s theorem; the interval is the Wald interval. “Give or take 3 points” is a poll’s margin of error.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss and Pierre-Simon Laplace worked out the error of an average around 1810. Friedrich Bessel used n − 1 in astronomy in the early nineteenth century. William Sealy Gosset, a brewer at Guinness in Dublin who measured barley and yeast in small samples, published the t distribution in 1908 under the name Student, because the brewery did not let its staff publish. Ronald Fisher proved its form in 1925 and gave it the letter t. Jerzy Neyman defined confidence intervals in 1937.
