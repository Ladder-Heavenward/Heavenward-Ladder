27·∎ · conclusion

Arrows add and stretch, and have dimension

Anything that adds and stretches by the rules of arrows is a space of arrows, and every basis of it has the same size. Straight maps are grids; the determinant says whether a grid can be undone; the arrows a grid only stretches straighten it out; and every grid obeys its own polynomial.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 27 asked what else adds and stretches like arrows, and whether such a space has a size of its own. Every space of arrows that a finite list builds has a dimension, and every straight map between lists of numbers is a grid whose determinant says whether it can be undone. The price is choice: a basis is chosen, and the grid of a map depends on the basis.

27a laid out the rules and found them kept far from the plane. 27b traded arrows to prove every basis the same size. 27c weighed what a map loses against what it reaches. 27d turned maps into grids and doing into multiplying. 27e built the determinant from three rules. 27f found the arrows a grid only stretches, and 27g fed a grid its own polynomial. 27h measured arrows with readings. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

If a list of m arrows builds a space V, every free list in V has at most m arrows; so every basis of V has the same number n of arrows, the dimension of V.

If L is straight from V, of dimension n, the dimension of what L loses plus the dimension of what it reaches is n. From lists of n numbers to lists of m, L is x ↦ Ax for exactly one grid A, and doing B then A is AB.

Exactly one D on n × n grids is straight in each column, flat, and 1 on I. It obeys det(AB) = det A · det B, and A can be undone exactly when det A ≠ 0.

λ is a stretch of A exactly when det(A − λI) = 0. With n free stretched arrows, A = PDP⁻¹. And every A obeys its own polynomial: if p(λ) = det(A − λI), then p(A) = 0.

The proof

Each part below is a technique already proved on a step of this rung. The proof cites them by name and joins them.

v_1, \dots, v_k \text{ not free} \Rightarrow \text{some } v_j \text{ can be dropped} @@ the spare-arrow technique (27b)
u_1, \dots, u_k \text{ free},\ w_1, \dots, w_m \text{ build } V \Rightarrow k \le m @@ the exchange technique (27b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every basis of } V \text{ has } n \text{ arrows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same-size technique (27b)
  
\text{what } L \text{ loses and what it reaches are spaces} @@ the nothing-sent technique (27c)
\htmlClass{new}{\dim(\text{lost}) + \dim(\text{reached}) = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lost-plus-reached technique (27c)
  
L(x) = Ax,\ \ \text{column } j \text{ of } A = L(e_j) @@ the columns technique (27d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{doing } B \text{ then } A \text{ is } AB} \quad \blacksquare @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d)
  
\text{straight, flat, unit} \Rightarrow D(A) = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}\sigma \; a_{\sigma(1)1} \cdots a_{\sigma(n)n} @@ the three-rules technique (27e)
\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B @@ the det-product technique (27e)
\htmlClass{new}{A \text{ can be undone} \iff \det A \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nonzero-det technique (27e)
  
Av = \lambda v \text{ for some } v \ne 0 \iff \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 @@ the stretch-test technique (27f)
\text{different stretches} \Rightarrow \text{free arrows} @@ the different-stretches technique (27f)
\htmlClass{new}{A = PDP^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the straighten-out technique (27f)
  
S\,(A - \lambda I) = \det(A - \lambda I)\, I @@ the stand-in technique (27g)
\htmlClass{new}{p(A) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the own-polynomial technique (27g)

So the rung’s claim holds: anything that adds and stretches by the rules of arrows has a dimension no choice can change, and the maps between such spaces are grids that one number and one polynomial describe.
