42·logi2 · logistics

Which Truck Should Visit Which Stop?

Which truck should visit which stop? Every morning a parcel depot sends out vans, each with a few hundred stops, and every mile saved on every van, every day, adds up to millions. The question is how to split the stops among vans and order each van’s stops. Even one van’s order has more choices than can be tried, and no quick method is known that always finds the best. So routers build a good plan by a simple rule and then improve it. This page counts the orders, tries nearest-first, builds routes by the savings of joining stops, fits them to vans that fill up, and untangles crossings with 42’s graphs and the triangle inequality.

For one van, the round trips through n stops number n!/2. Join stops i and j into one trip when the saving d(0, i) + d(0, j) − d(i, j) is largest, while the van has room. Then improve: two links of a route that cross can always be swapped for two that do not, and shorter, by the triangle inequality.

42logi21Many Stops, One Van

Terry drives one van from the depot to n stops and back. Every order of stops is a round trip through 42a’s dots.

\text{orders of } n \text{ stops: } n! @@ the counting principle (4·stat)
\text{the same trip backwards: halve} @@ and the depot fixes where it starts
n = 15:\ 15!/2 \approx 6.5 \times 10^{11} @@ trying each takes days
\htmlClass{new}{n = 30:\ 30!/2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{32}} @@ beyond every computer

From here on, on this page, a route is one van’s round trip from the depot; d(i, j) is the distance from stop i to stop j, with the depot as stop 0.

42logi22Nearest First

\text{from where you are, go to the nearest stop not yet visited} @@ 42m’s greedy choice
\text{quick: } n^2 \text{ looks} @@ fine for thousands of stops
\text{the last stops are left scattered, far apart} @@ the rule paints itself into a corner
\htmlClass{new}{\text{typically about } 25\% \text{ longer than the best}} @@ on random stops

Nearest first is a fine start but a poor finish. It never looks ahead, so it often ends with a long run home.

42logi23The Savings of Joining Two Stops

Start with one round trip per stop, then join trips. The depot D sits at (0, 0), and stops A at (4, 0), B at (4, 3), C at (0, 3), in miles.

s(i,j) = d(0,i) + d(0,j) - d(i,j) @@ two trips home and out, against one link
s(A,B) = 4 + 5 - 3 = 6,\ s(B,C) = 4,\ s(A,C) = 2 @@ largest first
24 \xrightarrow{\ A{-}B\ } 18 \xrightarrow{\ B{-}C\ } 14 @@ three trips, then two, then one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{D, A, B, C, D: } 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 14} @@ the whole square, here the best

Savings technique: join the two route ends whose saving, out and home again against one direct link, is largest, as long as the joined route still fits a van. The joins are tried in order of saving, each once.

42logi24Vans That Fill Up

Each stop takes one cage of parcels, and a van holds two.

\text{join A and B: one van, full} @@ saving 6, total 18
\text{join B and C: would need 3 cages} @@ refused
\text{join A and C: A’s van is full} @@ refused
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two vans: D, A, B, D and D, C, D; } 12 + 6 = 18} @@ room limits the joins

With room limits the savings rule still works: it simply refuses joins that overfill a van, so it splits the stops among vans and orders them at once. Time windows, when a stop must be served between 9 and 11, refuse joins the same way.

42logi25Untangling Crossings

A route drawn on a map sometimes crosses itself. Say the links P to Q and R to S cross at a point X.

d(P,R) \le d(P,X) + d(X,R) @@ the triangle inequality
d(Q,S) \le d(Q,X) + d(X,S) @@ again
d(P,R) + d(Q,S) \le d(P,Q) + d(R,S) @@ X lies on both links
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reverse the stretch from } Q \text{ to } R:\ \text{shorter, no crossing}} @@ strictly, unless the four lie in a line

Two crossing links of a route can always be swapped for two that do not cross, and the route gets no longer. Repeating such swaps until none helps brings nearest-first routes to within a few per cent of the best on typical maps, which is how delivery software is built.

42logi26Moves to Watch Out For

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

\text{try every order of } 30 \text{ stops on a fast computer}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> 30!/2 ≈ 10³², beyond every computer (42logi21)
s(i,j) = d(i,j) - d(0,i) - d(0,j)\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-a">out of arrangement</span> the saving is the two depot legs minus the link: d(0, i) + d(0, j) − d(i, j) (42logi23)
\text{join the largest saving even if the van overfills}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> joins that overfill a van are refused (42logi24)
\text{order the stops first, then split them among vans}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-s">out of sequence</span> the savings rule splits and orders at once (42logi24)
\text{a crossing can be left, it costs nothing}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> swapping it never lengthens the route (42logi25)

Accepted names

One van’s round trip is the travelling salesman problem; many vans with room limits the vehicle routing problem (with time windows, VRPTW). The joins of 42logi23 are the Clarke-Wright savings algorithm, nearest first the nearest neighbour heuristic, and swapping two links 2-opt. No quick exact method is known: the problem is NP-hard.

Who said it first

George Dantzig and John Ramser posed the vehicle routing problem in 1959, for petrol trucks. Geoffrey Clarke and John Wright gave the savings method in 1964, and G. A. Croes the swap of two links in 1958.
