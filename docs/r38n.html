38n · technique

Natural Deduction by Hand

A column is one way to write a proof. A tree is another. Put the foot at the bottom, and above each line put the lines it leans on, so the givens and suppositions are the leaves at the top and every move is a bar with its inputs above and its output below. A supposition that a move drops is marked with a number, and the move that drops it carries the same number. Trees show at a glance which lines lean on which, and they make one thing easy to see that columns hide: a detour, where a mark is made and used at once. Cutting detours out leaves a proof that never makes a mark only to take it apart again. Then the step looks hard at two moves that have always been allowed. Prove an if may drop a supposition the proof never used, and from the totally false anything may be written. Both make sense of the tables of rung one. Both have been argued against for as long as there have been tables, and the trees show exactly where each one enters.

A tree has the foot at the root, the givens and suppositions at the leaves, and one bar for each move. A mark made and used at once is a detour, and can be cut out. Dropping an unused supposition, and writing anything from the totally false, are where the if of the tables and its critics part.

38n1Proofs as Trees

A tree writes a proof upside down from a column. Each move is a bar: the lines it leans on above, the line it writes below, the move named at its side. The foot is at the bottom. The leaves are givens and suppositions. A supposition dropped by a move is written in square brackets with a number, and the move carries the same number.

Tree-column technique: every column of 38b’s calculus can be drawn as a tree with the same foot and the same givens, and every tree can be written as a column. Read the column from the foot up, putting above each line the lines it leans on; read the tree from the leaves down, writing each bar’s output after its inputs.

\dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{[P \land Q]^1}{P} \quad P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R)}{Q \Rightarrow R} \qquad \dfrac{[P \land Q]^1}{Q}}{\dfrac{R}{(P \land Q) \Rightarrow R}\ 1} @@ keep left, carry through; keep right; carry through; prove an if, dropping 1
P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R) @@ the column: given
\qquad \text{assume } P \land Q;\ \ P;\ \ Q \Rightarrow R;\ \ Q;\ \ R @@ supposed; keep left; carry through; keep right; carry through
\htmlClass{new}{(P \land Q) \Rightarrow R} \quad \blacksquare @@ prove an if: the supposition leaves

The supposition P ∧ Q appears twice in the tree, once for each use. In the column it is written once and cited twice. The tree pays for its clarity in copies.

38n2Every and Some in a Tree

The fresh-name conditions of 38b2 are checked at the bar: when speak of everything writes ∀x φ(x) from φ(y), look up every branch above it for a leaf still open in which y is free. When use a some drops φ(y), look for y anywhere outside the bracketed leaves.

Swapped-order technique: if something stands in R to everything, then everything has something standing in R to it. In marks: ∃x ∀y R(x, y) ⊢ ∀y ∃x R(x, y). It is 2b’s licensed-swap technique, here a tree.

\dfrac{\exists x\,\forall y\,R(x, y) \qquad \dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{[\forall y\,R(u, y)]^1}{R(u, v)}}{\exists x\,R(x, v)}}{\forall y\,\exists x\,R(x, y)}}{\forall y\,\exists x\,R(x, y)}\ 1 @@ use an every with v; show an example with u; speak of everything on v; use a some, dropping 1
v \text{ is free in no open leaf above the speak-of-everything bar} @@ the only open leaf is [∀y R(u, y)], with no v
\htmlClass{new}{u \text{ is free in neither the foot nor } \exists x\,\forall y\,R(x, y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the use-a-some condition

The other way, from ∀y ∃x R(x, y) to ∃x ∀y R(x, y), fails at the speak-of-everything bar: the example found for v depends on v, so the name it gets is free in an open leaf. 38c.5.1 built the world that shows no other tree can do it.

38n3Detours

A detour is a bar that makes a mark, followed at once by a bar that uses the same mark: join and then keep left; prove an if and then carry it through; show an example and then use a some.

Detour-cut technique: a detour can be cut out of a tree, and the tree that is left reaches the same foot from the same givens.

\dfrac{\dfrac{\dfrac{[P]^1\ \vdots}{Q}}{P \Rightarrow Q}\,1 \qquad \dfrac{\vdots}{P}}{Q} @@ prove an if, then at once carry it through on a tree for P
\text{put the tree for } P \text{ in the place of every leaf } [P]^1 @@ each leaf was waiting for a P, and now has one
\text{the fresh-name conditions still hold} @@ rename any placeholder of the inserted tree that would be caught (38b2)
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\vdots\ \dfrac{\vdots}{P}\ \vdots}{Q}\text{: the same foot, no if made or used}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for join, add an option, show an example

Cutting one detour can make others, where the inserted tree meets the bars below it. Cutting them all does stop, and leaves a tree in which every formula is a part of the foot or of a given. That fact is proved much higher up the ladder. Its proof is a falling count of the kind 38·cb29 uses.

38n4Where Relevance and the If Enter

Two moves let a tree reach a foot that has nothing to do with its leaves. Prove an if may drop a supposition that no bar above it used. And everything permitted writes any formula at all from ⊥.

\dfrac{Q}{P \Rightarrow Q}\ 1 \quad \text{(no leaf } [P]^1\text{)} @@ the free-if technique (3a): the supposition was never used
\dfrac{\dfrac{P \quad \neg P}{\bot}}{R} @@ clash, then everything permitted: R may be anything
\dfrac{\dfrac{P}{P \lor R} \qquad \neg P}{R} @@ add an option, then the remaining option (1c): R again, with no ⊥ written
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the third tree reaches the second’s foot with no } \bot \text{ written}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remaining option, laid down by 1c as a move of its own, writes no ⊥; this calculus derives it through cases and everything permitted

1e’s table for ⇒ is true in every row where P is false. The first tree is that row, as a move. A logic that refuses it, and refuses everything permitted, must also refuse one of add an option, the remaining option, or chaining two proofs together: the third tree uses only those. Such logics exist, and are worked on. Their accepted name is relevance logic. The calculus of this rung keeps both moves, and so keeps 1e’s tables and the true-everywhere technique of 38c.

The accepted names

Trees are Gentzen-style natural deduction, or Prawitz-style proof trees; brackets with a number mark a discharged assumption. The tree-column technique says that Fitch-style and Gentzen-style presentations prove the same things. A detour is a maximal formula, or a cut; the detour-cut technique is a reduction step, and the fact held for later is the normalization theorem, with its corollary, the subformula property. That making and using moves undo each other is harmony. Dropping an unused supposition is vacuous discharge; the free-if facts are the paradoxes of material implication; everything permitted is ex falso quodlibet. The third tree of 38n4 is C. I. Lewis’s proof. Relevance (or relevant) logic refuses such moves.

Who said it first

Gerhard Gentzen drew proofs as trees in 1934 and proved the subformula property for his sequent calculus; Dag Prawitz proved normalization for natural deduction in 1965. Clarence Irving Lewis gave the argument of 38n4 in Symbolic Logic (1932), with C. H. Langford, against those who would refuse ex falso. Alan Ross Anderson and Nuel Belnap built relevance logic in Entailment (1975).

A proof that is not allowed

“From ∀y ∃x R(x, y): use an every with v, giving ∃x R(x, v). Use a some with u, supposing R(u, v). Speak of everything on v: ∀y R(u, y). Show an example: ∃x ∀y R(x, y).” Not allowed: at speak of everything, the leaf R(u, v) is still open and v is free in it. In the world of 38c.5.1 the foot is false and the given is true.
