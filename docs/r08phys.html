8·phys · physics

How Big and How Small Do Things Get?

How big and how small do things get? A proton is about two millionths of a millionth of a millimetre across. The part of the universe whose light has reached us is about nine hundred million million million million metres across. Between them lie some forty-one factors of ten, and everything you have ever seen sits somewhere on that ladder. Physicists move along it with three tools. Powers of ten, now with negative ones, let huge and tiny sizes multiply in your head. Significant figures keep you from claiming more than you measured. And chain estimates let you know the size of an answer before you know how to get it. All three are the arithmetic of the line, aimed at the world.

Write every quantity as a number from 1 up to 10, times a power of ten; then multiplying is adding exponents, and an estimate is a chain of rough factors whose exponents add.

Counting the sand

An old Greek book on counting sand, addressed to a king of Syracuse, opens by noting a quarrel. Some people think the number of grains of sand is endless. Others think it is finite but larger than any number that could be named. The author sets out to name a larger one.

To do it he invented a way of naming numbers in tiers of a myriad myriad (a myriad was ten thousand, so a myriad myriad is 10⁸). He estimated the size of a grain of sand, and of the universe as the astronomers of his day pictured it, and multiplied. His answer, in the notation below, was that fewer than 10⁶³ grains would fill it. The universe is far larger than he thought, but his method is the method of this page. A big number is a modest number times a power of ten, and powers of ten are easy to multiply.

Scientific notation, with powers below zero

5·chem wrote 6.022 · 10²³ for the count of particles in a mole. Its power of ten had a number in the high place. 8i put negative integers in the high place: 10⁻³ is (10³)⁻¹, a thousandth, and the whole-exponent techniques keep every exponent rule. So:

From here on, scientific notation allows any integer power of ten. A quantity is written u · 10ⁿ. Here u is a decimal from 1 up to but not including 10, and n is an integer. n is called the order of magnitude: the rung of the ladder; u says where on that rung.

Here “rung” is said of sizes: a power of ten on a ladder of sizes. The rungs of this ladder of pages keep their name, and “rung 18” still names a page.

The speed of light is 299 792 458 m/s exactly, since 5j's metre is defined as the length light covers in 1/299 792 458 of a second. Rounded, it is 3.00 · 10⁸ m/s. A hydrogen atom is about 1.1 · 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Why does every positive quantity have exactly one such form? Rung 8. One-scientific-form technique: every positive real x is u · 10ⁿ for exactly one integer n and one real u with 1 ≤ u < 10.

Multiplying and dividing then split into two easy jobs: the u's by hand, the powers by adding or taking away exponents (whole-exponent techniques). Then tidy, so that u is back between 1 and 10.

(3 \cdot 10^{8}\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}})(2 \cdot 10^{-3}\ \text{s}) @@ how far light goes in 2 milliseconds
= (3 \cdot 2)(10^{8} \cdot 10^{-3})\ \text{m} @@ regroup for times on ℝ, turn-about for times on ℝ; s cancels against per s (5j)

\tfrac{8 \cdot 10^{4}}{2 \cdot 10^{-2}} = 4 \cdot 10^{4 - (-2)} = \htmlClass{new}{4 \cdot 10^{6}} @@ dividing takes the exponent away; taking away a negative adds
(5 \cdot 10^{3})^2 = 25 \cdot 10^{6} = \htmlClass{new}{2.5 \cdot 10^{7}} @@ whole-exponent techniques; tidy: 25 = 2.5 · 10

From here on, μ (the Greek letter mu, in its small form) is used only as a front part of a unit, as below. The front parts mega (M), giga (G) and tera (T) mean 10⁶, 10⁹ and 10¹² of. Micro (μ), nano (n), pico (p) and femto (f) mean 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁹, 10⁻¹² and 10⁻¹⁵ of. With 5j's kilo, centi and milli they name the rungs, mostly three at a time.

Like 5j's kilo, centi and milli, these front parts are upright parts of unit marks. The M, G, T, n, p and f here are not letters naming things, and T is not 1e's table mark.

So a hair 70 μm wide is 7 · 10⁻⁵ m, and a proton's 1.7 fm is 1.7 · 10⁻¹⁵ m. Converting is multiplying by one (5j): 70 μm · (10⁻⁶ m / 1 μm) = 7 · 10⁻⁵ m.

The ladder from proton to the edge of what can be seen

Lengths in metres, each a measured value rounded to one or two figures. Length is 5j's: how long a thing is, end to end. “Across” means the length straight through its middle, the longest way for a long thing like a bacterium. A few things need words first. A proton is one of the particles packed into the tiny central core of every atom (5·chem). A hydrogen atom's core is a single proton, with one electron (7·phys) about it. DNA is the long molecule that carries a living thing's inherited instructions, the genes of 5·bio. A cell is the smallest piece of a living thing that is alive on its own. A bacterium such as E. coli, common in the human gut, is a whole living thing of one cell. A red blood cell carries oxygen in your blood. A virus is a packet of inherited instructions in a coat, which can copy itself only inside a living cell. A light-year is a length, not a time: how far light goes in a year of 365.25 days, 299 792 458 · 31 557 600 m ≈ 9.46 · 10¹⁵ m. The Milky Way is our galaxy, the great swarm of stars the Sun belongs to. The observable universe is the part whose light has had time to reach us.

Ratios are where the ladder pays. How many hydrogen atoms fit across a hair? (7 · 10⁻⁵)/(1.1 · 10⁻¹⁰) ≈ 6 · 10⁵, more than half a million. How many Earths, side by side, reach the Moon? (3.84 · 10⁸)/(1.27 · 10⁷) ≈ 30. A person is about 10¹⁵ protons tall, and the observable universe is about 5 · 10²⁶ people across. Each of these is one subtraction of exponents and one division of u's.

Significant figures: say only what you measured

From here on, the significant figures of a written measurement are its digits from the first that is not 0 to the last written, the last included even if it is 0. The last written digit says “measured to here”. 0.00520 has three: 5, 2 and the final 0.

From here on, 5h's rounding and its mark ≈ apply to any positive real x. The number k with k/10^m ≤ x < (k + 1)/10^m exists, by counting-passes-any-real and least-member-for-any-set as in one-scientific-form. The rest of 5h's line is unchanged. Rounding to s significant figures is rounding the u of x's scientific form to s − 1 places, and x ≈ u · 10ⁿ then says that.

A cart is timed rolling at a steady 2.4 m/s for 3.15 s. Multiply: 7.56 m. But “2.4 m/s” means a speed that rounds to 2.4, somewhere from 2.35 up to 2.45. And “3.15 s” means a time from 3.145 up to 3.155. Bound the product with 8g's rules for positives (multiply the low ends together, and the high ends):

2.35 \le v \lt 2.45,\ \ 3.145 \le t \lt 3.155 @@ what the two measurements say (5h's rounding, run backwards)
2.35 \cdot 3.145 \le vt \lt 2.45 \cdot 3.155 @@ keep-order for times on ℚ twice and at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ (8g): the low ends with the low, the high with the high
7.39 \le vt \lt 7.73 @@ the length could be anywhere in here (ends rounded outward)
vt \approx \htmlClass{new}{7.6\ \text{m}} @@ two figures: the second is already uncertain, a third would be noise

That gives the rules of thumb, which are habits, not techniques. Multiplying or dividing: keep as many significant figures as the least precise factor (2.4 has two). Adding or taking away: keep the decimal places of the least precise term. 12.3 + 0.456 = 12.756 ≈ 12.8, since the first is known only to tenths. Scientific notation makes the figures plain: 3000 m is unclear, 3.0 · 10³ m says two figures. Why figures for products and places for sums? In a product the errors grow as parts of the whole. A part in 48 of v and a part in 630 of t give about a part in 45 of vt, and the factor with the biggest part wins. The number of figures is a rough measure of that part.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, measured

8·cb5 gave √2's decimals a width, because no decimal can hold √2. A measurement has a width for a different reason: the instrument. “2.4 m/s” is a speed somewhere from 2.35 to 2.45. The product 7.6 m carries the widths of both its factors, and the length is somewhere from 7.39 to 7.73 m.

Be rigorous about the width, not the dot.

7.56 is a dot inside that stretch, and nothing measured says the dot. The last written digit is where the width begins. Writing more digits does not make a measurement sharper; it only hides how wide it is.

A stopwatch shows hundredths of a second, and a thumb starts it. A runner's 100 m time reads 11.27 s. How many of those figures did the stopwatch measure, and how many did the thumb?

Chain estimates

On 16 July 1945, at the first test of a nuclear bomb, in New Mexico, one of the physicists who had built it stood at the base camp about 16 km away. He dropped scraps of paper as the blast arrived, the push of air racing out from the explosion. They were carried about 2.5 m. From that, in his head, he estimated the energy released at about ten thousand tons of TNT. Later measurements put it at about twice that.

From here on, a kiloton of TNT, written kt, is the unit of energy 4.184 · 10¹² J, fixed by convention: about what a thousand tonnes of the explosive TNT releases (a tonne is 1000 kg).

So his figure was about 10 kt, 4 · 10¹³ J, and the measured one about 2 · 10¹ kt. Off by a factor of 2, from a handful of paper: that is a chain estimate. He was famous for asking students questions with no obvious way in. The classic, traditionally credited to his Chicago years, is: how many piano tuners are there in Chicago? You do not know. But you can guess, within a factor of a few, a chain of things whose product is the answer. A chain estimate has the accepted name Fermi estimate.

2.7 \cdot 10^{6}\ \text{people} @@ Chicago, 2020 census: 2 746 388
\cdot \tfrac{1\ \text{household}}{2\ \text{people}} \cdot \tfrac{1\ \text{piano}}{10\ \text{households}} = 1.35 \cdot 10^{5}\ \text{pianos} @@ guesses, as unit fractions (5j)
\cdot \tfrac{1\ \text{tuning}}{\text{piano} \cdot \text{year}} = 1.35 \cdot 10^{5}\ \tfrac{\text{tunings}}{\text{year}} @@ each tuned about once a year
\text{one tuner: } 4\ \tfrac{\text{tunings}}{\text{day}} \cdot 250\ \tfrac{\text{days}}{\text{year}} = 10^{3}\ \tfrac{\text{tunings}}{\text{year}} @@ two hours each with travel; a working year
\tfrac{1.35 \cdot 10^{5}}{10^{3}} \approx \htmlClass{new}{1 \cdot 10^{2}\ \text{tuners}} @@ take away exponents; one figure is all the guesses earn

Is it right? The method's claim is modest: the answer is a hundred or two, not ten and not ten thousand. And that claim is usually true, for a reason the ladder makes plain. Each guess is off by some factor, up or down, and the factors multiply. So on the exponent scale the errors add. Some are too high and some too low, and they partly cancel. Two more, to calibrate your hand. Heartbeats in a life: 70 a minute, for 80 years, is 70 · 60 · 24 · 365.25 · 80 ≈ 2.9 · 10⁹. Atoms in your body: 70 kg is 7 · 10⁴ g. Counted by number, your atoms are mostly hydrogen, then oxygen and carbon, for an average of about 6.5 g per mole of atoms (5·chem). So there are about (7 · 10⁴ / 6.5) · 6.0 · 10²³ ≈ 6 · 10²⁷ atoms. And a handy fact: a year is 31 557 600 s ≈ 3.16 · 10⁷ s, while 10^7.5 = √10 · 10⁷ ≈ 3.16 · 10⁷ too (8i's fraction-exponent techniques). A year sits halfway up its rung in exponents.

Who said it first

The book on counting sand is Archimedes’ Sand-Reckoner, addressed to King Gelon of Syracuse.

Archimedes opens his Sand-Reckoner, addressed to King Gelon of Syracuse, by noting that some people think the number of grains of sand is endless, and others that it is finite but larger than any number that could be named. He sets out to name a larger one.

Archimedes, The Sand-Reckoner, opening, after Heath's translation in The Works of Archimedes (1897)

The physicist with the scraps of paper at the 1945 test was Enrico Fermi, and the piano-tuner question is traditionally his. Accepted names: a chain estimate is a Fermi estimate, or a Fermi problem. The count of particles in a mole, 6.022 · 10²³, is Avogadro’s number, as 5·chem said.
