How Do the Stretches of a Random Grid Spread?

How do the stretches of a random grid spread? Fill a square grid with coin tosses, the same number on each side of the diagonal, so that rung 31 promises it real stretches along axes at right angles. Each grid has its own stretches, and nothing about one grid can be foreseen. Yet as the grid grows, their spread can. Shrunk by the square root of the size, the stretches crowd into a half disc, densest in the middle and thinning to nothing at two edges, and the same half disc appears whatever coin is tossed, so long as it is fair and its spread is one. The proof counts walks on the grid that come home, and the walks that matter trace trees; the trees are counted by a mirror trick, and the half disc has exactly those counts as its averages. Then the finer question: how far apart are neighbouring stretches? Two stretches of a grid push each other away, and a grid of two rows already shows why: a small gap needs two independent things to be small at once. For large grids the pattern of the gaps is again the same for every coin, which was proved around 2010 by two groups with two different methods. The same pattern turned up where nobody had put a grid: in 1972 the gaps between the zeros of rung 43’s ζ were found to follow the pattern of grids with turning numbers in them, and computations far up the line agree to many decimal places. And the largest stretch, pushed against the edge of the half disc, wobbles by a pattern found in 1994 that also governs the longest rising run in a shuffled pack of cards. Why the zeros of ζ should behave like the stretches of a grid nobody knows; no grid behind them has been found.

A symmetric grid A with independent entries above the diagonal, average 0 and spread 1: tr Aᵏ = Σ λᵢᵏ counts closed walks, and only walks using every link at least twice survive the average. Of length 2m, the ones that matter trace trees with m links, and there are Cₘ = (2m choose m)/(m + 1) of them, by mirroring the first dip. The half disc s(x) = √(4 − x²)/(2π) has moments Cₘ, from the constant term of (z + z⁻¹)²ᵐ. So the stretches over √n spread as s. For two rows, the gap g has P(g ≤ r) = 1 − e^(−r²/4): small gaps are rare as r². For large grids the gap pattern is one pattern for every coin (2009 to 2011), the zeros of ζ follow its turning-number form (1973, 1987), and the edge pattern of 1994 governs the longest rising run of a shuffle (1999).

inf53.1A Grid of Coin Tosses

Let A be an n by n grid with 0 down the diagonal, and above it independent entries xᵢⱼ, each +1 or −1 by a fair coin; below it the same entries again, xⱼᵢ = xᵢⱼ. The grid is symmetric, so by 31d’s real-stretches technique its stretches λ₁, …, λₙ are real, and by 31d3’s spectral technique it only stretches some right-angled basis. A new grid gives new stretches. The question is not where they are for one grid but how they spread.

Trace-of-powers technique: for a symmetric grid A and every whole k ≥ 1, the sum of the diagonal entries of Aᵏ is λ₁ᵏ + ⋯ + λₙᵏ. Write tr M for the sum down the diagonal of M.

A e_i = \lambda_i e_i \text{ for a right-angled basis } e_1, \ldots, e_n @@ the spectral technique (31d3)
A^k e_i = \lambda_i^k e_i @@ apply A k times
\text{the stretches of } A^k \text{ are } \lambda_1^k, \ldots, \lambda_n^k @@ n free vectors, each only stretched
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{tr} A^k = \lambda_1^k + \cdots + \lambda_n^k} \quad \blacksquare @@ stretches add to the middle sum (27l)
  
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},\ \ \lambda = 2, -1, -1 @@ (1, 1, 1) is doubled; vectors adding to 0 are reversed
\operatorname{tr} A^2 = 6 = 4 + 1 + 1 @@ A² has 2 down its diagonal
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{tr} A^4 = 18 = 16 + 1 + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ A⁴ has 6 down its diagonal

All eight coin grids of size 3 have tr A⁴ = 18; the script that checked it also checked tr A² = 6 for each. In general tr A² is the sum of the squares of all the entries, n(n − 1) for every coin grid, so the average of λᵢ² is n − 1: a stretch is typically about √n. Shrink by that. The k-th moment of a grid is mₖ = (1/n) Σ (λᵢ/√n)ᵏ = tr Aᵏ / n^(1 + k/2), the average k-th power of the shrunk stretches. For coin grids m₂ = (n − 1)/n, which heads to 1. The whole page is about where the others head.

inf53.2Walks That Come Home

Read A as the links of 42a’s complete graph on n dots, the link between i and j carrying the weight xᵢⱼ. The (i, i) entry of Aᵏ is a sum over walks of k steps from i back to i of the product of the weights met on the way, by the technique for multiplying grids. So tr Aᵏ is the sum over all closed walks of k steps of their products. Now let the entries be any independent chance variables above the diagonal with average 0, spread 1, and every average E|x|ᵏ finite; the coin is one of them.

Twice-used technique: the average of tr Aᵏ is the sum, over closed walks of k steps that use each of their links at least twice, of the averages of their products; for the coin each such product is +1 if every link is used an even number of times and 0 otherwise. A link used once contributes a factor independent of the rest, with average 0, so its whole product averages 0 (30g3). For the coin, x² = 1.

Tree-walk technique: a closed walk of 2m steps that uses each of its links at least twice visits at most m + 1 dots, and visits exactly m + 1 only when its links form a tree with m links, each walked exactly twice, once out and once back.

2m \text{ steps, each link at least twice} \Rightarrow \text{at most } m \text{ links} @@ count the steps link by link
\text{the dots and links walked are in one piece} @@ it is one walk
\text{dots} \le \text{links} + 1 @@ a spanning tree inside it (42b3) has dots − 1 links (42b1)
\text{dots} = m + 1 \Rightarrow \text{one piece with } m = \text{dots} - 1 \text{ links} @@ so it has exactly m links, each used exactly twice
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the links form a tree}} \quad \blacksquare @@ any two of three (42b2); a tree link is crossed once each way

The moments follow. Each tree walk has a shape, its order of going out and coming back, and its dots can be named in n(n − 1)⋯(n − m) ways. Every other surviving walk of 2m steps has at most m dots, and the number of shapes of walk of a fixed length does not grow with n, so all of them together are at most a fixed number times nᵐ. Divided by n^(1 + m), they fade. A walk of odd length 2m + 1 has at most m links and m + 1 dots, and n^(m + 1)/n^(m + 3/2) fades too. So the average of m₂ₘ heads to the number of tree shapes with m links, and the odd moments to 0.

i \to j \to i \to l \to i,\ \ l \ne j\text{: two links, a tree} @@ two shapes of tree walk: this one, and i → j → l → j → i
\text{shapes} \times n(n-1)(n-2) = 2n(n-1)(n-2) @@ the tree walks
i \to j \to i \to j \to i\text{: one link, four times} @@ n(n − 1) more, only two dots
\operatorname{E}\operatorname{tr} A^4 = n(n-1)(2n-3) @@ n = 3: 18, as in inf53.1
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E} m_4 = 1.53,\ 1.9503,\ 1.995 \text{ for } n = 10,\ 100,\ 1000} \quad \blacksquare @@ heading to 2, the two shapes

inf53.3Counting the Trees

Walk a tree from its root, always taking the leftmost link not yet walked, and write U for a step out to a new dot and D for a step back. The list has m of each, and read from the left it never has more Ds than Us: one cannot come back further than one went. Every such list draws exactly one tree, with a root and an order among the links at each dot, so the tree shapes of inf53.2 are counted by these lists. For m = 2 there are two: UUDD, a path hanging from the root, and UDUD, a root with two links. 42b4 counted nⁿ⁻² trees on named dots; here the dots are not named but the links at each dot are ordered, and the count is another.

Mirror-the-dip technique: the lists of m Us and m Ds that never have more Ds than Us so far number Cₘ = (2m choose m) − (2m choose m + 1) = (2m choose m)/(m + 1).

\text{all lists of } m \text{ Us and } m \text{ Ds} @@ (2m choose m) of them
\text{a bad list first has one more D than U at some place } p @@ the first dip
\text{swap U and D in every step after } p @@ the mirror
\text{the new list has } m - 1 \text{ Us and } m + 1 \text{ Ds} @@ after p the old list had one more U than D; now one more D
\text{every list with } m + 1 \text{ Ds dips somewhere; mirroring after its first dip undoes the swap} @@ a matching, both ways
\htmlClass{new}{C_m = \tbinom{2m}{m} - \tbinom{2m}{m+1} = \tfrac{1}{m+1}\tbinom{2m}{m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (2m choose m + 1) = (2m choose m) · m/(m + 1)
  
\tbinom{6}{3} = 20,\ \ \tbinom{6}{4} = 15 @@ all lists, bad lists
\htmlClass{new}{C_3 = 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ UUUDDD, UUDUDD, UUDDUD, UDUUDD, UDUDUD

The run 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 is C₀ to C₆; a script listing the good lists one by one agrees up to m = 5. So the average moments of a grid head to 0, 1, 0, 2, 0, 5, 0, 14, …, the same for every coin with average 0 and spread 1: nothing of the coin but its spread survived the count, as nothing but the spread survived 30j3’s central limit technique.

The trees counted here are ∞Ε.1’s bracketings: the 2m-th moment of the half disc is B(m + 1) of that step, and both obey the last-split technique, since a walk that comes home splits at its first return. The plan is the split; which entries of the grid were drawn, and in what order, are let go, and the half disc is what is left.

inf53.4The Half Disc

Which spread has moments 0, 1, 0, 2, 0, 5, 0, 14? The bell of 30j has 1, 3, 15 in the even places: too heavy at the ends. The answer is the half disc s(x) = √(4 − x²)/(2π) on [−2, 2], a half circle of radius 2 squashed to total 1.

Semicircle-moment technique: ∫ x²ᵐ s(x) dx over [−2, 2] is Cₘ, and every odd moment of s is 0. The odd ones vanish because s is the same on both sides of 0.

x = 2\cos\theta,\ \ \sqrt{4 - x^2} = 2\sin\theta,\ \ dx = -2\sin\theta\,d\theta @@ θ from π to 0 as x runs from −2 to 2
\int x^{2m} s(x)\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} (2\cos\theta)^{2m}\,4\sin^2\theta\,d\theta @@ the change of variables
2\cos\theta = z + z^{-1},\ \ 4\sin^2\theta = 2 - z^2 - z^{-2},\ \ z = e^{i\theta} @@ 26a’s turning
= \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (z + z^{-1})^{2m}(2 - z^2 - z^{-2})\,d\theta @@ the same on both halves of the turn
\text{the average of } z^j \text{ over a turn is } 0 \text{ for } j \ne 0 @@ the right-angled-turnings technique (32a)
\tfrac12\big(2\tbinom{2m}{m} - \tbinom{2m}{m+1} - \tbinom{2m}{m-1}\big) @@ only the constant term survives; z^±2 meets z^∓2
\htmlClass{new}{= \tbinom{2m}{m} - \tbinom{2m}{m+1} = C_m} \quad \blacksquare @@ the mirror-the-dip count, from a turning

So the averages of the moments of the shrunk stretches head to the moments of s. Two steps are held in trust. Each grid’s own moment stays near its average, its spread shrinking like 1/n², which is proved by the same walk count on pairs of walks. And spreads whose moments all match in the limit match in the limit, which is 30j3’s matching step again. Together: the shrunk stretches of a large grid spread as the half disc, whatever the coin.

\text{the half disc puts } 0.609 \text{ of its weight in } [-1, 1] @@ (1/π)(√3/2 + π/3), from the area under s
\text{the draw: } 607 \text{ of the } 1000 \text{ shrunk stretches lie in } [-1, 1] @@ one grid, one fixed seed
m_2, m_4, m_6 = 0.999,\ 1.995,\ 4.981 @@ against 1, 2, 5
\htmlClass{new}{\text{largest } 1.981,\ \text{smallest } -1.987} \quad \blacksquare @@ near the edges ±2

The largest shrunk stretch heads to 2, the edge of the half disc. The moments alone show only that it cannot stay below 2 − ε; that it cannot stay above 2 + ε needs moments whose order grows with n, and is held in trust.

inf53.5Gaps That Push Apart

The half disc says how many stretches lie in a stretch of the line. It says nothing of how they sit next to each other. Throw n points independently at the half disc’s weights and they clump: neighbours often land almost on top of each other. The stretches of a grid do not. Two rows show why.

A symmetric grid with rows (x, z) and (z, y) has stretches (x + y)/2 ± √(((x − y)/2)² + z²), so its gap is g = √((x − y)² + 4z²). With coins, (x − y)² + 4z² ≥ 4 and the gap is never below 2. Take bells instead: x and y bells of spread 1, z a bell of spread √½, all independent. Then u = x − y and v = 2z are independent bells, each of spread √2, and g is the distance of (u, v) from 0.

Gap-pattern technique: for this grid, P(g ≤ r) = 1 − e^(−r²/4) for every r ≥ 0.

\text{the pair } (u, v) \text{ has density } \tfrac{1}{4\pi}e^{-(u^2 + v^2)/4} @@ independent bells of spread √2 multiply (30f2, 30j1)
P(g \le r) = \textstyle\int\!\!\int_{u^2 + v^2 \le r^2} \tfrac{1}{4\pi}e^{-(u^2+v^2)/4}\,du\,dv @@ the disc of radius r
= \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^r \tfrac{1}{4\pi}e^{-\rho^2/4}\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta @@ polar change of variables (28i), as in 30j1
\htmlClass{new}{= 1 - e^{-r^2/4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope of −e^(−ρ²/4) is (ρ/2)e^(−ρ²/4)

Gap-repulsion technique: for this grid, P(g < ε) ≤ ε²/4: a gap needs both u and v small at once. From the gap pattern, since 1 − e^(−t) ≤ t. Against it, two independent bells x and y land within 0.1 of each other with chance 0.056; the grid’s two stretches do so with chance 0.0025, more than twenty times rarer. Small gaps fall off like ε² rather than ε. The average gap is √π ≈ 1.772; shrunk to average 1, the gap has density (πs/2)e^(−πs²/4), which vanishes at s = 0.

With turning numbers in place of real ones, as 31d allows, z has two independent parts, the gap is the distance of three bells from 0, and small gaps fall off like ε³. These are two different patterns. For large grids the gaps, each shrunk by the local average gap, follow patterns close to these two-row ones but not equal to them; that is held in trust. The real grids follow the first family, the turning-number grids the second.

inf53.6One Pattern in Many Places

Every coin. inf53.4 showed that the half disc forgets the coin. Whether the gap pattern forgets it too was long guessed, and proved for grids with independent entries between 2009 and 2011, by two groups with two methods. One swaps the entries one at a time, coin for bell, and shows that each swap moves the gaps so little that all the swaps together move them by almost nothing, as long as the first four averages of coin and bell agree. Behind it is a statement of the kind of inf41.7’s moved-cloud technique, held in trust here: changing a symmetric grid by E moves no stretch by more than ‖E‖, the stretch size of 31f3. The other lets the grid drift by adding a little bell to every entry, shows that the gaps settle into their pattern after a very short drift, and then shows that so short a drift changes nothing that matters. Both are stated here; their proofs are far above this page.

The edge. The largest stretch of a coin grid sits near 2√n, the edge of the half disc, and wobbles about it by an amount of order n^(−1/6), much less than √n. The pattern of that wobble, for turning-number grids with bells, was found in 1994; it is not the bell, and leans to one side.

The zeros. 43·now2 listed the zeros ½ + iγ of ζ. Shrink their gaps by the local average gap, which near height γ is 2π/ln(γ/2π). In 1972 a formula for how often two zeros sit a given shrunk distance apart, published in 1973, was found to be the formula for the stretches of large turning-number grids, 1 − (sin πu/πu)². Computations from 1987 on, of millions of zeros near the 10²⁰-th and beyond, match the gap pattern of those grids closely. The match is measured, not proved.

\gamma_1 = 14.1347,\ \ \gamma_2 = 21.0220 @@ the first two zeros, 43·now2
\text{local average gap near } \gamma = 17.6\text{: } 2\pi/\ln(17.6/2\pi) \approx 6.10 @@ the zeros thin out slowly
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shrunk gap } (21.0220 - 14.1347)/6.10 \approx 1.13} \quad \blacksquare @@ a single gap: the pattern is about millions

The shuffles. In a shuffle of 1, …, n (19e), a rising run is a list of places i₁ < ⋯ < iₖ whose entries rise; it need not be in neighbouring places. In 3 1 4 2 5 the longest has length 3 (1, 2, 5 or 3, 4, 5). Of the six shuffles of 1, 2, 3 only 1 2 3 has a rising run of length 3, so five do not, and five is C₃. The script finds 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429 shuffles with no rising run of 3, for n = 1 to 7: the numbers of inf53.3, held in trust for every n. The longest rising run of a large shuffle is near 2√n, also held in trust, and in 1999 its wobble about 2√n was proved to follow the 1994 edge pattern of turning-number grids, at scale n^(1/6). The script shuffled 1000 cards 2000 times: the longest run averaged 58.2, against 2√1000 = 63.2, falling short by 5.1, about 1.6 times n^(1/6) ≈ 3.16. There is no grid in a pack of cards.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞ק‎.oq.1Why do the zeros of ζ follow the gap pattern? If some symmetric map, with turning numbers allowed, had the heights γ of the zeros as its stretches, 31d’s real-stretches technique would put every zero on the line of 1859 at once. That hope is about a century old. No such map has been found, and nobody knows what space it would act on.
∞ק‎.oq.2How far does the 1973 pair formula go? Assuming the 1859 hypothesis, the formula 1 − (sin πu/πu)² is proved only when tested against smooth weights whose turnings lie in a fixed window. Beyond the window it is a guess, backed by computation. Going past the window would say new things about how often zeros are close together, and about gaps between primes.
∞ק‎.oq.3Why do the energy levels of chaotic systems follow the gap pattern? A guess of 1984 is that the levels of a quantum system whose classical motion is chaotic, such as a ball bouncing in a stadium-shaped table, have the gaps of random grids, while orderly systems have the clumped gaps of independent points. Experiments and computations agree widely. A proof for a single chaotic system is not known.
∞ק‎.oq.4How much independence does one pattern need? The proofs of 2009 to 2011 use entries independent above the diagonal. Grids with mostly zeros, or entries tied to their neighbours along a band, are expected to switch from the clumped pattern to the grid pattern as the band widens past a threshold. Where exactly the switch happens, and why, is proved only in part.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Grids of chance entered physics in the 1950s as a model of something too tangled to compute: the energy levels of heavy nuclei, whose hundreds of close levels no theory could predict one by one. The proposal was to give up on the levels and ask for their statistics, as if the forces were drawn at random. The half disc was proved by counting moments in 1955. Many physicists doubted that a model with no physics in it could predict anything; measured nuclear levels later showed the repulsion of inf53.5.

The gap pattern came first as a guess from two rows, and for some years the exact pattern for large grids was not known. In 1962 the grids were sorted into three families by their symmetry, real, turning-number and a third, each with its own repulsion. Whether the pattern depended on the coin was a question nobody could settle; computations said no.

In 1972, at afternoon tea at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, a number theorist described his formula for how close the zeros of ζ sit in pairs to a physicist, who recognised it as the formula for turning-number grids. The result was published in 1973. In 1987 computations of zeros far up the line showed the gaps matching the grid pattern, and later runs went near the 10²⁰-th zero and beyond. Some number theorists saw a sign of a hidden map behind the zeros; others warned that a statistical match had been found in many places and explained in none.

In 1984 physicists proposed that every quantum system with chaotic classical motion has the grid’s gaps. The edge pattern of 1994 then appeared in places with no grid: the longest rising run in a shuffle (1999), and in 2010 the edges of growing surfaces in experiments on liquid crystals. The question changed from why grids behave this way to why so many things behave like grids.

Between 2009 and 2011 two groups proved, by different methods, that the gap pattern does not depend on the coin, for grids with independent entries. What is argued now is what the pattern covers: grids with dependent or sparse entries, the systems of 1984, and above all the zeros, where no grid is known and the match is still only measured.

The accepted names

The coin grid is a Wigner matrix (a symmetric Bernoulli matrix), and its stretches its eigenvalues; the half disc is Wigner’s semicircle law, and the proof by tr Aᵏ is the moment method. The tree shapes are plane (ordered) rooted trees, the U and D lists Dyck paths, the counts Cₘ the Catalan numbers, and the mirror trick the reflection principle. Real grids with bells are the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble, turning-number grids the Gaussian unitary ensemble; the two-row gap pattern is the Wigner surmise, and the pushing apart is level repulsion. The edge pattern is the Tracy–Widom distribution; the longest rising run is the longest increasing subsequence (Ulam’s problem). That the gap pattern forgets the coin is universality, the Wigner–Dyson–Mehta conjecture; the one-entry-at-a-time method is the four moment theorem, and the drift method uses Dyson Brownian motion and the local relaxation flow. The pair formula is Montgomery’s pair correlation conjecture; the hoped-for map is the Hilbert–Pólya idea; the 1984 guess is the Bohigas–Giannoni–Schmit conjecture.

Who said it first

Eugene Wigner proved the semicircle law in 1955 (Annals of Mathematics) and proposed the two-row surmise. Freeman Dyson sorted the ensembles in 1962. Hugh Montgomery met Freeman Dyson at tea at the Institute for Advanced Study in 1972, and his pair correlation paper appeared in 1973. Andrew Odlyzko published his computations of spacings in Mathematics of Computation in 1987, and later ran them near the 10²⁰-th zero. Oriol Bohigas, Marie-Joya Giannoni and Charles Schmit made the 1984 conjecture (Physical Review Letters). Craig Tracy and Harold Widom found the edge pattern (Communications in Mathematical Physics, 1994). Jinho Baik, Percy Deift and Kurt Johansson proved the shuffle result (Journal of the American Mathematical Society, 1999). Kazumasa Takeuchi and a coworker saw the edge pattern in turbulent liquid crystals (2010). László Erdős, Horng-Tzer Yau and coworkers, among them Benjamin Schlein and Jun Yin, proved universality by the drift method; Terence Tao and Van Vu by the four moment method (Acta Mathematica, 2011). The hoped-for map is credited to David Hilbert and George Pólya.
