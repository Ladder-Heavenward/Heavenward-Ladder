1h · technique

Every and some by hand

1e gave “every” and “some” their marks and their techs. This step is about handling them at speed: writing English into them and back, choosing the right connective inside each, negating them by pushing a not through, and spotting the sentence that looks right and says nothing. Alongside the marks runs the oldest piece of logic there is: four kinds of sentence about every and some, and a diagram of how they oppose one another. With them come the syllogisms, the old arguments built from those four kinds. The syllogisms turn out to be chains of the techs you already own, with a famous form that the techs refuse.

Every goes with if; some goes with and. A not turns each into the other as it passes. When the subject is empty, every is cheap and some is impossible, and the old diagram cracks along exactly the links that assumed otherwise.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1e (∀, ∃, the techs, the techniques not-some and counterexample, the withheld direction), 1d (the free-if technique, the free ifs) and 1g (negated-if, pushing a not inward). 1i uses the counterexample as a shape; rung 2 writes its principles with these very sentences; rung 7 licenses the direction withheld here.

1h1Every and some over a list

Stands on 1e (∀ and ∃) and 1c (∧ and ∨ and their techs).

When the things in question can be listed, an every is an and across the list and a some is an or across it. Example: the people in the room are Ann, Ben and Cal, and P(x) is “x is asleep”. Then:

Reading an every as an and across a list is a way of seeing, not a technique of the rulebook, and it is foreclosed. The marks ∀ and ∃ on their own range over every thing whatever, not over a room. To swap the every for the and, you would need the room as a collection with exactly these members and nobody else. A collection is a set, and “exactly these and nobody else” is 2a's listing, so the swap waits for rung 2. Footing 1h2 writes the restriction as far as this rung can.

But the seeing explains everything that follows: whatever 1c and 1g did with ∧ and ∨, this step does with ∀ and ∃. Use an every is keep-a-part, applied to the part you want. Example: with Ann, Ben and Cal, using the every to get “Ann is asleep” is keep-a-part. Show an example is add-an-option: showing Ann asleep gives “Ann or Ben or Cal is asleep”. Use a some is reason by cases over the list. And pushing a not through is not-through (De Morgan's).

1h2Writing a restriction

Stands on 1h1 (every and some over a list) and 1e (every with if, some with and).

Most everyday sentences restrict their subject. Example: “every cat is an animal” is about cats, not about everything. Let A(x) be “x is a cat” and B(x) “x is an animal”. 1e gave the spellings, and here they are again with their denials:

Every goes with ⇒; some goes with ∧. Swapping the connectives is tempting, and both swaps are wrong. ∀x (A(x) ∧ B(x)) says everything is a cat and an animal, which the shoe on your foot refutes. The other swap, ∃x (A(x) ⇒ B(x)), is worse, because it looks innocent. Footing 1h3 shows why.

1h3The some-with-if trap

Stands on 1h2 (the spellings) and 1d (the free-if technique).

∃x (A(x) ⇒ B(x)) is proved by anything at all that is not an A, by 1d's free ifs. Here is the some-with-⇒ spelling, argued in a world where it ought to fail.

Take a world with three things: a grey cat t, a shoe s and a black dog r. Let D(x) say “x is a dog” and B(x) say “x is brown”. “Some dog is brown” is not so here: the only dog is black.

Now argue. The shoe is not a dog: that is given. Suppose, until we drop it, that the shoe is a dog. That clashes with the given, so we have ⊥. From ⊥ everything is permitted: the shoe is brown.

Drop the supposition. We have proved an if: . If the shoe is a dog, the shoe is brown. That is 1d's free-if technique, written out. “The shoe is brown” went when the supposition was dropped, so nothing has been learned about the shoe's colour. Only the if leaves, and the if can never be carried through, since the shoe is not a dog.

Now show an example, the shoe: . So the some-with-⇒ spelling is so, in a world where no dog is brown. The spelling said something other than “some dog is brown”.

1h4An every with nothing to check

Stands on 1h3 (the cheapness of ⇒) and 1f2 (the if machine).

The same cheapness of ⇒ makes “every living mammoth is purple” true. There is no living mammoth to check, so the if is never broken. The accepted word is that such an every is vacuously so. A vacuous every is not a defect. It is what the if machine does with an empty hypothesis, and 1f showed why it must. It matters for the old sentences, as footing 1h7 shows.

1h5The four old sentences

Stands on 1h2 (the spellings with their denials).

The oldest logic that has come down to us sorts sentences of the form “S is P” into four kinds. Medieval teachers named the kinds with the vowels of the Latin AffIrmo (I affirm) and nEgO (I deny):

sentencein marks
Aevery S is P∀x (S ⇒ P)
Eno S is P∀x (S ⇒ ¬P)
Isome S is P∃x (S ∧ P)
Osome S is not P∃x (S ∧ ¬P)

Example, with S for cats and P for animals or for things asleep: A, every cat is an animal; E, no cat is a dog; I, some cat is asleep; O, some cat is not asleep. Here the letters S and P are names of kinds, as A(x) and B(x) were in footing 1h2, and not the statement letters of 1c.

1h6The diagram of opposition

Stands on 1h5 (the four old sentences).

Then the old logic asks how the four kinds oppose one another, and later teachers drew the answers as a diagram. A and E sit along the top, I and O along the foot, with links between them. (The picture draws the diagram.)

  • Contradictories (the crossing links, A with O and E with I): never both so and never both not so. Each is the other's denial.
  • Contraries (A and E): never both so, though both can be not so. Example: “every swan is white” and “no swan is white” are both not so, because some swans are white and some are not.
  • Subcontraries (I and O): never both not so.
  • Subalterns (A to I, E to O): if every S is P, then some S is P.
The picture · the diagram of opposition in a world you build
Tap a bin to put things in it or empty it: things that are S but not P, things that are both, things that are P but not S. (Try S as swans, P as white things.)
Letters: teal so, ember not so. A link is solid teal when its relation is true in this world, dashed ember when the world breaks it. Empty S entirely and watch which links survive.

1h7When the subject is empty

Stands on 1h6 (the diagram) and 1h4 (an every with nothing to check).

Now the catch. Contraries, subcontraries and subalterns quietly assume that there are some S. Empty the subject (no living mammoths) and A, “every living mammoth is purple”, is vacuously so. So is E, “no living mammoth is purple”. So two contraries are both so. I and O each need a living mammoth to point at, so both are not so. So two subcontraries are both not so, and A no longer gives I.

Only the crossing links survive, because they are not a fact about mammoths at all. They are the negation techniques of 1e, which may be used whatever the subject. Modern logic keeps the crossing links for every subject. It keeps the other links only when “and there is an S” is added as a given. In the picture of footing 1h6, empty S and watch the diagram crack along exactly those links.

1h8Pushing a not through an every

Stands on 1e (not-some, counterexample, the withheld direction) and 1g7 (negated-if).

The denial of “every” is “some … not”, and the denial of “some” is “every … not”, which is “no”. 1e proved these as the techniques not-some and counterexample. 1e also marked the direction that waits: from “not every” to “some … not”.

Pushing a not through a run of marks takes a move for each mark. Each mark turns into its partner, and the ⇒ inside turns to ∧ by 1g's negated-if. Example first, in words: “not every cat is asleep” becomes “some cat is not asleep”. Here is the same push in marks, on the A sentence.

Start from : not every S is P. Push the not through the every. The every becomes some-not: . That direction waits for rung 7. Over a subject you can list, you look.

Then push the not through the if, by 1g's negated-if. The negation of an if is an and: . By itself, that is negated-if's rung-7 direction too. Inside a some the place is not turned about, so the step still waits for rung 7, as the rulebook marks it.

The result is O, “some S is not P”. The diagram's crossing link is nothing but this push. Two marks change: the every becomes a some, and the ⇒ turns into ∧. Every goes with ⇒, some with ∧, as promised. And the ¬ ends up on the kind P.

The other way, from O to “not A”, is 1e's counterexample technique and negated-if's licensed direction: good for any subject at all. So the crossing link is true: by techs in the licensed direction, and by looking in the other. That is why the crossing link survives the empty subject: a counterexample kills an every whether or not anything else exists.

1h9A not through several marks

Stands on 1h8 (pushing a not through an every) and 1e (marks in a row).

With more than one mark the method is the same: a change for each mark. Example: “everyone has a mother”, ∀x ∃y M(y, x). Denied, it becomes ∃x ¬∃y M(y, x), and then ∃x ∀y ¬M(y, x): someone has nobody for a mother. The order of the marks stays put; only the kinds of mark change.

1h10Books that balance, and the window

Stands on 1h9 (pushing a not), 1e (counterexample) and 0c (nature's books).

Every step so far worked on paper. This step puts a careful paper argument next to the world, to see what the paper can and cannot promise.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, out the window

0c kept nature's books: stuff, push and order, each paid in and paid out. 0c's rule ran one way: books that don't balance are wrong. The other way is worth a look. A ledger is a list of claims, each with what pays for it. Here is a weather forecast kept as a ledger. Each line is a claim the forecast makes, or is paid for by techs you have.

Every line is paid for. Nowhere in town is it raining, and wherever it rains, the ground gets wet. The books balance.

Now put your head out the window. There are clouds, and the sun is out too. That denies line 1. A light rain is falling through the sunshine. That is rain and shine together, and it denies line 4. Under the porch roof, rain falls past a dry step. That is a counterexample to line 10, in 1e's sense.

One look, and three entries fail. No step failed. Each tech did what it promises: true in, true out. The trouble was in what went in. Books can balance on paper and still be false the moment you look out the window.

A real forecast keeps 0c's books for the air: water, heat, wind. The ledger is the size of the whole world. Weather anywhere becomes weather here within days, so no town's books balance alone. The books would have to list every parcel of air on Earth, and the sky is a crowd, 1e's third size. No one can write down every parcel. So the ledger is kept in chunks, each many kilometres wide. Each entry is a little off, and the error grows each day the books run forward. That is why a forecast reaches a week or so ahead and stops.

A forecast reads: “tomorrow it rains somewhere in the valley, or it rains nowhere.” Can a window prove it false? If not, what has it told you?

1h11The best syllogism

Stands on 1h5 (the four old sentences) and 1e (speak of anything, use an every, prove an if).

A syllogism draws a conclusion from given lines, each an A, E, I or O sentence. The given lines of a syllogism are called its premises. The premises are linked by a middle term, a kind that appears in both premises and vanishes from the conclusion. Example: every mammal is an animal; every dog is a mammal; so every dog is an animal. “Mammal” is the middle term.

Medieval students memorised the valid forms by names whose vowels spell the sentence kinds. The best of them is Barbara, A, A, A: every M is P, every S is M, so every S is P. Each valid form is a brief argument by 1e's techs. Barbara's argument uses nothing but speaking of anything, using an every, proving an if, and carrying an if through, again and again.

Suppose every M is P, and every S is M. Speak of anything; call it a. Suppose, until we drop it, that a is S.

Every S is M, so a is M: use the every on a, then carry the if through. The middle term has appeared.

Every M is P, so a is P: use that every on a, and carry it through. The middle term has done its work and vanishes.

Drop the supposition. We have proved an if: if a is S, then a is P. And a was anything, so speak of everything: . Every S is P.

1h12Three more valid forms

Stands on 1h11 (Barbara) and 1h8 (some and no).

Celarent is E, A, E: no M is P, every S is M, so no S is P. Example: no fish is a mammal, every shark is a fish, so no shark is a mammal.

Darii is A, I, I: every M is P, some S is M, so some S is P. Example: every dog is an animal, some pet is a dog, so some pet is an animal.

Ferio is E, I, O: no M is P, some S is M, so some S is not P. Example: no fish has fur, some pet is a fish, so some pet has no fur.

The three run the way Barbara runs. Where a premise says “no”, the argument reaches a clash. Where a premise or the conclusion says “some”, the argument calls the example and later shows it.

1h13Two forms that fail

Stands on 1h11 (Barbara's argument), 1h7 (the empty subject) and 1e (counterexample).

The commonest invalid form is the undistributed middle: every cat is a mammal, every dog is a mammal, so every dog is a cat. Try to run Barbara's argument. You suppose S of the thing and reach M of it, and then no premise leaves M. Both kinds sit inside the middle, and the middle never links them. Rex convicts the form: both premises are true, and he is a dog that is not a cat, a counterexample in 1e's sense.

And a form the old logic accepted fails the modern reading: Darapti, “every M is P, every M is S, so some S is P”. If there are no M, both premises are vacuously so, and the conclusion, a some, has nothing to point at. Example: every unicorn is purple, every unicorn has a horn, so something with a horn is purple. Darapti is the diagram's empty-subject problem again, and it is mended the same way: add “there is an M” as a given.

1h14Finding the missing premise

Stands on 1h11 and 1h12 (the valid forms).

Run a syllogism backwards. Given a conclusion and a premise, find the other: the missing premise must supply the link the middle term makes. Wanting “no S is P” from “every S is M”, you need the middle M kept apart from P: “no M is P”, and the form is Celarent. Wanting “some S is not P” from “no M is P”, you need some S to be M: Ferio. Wanting an every, the premises must both be everys (Barbara, Celarent); a some in the premises gives only a some in the conclusion. The syllogism bench in footing 1h13 lets you try forms against a small world and see which premise is doing the work.

A writer of puzzle books set chains of syllogisms to be untangled. One gives three lines: babies are illogical; nobody is despised who can manage a crocodile; illogical persons are despised. (“Illogical” means unable to reason; “despised” means looked down on.)

Who said it first

The four sentences and the syllogisms are Aristotle’s; the diagram is the square of opposition, drawn by later commentators from his remarks. The puzzle writer is Lewis Carroll.

“The affirmation ‘every man is white’ is the contradictory of the denial ‘not every man is white’, or again, the proposition ‘no man is white’ is the contradictory of the proposition ‘some men are white’.”

Aristotle, On Interpretation 7 (17b), trans. E. M. Edghill (Oxford, 1928)

“If A is predicated of all B, and B of all C, A must be predicated of all C.”

Aristotle, Prior Analytics I.4 (25b), trans. A. J. Jenkinson (Oxford, 1928)

The ledger out the window: Edward Lorenz found in 1961 that his weather model, rerun from numbers rounded from six decimal places to three, drifted to entirely different weather within about two months of model time. A small error in the entries, run forward, swamps the books. It is why forecasts stop where they do.

Lewis Carroll wrote chains of syllogisms meant to be untangled. One gives three lines: babies are illogical; nobody is despised who can manage a crocodile; illogical persons are despised. Carroll asked what follows about babies and crocodiles.
