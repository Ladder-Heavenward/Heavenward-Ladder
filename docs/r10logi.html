How Many Trucks Does a Route Need?

How many trucks does a route need? Every summer morning in the Salinas Valley, crews cut lettuce in the fields, and trucks carry it to the coolers in Salinas, where it is chilled within hours of being cut. Too few trucks, and cut lettuce wilts in the sun. Too many, and drivers sit idle and are paid to wait. The question is hard because nothing about it stays still: trucks leave at odd times, some trips run long, and a jam at the cooler backs up into the fields. This page proves one exact fact about any queue at all: the number of items in it, on average, is how often they arrive times how long each one stays. It proves the fact for a day by counting, and for the long run by rung 10's limits. Then it counts the trucks, and shows why one slow step fills the whole line.

Count the item-hours two ways: hour by hour, adding how many are waiting, and item by item, adding how long each stays. Divide by the hours: the average number waiting is the arrivals per hour times the average stay. Trucks on the road are trips per hour times hours per trip. And the line can pass on no more per hour than its slowest step: past that, the queue passes every bound.

10logi1A Queue, Counted by the Hour

Logistics is the work of getting things where they are needed, when they are needed. Its first object is the queue, read broadly: any place where items arrive, stay a while, and leave. A cooler holding pallets is one; so is a dock holding trucks, or a road holding the trucks on it.

From here on, on this page, time is counted in whole hours, 0, 1, 2, …, and the queue is looked at once in each hour. Item j arrives at hour a_j and leaves at hour d_j, with a_j ≤ d_j. It is in the queue at the hours a_j, a_j + 1, …, d_j − 1, and its stay is W_j = d_j − a_j hours. N(t) names the number of items in the queue at hour t.

A pair (j, t) with item j in the queue at hour t is an item-hour. A truck that waits three hours at a dock makes three item-hours there.

Item-hours technique: if every item that is ever in the queue during hours 0 to T − 1 arrives and leaves within those hours, then Σ_{t<T} N(t) = Σ_j W_j.

H = \{(j, t) : \text{item } j \text{ is in the queue at hour } t\} @@ a finite set: finitely many items, hours below T
H \text{ by hours: } \htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{t \lt T} N(t)} \text{ members} @@ the pairs at hour t are N(t), and different hours share none (separate-piles, 4k)
H \text{ by items: } \htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_j W_j} \text{ members} @@ the pairs of item j are its W_j hours, all below T by the supposition; different items share none
\textstyle\sum_{t \lt T} N(t) = \htmlClass{keep}{\sum_j W_j} \quad \blacksquare @@ one set has one number of members (4k)

Now share both sides out. Let A be the number of items. The average number in the queue over the window is L = (1/T) Σ N(t). The arrivals per hour are λ = A/T. The average stay is W = (1/A) Σ W_j. Here λ is the Greek letter lambda, in its small form: on this page it names arrivals per hour, and its capital form is not used.

L = \tfrac1T \textstyle\sum_{t \lt T} N(t) = \tfrac1T \sum_j W_j @@ item-hours
= \tfrac{A}{T} \cdot \tfrac1A \textstyle\sum_j W_j @@ times A/A, which is 1 when A ≠ 0
= \htmlClass{keep}{\lambda\,W} @@ arrivals per hour times the average stay

Queue identity: on average, the number of items waiting equals how often they arrive times how long each stays. You may use it for any queue that starts and ends a window empty, whatever the order of arrivals, whatever the spread of stays, whoever is served first. Check the units by 5j: items per hour times hours is items.

\text{pallets in, } 4 \text{ hours, empty to empty: } N = 3, 5, 6, 4, 2, 0 @@ six looks, hours 0 to 5, a model
\textstyle\sum N(t) = 20 \text{ pallet-hours} @@ by hours
10 \text{ pallets, stays } 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 2, 2, 2 @@ by items: 20 pallet-hours, the same
L = \tfrac{20}{6},\ \ \lambda = \tfrac{10}{6} \text{ per hour},\ \ W = 2 \text{ hours} @@ L = λW: 10/3 = (5/3) · 2

10logi2The Long Run

A cooler that runs day and night never starts a window empty and ends it empty. Then the item-hours inside the window fall short of the stays: items still in the queue at the end have hours to come. Rung 10 says how much that matters: in the long run, nothing.

For each T, let L_T be the average number in the queue over hours 0 to T − 1, let λ_T be the arrivals per hour over those hours, and let W_T be the average full stay of the items that arrived in them. These are three sequences (9·cb4), and 10f says what it means for each to head to a limit.

Long-run technique: if the queue never holds more than M items, no item stays more than S hours, λ_T → λ and W_T → W, then L_T → λW. Suppose also the queue is empty at hour 0.

\textstyle\sum_{\text{arrived} \lt T} W_j - \sum_{t \lt T} N(t) = \text{the hours still to come} @@ item-hours, with the pairs at hours T and after left out
0 \le \text{hours still to come} \le \htmlClass{new}{M\,S} @@ at most M items are in the queue at hour T, each with at most S hours left
0 \le \lambda_T W_T - L_T \le \tfrac{MS}{T} @@ divide by T, as in the queue identity
\tfrac{MS}{T} \to 0 @@ one-over-n and constant-factor (10f, 10c)
\lambda_T W_T - L_T \to 0 @@ the squeeze, far out (10d, 10f), between 0 and MS/T
L_T = \lambda_T W_T - (\lambda_T W_T - L_T) \to \htmlClass{keep}{\lambda W - 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ limits-multiply, then limits-subtract

The bounds M and S are not decoration. A queue that grows without end has no M, and then L_T need not head anywhere; the slow step below makes one.

10logi3The Trucks

Read the road as the queue and the trucks as its items. A truck arrives when it sets out on a trip, and leaves when it is back and free. Its stay is the hours per trip: the drive out, the loading, the drive back, the unloading, and any waiting between. So the average number of trucks on the road is the trips started per hour times the hours per trip.

Truck-count technique: a route needs at least (trips per day) × (hours per trip) ÷ (hours each truck works a day) trucks. Each truck gives at most its working hours to the road each day, and the route uses trips × hours-per-trip truck-hours: the item-hours technique, counted by trucks and by trips.

\text{about } 40 \text{ km each way; a trip of } 2.5 \text{ hours} @@ drive both ways, load in the field, unload at the cooler: a model
\begin{aligned}&\text{crews fill } 6 \text{ loads an hour}\\ &\quad\text{for } 10 \text{ hours: } 60 \text{ trips}\end{aligned} @@ λ = 6 trips per hour over the working day
L = 6 \times 2.5 = \htmlClass{new}{15} \text{ trucks on the road} @@ the queue identity
\tfrac{60 \times 2.5}{10} = \htmlClass{keep}{15} \text{ trucks, at the least} @@ truck-count; a sixteenth for the one that breaks down

Two things change the count, and the identity says by how much. More loads an hour raise λ, and the trucks rise in proportion. Longer trips raise W, and the trucks rise in proportion too. A half-hour wait at the cooler, on every trip, adds 6 × 0.5 = 3 trucks, bought only to stand in a line.

10logi4Why a Slow Step Fills the Whole Line

A line is a chain of steps that every item passes in turn: loading, driving, unloading, cooling. Write μ for the most items a step can pass on in an hour, its capacity, and D(n) for the items that have left the last step by hour n. Here μ is the Greek letter mu, in its small form: on this page it names a capacity per hour, and its capital form is not used.

Slowest-step technique: a line passes on no more items an hour than its slowest step: D(n) ≤ μ_min · n. Every item that leaves the line has passed the slowest step, which passes on at most μ_min an hour. ∎ Speeding up any other step does not change the bound.

Now let items arrive at λ an hour, steadily, with λ > μ_min. By hour n, about λn have arrived and at most μ_min · n have left. So at least (λ − μ_min) · n are still in the line. That is a sequence that passes every bound (10f): for any number B, past n = B/(λ − μ_min) the line holds more than B. They pile up before the slow step, then fill the steps before it, back to the field. By the queue identity, read backwards, a line that keeps growing makes every item stay longer: W = L/λ grows with it.

\lambda = 6 \text{ loads an hour},\ \ \mu_{\text{cooler}} = 5 @@ the cooler is the slowest step; a model
\text{line at hour } n \ge (6 - 5)\,n = n @@ one more truck waiting each hour
\text{hour } 10:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{10} \text{ trucks waiting} @@ the last load of the day waits about 2 hours (10 ahead, 5 an hour)
\tfrac{D(n)}{n} \to \htmlClass{keep}{5} @@ the cooler's pace, whatever the trucks or the crews do

When λ ≤ μ_min and arrivals are steady, the line settles instead, and D(n)/n heads to λ: the line passes on what it is given. Real arrivals are not steady, and a line near its capacity wobbles badly. That is why planners leave room above λ; the cellar says a little more.

10logi6Running It Backwards

Handed any two of L, λ and W, find the third: W = L/λ, λ = L/W. Handed a fleet and a trip time, find the most trips a day: trucks × working hours ÷ hours per trip. Handed a line whose output per hour has settled, look for the step whose capacity matches it: that is the slow step. Handed a wish, a shorter wait, shorten the stay or slow the arrivals; nothing else in the identity moves.

Unsteady arrivals

How badly a line near its capacity wobbles when arrivals come at random is a question about chance, and the ladder takes it up with the statistics rungs. Nothing on this page leans on it.

Who said it first

Queueing as mathematics began with Agner Krarup Erlang, who worked out in Copenhagen from 1909 how many lines a telephone exchange needed. Philip Morse noticed in 1958 that L = λW was true in every queue he had solved, and challenged his readers to find one where it failed. John Little proved it in 1961 for queues that run forever, by a long-run argument. Shaler Stidham gave the short proof by counting item-hours in 1974. The slow step that sets a whole line's pace was made the centre of a method of running factories by Eliyahu Goldratt, in the novel The Goal (1984). The just-in-time way of running a line, with little stock, grew at Toyota in Japan from the 1950s under Taiichi Ohno.

Little began by noting that the formula L = λW had been used for years as something everyone believed, and set out to prove that it is true under very general conditions.

John D. C. Little, “A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW”, Operations Research 9 (1961); paraphrase

Accepted names: the queue identity is Little's law, though on this ladder it is a technique: it is proved, not measured. The slow step is the bottleneck, and its capacity the line's throughput. Item-hours are a kind of person-hours or truck-hours. The two ways of running a warehouse in 10·logi.7.4 are called just-in-time and just-in-case.
