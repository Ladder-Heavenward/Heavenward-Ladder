How Does Radar Tell How Fast the Rain Is Moving?

How does radar tell how fast the rain is moving? A weather radar sends out short bursts of radio waves and listens for echoes off raindrops. From the echoes it maps not only where the rain is, but how fast it moves toward or away from the dish, which is how forecasters spot a storm starting to spin. This page finds range from the echo’s time, writes each echo as a turning arrow (26a), reads speed from how far the arrow turns between bursts, finds the speed beyond which the turn wraps past a full circle (26c), and reads how much rain from echo strength in decibels (17).

An echo from distance d comes back as an arrow turned by 4πd/λ. Between bursts T apart, a drop moving at v turns it a further 4πvT/λ, so v = λΔφ/(4πT). The turn is known only up to whole circles, so speeds beyond λ/(4T) fold back and look slower. Echo strength in decibels gives the rain rate through a power law.

26nav1Range From Echo Time

Evita is a forecaster reading a weather radar. The radar sends a burst a millionth of a second long, then listens. An echo that comes back after time t has gone out and back.

d = \dfrac{c\,t}{2} @@ there and back at the speed of light
\htmlClass{new}{t = 1 \text{ ms}:\ d = \dfrac{3 \times 10^8 \times 0.001}{2} = 150 \text{ km}} @@

From here on, on this page, λ = 10.7 cm is the radar’s wavelength and the radar sends 1,000 bursts a second, so T = 1 ms between them.

26nav2Each Echo Comes Back Turned

The radar wave is a swing, which the receiver writes as a turning arrow, A e^(iφ) (26a). Going out to distance d and back covers 2d, which is 2d/λ whole waves, and each wave turns the arrow once round.

\varphi = 2\pi \cdot \dfrac{2d}{\lambda} = \dfrac{4\pi d}{\lambda} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{echo} = A\,e^{-i\,4\pi d/\lambda}} @@ its size from how much rain, its turn from where the rain is

26nav3Speed From the Turn Between Pulses

The distance is far too many wavelengths to read from one echo. But between two bursts a drop moving toward the dish at v gets vT closer, and the arrow turns by a small, readable amount. Multiplying arrows adds their turns (26b), so the receiver multiplies one echo by the flip of the last and reads the turn.

\Delta\varphi = \dfrac{4\pi\,vT}{\lambda} @@ 
v = \dfrac{\lambda\,\Delta\varphi}{4\pi T} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta\varphi = 60°:\ v = \dfrac{0.107 \times \pi/3}{4\pi \times 0.001} \approx 8.9 \text{ m/s}} @@ toward the dish

Phase-step technique: the speed of rain toward the radar is its wavelength times the turn of the echo between bursts, over 4π times the time between bursts. The radar crew checks it on a car driven at a known 20 m/s past a test dish: the turn per burst comes out at the 135° the technique predicts.

26nav4Too Fast to Tell: the Turn Wraps Past a Full Circle

A turn of 200° looks the same as a turn of −160°: the arrow only shows where it ends, not how many times it went round. Like the many logs of −1 (26c), the turn is known only up to whole circles.

|\Delta\varphi| < \pi:\ \ |v| < \dfrac{\lambda}{4T} @@ 
\dfrac{0.107}{4 \times 0.001} \approx 27 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a drop at } 35 \text{ m/s toward the dish reads as } 35 - 54 = -19 \text{ m/s, away}} @@ folded back

Speeds past λ/(4T) fold back and read as slower, even backwards; more bursts a second raise the limit, but echoes from beyond c·T/2 then arrive after the next burst and land at the wrong range. Forecasters unfold the speeds by eye or by computer, using the speeds of the neighbouring rain.

26nav5How Much Rain, From Echo Strength in Decibels

Bigger drops echo far more strongly. Echo strengths span a range of a million million, so they are read in decibels (17): ten times the log, base 10, of the echo strength.

Z = 200\,R^{1.6} @@ R the rain rate in mm an hour; a fitted power law
\text{dBZ} = 10\log_{10} Z @@ 
40 \text{ dBZ}:\ Z = 10^4,\ R = 50^{1/1.6} \approx 12 \text{ mm an hour} @@ heavy rain
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each } 10 \text{ dBZ multiplies the rain by about } 4.2} @@ 10^(1/1.6)

Echo strength read in decibels gives the rain rate through a power law, and each 10 decibels means about four times the rain. Evita reads the colours on the map this way, and checks them against rain gauges on the ground after each storm.

Accepted names

This is pulse-Doppler radar; the turn is the phase, the fastest true speed the Nyquist velocity, and folding is velocity aliasing. The trade between speed limit and range is the Doppler dilemma. Z is the reflectivity factor, and Z = 200R^1.6 the Marshall-Palmer relation.

Who said it first

John Marshall and Walter Palmer fitted the rain power law in 1948 (not yet revisited for the date). The United States weather radar network uses a wavelength of about 10 cm.
