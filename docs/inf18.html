Can a World Have Its Own Logic?

Can a world have its own logic? Rung 7’s two-stage world had a statement established later but not now, and there the either-way principle failed. Read that world as data: a set of things at each stage, and a map carrying each thing now to itself later. Its statements then take three truth values, not two, and and, or, if and not can be computed on them like a table. Every world of data over a shape carries a logic of its own in this way, and choosing the shape chooses the logic: with one stage, or stages that never come after one another, it is rung 1’s two-valued logic; with a stage after another, either-way fails. ∞2’s every and some are found in every such world, and every looks ahead. Since 2010 such worlds have been used as bridges, carrying a result from one theory to another that shares the world.

Data over the order now ≤ later: sets X(now), X(later) and a map X(now) → X(later). A part keeps the map inside it. The one-point data has three parts: truth values 0 < m < 1, m meaning true later and not now. And is least, or is greatest, p ⇒ q is 1 when p ≤ q and q otherwise, ¬p = p ⇒ 0. Then m ∨ ¬m = m and ¬¬m = 1. Every x has P holds at a stage when it holds for every x at every stage from there on. Two values exactly when no stage comes after another.

inf18.1Data Over Two Stages

7b7’s world has two stages, now and later, with later after now. Data over it is a set X(now) of things present now, a set X(later) of things present later, and a map from the first to the second that says what each thing now has become later. This is ∞17.1’s data with the arrow pointing forward in time instead of down to a smaller piece. A part of the data is a part of each set, with the map carrying the part now into the part later: what holds of a thing now still holds of it later.

Three-parts technique: the data with one thing at each stage has exactly three parts.

(\varnothing, \varnothing),\ \ (\varnothing, \{*\}),\ \ (\{*\}, \{*\}) @@ the part later contains the image of the part now
(\{*\}, \varnothing) @@ not a part: * now is carried to * later, which is outside
\text{four candidates, one fails} @@ each set has two parts
\htmlClass{new}{\text{three parts: } 0,\ m,\ 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ false, true later and not now, true now

A statement about the world has as its truth value the stages at which it is established. Since what is established stays established, the truth value is a part of the one-thing data, and there are three of them.

inf18.2Three Truth Values

Order the values 0 < m < 1. And is the least of two values, or the greatest. If is the largest r with r and p at most q: p ⇒ q is 1 when p ≤ q, and q otherwise. Not p is p ⇒ 0. This is how 7b7 read each mark at now, stage by stage, written as a table.

Middle-value technique: at the value m, either-way gives m and not-not gives 1, so neither P ∨ ¬P nor ¬¬P ⇒ P is true at now.

\neg 0 = 1,\ \ \neg m = 0,\ \ \neg 1 = 0 @@ m ≤ 0 fails, so m ⇒ 0 = 0
m \lor \neg m = m \lor 0 = m \ne 1 @@ either-way is not true now
\neg\neg m = \neg 0 = 1 @@ not-not P is true now
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\neg m \Rightarrow m = 1 \Rightarrow m = m \ne 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ not-not out is not true now: 7b7’s table, computed

The if-out rule holds at every value: p and (p ⇒ q) is p when p ≤ q and q otherwise, and in both cases it is at most q. So rung 1’s columns without either-way stay true in this world, and either-way does not.

inf18.3Every Looks Ahead

∞2.3 found some as the side of pulling back on the left and ∞2.4 found every as the side on the right. The same two sides exist in every world of data, computed stage by stage, with one change: the answer must stay established. Take X with X(now) = {a} and X(later) = {a, b}, a thing a present throughout and a new thing b arriving later. Let P be the part ({a}, {a}).

Look-ahead technique: “every x has P” holds at a stage only when every x present at every stage from there on has P.

\text{some } x \text{ has } P \text{ at now: } a \in P(\text{now}) @@ true now, and so later: value 1
\text{every } x \text{ present now has } P @@ a is the only thing now
b \in X(\text{later}),\ b \notin P(\text{later}) @@ at later, every fails
\htmlClass{new}{\text{“every } x \text{ has } P\text{” has value } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a value true now must stay true later, and this one would not

So every in a world of stages is a promise about what is still to come, and some is a report of what is already here. That is the reading of the two sides by ∞2, now in a world where things arrive.

inf18.4Worlds That Settle What Proofs Cannot

35f3 showed that a world in which every axiom is true and a statement false is a proof that the statement has no proof. The two-stage world does this for either-way: rung 1’s columns without it stay true there, and either-way fails at m. 35f3’s second world, made by the 1963 method, can be read in the same way: it is data over an order of finite pieces of information about new parts of ℕ, with more information further along.

Settling-world technique: a statement false at some stage of some world of data, in which the techniques used are all true, has no proof by those techniques.

p \land (p \Rightarrow q) \le q \text{ at every value} @@ if-out holds, by the middle-value footing
\text{each of rung 1’s other rules: checked on } \{0, m, 1\} @@ a finite table, nine pairs at most
P \lor \neg P = m \text{ at now} @@ the middle-value technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no column of those rules proves } P \lor \neg P} \quad \blacksquare @@ a proof would make it 1 at every stage

For the line, the world of the 1963 method is not yet two-valued: a further step keeps only the statements that cannot be refuted by more information, and the world that results has two values and a size between the counting numbers and the line. That step is named here; the proof is far up the ladder.

inf18.5When the Logic Is Two-Valued

Choose the shape and the logic follows. A world of one stage has two truth values, and its parts are the parts of a set: 44l5’s algebra of and, or and not. A world of several stages none of which comes after another is many one-stage worlds side by side.

Two-values technique: either-way holds in every world of data over an order exactly when no stage comes strictly after another.

s < t:\ \text{the value “from } t \text{ on” at } s @@ established at t, not at s
\neg(\text{from } t \text{ on}) \text{ at } s = 0 @@ some stage after s establishes it
(\text{from } t \text{ on}) \lor \neg(\ldots) \ne 1 \text{ at } s @@ the middle-value technique, at s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no } s < t \Rightarrow \text{values at each stage are } 0, 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stages from s on are s alone, so a part is all or nothing

∞18.oq.1What is the right notion of space when a world of data stands in for one? Data over the open pieces of a space, as in ∞17, gives a world; so does data over an order, as here; and the same world can come from very different shapes, which is what makes it a bridge. When two such presentations meet, a result proved in one can be read in the other, and the project of finding such meetings across mathematics, begun in 2010, is still under way.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In the years after the 1963 method of 35f3, the method was recast as building a world of data over an order of finite pieces of information, and keeping only the statements that further information cannot refute; this world has two values and a size strictly between the counting numbers and the line.

In 2010 a mathematician proposed using such worlds as bridges: one world, presented in two ways by two theories, carries results between them.

The accepted names

Data over an order is a presheaf on a poset; the world of all such data is a presheaf topos; its truth values are the subobject classifier Ω, here the three up-sets or sieves. A logic with three ordered values computed this way is a Heyting algebra, the logic of the world intuitionistic; the two-stage world is a Kripke model. Some and every are ∃ and ∀ as adjoints to pulling back. The 1963 world is the Cohen topos, sheaves for the double-negation topology on the Cohen poset. A world presented in two ways is a topos-theoretic bridge; two theories with one world are Morita-equivalent.

Who said it first

Paul Cohen (1963), forcing. The topos form of the independence of the continuum hypothesis is given in Saunders Mac Lane and Ieke Moerdijk, Sheaves in Geometry and Logic (Springer), sections VI.2 and VI.3. Olivia Caramello, “The unification of mathematics via topos theory” (2010).
