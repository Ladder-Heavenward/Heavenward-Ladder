Can every symmetry group come from an equation?

Can every symmetry group come from an equation? The rung went from an equation to its group. Go the other way: pick a finite group, any one, and ask for an equation with rational coefficients whose group it is. Over some fields this is easy, and the page shows why: shuffles of letters do it at once. Over the rationals it is one of the oldest open questions of the subject. All shuffles of n things come from equations, and the page has already met one for five. The turn-about groups come from the fields that cut the circle into equal arcs, with help from the primes. The solvable groups were done in 1954, after a long argument with a gap that was later mended. The largest of the twenty six groups of 44·now was done in 1984, by a method that turns counting in a group into an equation. And for some groups of a few thousand members, nobody knows.

Over a field of letters, every finite group is a symmetry group, by fixing part of the shuffles. Over the rationals: all the shuffles of any number of things make the group of an equation, every turn-about group comes from cutting the circle, the solvable groups were done in 1954 and the largest sporadic group in 1984. Whether every finite group comes from an equation over the rationals is open.

43now21Easy over a field of letters

Let K be the field of fractions of polynomials in letters x₁, …, xₙ with rational coefficients. Each shuffle of the letters is a symmetry of K. By 44b the fractions every shuffle leaves alone are fractions in e₁, …, eₙ; call that field F. K is the splitting field over F of (x − x₁)⋯(x − xₙ) = xⁿ − e₁xⁿ⁻¹ + ⋯, and its group is all of Sₙ.

Any-group technique: every finite group G is the group of some splitting field over some field.

G \text{ shuffles its own members: } g \mapsto (h \mapsto gh) @@ one-to-one, by 19b’s cancel technique; so G is a part-group of Sₙ, n = |G|
G \text{ acts on } K \text{ by shuffling the letters} @@ as a part-group of Sₙ
E = K^G:\ \ [K : E] = |G| \text{ and } \mathrm{Aut}(K/E) = G @@ the fixed-size technique (44d2)
\htmlClass{new}{K \text{ is the splitting field over } E \text{ of the same polynomial}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its coefficients are in F ⊆ E

The catch: E is a field of fractions in letters, not ℚ. Putting numbers in for the letters can change the group, and whether some choice keeps it is exactly the hard part.

43now22All shuffles over ℚ

44g4 found S₅ as the group of x⁵ − 4x + 2. The same argument works for any prime p: an irreducible polynomial of degree p over ℚ with exactly two non-real roots has group Sₚ.

f = x^7 - 14x + 2 @@ 43d’s test with 2: 2 divides −14 and 2, and 4 does not divide 2
f(-2) = -128 + 28 + 2 = -98,\ f(-1) = 15,\ f(0) = 2,\ f(1) = -11,\ f(2) = 102 @@ changes of sign: three real roots
f' = 7x^6 - 14 = 0 \text{ only at } x = \pm 2^{1/6} @@ at most three real roots (14)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two non-real roots, a swap and a 7-loop: } G_f = S_7} \quad \blacksquare @@ 44g4’s argument, with 7 for 5

For every n, not only primes, xⁿ − x − 1 has group Sₙ: it was proved irreducible in 1956 and its group found in 1987. A result of 1892 says more: for a polynomial in letters with group G, most choices of rational numbers for the letters keep the group. So Sₙ and Aₙ come from equations over ℚ for every n. The trouble is to find, for a given G, a polynomial in letters over ℚ with group G in the first place.

43now23Turn-about groups from the circle

The field ℚ(ζ) of the turn by a p-th, p prime, has group (ℤ/p)ˣ, a clock of p − 1 hours (44i4, 42e). Its middle fields have every clock group of size dividing p − 1 as group.

\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_7)/\mathbb Q) = \{\zeta \mapsto \zeta^k\},\ \text{a clock of } 6 @@ 3 reaches every hour of the clock of 7
H = \{\zeta \mapsto \zeta,\ \zeta \mapsto \zeta^6\},\ \ K^H = \mathbb Q(\zeta + \zeta^{-1}) @@ the flip: fixes 2cos(2π/7)
\mathrm{Aut}(K^H/\mathbb Q) \cong (\text{clock of } 6)/H,\ \text{a clock of } 3 @@ the normal-middle technique; every part of a turn-about group is normal
\htmlClass{new}{y^3 + y^2 - 2y - 1:\ \ \Delta = 49 = 7^2,\ \text{group } A_3} \quad \blacksquare @@ 44i2’s cubic; 44k4’s square-discriminant technique agrees

For a clock of m hours, choose a prime p that leaves 1 on the clock of m. 42g states that such primes are endless; then the clock of p − 1 has a quotient that is a clock of m. Every turn-about finite group is a product of clocks, and the same trick with several primes builds it. So every turn-about group comes from an equation over ℚ.

43now24What is done, and what is open

Solvable groups, which come apart into clocks, were done in 1954, by building towers of fields one turn-about step at a time over ℚ, and controlling at each step which primes the field sees. A gap at the prime 2 was found and mended later; the full account is in a book of 1999. The largest sporadic group was done in 1984, by a method that reads the count of solutions of g₁g₂g₃ = e, with each gᵢ in a given conjugate family, off the table of characters (44·cb27): when the count says there is only one such triple up to conjugation, the group is the group of a field over ℚ. Many simple groups fall to that count.

\text{triples in } S_3 \text{ with } g_1 g_2 g_3 = e,\ g_1, g_2 \text{ swaps},\ g_3 \text{ a 3-loop} @@ choose g₁ and g₂, then g₃ = (g₁g₂)⁻¹
g_1 g_2 \text{ is a 3-loop exactly when } g_1 \ne g_2 @@ two different swaps make a 3-loop; equal ones make e
3 \cdot 2 = 6 \text{ triples},\ \text{one family under conjugation by } S_3 @@ S₃ moves them around transitively, and |S₃| = 6
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one triple up to conjugation, and } g_1, g_2 \text{ make all of } S_3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the condition the method needs, met

Still open in 2026: is every finite group the group of some equation over ℚ? Among the 26 sporadic groups, one, of size 10 200 960, is not known to be. Many groups of grids over finite fields are not known either. No group is known not to be; a single one would settle the question the other way.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The question is the inverse Galois problem. The any-group technique is Cayley’s theorem with Artin’s theorem on fixed fields; the field of letters is the field of rational functions. The theorem of 1892 is Hilbert’s irreducibility theorem. The fields that cut the circle are the cyclotomic fields, and every abelian extension of ℚ lies in one (the Kronecker–Weber theorem). The method of 1984 is the rigidity method, and a triple counted once up to conjugation is a rigid triple. The sporadic group still open is the Mathieu group M₂₃.

Who said it first

David Hilbert proved his irreducibility theorem in 1892, and used it to get Sₙ and Aₙ. Emmy Noether proposed a route by fields of letters in 1918. Igor Shafarevich solved the solvable case in 1954; the gap at the prime 2 was pointed out and repaired, and the account by Jürgen Neukirch, Alexander Schmidt and Kay Wingberg appeared in 1999. John Thompson realized the Monster in 1984, by the rigidity method built from the work of Kuang-yen Shih (1974), Gennadii Belyi (1979), Michael Fried and B. Heinrich Matzat. Ernst Selmer proved xⁿ − x − 1 irreducible in 1956, and Hiroyoshi Osada found its group in 1987.

A proof that is not allowed

“A turn-about group of size m is a part of the clock of p − 1 for a prime p, so it is the group of the field fixed by that part.” Not allowed as it stands: the field fixed by a part H has the quotient by H as its group, not H. The page uses a quotient of the clock that matches the group, which is why it needs p − 1 to be a multiple of m.
