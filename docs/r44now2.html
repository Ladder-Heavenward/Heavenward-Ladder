Will Today's Codes Survive Quantum Computers?

Will today’s codes survive quantum computers? The secret shared by 43l on a prime clock and by 44·crypt on a curve rests on one belief: that finding a count a from gᵃ, or from aP, is slow. In 1994 a method was found that does it fast on a quantum computer, a machine that does not yet exist at the size needed. Someone who records today’s traffic can read it once such a machine is built. So on 13 August 2024 the United States published three new standards, and two of them rest on a different belief, about rings like ℤ_q[x]/(xⁿ + 1): that a list of equations on a clock, each off by a small error, cannot be solved. This page builds that ring, solves a small system without errors, shows how errors hide it, sends one bit with them by hand, and counts the sizes of the keys in the standard.

A quantum computer would find the order of a member of a clock fast, and that breaks the secrets of 43l and 44·crypt. In ℤ_q[x]/(xⁿ + 1), xⁿ = −1, so multiplying wraps round with a change of sign. Equations As = b on a clock are solved by row moves; equations As + e = b with a small unknown error e are believed hard, and that belief, not a proof, carries the new standards. One bit is sent by adding q/2 or 0 to a noisy sum and rounding. This e is not 17e's number e.

44now21What a Quantum Computer Would Break

The fast quantum method, held in trust and named in the cellar, does one thing: it finds the order r of a member g on a clock, the least r with gʳ = 1, in a number of steps that grows like a power of the number of digits. Finding the count a from gᵃ, on a clock or on a curve, is done the same way, and so is splitting a number into primes.

Order-splits technique: if g has even order r on the clock of N and g^(r/2) is not −1 there, then the greatest common divisors of g^(r/2) − 1 and g^(r/2) + 1 with N are factors of N other than 1 and N. N divides g^r − 1 = (g^(r/2) − 1)(g^(r/2) + 1), but neither factor alone, since r is the least order and g^(r/2) ≠ −1.

2^1, 2^2, \ldots, 2^6 = 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 11,\ 1 \text{ on the clock of } 21 @@ the order of 2 is r = 6
2^{3} = 8 \ne -1 = 20 @@ the condition holds
\gcd(8 - 1, 21) = 7,\ \ \gcd(8 + 1, 21) = 3 @@ 4j’s greatest common divisor
\htmlClass{new}{21 = 3 \cdot 7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the order-splits technique

On an ordinary computer, finding the order is the slow part: for a number of 600 digits no fast way is known. The quantum method makes that part fast. Every secret built on a hidden count in a group that turns about falls to it, and the new codes are built in a different way.

44now22A Ring That Wraps With a Sign

Take the clock ℤ_q = ℤ/q and the quotient ring R_q = ℤ_q[x]/(xⁿ + 1) of 44b. Its members are polynomials of degree below n with coefficients on the clock of q.

Sign-wrap technique: in ℤ_q[x]/(xⁿ + 1), xⁿ = −1, so a product is found by multiplying as polynomials and then replacing each xⁿ⁺ᵏ by −xᵏ. This is 44c’s remainder on dividing by xⁿ + 1, done in one step, since xⁿ⁺ᵏ = xᵏ(xⁿ + 1) − xᵏ.

(1 + 2x + x^3)(3 + x^2) = 3 + 6x + x^2 + 5x^3 + x^5 @@ multiplied as polynomials
x^5 = x \cdot x^4 = -x @@ the sign-wrap technique
3 + 6x - x + x^2 + 5x^3 @@ the x⁵ comes back as −x
\htmlClass{new}{3 + 5x + x^2 + 5x^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the product in R₁₇

The standard takes n = 256 and q = 3329. Then q − 1 = 3328 = 13 · 256, so by 44g’s whole-hand technique the clock of 3329 has a member of order 256: 17 is one, with 17¹²⁸ = −1. This lets x²⁵⁶ + 1 split on that clock into 128 factors of degree 2, and multiplying in R_q is then done factor by factor, which is far faster than multiplying out.

44now23Errors Hide a Secret

Without errors, a list of equations As = b on a prime clock is solved by row moves, as on 27j, since the clock is a field (44b). With a small error added to each, the moves smear the errors across the answer.

Noise-hides technique: from A and b = As on a prime clock, row moves find s; from A and b = As + e, with each entry of e small and unknown, the same moves give a wrong s, and no fast way is known to find s. The first part is 27j. The second is a belief: the hardness of learning with errors, believed and not proved, and believed also for quantum computers.

A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 5 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ \ As = (13, 12, 3) @@ 3 + 10, 9 + 20 = 29 = 12, 15 + 5 = 20 = 3
e = (1, -1, 0),\ \ b = As + e = (14, 11, 3) @@ the errors are small: −1, 0 or 1
\text{the first two rows, solved by row moves:}\ \ (0, 7) @@ the wrong secret
\htmlClass{new}{\text{third row: } 5 \cdot 0 + 7 = 7 \ne 3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the errors spread; from b without e, the moves give (3, 5)

With two unknowns, trying all 17² = 289 choices of s finds it. The standard has 768 unknowns on the clock of 3329, and the best known ways, on ordinary or quantum machines, take more than 2¹⁶⁰ steps. In R_q the 768 unknowns are 3 members of R_q, a list in the module R_q³ (44i): one multiplication in R_q does the work of a 256 by 256 grid.

44now24Sending One Bit Through the Noise

The receiver keeps s and publishes A and b = As + e. To send a bit m, the sender picks a list r of 0s and 1s, sends u = rA and v = r·b + m·8 on the clock of 17, where 8 is about half of 17.

Round-off technique: v − u·s = r·e + m·⌊q/2⌋, so if r·e is smaller than q/4, the receiver reads m = 0 when v − u·s is nearer 0 than q/2, and m = 1 when it is nearer q/2. v − u·s = r(As + e) + m⌊q/2⌋ − rAs, by regrouping in the clock. The sender’s r is never sent, and u and v look like any numbers to a listener, by the noise-hides technique.

u = \text{row}_1 + \text{row}_3 = (6, 3),\ \ v = 14 + 3 + 8 = 25 = 8 @@ on the clock of 17
u \cdot s = 6 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 5 = 33 = 16 @@ the receiver uses its s
v - u \cdot s = 8 - 16 = -8 = 9 @@ r·e = 1 + 0 = 1, plus 8
\htmlClass{new}{9 \text{ is nearer } 8 \text{ than } 0:\ \ m = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ with m = 0, v = 0 and v − u·s = 1, nearer 0

The standard sends a secret of 256 bits this way at once, one bit on each coefficient of a member of R_q, and then the two sides use it as the key for ordinary fast codes. The errors are chosen so that r·e reaches q/4 less than once in 2¹⁶⁴ tries.

44now25The Sizes of the Standard

Module-size technique: a list of k members of R_q, with n coefficients each of b bits, takes k · n · b / 8 bytes. Each member of R_q is its n coefficients (44i: R_q is free over ℤ_q with basis 1, x, …, xⁿ⁻¹), and q = 3329 is below 2¹² = 4096, so 12 bits hold each.

3 \cdot 256 \cdot 12 / 8 = 1152 @@ the public b, in bytes
1152 + 32 = 1184 @@ A is not sent: both sides grow it from 32 bytes of seed
3 \cdot 256 \cdot 10 / 8 + 256 \cdot 4 / 8 = 960 + 128 = 1088 @@ u and v, rounded to 10 and 4 bits
\htmlClass{new}{1184 \text{ bytes of key},\ \ 1088 \text{ of message}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against 32 or 33 bytes for 44·crypt’s curve

So the new keys are about 36 times as long as a curve’s, and much faster to use. Of the three standards of 2024, one shares keys this way, one signs messages with errors on the same ring, and one signs using nothing but scrambling functions, a fall-back that rests on no ring at all, should the belief about errors ever fail.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The fast quantum method is Shor’s algorithm, and the order-splits technique is its classical half. The noise-hides technique is the learning with errors problem, LWE; on R_q it is Ring-LWE, and on lists of members of R_q Module-LWE. The scheme of 44·now2.4 is Regev’s public-key encryption. The fast multiplying is the number-theoretic transform. The three standards of 13 August 2024 are FIPS 203, ML-KEM, from the scheme CRYSTALS-Kyber; FIPS 204, ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium; and FIPS 205, SLH-DSA, from SPHINCS+, based on hash functions. The sizes are those of ML-KEM-768. Recording today to read later is “harvest now, decrypt later”.

Who said it first

Peter Shor found his algorithm in 1994. Miklós Ajtai linked codes to hard problems on lattices in 1996. Oded Regev defined learning with errors in 2005 and showed that solving it would solve hard lattice problems in the worst case, by a quantum argument. Vadim Lyubashevsky, Chris Peikert and Regev gave the ring form in 2010. The National Institute of Standards and Technology ran an open competition from 2016 and published the three standards in 2024.

A proof that is not allowed

“Learning with errors is as hard as the hardest lattice problems, so it is proved safe.” Not allowed. What was proved is a link: a fast way to learn with errors would give a fast way for the lattice problems. That those problems are hard is itself believed, not proved, after decades of attempts. The link moves the belief to a better studied place; it does not remove it.
