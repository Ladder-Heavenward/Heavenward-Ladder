Is Learning a Kind of Composing?

Is learning a kind of composing? A machine that learns, as rung 28 built it, runs forward to an answer, measures its error, and carries the error backwards through its layers to learn how to turn each knob. The backward run works because of the chain rule: the slope of a chain is the product of the slopes along it. Read with rung 47’s eyes, the chain rule says something larger. Taking slopes is a map of categories: it sends running in a row to running in a row, and doing nothing to doing nothing. A pair of programs, one that goes forward and one that comes back with a correction, is a lens, a word that came from keeping two copies of data in step. Lenses run in a row, and the backward run of a learning machine is exactly two lenses run in a row, then two more. A learner is a lens with knobs, and two learners in a row are a learner, whose backward run is the layered one of rung 28. This was written down in 2017 and published in 2019. What is open is whether this way of seeing is only a description of the machines people build, or whether it can say which machines to build.

Slopes at a chosen point form a map of categories: D(g ∘ f) = D(g) · D(f), D(id) = I. A lens A ⇄ B is a pair get : A → B, put : A × B′ → A′; lenses compose by get₂ ∘ get₁ and put₁(a, put₂(get₁(a), c′)). The slope lens of f is (f, (x, b′) ↦ b′ · Df(x)), and taking it is a map of categories. With knobs added, two learners in a row are a learner, and its backward run is 28·now’s.

inf4.1The Chain Rule Is a Map of Categories

47b1 asked two things of a map of categories: it keeps running in a row, F(g ∘ f) = F(g) ∘ F(f), and it keeps doing nothing, F(id) = id. 47b1’s example was pointed spaces, each with a chosen point, and maps that send chosen point to chosen point. Do the same with flat spaces: the things are ℝⁿ with a chosen point p, and the arrows are smooth maps f with f(p) = q, the chosen point of the target. Send each such arrow to its slope grid at p, Df(p), an m × n grid, and each thing to ℝⁿ as a space of vectors.

Slope-map technique: taking slope grids at the chosen points is a map of categories from pointed flat spaces with smooth maps to spaces of vectors with grids.

D(g \circ f)(p) = Dg(f(p))\,Df(p) @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c2)
f(p) = q \ \Rightarrow\ D(g \circ f)(p) = Dg(q)\,Df(p) @@ arrows send chosen point to chosen point
D(\mathrm{id})(p) = I @@ the function that changes nothing has slope grid I (28c2)
\htmlClass{new}{D(g \circ f) = D(g)\,D(f),\ \ D(\mathrm{id}) = I} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two demands of 47b1

With one input and one output it is 13e4’s chain rule. Take f(x) = x² at p = 2 and g(y) = 3y + 1 at q = 4. Then g ∘ f is 3x² + 1, whose slope at 2 is 12, and the slopes along the chain are g′(4) = 3 and f′(2) = 4, with 3 · 4 = 12. The chain rule was a fact about slopes. Seen as a map of categories, it is a fact about running in a row.

inf4.2A Lens: Get and Put

A lens from A to B, with backward sets A′ and B′, is two functions: get : A → B, which goes forward, and put : A × B′ → A′, which takes where you were and something sent back, and returns something to send further back. Its name came from data. A record keeps a person’s name and age; a screen shows only the name. Get reads the name off the record. Put takes the old record and an edited name, and writes a new record: put((n, g), n′) = (n′, g).

That lens obeys two rules. Putting back what you got changes nothing: put((n, g), n) = (n, g). Getting what you put returns it: the name of put((n, g), n′) is n′. A record (Zoe, 14) with the name edited to Evita becomes (Evita, 14), and its name is Evita.

Now a lens of a different kind. A = B = ℝ, and A′ = B′ = ℝ hold slopes. Get is x ↦ x². Put is (x, b′) ↦ b′ · 2x: a slope b′ that arrives at the output is sent back to the input, multiplied by the slope of x² at the place you were. At x = 3, a slope 1 at the output goes back as 6. This lens obeys neither rule, and does not need to: A and A′ are different sets doing different jobs. What it keeps is the shape: forward, then back, remembering where it stood.

inf4.3Lenses in a Row

Two lenses meet end to start when the first’s B and B′ are the second’s. Run them in a row: get is get₂ after get₁. Put takes a and c′, lets the second lens send c′ back to where the first lens’s get landed, and lets the first lens send that on: put(a, c′) = put₁(a, put₂(get₁(a), c′)).

Lens-chain technique: lenses in a row are a lens; running them in a row does not care about brackets; and the lens with get the identity and put (a, a′) ↦ a′ does nothing on both sides. So lenses are a category, as 47a1 asks.

\mathrm{put}_{(3\,2)\,1}(a, d') = \mathrm{put}_1(a,\ \mathrm{put}_{3\,2}(\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ d')) @@ the rule, with the second lens 3 after 2
\phantom{\mathrm{put}_{(3\,2)\,1}(a, d')} = \mathrm{put}_1(a,\ \mathrm{put}_2(\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ \mathrm{put}_3(\mathrm{get}_2\,\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ d'))) @@ the rule again, inside
\mathrm{put}_{3\,(2\,1)}(a, d') = \mathrm{put}_{2\,1}(a,\ \mathrm{put}_3(\mathrm{get}_2\,\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ d')) @@ the rule, with the first lens 2 after 1
\phantom{\mathrm{put}_{3\,(2\,1)}(a, d')} = \mathrm{put}_1(a,\ \mathrm{put}_2(\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ \mathrm{put}_3(\mathrm{get}_2\,\mathrm{get}_1 a,\ d'))) @@ the rule again, outside
\htmlClass{new}{(L_3 \circ L_2) \circ L_1 = L_3 \circ (L_2 \circ L_1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gets agree by 9b’s brackets; the puts are the same line

Run the slope lens of x² and then the slope lens of y ↦ 3y. Get is 3x². Put sends c′ back through y ↦ 3y as 3c′, then through x² as 3c′ · 2x = 6x c′. And 6x is the slope of 3x²: at x = 2 a slope 1 at the end arrives as 12. The doing-nothing lens: put₁(a, a′) with put₁ (a, a′) ↦ a′ on either side leaves put alone, so it does nothing.

inf4.4The Backward Pass Is a Lens

For a smooth f : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ, its slope lens has get f and put (x, b′) ↦ b′ · Df(x), with b′ a row of m slopes sent back as a row of n. 28·now.3 carried one row of slopes back from the error, multiplying by each layer’s slope grid in turn: gk−1 = gk Σk Wk. That is a chain of slope lenses, run in a row.

Slope-lens technique: sending f to its slope lens is a map of categories from flat spaces with smooth maps to lenses: the slope lens of g after f is the slope lens of g run after the slope lens of f.

\mathrm{put}_{g \circ f}(x, c') = \mathrm{put}_f(x,\ \mathrm{put}_g(f(x),\ c')) @@ the lens-chain rule
\phantom{\mathrm{put}_{g \circ f}(x, c')} = (c'\,Dg(f(x)))\,Df(x) @@ each put is a row times a slope grid
\phantom{\mathrm{put}_{g \circ f}(x, c')} = c'\,(Dg(f(x))\,Df(x)) @@ grids in a row do not care about brackets (27d2)
\phantom{\mathrm{put}_{g \circ f}(x, c')} = c'\,D(g \circ f)(x) @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the slope lens of } g \circ f \text{ is the chain of the slope lenses}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gets are g ∘ f on both sides

The order of work follows. Forward, each lens stores where it stood; backward, each lens uses only what it stored and the row that arrives. That is why 28·now.3’s backward step costs about what the forward step cost. The slope-map technique of the first footing ran forward, multiplying grids from the input; this one runs back, multiplying rows from the output. Both are the chain rule, and the second is the one machines use.

inf4.5A Learner Is a Lens With Knobs

A learner has knobs as well as an input. Its get takes a setting p of the knobs and an input a to an output b. Its put takes p, a and a slope b′ sent back from the error, and returns two slopes: one for the knobs, saying how to turn them, and one for the input, to send further back. It is a lens from the pair (knobs, input) to the output.

One unit: b = w x, error ℓ = ½(b − t)². Take x = 2, t = 1, w = 0.75. Forward, b = 1.5 and ℓ = 0.125. The slope of ℓ at b is b − t = 0.5. Put returns 0.5 · x = 1 for the knob and 0.5 · w = 0.375 for the input. 28·now.4’s walk downhill turns the knob by a step ε = 0.1 against its slope: w becomes 0.75 − 0.1 · 1 = 0.65. Run again: b = 1.3, ℓ = 0.045. The error fell.

One-step-learner technique: for a learner b = w x with error ½(b − t)², the knob’s slope is (b − t) x and the input’s slope is (b − t) w; and a step against the knob’s slope small enough lowers the error unless the slope is 0.

\tfrac{d\ell}{db} = b - t @@ the chain technique of 13e, on ½(b − t)²
\tfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial w} = (b - t)\,x,\ \ \tfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial x} = (b - t)\,w @@ b = wx: the slope in w is x, in x is w
\ell(w - \varepsilon s) = \ell(w) - \varepsilon s^2 + \tfrac12 \varepsilon^2 s^2 x^2 @@ s = (b − t)x; expand the square
\htmlClass{new}{0 \lt \varepsilon \lt 2/x^2,\ s \ne 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \ell \text{ falls}} \quad \blacksquare @@ −εs² + ½ε²s²x² < 0 exactly then

With x = 2 the step must be below 2/4 = 0.5, and 0.1 is. The knob and the input are treated alike by put; only the update rule tells them apart, turning the knobs and passing the input’s slope back to whoever made the input.

inf4.6Two Learners in a Row Make Layers

Run two learners in a row. The knobs are both sets of knobs. Forward, the first learner’s output is the second’s input. Backward, the second learner returns a slope for its knobs and a slope for its input, and the slope for its input is what the first learner receives as the slope at its output.

Learner-chain technique: two learners in a row are a learner, whose knobs are the pair of knob settings; its put is the second learner’s put followed by the first’s, fed the second’s input slope; and the knob slopes it returns are the slopes of the error in each knob. The last clause is the slope-lens technique with the knobs carried along as inputs that are not passed on.

Take b = w₁ x and c = w₂ b, with x = 1, w₁ = 2, w₂ = 0.5 and t = 2. Forward: b = 2, c = 1. Back: the slope at c is c − t = −1. The second learner returns −1 · b = −2 for w₂ and −1 · w₂ = −0.5 for its input. The first learner receives −0.5 and returns −0.5 · x = −0.5 for w₁. Check directly: ℓ = ½(w₂ w₁ x − t)² has slope (c − t) w₁ x = −2 in w₂ and (c − t) w₂ x = −0.5 in w₁.

c = w_2 w_1 x = 1,\ \ \tfrac{d\ell}{dc} = c - t = -1 @@ forward, then the error’s slope
\mathrm{put}_2:\ \ (-1 \cdot b,\ -1 \cdot w_2) = (-2,\ -0.5) @@ knob slope, input slope
\mathrm{put}_1:\ \ (-0.5 \cdot x,\ -0.5 \cdot w_1) = (-0.5,\ -1) @@ fed −0.5 at its output
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial w_2} = -2,\ \ \tfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial w_1} = -0.5} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same as the direct slopes

Stack D learners and the chain is 28·now’s layered machine, with each layer’s grid Wk as its knobs. The layers can be counted, but the backward run never asks how they are arranged. Each learner sees only what arrives and what it stored. The arrangement hides inside the running in a row, and what is learned is the plan of the whole.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞4.oq.1Every machine that learns by slopes can be written as learners in a row and side by side. Does the writing say anything a builder would not have known? The open question is whether the shapes that work, the layers of today’s large machines, follow from such a description, or are only described by it after they were found by trial.
∞4.oq.2The slope lens obeys neither rule of the record lens. Is there a family of lenses, between the two, whose rules say something true about learning: that a step against the slope never makes the error worse, for instance? Nobody has found rules of that kind that hold for the machines in use.
∞4.oq.3Rung 28’s walk downhill turns every knob by the same rule. The rules in use change the step as they go, by what earlier slopes were. Written as lenses, those rules carry a memory from step to step. What is the right picture of a learner with a memory, and does it still run in a row as cleanly?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The backward pass is older than the machines it now trains. A 1970 master’s thesis gave the reverse mode of differentiation, carrying slopes back through any chain of operations, with code, as a way of studying rounding errors in calculations. A paper of 1976 restated it. Its use for machines with layers came later, and who deserves credit for that use is still argued.

Lenses came from databases. A screen that shows part of a record must write edits back into the record, and many edits have no single right way back. A 2005 conference paper gave a language of small two-way programs, each a get and a put, that compose into large ones, with two rules for good behaviour; a journal version followed in 2007. The name lens stuck, and the rules became the measure of a two-way program.

In late 2017 a preprint described a learner as knobs, a forward run, an update and a request sent back, showed that learners compose, and showed that the rule of walking downhill by slopes sends every smooth map with knobs to a learner, keeping running in a row. It was published at a logic conference in 2019 under a title that called backpropagation a map of categories. A 2021 preprint, published in 2022, rebuilt the account on lenses with knobs and covered the step rules and error measures in daily use.

The account explains two things the machines already did: why the backward pass costs about what the forward one costs, and why machines can be built from parts and trained as one. Whether it can tell anyone how to build a better machine is the first open question above. The page proves the composing, and leaves the building open.

The accepted names

Taking derivatives at a point as a map of categories is the chain rule as functoriality. The lens is a (bidirectional) lens, from the database view-update problem; its two laws are GetPut and PutGet, and a lens obeying both is well-behaved. Slope lenses are the lenses of a reverse derivative category; a learner is a parametrised lens, built by the Para construction; the backward pass is reverse-mode automatic differentiation, or backpropagation; the walk downhill is gradient descent.

Who said it first

Geoffrey Cruttwell, Bruno Gavranović, Neil Ghani, Paul Wilson and Fabio Zanasi, “Categorical Foundations of Gradient-Based Learning” (arXiv 2021; ESOP 2022).

Earlier Proofs, in Arrows

13e4’s chain-rule proof and 28c2’s chain of flat maps are the analysis under ∞4.1’s slope-map technique. Once they are done, the arrow statement is one line: taking slopes sends g ∘ f to Dg ∘ Df and the do-nothing map to the do-nothing grid, which is what a map of categories must do. The helper w and the leftover bounds are the price of that line; the line is why a chain of any length has the product of its slopes, by induction on the row with the brackets free.
