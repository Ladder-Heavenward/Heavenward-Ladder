How Many Points Are on a Line?

How many points are on a line? This rung found that the answer is the size of all the parts of the counting numbers, and that whether that size is the first one past the counting numbers cannot be settled by the axioms. That was known by the middle of the nineteen sixties. Many took it as the end of the question. Others did not. If the axioms leave a question open, perhaps they are not all the axioms there should be, as the ladder found when it needed to lay down replacement to count past the endless. So the search became a search for new axioms: statements that most people who work with sets would accept, and that settle what the old ones leave open. Two very different kinds of new axiom were proposed, one about how rich the world of sets is and one about how tidy its sets of reals are. Each was shown to make the line’s size the second size past the counting numbers, not the first. In two thousand and twenty one, one was shown to imply the other. Meanwhile a different school argued that there is no one world of sets to find axioms for, only many, and that the question has been answered already: in some worlds yes, in others no. This page sets out what is proved, what is proposed, and what is still argued.

The size of the line cannot be the top of a countable climb of smaller sizes, and beyond that the axioms leave it open. Two strong new axioms, of very different kinds, each make it the second size past the counting numbers, and since 2021 one is known to imply the other. Whether there is one world of sets, in which the question has an answer, or many, is still argued.

35now1What the Axioms Do Say

35f held in trust that ω₁ exists. In the same way there is a least point of the run too large to go one-to-one into ω₁, written ω₂, and on: ω₃, ω₄, …, with sizes ℵ₂, ℵ₃, ℵ₄, …. Replace (29·cb3) gathers them into one set, and their merge is a point ω_ω, of size ℵ_ω. It is the top of a countable climb: a union of countably many sets, each smaller than it.

No-countable-climb technique: with choice, the line is not a union of countably many sets each smaller than the line, so 2^ℵ₀ is not ℵ_ω.

35now2One World of Sets, or Many

One view holds that there is one world of sets, that 2^ℵ₀ has one value in it, and that the task is to find axioms true there; the other holds that every world the methods build is as real as any other, and that the question is answered by listing the worlds.

The first view takes the axioms as a description, still incomplete, of one thing. Its programme since the 1970s has been to find a world like 6d3’s described world, built in stages from below, but large enough to hold every large size that has been proposed (35·now.4). If such a world is the right one, as its supporters argue, then 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁, since it is so in every world built that way.

The second view was set out in 2012. It reads the 1963 method as a way of moving between worlds, every one of them a world of sets, and none the true one. On that view, the question “is 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁?” has been settled as fully as it can be: it is true in some worlds and false in others, and it is known how to reach each from the other. Asking which is right is, on this view, like asking which of rung 29’s geometries is the right one.

35now3Two Strong Axioms, and ℵ₂

Two new axioms of very different kinds each make 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂, and in 2021 it was proved that the stronger form of the first implies the second.

  • The first kind says the world is rich: any set the 1963 method could build, by a construction that keeps ω₁ where it is, already exists. The strongest such axiom was proposed in 1988, and the same paper proved that it makes 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂.
  • The second kind says the sets of reals and of countable points are as tidy as they can be: a statement about them that some tame world could make true is already true. It was proposed in 1999, built on large sizes, and it too makes 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂.
  • For twenty years the two were thought likely to clash, or to be unrelated. A paper of 2021 proved that a slightly stronger form of the first axiom implies the second. Two routes, one from richness and one from tidiness, reach the same place.
  • So the strongest proposed axioms of these two kinds agree on the size of the line: ℵ₂. The described-world programme of 35·now.2 points the other way, to ℵ₁. Neither side has persuaded the other.

35now4Large Sizes, Named

35c3 found that sizes climb for ever. Some sizes are so large that the axioms cannot prove they exist, as 3f found for an endless set before the axiom of infinity was laid down. They are laid down as axioms, one at a time, and each is earned by what it decides.

  • A size κ is out of reach when it is not a union of fewer than κ smaller sets, and 2 to every smaller size is still smaller than κ. The layers of 29·cb3 below such a size form a world in which every axiom the ladder has laid down is true, so the axioms cannot prove it exists. ℵ₀ is the one out of reach that the axiom of infinity already gives.
  • Larger still are sizes defined by how they treat their own parts: sizes κ on which a two-valued chance (rung 30) can sit: every part of κ gets chance 0 or 1, every single point gets 0, and chances add over fewer than κ separate pieces. These were proposed in 1930.
  • Larger again are the sizes named for the mathematician who found, in the 1980s, that they settle almost every question about the sets of reals that can be described: whether every such set has a length, and whether games on the reals have winning plans.
  • None of them settles 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁. It was proved in 1967 that the 1963 method leaves large sizes as they were, so with any of them in hand the line can still be made of size ℵ₁ or ℵ₂.

35now5What Is Shown, and What Is Open

The axioms leave the size of the line open past what this rung proved; the strongest new axioms of two kinds agree on ℵ₂, a rival programme points to ℵ₁, and whether there is one answer at all is still argued.

  • Shown, 1905. 2^ℵ₀ is not the top of a countable climb (35·now.1).
  • Shown, 1938 and 1963. Neither 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁ nor its denial follows from the axioms (35f3).
  • Shown, 1967. Large sizes do not settle it.
  • Shown, 1988 and 1999. Two kinds of strong axiom each make 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂.
  • Shown, 2021. The stronger form of the first kind implies the second.
  • Proposed, from the 1970s on. A described world large enough for every large size, in which 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁. Whether it can be built is open.
  • Argued, 2012 on. Whether there is one world of sets or many, and so whether the question has one answer.

The accepted names

The no-countable-climb technique is König’s theorem, in the form cf(2^ℵ₀) > ℵ₀. The view of one world of sets is the universe view, and Woodin’s programme for it is Ultimate L; the view of many is the set-theoretic multiverse. The first kind of axiom is a forcing axiom: Martin’s Maximum (MM) and its strengthening MM⁺⁺. The second is Woodin’s axiom (*), from the theory of ℙ_max. A size out of reach is a strongly inaccessible cardinal; the sizes carrying a two-valued chance are measurable cardinals; those that settle the described sets of reals are Woodin cardinals, and what they settle is projective determinacy. The result of 1967 is the Lévy–Solovay theorem. All of these are large cardinals.

Who said it first

Julius König proved the 1905 theorem; his proof is the diagonal of 35·now.1. Felix Hausdorff named the out-of-reach sizes in 1908, and Wacław Sierpiński and Alfred Tarski gave them their modern form in 1930. Stanisław Ulam defined measurable cardinals in 1930. Azriel Lévy and Robert Solovay proved in 1967 that small forcing keeps large cardinals. Matthew Foreman, Menachem Magidor and Saharon Shelah introduced Martin’s Maximum in 1988 and proved it gives 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂. W. Hugh Woodin introduced (*) in 1999, and with Donald Martin and John Steel proved projective determinacy from Woodin cardinals in 1985 to 1988. Joel David Hamkins set out the multiverse view in “The set-theoretic multiverse” (2012). David Asperó and Ralf Schindler proved that MM⁺⁺ implies (*) in “Martin’s Maximum⁺⁺ implies Woodin’s axiom (*)”, Annals of Mathematics (2021).
