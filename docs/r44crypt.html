How Do Earbuds Share a Secret in Public?

How do earbuds share a secret in public? A pair of earbuds taken out of a box on a bus in Portland and paired with a phone has never met it before, and everything the two say to each other goes out by radio, where anyone nearby can listen. Within a second they share a secret key that nobody listening can work out, and they check, by showing six digits on the phone, that no third device slipped in between them. Then they talk by hopping across 79 radio channels, sixteen hundred times a second, in an order only the two of them know. Rung forty one agreed on a secret in public with powers on a prime clock. The earbuds do the same with the points of a curve over a finite field, which can be added like numbers and give the same safety with much shorter keys. This page builds the adding of points, agrees on a secret with a small curve by hand, counts the chance that a device in the middle fools the six digits, and counts what the hopping buys.

The points of y² = x³ + ax + b over a prime clock, with one far point, can be added by drawing lines, and they form a group. Two devices each keep a secret count, send that many copies of a public point, and each multiplies what it receives by its own count: both reach the same point. A device in the middle is caught by six digits both screens show, with a chance of one in a million of slipping through. Hopping over 79 channels leaves a listener on one channel a seventy ninth of the talk.

44crypt1A Curve With Finitely Many Points

Over the prime clock ℤ/17, take the curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2. Its points are the pairs (x, y) of the clock that make it true, and one more, the far point O, which the next footing needs.

Square-count technique: for each x, the curve has two points if x³ + 2x + 2 is a square other than 0 on the clock, one if it is 0, and none if it is not a square. On ℤ/17 the squares other than 0 are 1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16, each the square of two hours (43f).

x = 0:\ 2 = 6^2 \Rightarrow (0, 6), (0, 11) @@ 36 = 2 on the clock of 17
x = 5:\ 125 + 10 + 2 = 137 = 1 \Rightarrow (5, 1), (5, 16) @@ 137 = 8 · 17 + 1
\text{nine values of } x \text{ give a square other than } 0,\ \text{none give } 0 @@ checking the 17 values of x³ + 2x + 2 against the squares
\htmlClass{new}{9 \cdot 2 + 1 = 19 \text{ points, with } O} \quad \blacksquare @@ the far point

Each x gives a square about half the time, so a curve over ℤ/p has about p + 1 points: never further from p + 1 than 2√p, a technique proved much higher up the ladder.

44crypt2Adding Points

Draw the line through two points P and Q of the curve. Put it into the curve: a cubic in x, with zeros at P’s x and Q’s x, so a third zero R (44c3’s factor technique). The sum P + Q is R flipped, (x_R, −y_R). P + O = P; P plus its flip is O; and P + P uses the tangent line.

Chord-add technique: with slope λ = (y_Q − y_P)/(x_Q − x_P), or λ = (3x_P² + a)/(2y_P) when Q = P, the sum has x = λ² − x_P − x_Q and y = λ(x_P − x) − y_P. The cubic’s three zeros add to λ², by the sum-of-zeros technique of 9g, which gives x.

\lambda = \frac{3 \cdot 25 + 2}{2 \cdot 1} = 77 \cdot 2^{-1} = 9 \cdot 9 = 81 = 13 @@ the tangent; 77 = 9 and 2⁻¹ = 9 on the clock of 17
x = 13^2 - 5 - 5 = 159 = 6 @@ 169 − 10; 159 = 9 · 17 + 6
y = 13(5 - 6) - 1 = -14 = 3 @@ on the clock
\htmlClass{new}{2P = (6, 3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 9 = 3², and 216 + 12 + 2 = 230 = 9 on the clock

With this adding the points form a group that turns about, with O as its nothing. Held in trust: that the adding regroups, which takes a page of algebra. The group here has 19 members, a prime, so by 19d every point but O has order 19, and the multiples of P run through all 18 other points.

44crypt3A Secret From Public Points

Shared-multiple technique: if one device keeps a count a and sends aP, and the other keeps b and sends bP, then a(bP) = b(aP) = abP, which both can find and a listener who knows P, aP and bP is believed unable to find. The first part is regrouping in the group: a copies of b copies of P are ab copies. The second is a belief, as on 43l: no fast way is known to find a from P and aP.

a = 3:\ \ A = 3P = (10, 6) @@ the phone’s secret count, and the point it sends
b = 7:\ \ B = 7P = (0, 6) @@ the earbuds’ secret count, and the point they send
3B = 21P,\ \ 7A = 21P @@ each multiplies what it received by its own count
\htmlClass{new}{21P = 2P = (6, 3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 19P = O, so 21P = 2P

Real devices use a curve over a prime clock of about 2²⁵⁶, and counts of 256 bits. Finding aP takes about 256 doublings and at most 256 additions, by 43h’s halving trick done with adding. The best known way to find a from aP takes about 2¹²⁸ steps. A key on 43l’s prime clock needs about 3000 bits for the same safety, and the curve’s shorter keys are why small devices use them.

44crypt4A Device in the Middle

A device in the middle M can catch the phone’s A and send its own M₁ to the earbuds, and catch their B and send M₂ to the phone. Each side then shares a secret with M, and M passes everything on, reading it. The counts do not help: they were never checked against who sent them.

Six-digit technique: if both devices show six digits made from both public points and a fresh random number each committed to before seeing the other’s, then a device in the middle makes the two screens agree with chance one in a million. M must commit to its numbers before it learns what the phone will send, so the two six-digit numbers it causes are, as far as it can steer them, unrelated draws from a million.

\text{phone shows } f(A, M_1, \ldots),\ \ \text{earbuds show } f(M_2, B, \ldots) @@ different inputs once M is in the middle
\text{chance the six digits agree} = 10^{-6} @@ 30f: each of 10⁶ values equally likely, independently
\text{the person presses yes only when they agree} @@ the check is a person reading two screens
\htmlClass{new}{\text{M is caught except once in a million tries}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and each failed try is seen

Earbuds with no screen cannot show digits. They pair with no check, trusting that nobody is in the middle during the few seconds of pairing, or use the opening of the case beside the phone as the check.

44crypt5Hopping in an Order Only the Pair Knows

The earbuds and phone talk on 79 channels, each 1 MHz wide, from 2402 to 2480 MHz. They hop to a new channel every 625 microseconds, 1600 times a second, in an order computed from the phone’s address and its clock, which both share.

Hop-share technique: a receiver or a jammer fixed on one channel meets, on average, one slot in 79 of a pair hopping over 79 channels evenly. Each slot’s channel is one of 79, equally often over a long run, so the share of slots on any one is 1/79 (30f).

1600 \text{ slots a second},\ \ \tfrac{1}{79} \text{ on the jammed channel} @@ the hop-share technique
1600 / 79 \approx 20 \text{ slots a second lost} @@ about 1.3%
\text{a lost slot is sent again} @@ the receiver did not confirm it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the music plays on}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a narrow jammer, or a microwave oven, spoils one channel, not the link

The order hides nothing from a listener with a wideband radio that hears all 79 channels at once, so the hopping is not the secret. The key from the shared-multiple technique is. Hopping spreads the talk so that no one narrow source of noise can stop it.

The accepted names

The curve is an elliptic curve over a finite field, and its points with the point at infinity O form the group E(F_p). The chord-add technique is the chord-and-tangent law. The shared-multiple technique is elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, ECDH; finding a from aP is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem, and the 2¹²⁸ steps are Pollard’s rho method. The bound |#E − (p + 1)| ≤ 2√p is Hasse’s theorem. Pairing with six digits is numeric comparison in Bluetooth Secure Simple Pairing and LE Secure Connections, and the hopping is frequency-hopping spread spectrum. The curve over a prime of about 2²⁵⁶ is P-256.

Who said it first

Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller proposed curves for cryptography independently in 1985. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published agreeing in public in 1976. Helmut Hasse proved his bound in 1933. Hedy Lamarr and George Antheil patented frequency hopping, with a paper roll like a player piano’s, in 1942. Bluetooth took its name from Harald Bluetooth, a king of Denmark; its first standard is from 1999, and numeric comparison came with version 2.1 in 2007.

A proof that is not allowed

“The group has 19 points, so a secret count can only be one of 19, and the scheme is broken.” Not allowed as a judgement of the scheme: it is broken for this curve, which is a toy for working by hand. The argument shows only that the group must be large. Real curves have about 2²⁵⁶ points, and trying them is hopeless.
