Why Does Fourier Work on Clocks, Circles and Lines Alike?

Why does Fourier work on clocks, circles and lines alike? Rung 32 built three transforms: coefficients for a function that repeats, the transform on the whole line, and the stepped transform on N points. Each broke a signal into turnings, and each was rebuilt from them. They are one transform. A turning of a group is a map from it into the circle of size 1 that turns combining into multiplying. The turnings of a group form a group of their own: for the clock of N it is a clock of N, for the circle it is the whole numbers, for the whole numbers it is the circle, for the line it is the line. And the turnings of the turnings are the group again, with no choice made, as rung 47 found for readings of readings. Rung 41 factored a number by finding a period; that is finding a hidden part-group of a clock by this transform. For groups whose combining does not turn about, the same question is open, and an answer would break the lattice locks of rung 44.

A turning of a turn-about group G is χ : G → {|z| = 1} with χ(g + h) = χ(g)χ(h); the turnings form a group Ĝ. The clock: χₖ(j) = ωjk, so Ĝ ≅ ℤ/N. The whole numbers: n ↦ zⁿ, so the dual is the circle. Products: (G × H)^ ≅ Ĝ × Ĥ. Finite G: Ĝ ≅ G by a choice, G ≅ Ĝ̂ naturally. Σg χ(g) = 0 unless χ = 1, so f(g) = (1/|G|) Σχ f̂(χ) χ(g). The transform of a coset of H lives on the turnings that are 1 on H.

inf20.1Turnings of a Clock

Let G be a group whose combining turns about, written +. A turning of G is a map χ from G to the complex numbers of size 1 with χ(g + h) = χ(g) χ(h). Two turnings multiply value by value, and the product is a turning; so the turnings of G form a group, written Ĝ, with the turning that is always 1 as its do-nothing.

Clock-turnings technique: the turnings of ℤ/N are χₖ(j) = ωjk for k = 0, …, N − 1, and χₖ χₗ = χₖ₊ₗ: the turnings of a clock of N are a clock of N.

\chi(j) = \chi(1)^{j} @@ χ(1 + ⋯ + 1) = χ(1)⋯χ(1)
\chi(1)^{N} = \chi(0) = 1 \Rightarrow \chi(1) = \omega^{k},\ 0 \le k < N @@ the Nth roots of 1 (21·cb20)
\chi_k(j) = \omega^{jk} \text{ is a turning} @@ ω^{(i+j)k} = ω^{ik}ω^{jk}, and well defined since ω^{Nk} = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\chi_k \chi_l = \chi_{k+l},\ \widehat{\mathbb Z/N} \cong \mathbb Z/N} \quad \blacksquare @@ ω^{jk}ω^{jl} = ω^{j(k+l)}

32k1’s stepped transform is Xk = Σ xj ω−jk: the list read against each turning χₖ, with the turning’s value taken backwards.

inf20.2Whole Numbers, Circle and Line

On the whole numbers ℤ a turning is again fixed by its value at 1, but now nothing forces that value round to 1: any z of size 1 will do.

Whole-turnings technique: the turnings of ℤ are n ↦ zⁿ, one for each z on the circle, and they multiply as the z do: the dual of the whole numbers is the circle.

\chi(n) = \chi(1)^{n} \text{ for } n \ge 0,\quad \chi(-n) = \chi(1)^{-n} @@ χ(n)χ(−n) = χ(0) = 1
z^{m+n} = z^{m} z^{n} \text{ for every } |z| = 1 @@ each z gives a turning
z^{n} w^{n} = (zw)^{n} @@ multiplying turnings multiplies the z
\htmlClass{new}{\widehat{\mathbb Z} \cong \{|z| = 1\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one turning for each point of the circle

Turned the other way: the continuous turnings of the circle are 32a1’s eₙ(x) = einx, one for each whole number n, and the continuous turnings of the line are 32e1’s x ↦ e2πixξ, one for each real ξ. That continuity leaves no others is told here, not proved. So 32a’s coefficients are a function on the circle read against the turnings of the circle, and they live on ℤ; 32e1’s transform lives on the line again.

inf20.3A Product, and a Choice

Product-turnings technique: the turnings of G × H are the products χ(g)ψ(h) of a turning of G and a turning of H: (G × H)^ ≅ Ĝ × Ĥ.

\theta(g, h) = \theta(g, 0)\,\theta(0, h) @@ (g, h) = (g, 0) + (0, h)
\chi(g) = \theta(g, 0),\ \psi(h) = \theta(0, h) \text{ are turnings} @@ θ restricted to each factor
\chi(g)\psi(h) \text{ is a turning of } G \times H \text{ for any } \chi, \psi @@ both factors turn combining into multiplying
\htmlClass{new}{(G \times H)^{\wedge} \cong \widehat G \times \widehat H} \quad \blacksquare @@ θ ↔ (χ, ψ), both ways

By 44i4’s clock-list technique a finite turn-about group G is a copy of ℤ/d₁ × ⋯ × ℤ/dₖ. Its turnings are then a copy of the dual clocks side by side, which is a copy of the same clocks. So Ĝ ≅ G, and |Ĝ| = |G|. But the matching took two choices: a way of splitting G into clocks, and in each clock which turning to pair with 1. For ℤ/5, pairing 1 with χ₁ or with χ₂ gives different matchings, and nothing in the group prefers one. That is 47c3’s V and V*: matched, and not naturally.

inf20.4Turnings of the Turnings

Each g in G gives a turning of Ĝ: read every turning at g, ev(g)(χ) = χ(g). This needs no choice. It is 47c2’s double-reading technique with turnings in place of readings.

Double-turnings technique: ev : G → Ĝ̂ is natural, f̂̂ ∘ ev = ev ∘ f, and for finite G it is a matching.

\hat{\hat f}(\mathrm{ev}\,g)(\psi) = \mathrm{ev}\,g\,(\hat f \psi) = \psi(f(g)) = \mathrm{ev}(f g)(\psi) @@ unwind the two turnings round: the square closes
g \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{some } \chi \text{ has } \chi(g) \ne 1 @@ g has a coordinate c ≠ 0 in some clock ℤ/d of 44i4; take χ₁ there, ω^{c} ≠ 1
\mathrm{ev}\,g = \mathrm{ev}\,h \Rightarrow \mathrm{ev}(g - h) = 1 \Rightarrow g = h @@ ev turns combining into multiplying
|\hat{\hat G}| = |\hat G| = |G| @@ the last footing, twice
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{ev} : G \cong \hat{\hat G}\text{, naturally}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a one-to-one map between finite sets of one size is onto

The splitting into clocks was used to show ev is one-to-one, but ev itself was defined without it. For the circle and the line the same holds with continuous turnings: the turnings of ℤ are the circle, and the turnings of the circle are ℤ again. Told, not proved.

inf20.5One Transform

For a function f on a finite turn-about group G, its transform is the function on Ĝ given by f̂(χ) = Σg f(g) χ(g)*, where * is the mirror of a complex number; for size 1, χ(g)* = χ(g)⁻¹.

Turning-sum technique: Σg χ(g) is |G| when χ is the do-nothing turning and 0 otherwise; so f(g) = (1/|G|) Σχ f̂(χ) χ(g).

\chi(h) \ne 1 \text{ for some } h @@ χ is not the do-nothing turning
\chi(h)\,\textstyle\sum_g \chi(g) = \sum_g \chi(h + g) = \sum_g \chi(g) @@ g ↦ h + g only reorders G
(\chi(h) - 1)\textstyle\sum_g \chi(g) = 0 \Rightarrow \sum_g \chi(g) = 0 @@ χ(h) − 1 ≠ 0
\textstyle\sum_\chi \hat f(\chi)\chi(g) = \sum_{g^{\prime}} f(g^{\prime}) \sum_\chi \chi(g - g^{\prime}) @@ χ(g)χ(g′)* = χ(g − g′)
\htmlClass{new}{= |G|\, f(g)} \quad \blacksquare @@ by ev, Σ over χ of χ(g − g′) is |G| when g = g′ and 0 otherwise

For ℤ/N this is 32k1’s rebuilding, xj = (1/N) Σ Xk ωjk. For the circle the sum over G becomes a total with weight 1/2π and the sum over the turnings is a sum over ℤ: 32a1’s right-angled turnings. For the line both become totals. Three transforms, one shape: a group, its turnings, and the sum of a function read against each.

inf20.6Finding a Hidden Part-Group

A function on G hides a part-group H when it is the same on each coset x + H and different on different cosets. In 41·phys.6, x ↦ aˣ by N hides the multiples of the period r.

Hidden-part technique: the transform of the even mix over a coset x + H is nonzero exactly at the turnings χ with χ(h) = 1 for every h in H.

\textstyle\sum_{h \in H} \chi(x + h)^{*} = \chi(x)^{*} \sum_{h \in H} \chi(h)^{*} @@ χ turns sums into products
\chi \text{ restricted to } H \text{ is a turning of } H @@ H is a group of its own
\htmlClass{new}{= |H|\,\chi(x)^{*} \text{ if } \chi = 1 \text{ on } H\text{, else } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turning-sum technique, on H

Take G = ℤ/12 and r = 4, so H = {0, 4, 8}. χₖ is 1 on H when ω4k = 1, so 12 divides 4k: k = 0, 3, 6 or 9, the multiples of 12/r = 3. A reading after the transform gives one of them, whichever coset x was; a few readings give 3, so r = 4. The quantum machine of 41·phys.6 does the transform on all cosets at once. There 2ᵐ is not a multiple of r, and the readings land near the multiples of 2ᵐ/r instead of on them; recovering r from them is held in trust.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞J.oq.1Can a quantum machine find a hidden part-group fast when the group does not turn about? For the symmetries of a polygon, a 2002 paper showed that a fast method of a certain kind would solve the hard problems on grids of points on which the lattice locks of 44·now2 rest; a 2003 method is faster than any known ordinary one, but not fast. For the shuffles of n things, a fast method would decide when two graphs are the same shape, and a 2005 technique showed that the plain approach, one transform followed by a reading, cannot. Both questions are open.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Turnings of a group whose combining turns about were used for finite groups in the nineteenth century, in counting primes along clocks. In the 1930s the duality was proved for groups with a continuous combining that are compact up close: the turnings of the turnings are the group. The transform on any such group then followed the pattern of rung 32.

In 1994 a quantum method found the period of a remainder, and so factored numbers, by this transform on a clock. Soon after, it was seen that the method, and an earlier one for a related problem, are cases of one question: find a part-group hidden by a function. For every group whose combining turns about, a quantum machine answers it fast.

For other groups the transform must use the ways a group acts on spaces, the pieces of ∞γ, not turnings, and it is harder to read. Since 2000 two groups have been studied most: the symmetries of a polygon, tied to the lattice locks, and the shuffles of n things, tied to the sameness of graphs. Neither is solved.

The accepted names

A turning is a character, and Ĝ is the dual group, or Pontryagin dual; G ≅ Ĝ̂ is Pontryagin duality. The transform on a finite turn-about group is the discrete Fourier transform on G; the turning-sum technique is the orthogonality of characters; the rebuilding is Fourier inversion. The turnings that are 1 on H form the annihilator H⊥. Finding H is the hidden subgroup problem; the symmetries of a polygon are the dihedral group, the shuffles the symmetric group, and the lattice problem the unique shortest vector problem.

Who said it first

Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet used characters of clocks for primes in progressions (1837). Lev Pontryagin proved the duality (1934), extended by Egbert van Kampen (1935). Peter Shor, factoring (1994); Daniel Simon, the earlier problem (1994); the hidden subgroup frame from Alexei Kitaev (1995) and Richard Jozsa (1997) among others. Oded Regev, “Quantum computation and lattice problems” (FOCS 2002). Greg Kuperberg, a subexponential algorithm for the dihedral group (2003). Cristopher Moore, Alexander Russell and Leonard Schulman, “The symmetric group defies strong Fourier sampling” (2005).
