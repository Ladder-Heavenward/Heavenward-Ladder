Why Does Water Bead on Wax and Spread on Glass?

Why does water bead on wax and spread on glass? Claire washes her car and the water lies in a sheet on the windscreen and stands in round drops on the waxed bonnet. Same water, same morning. This page finds what decides it, and then what the answer has to do with glue, with solder, and with the oil on a chainsaw.

Every surface costs energy. A liquid spreads on a solid when that lowers the total cost, and the angle at its edge reads the balance. Wetting comes first; sticking is wetting followed by setting; tack is a liquid that holds to itself when pulled.

21chem21A Surface Costs Energy

14·chem had molecules clinging to their neighbours. A molecule inside a drop has neighbours all round. One at the surface has them on one side only, so it is held less and sits higher in energy. To make more surface, molecules must be brought out from inside, and that takes work.

So a surface has a price: so much energy for each square metre. The same number is the pull along each metre of the surface’s edge, since stretching the surface a little is pulling its edge along.

surfacejoules for each square metre
a plasticabout 0.04
waterabout 0.07
a clean metalten times that and more

A liquid left alone takes the shape with least surface. That is why a small drop is round.

Surface technique: a surface costs energy in proportion to its area, since the molecules in it have fewer neighbours. The same number is the pull along each unit of its edge.

21chem22Three Pulls at the Edge

Look at the rim of a drop sitting on a plate. Three surfaces meet there: plate to air, plate to water, water to air. Each pulls on the rim along itself with its own price. Two of them lie flat along the plate and pull opposite ways. The third, the water’s own skin, leaves the plate at an angle θ.

21·phys adds pulls as arrows, and the rim is at rest, so along the plate they sum to zero.

\theta \text{ near 0}^\circ \text{, cos }\theta \text{ near 1} @@ the drop lies flat. The liquid wets
\theta \text{ = 90}^\circ \text{, cos }\theta \text{ = 0} @@ the plate does not care whether it is covered
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \text{ above 90}^\circ \text{, cos }\theta \text{ below 0}} @@ the drop stands up. It beads

The angle can be seen with a hand lens, and it reports a difference of two energies that nobody can measure directly. Workers who need a liquid to spread ask for an angle under 30°, and prefer under 10°.

Edge technique: at the rim of a drop the three surface pulls balance along the solid. The angle of the liquid’s skin there tells which the solid prefers, liquid or air.

21chem23What Wets What

Read 21chem22’s line as a rule. A bare solid with a high price is glad to be covered. A liquid with a low price is cheap to spread. So cheap liquids wet dear solids, and not the other way.

Clean glass or metal, dear: water spreads. Claire’s windscreen.

Wax, cheap: water costs more than the wax surface it would cover. It beads. Her bonnet.

A plastic of carbon and fluorine with every bond satisfied: among the cheapest solids there are. Almost nothing wets it, and what cannot wet cannot stick. That is the pan an egg slides off. The coating is held to the pan only by being melted into a roughened surface.

A rinse aid in a dishwasher: something dissolved in the water that lowers the water’s own price, so it runs off glasses in a sheet and leaves no drops to dry as spots.

Solder on copper: liquid metal on a dear solid should spread at once. It does not, because the copper wears a skin of oxide, which is cheap. A flux dissolves the skin and the solder runs.

Roughness helps a liquid that already wets, by offering more area to cover.

21chem24Small Things Belong to the Surface

A puddle is flat and a dewdrop is round. Two things act on a liquid: its weight, which goes with its volume, and its surface, which goes with its area. Shrink the drop and volume falls faster than area (14·bio). Somewhere they are equal.

\rho\,g\,r @@ weight’s push for a drop of height r
\gamma / r @@ the surface’s push
r^{2} = \gamma / \rho g @@ where the two are equal
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{0.07}{1000 \times 9.8} = 0.0000071\text{ m}^{2}} @@ water: so r = 2.7 mm

Below about 3 mm the surface wins and it stops mattering which way is up. A liquid that wets is drawn into any gap narrower than that, against its weight: water into paper, solder up into a joint, glue into the grain of wood. A stamp wets its envelope and not a window, since the paper has gaps to draw the water in and the glass has none.

Smallness technique: compare a liquid’s weight with its surface pull. Below a few millimetres the surface pull is the larger, and a wetting liquid is drawn into gaps whichever way they face.

21chem25Sticking Is Wetting, Then Setting

Two steel blocks ground very flat cling together if wiped with a trace of oil from the hand. A liquid that wets both faces fills the gap of 33eng41’s hills, and the pulls between molecules now act across the whole face and not at a few hilltops. Worked out for clean contact, even the weak cling of 14·chem should hold a hundred thousand pounds on the square inch.

The blocks still slide apart sideways. A liquid film holds against a straight pull and gives no hold against sliding. To stick two things, the liquid has to stop being a liquid.

It freezes: hot glue, tar.

Its solvent leaves: wallpaper paste, wood glue. This needs somewhere for the solvent to go, so wood glue fails on metal.

It reacts into one tied network (38·bio’s sense): two-part resin, and instant glue, which is set off by the trace of water on any surface.

Most of the hold in practice, nine parts in ten by one engineer’s reckoning, is the set glue keyed into pits and pores. That is why a surface to be glued is roughened first.

Sticking technique: to stick two solids, a liquid must first wet both, and then become solid. A glue fails either by not wetting or by not setting.

21chem26A Thin Film Is Slow

There is a reason the liquid film holds at all against a straight pull. To part the plates, liquid has to run in from the rim to fill the widening gap, and a liquid runs slowly through a thin gap. Pressing the plates together is the same problem backwards.

Halve the gap and the 1/h² term is four times larger: thin films are far slower than thick ones.

Double the disc’s radius: sixteen times slower.

The same force for a tenth of the time moves a tenth as much liquid: a quick pull meets a stiff joint, a slow pull a soft one.

Thickness, in this sense, runs over an enormous range: water 1, molasses some hundreds, and a liquid on its way to setting climbs by ten thousand million times.

21chem27Tacky Is Not Sticky

Mike fills the oil tank of a chainsaw. The chain runs round the nose of the bar at twenty metres a second, and ordinary oil is thrown off the nose in a spray within a turn. Bar oil stays on. Touch a drop and draw finger and thumb apart, and it pulls out into threads that stretch a hand’s width before they part.

It is no glue. It is a lubricant: the chain slides in its groove on it, and it holds nothing to anything. So there are two different things here.

holds two solids against being partedholds to itself when drawn out
waternono
bar oilnoyes
set glueyes(it is a solid)
the gum on tapeyesyes

Sticky is about two bodies. It needs wetting and then enough stiffness to resist both pulling and sliding (21chem25).

Tacky is about one liquid. Pulled out, it makes a thread and does not break into drops. It still flows freely when sheared, which is why it lubricates.

A liquid of small molecules has no way to make a thread: 21chem21’s surface pinches any thin neck off into drops, the cheapest shape. Bar oil has a little of something else dissolved in it, molecules thousands of atoms long. Sliding past each other sideways they hardly notice one another. Drawn out lengthwise they catch and line up, and the thread holds. What long chains do when stretched is held in trust until 38·chem2.

That is why the oil stays on the chain. To be thrown off, a drop must first be drawn out into a neck and pinched off, and this oil does not pinch.

The gum on tape is both. It is tacky enough to flow into the hills of a surface under a thumb in a second, and stiff enough against a quick pull to hold.

Tack technique: ask two separate questions of a liquid. Does it hold two solids together? That is stickiness, and needs wetting and stiffness. Does it draw into threads without breaking? That is tack, and comes from long molecules that catch when pulled lengthwise.

Accepted names

The price of a surface is surface energy, in J/m², and the same number as a pull is surface tension, in N/m. The edge angle is the contact angle, the line of 21chem22 Young’s equation. Spreading is wetting; a dissolved thing that lowers a liquid’s price is a surfactant or wetting agent. Drawing into gaps is capillary action; the ratio of 21chem24 is the Bond number and its balance size the capillary length. Holding between unlike things is adhesion, within one thing cohesion. Keying into pits is mechanical interlocking. The line of 21chem26 is Stefan’s equation; thickness is viscosity. Resistance to being drawn out is extensional viscosity, thread-making is spinnability or stringiness, and the additive is a tackifier, in bar oil usually polyisobutylene. Tape gum is a pressure-sensitive adhesive. The fluorine plastic is PTFE.
