37d · proof

Same note, another sound

A flute and a clarinet play the same note at the same loudness, and anyone can tell them apart. Rung 32’s violin page found why: a note is a sum of turnings, and each instrument puts in its own amounts. This step names that recipe as the sound’s quality and says when two recipes are the same sound. Then it lets waves meet. Sounds add, so two speakers playing one note make loud places and quiet places in a room, and two notes close in pitch swell and fade. Last, it finds the first thing the ear throws away: the turn of each turning, which changes the shape of the wave a great deal and the sound of a steady note hardly at all.

Quality is the recipe: how much of each whole multiple a note holds. Sounds add, so where crests meet crests the sound is loud and where crests meet troughs it is quiet. Two close notes swell and fade at their difference. The ear keeps the amounts of a steady note and drops their turns.

37d1Same pitch, same loudness, another sound

By the repeat-rate technique (37b1) a note of pitch f is a sum of turnings at f, 2f, 3f and so on. 32·phys1 found that a plucked string’s amounts fall like 1/n² and a bowed one’s like 1/n. Write the note as p(t) = Σ rₙ cos(2πnft + φₙ) with rₙ ≥ 0: rₙ is the amount of the nth turning and φₙ its turn, the polar form of 32a3’s cosine and sine.

From here on, the recipe of a note is its list of amounts and turns, (rₙ, φₙ) for n = 1, 2, 3, …, and its quality is the list of amounts as shares of the whole, rₙ / √(Σ rₖ²). Two notes of one pitch and one level can differ only in quality and turns.

Recipe technique: two notes that repeat every T are the same sound exactly when their recipes agree. Proof: the recipe is the list of 32a’s coefficients, so equal sounds have equal recipes; and if the recipes agree, the difference of the two sounds has every coefficient 0, so its energy is 0 (32b3) and the two pressures agree wherever they are continuous (32c2).

\text{clarinet, low notes: } r_1, r_3, r_5 \text{ strong},\ r_2, r_4 \text{ weak} @@ a tube closed at one end favours the odd multiples (37f3)

37d2Waves add

If p and q each obey p_tt = c²p_xx, so does p + q, since slopes of sums are sums of slopes. So two sounds in one place are their sum, and neither disturbs the other: each passes through the other and comes out as it went in.

Take two small speakers playing the same note of wavelength λ, in step. At a place a distance d₁ from one and d₂ from the other, their turnings arrive with turns that differ by 2π(d₁ − d₂)/λ. Where that is a whole number of turns the crests meet and the sound is strong; where it is half a turn more, a crest meets a trough and they take each other away.

Crest-meeting technique: two equal turnings from two sources in step add to the most where the paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths, and to nothing where they differ by a whole number and a half.

\cos\theta + \cos(\theta + \delta) = 2\cos\tfrac{\delta}{2}\,\cos\!\big(\theta + \tfrac{\delta}{2}\big) @@ 21d3, the sum formulas
\delta = 2\pi (d_1 - d_2)/\lambda @@ the turn the extra path costs
\htmlClass{new}{\text{size } 2|\cos(\pi (d_1 - d_2)/\lambda)|:\ 2 \text{ at } d_1 - d_2 = k\lambda,\ 0 \text{ at } (k + \tfrac12)\lambda} \quad \blacksquare @@ k a whole number

Two speakers playing 343 Hz, so λ = 1 m, set 3 m apart in a hall in Missoula: walk along a line in front of them and the note swells and fades about every metre or two. In a real room the walls add more sources (37e4), and the pattern blurs.

37d3Beats, then roughness, then two notes

32·phys3 found that two turnings at f₁ and f₂ add to a swell that fades and returns |f₁ − f₂| times a second: the throb by which a string is tuned. The difference here is what the ear does as the gap grows, and that is laid down, a fact by cases with its rate.

Up to a gap of about 15 Hz the ear hears one note that throbs. From there to a gap that depends on the pitch, about 100 Hz near 500 Hz and about a sixth of the frequency higher up, it hears one rough note. Past that, it hears two notes, each clean. The ear sorts sounds into bands, and two turnings in one band are heard together, not apart.

\cos 2\pi f_1 t + \cos 2\pi f_2 t = 2\cos\!\big(\pi (f_1 - f_2) t\big)\cos\!\big(\pi (f_1 + f_2) t\big) @@ 21d3
\text{the swell } 2|\cos(\pi(f_1 - f_2)t)| \text{ peaks } |f_1 - f_2| \text{ times a second} @@ a peak each time the cosine is ±1

37d4The ear drops the turn

Change only the turns of a note’s recipe and its wave changes shape, sometimes beyond recognition: cos t + cos 3t rises to a sharp peak of 2, and cos t − cos 3t to two flatter humps of about 1.54. Its amounts stay as they were.

Turn-free technique: changing the turns of a note’s turnings changes neither its pitch nor its intensity, nor the intensity at any one frequency. Proof: the period is set by which n are present (37b1), not their turns; and the intensity at the nth frequency is rₙ²/(2ρc), which has no φₙ in it; their sum is the whole intensity, by the adding-power technique (37c1).

p = \cos t + \cos 3t,\ \ q = \cos t - \cos 3t = \cos t + \cos(3t + \pi) @@ φ₃ = 0 against φ₃ = π
r_1 = r_3 = 1 \text{ for both} @@ the amounts agree
\htmlClass{new}{\langle p^2 \rangle = \langle q^2 \rangle = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turn-free technique; the shapes differ

What follows is laid down, measured: for steady notes, the ear hears the amounts and is nearly deaf to the turns. Play p and q above as tones at 200 Hz and 600 Hz, and most listeners cannot tell them apart. The ear keeps how much of each frequency there is and drops where in its turn each one is. That is the second thing it drops, after 37c4’s; the turns are not lost to the air, only to the ear (37j6).

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Quality is timbre, or tone colour. The amounts and turns are the magnitude and phase spectrum. The adding of waves is superposition, and its loud and quiet places are constructive and destructive interference. The bands of 37d3 are critical bands. The ear’s near deafness to phase is Ohm’s acoustic law.

Who said it first

Georg Ohm stated in 1843 that the ear hears a tone as its sinusoidal parts; Hermann von Helmholtz tested it in On the Sensations of Tone (1863) and found phase made little difference to steady tones. Harvey Fletcher named critical bands in 1940.

A proof that is not allowed

“cos t + cos 3t and cos t − cos 3t have different peaks, 2 against about 1.54. So one is louder.” Not allowed: loudness of a steady note goes with its intensity, the average of the square, which is 1 for both; the peak is not the power.
