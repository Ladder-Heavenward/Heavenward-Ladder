21a · proof

An Angle Is an Arc; π

Since the fifth rung you have carried a guess: the way around a round thing, divided by the way across, is always the same number, a little over three. This step settles it. You can already measure the length of a curved path, by adding up its speed. Measure around a circle of radius one, and the length to the halfway point gets its own name, pi. Every circle is a stretched copy of that one, so the guess was right for every circle there is. Pi is fixed exactly, yet no one will ever write all of it down. What you can do is fence it in, between a number that is too small and one that is too big, as close as you like.

An angle is a statement that names a length: how far you walk around a circle of radius one. Pi is how far it is to the halfway point. It is the same for every circle, and it can only ever be written as a fence around it.

21a1The Length Round the Circle

Arc-length technique: the arc of the circle of radius 1 from (1, 0) to the point above x has length L(x) = ∫ₓ¹ dt/√(1 − t²). The upper half of the circle is the graph of y = √(1 − x²). Its slope is −x/√(1 − x²), and the graph-length technique does the rest. The circle of radius 1 centred at the origin is called the unit circle.

f(t) = \sqrt{1 - t^2},\ \ f'(t) = \frac{-t}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} @@ the upper half of x² + y² = 1 (18d); chain rule
1 + f'(t)^2 = \frac{1 - t^2 + t^2}{1 - t^2} = \frac{1}{1 - t^2} @@ common bottom
L(x) = \int_x^1 \frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} @@ the graph-length technique (18i)
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - t}} \text{ on } [0, 1) @@ 1 + t ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{L(x) \text{ is a number for every } x \in [-1, 1]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the in-to-0 technique with p = ½ and the comparison technique (16i); likewise at −1

The bottom is 0 at t = 1, so the integral is improper there. It converges anyway: the curve is steep at (1, 0), but not steep enough to make the length endless.

21a2π, Defined

From here on, π = L(−1) = ∫₋₁¹ dt/√(1 − t²), the length of the upper half of the circle of radius 1.

Round-quotient technique: for every circle, the length around divided by the length across is π. The lower half is the flip of the upper half over the floor, and a flip keeps speeds, so the whole circle of radius 1 has length 2π. A circle of radius r is the stretch of it by r (20a3), and a stretch by r multiplies every length by r.

\text{length round, radius } 1 = 2\pi @@ the two halves, each π: a flip keeps the speed of every path
\text{length round, radius } r = 2\pi r @@ stretch by r: speeds, and so lengths, times r
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{\text{around}}{\text{across}} = \frac{2\pi r}{2r} = \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for every r: 5c’s guess, now a technique
The = dividendThe circle swallows its own size. 0b’s equal parts set a part as a unit, whatever it holds. Here the radius is the unit: around = 2π radii, whatever the radius is. That = holds between every two circles, because each is a stretch of the other.

21a3An Angle Is an Arc

Arc-picks-a-point technique: for each θ between 0 and π there is exactly one point on the upper half circle whose arc from (1, 0) is θ. L is continuous, falls as x rises, and runs from L(−1) = π down to L(1) = 0.

L'(x) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} < 0 \text{ on } (-1, 1) @@ the area-slope technique (16b): the moving end is the bottom one
L(-1) = \pi,\ \ L(1) = 0 @@ 21a2, and an empty arc
0 \le \theta \le \pi \Rightarrow L(x) = \theta \text{ for some } x @@ the in-between-value technique (12a)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{and only one } x} \quad \blacksquare @@ L falls, so it never takes a value twice (14d)

From here on, the angle between the rays from (0, 0) to (1, 0) and to a point P of the circle is the length θ of the arc from (1, 0) to P. The radius is its unit.

By the flip across the upright axis, L(−x) = π − L(x). So L(0) = π/2: the point straight up is a quarter of the way round.

21a4π in a Cloud

Eighth-arc technique: π = 4 ∫₀^{1/√2} dt/√(1 − t²), an integral with no trouble at either end. The arc from (0, 1) to (1/√2, 1/√2) is a flip, across y = x, of the arc from there to (1, 0). So each is π/4.

\frac{\pi}{4} = L(0) - L\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big) = \int_0^{1/\sqrt2} \frac{dt}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} @@ half of L(0) = π/2, by the flip across y = x
\text{lower sum} \le \frac{\pi}{4} \le \text{upper sum} @@ 15b, for any cut of [0, 1/√2]
n = 4:\ 3.015 < \pi < 3.309 @@ the integrand rises, so left heights are lowest, right heights highest
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every cut gives a fence; finer cuts, a narrower one}} \quad \blacksquare
Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

π is exact: one number, fixed for ever by an integral. Yet no list of digits is π, and no fraction is either (rung 23 proves that). Every value of π you will ever write is a fence, a lower and an upper number, and you choose how far apart they stand when you choose how finely to cut.

Every value that uses π carries a width, which you choose and say.

So every answer that uses π carries a width too. A wheel 28 cm across is 28π cm around. Written in centimetres, it is a fence, not a dot, and you say how wide.

With 4 strips, π is between 3.015 and 3.309. How many strips would you need before a carpenter cutting a rim for a 28 cm wheel could trust the fence to a millimetre?

The accepted names

The angle measured as arc length on the unit circle is measured in radians (21j). The letter π was first used for this number by William Jones (1706), and made standard by Euler. The round-quotient technique is usually stated: the circumference of a circle is 2πr, or πd.

Who said it first

Archimedes fenced π between 223/71 and 22/7 with polygons of 96 sides (about 250 BC), the fence of 5c. In 21a.4.5, the philosopher is Thomas Hobbes and the mathematician John Wallis (their quarrel ran from 1655 to 1679); Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental, so the circle cannot be squared with straightedge and compass; the bill was Indiana House Bill 246 (1897). In 21a.4.3, the curves of constant width, such as the Reuleaux triangle, all have length π times their width (Barbier, 1860).
