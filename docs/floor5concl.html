What the Statistics floor shows

An endless process is known by where it settles, and a chance is a size, so a guess from many cases comes with its width.

The floor conclusion · its principles, in the words and marks it built

This page opens once every step of the Statistics floor is ticked. Its principles are proved on the rungs below, so it waits for them.

What the floor set out to show

The Analysis floor built limits, and the Derivation and Geometry floors put them to work on slopes, areas and shapes. The Statistics floor asked what can be said about things too many, too small or too scattered to see one at a time: endless sums, functions rebuilt from a point, futures fixed by a rate, roots that hide off the line, arrows in many directions, and in the end, chance. It promised to make each of them exact, and to prove every answer.

Here are the floor’s nine principles again, one to a rung, each in the words and the marks the floor built. Each links to the rung conclusion that proves it.

The principles, rung by rung

22 · summing up infinity

A total is where the running totals settle, and on a line with no holes they settle exactly when they crowd together.

23 · rebuilding from point one

Inside its reach, a power sum is rebuilt from its centre: its coefficients are its slopes there over factorials, and it may be taken apart term by term.

24 · fixing rates and functions

When the rule is tame, exactly one solution passes through the start, and it is the settled end of repeated integration.

25 · finding every root

Every polynomial that is not constant, with complex numbers in front, has a root in ℂ and splits there into line factors.

26 · growing and turning, together

For every real θ, e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, and a function with a complex slope is fixed inside a loop by its values on the loop.

27 · finding your arrows

Every space of arrows that a finite list builds has a dimension, and every straight map between lists of numbers is a grid whose determinant says whether it can be undone.

28 · flattening the map

A function of several inputs has a slope at a point when one straight map matches it better than any step shrinks, and then that map’s grid is the grid of its partial slopes.

29 · multiplying geometries

The parallel axiom can be neither proved nor refuted from the other axioms, and the bend of a surface, which tells the geometries apart, can be read from its ruler alone.

30 · entering statistics

A chance is a size with total one, so averages are totals, and the techniques of size give the laws of averages, sums and fair games.

What the floor shows

The floor began with the endless. Rung 22 made an endless sum the place its running totals settle, and rung 23 rebuilt a function from its slopes at one point. Rung 24 showed that a tame rate and a start fix the future. Rungs 25 and 26 left the line for the plane of complex numbers, where every polynomial splits and growth and turning are one function. Rungs 27 and 28 measured in many directions at once, with arrows, grids and flat maps. Rung 29 found that flat is only one geometry among many, and that the bend of a space is read from its ruler.

On the Statistics floor, the endless became something that settles, and chance became a size, so a guess from a crowd of cases comes with its width and its exception rate.

Rung 30 turned the floor’s tools on the question the annexes had asked since rung 2. A chance is a size with total one, and an average is a total. Averages of many independent tries settle, sums of many small parts take the shape of the bell, and no system beats a fair game. With that, induction under the case with unpredictable variation, which is statistics, stopped being a story told beside the proofs and became a technique with its own.

So the floor demonstrates that guessing from things can be as careful as proving, though it is never the same thing. A proof says its foot cannot fail. A guess under the large case says how often it might, and how wide its answer is. The ladder now has both, and you have only just started to use the second.
