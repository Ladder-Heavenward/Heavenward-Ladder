14·phys · physics

What Stays the Same When Things Crash?

What stays the same when things crash? Two railcars meet in a yard and couple. A curling stone strikes another and stops dead while the other slides away. In each, the forces during the contact are huge, brief and impossible to measure in detail. Yet one number computed before the contact can be computed again after it and comes out the same. 13·phys's second law of motion says what a force does to one body. Add the third law of motion, that two bodies push on each other equally and oppositely. Then the sum of two quantities has slope 0 at every instant of the contact. 14d's zero-slope-is-constant then says the sum never changes. That is conservation of momentum: a law of nature turned, by one technique about slopes, into a statement that settles collisions without knowing their forces.

Momentum is mass times velocity. The second law makes its slope the force. The third law makes the two forces of a contact cancel. So the total momentum has slope 0 at every instant, and zero-slope-is-constant keeps it the same before, during and after.

14phys1Momentum, and the Second Law Again

From here on, the momentum of a body of mass m moving with velocity v is p = m · v. Its unit is kg · m/s. Its accepted name is momentum; older books say quantity of motion. Like v, it has a sign: positive one way along the track, negative the other.

For a body whose mass does not change, constant-factor for slopes gives p′ = (mv)′ = m · v′, the mass times the acceleration. So 13·phys's second law of motion, F = m · v′ for the total force on a body, says the same as

F = p′: the total force on a body is the slope of its momentum.

The law was first stated this way, as a change of motion. This form keeps working for a rocket burning fuel, where m changes and F = m · v′ is not true.

14phys2The First and Third Laws

The first law of motion: a body with no force on it keeps its velocity. With F = p′ it reads: if p′ = 0 at every instant of an interval, p is constant there, which is zero-slope-is-constant. 14d's standing question asked whether that makes the first law a technique. Physicists keep it as a law, because it says in which frames of reference the second law is to be read. For this ladder, on a straight track measured from the ground, the two agree.

The third law of motion is new, and it is a law of nature, not a technique. When two bodies act on each other, at every instant the force of the first on the second and the force of the second on the first are equal in size and opposite in direction. A cart that pushes another forward is pushed back by exactly as much.

The law's first statement put it that to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: the actions of two bodies on each other are always equal and directed to contrary parts. If a horse draws a stone tied to a rope, the stone draws the horse back equally.

14phys3Conservation, Proved

Take two carts on a straight track, with no force on them from outside the pair along the track. Friction is small enough to leave out, and the track is level so that weight acts across it. Their only forces along the track are the ones they exert on each other, and only while they touch.

p_1' = F_{21},\ \ p_2' = F_{12} @@ the second law for each cart; F₂₁ is the force of cart 2 on cart 1
F_{12} = -F_{21} \text{ at every instant} @@ the third law
(p_1 + p_2)' = F_{21} + F_{12} = 0 @@ slopes-add (13d)
\htmlClass{new}{p_1 + p_2 \text{ is constant}} @@ zero-slope-is-constant (14d), on the interval of the whole story
m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 @@ before and after: u₁ and u₂ name the velocities after

Its accepted name is conservation of momentum. Nothing about the contact was needed: not how long it lasted, not how the force rose and fell. The law of nature is the third law; the conservation is zero-slope-is-constant.

14phys4Sticking Together

m_1 = 30\,000 \text{ kg at } 2 \text{ m/s},\ \ m_2 = 20\,000 \text{ kg at rest} @@ a yard in Tacoma
60\,000 + 0 = 50\,000 \cdot v @@ conservation: they leave together at one velocity v
v = 1.2 \text{ m/s} @@ in the first car's direction

From here on, the kinetic energy of a body of mass m and velocity v is K = ½ m v². Its unit is kg · m²/s², 7·phys's joule. Its accepted name is kinetic energy. It has no sign: v² ≥ 0.

Before the coupling the railcars have ½ · 30 000 · 2² = 60 000 J; after, ½ · 50 000 · 1.2² = 36 000 J. The momentum is kept and 24 000 J of kinetic energy is not: it went to heat, sound and bent metal. A collision in which the bodies stick is called perfectly inelastic. One in which the kinetic energy is kept, as nearly happens with curling stones or steel balls, is called elastic.

14phys5Bouncing: Two Laws at Once

m_1(v_1 - u_1) = m_2(u_2 - v_2) @@ momentum kept; u₁, u₂ the velocities after
m_1(v_1^2 - u_1^2) = m_2(u_2^2 - v_2^2) @@ kinetic energy kept, times 2
v_1 + u_1 = v_2 + u_2 @@ divide the second line by the first, after difference-of-squares (5i) on each side, when v₁ ≠ u₁
\htmlClass{new}{u_1 = \tfrac{(m_1 - m_2)v_1 + 2m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}},\ \ u_2 = \tfrac{(m_2 - m_1)v_2 + 2m_1 v_1}{m_1 + m_2} @@ two linear equations in u₁ and u₂, solved by 5g's same change to both sides

The third line says that the speed at which the carts approach equals the speed at which they separate. With equal masses and the second at rest, u₁ = 0 and u₂ = v₁: two curling stones of 19.96 kg trade velocities, the striker stopping dead. A light cart striking a heavy one at rest bounces back: 1 kg at 3 m/s into 2 kg gives u₁ = −1 m/s and u₂ = 2 m/s.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, and a change with two sides

1a's ledger has a push column, and 7·phys and 11·chem kept it: push in equals push out. Momentum is a column of its own. The carts' total never changes, and the proof never needed to know how the contact went. The books close whatever happened in between.

You never prove the event happened; you show the books balance, in every column.

A crash is also a change with two sides, as 6·chem's rust was. What one cart loses the other gains, at every instant of the contact, because the forces are equal and opposite. Tell the story of one cart alone and its momentum simply vanishes. That is a different event, and one that never happens.

The railcars close one column and not the other. Momentum closes. 24 000 J of kinetic energy does not, until the heat, the sound and the bent metal are written in. The ledger asks where it went, and the answer is part of the description.

A rifle fires a 10 g bullet forward at 900 m/s. Where is the other side of that change, and why is it so easy to miss?

14phys6Impulse, and the Force a Helmet Spreads

From here on, the impulse on a body over the interval [t₁, t₂] is the change of its momentum, J = p(t₂) − p(t₁), in kg · m/s, which is also N · s. Its accepted name is impulse. The average force over the interval is J/(t₂ − t₁).

By average-slope on p, some instant inside the interval has p′, the force, exactly equal to the average force. So the peak force is at least the average. Take a 70 kg rider at 8 m/s, stopped by a post in 0.02 s. The rider takes an impulse of 560 N · s and an average force of 28 000 N, some instant at least that. The same stop spread over 0.2 s by a padded vest has the same impulse and an average force of 2800 N. Helmets, airbags and crumple zones do not change the impulse; they stretch the time.

14phys7A Spring Between Them, and the Instant of Closest Approach

13k's standing question asked whether the spring law is a law of nature or only the linearization of whatever a spring obeys at rest. Here it earns its place. Put a light spring on the front of the second cart, pushing with a force k times its squeeze. The squeeze is s = x₁ − x₂ + const, so s′ = v₁ − v₂. At the instant the squeeze is greatest, inside the contact, flat-top makes s′ = 0, so v₁ = v₂. At the greatest squeeze both carts move together, at (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂) by conservation. Take 1 kg at 2 m/s into 1 kg at rest. The common velocity is 1 m/s, and the kinetic energy has dropped from 2 J to 1 J. The missing 1 J sits in the spring. A spring squeezed by s holds ½ k s², a physical premise this ladder proves at rung 16. With k = 100 N/m, ½ · 100 · s² = 1 gives s ≈ 0.141 m.

Who said it first

Newton put it that to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: the actions of two bodies on each other are always equal and directed to contrary parts. If a horse draws a stone tied to a rope, the stone draws the horse back equally.

Newton, Principia (1687), the third law of motion and its comment; paraphrase

The three laws of motion are Isaac Newton’s, from the Principia (1687); he stated the second as a change of motion. The spring law is Robert Hooke’s. In the closer, the quantity of motion is René Descartes’s; the answers of 1668 are John Wallis’s, Christopher Wren’s and Christiaan Huygens’s; mv² as the measure of force (vis viva) is Gottfried Leibniz’s.
