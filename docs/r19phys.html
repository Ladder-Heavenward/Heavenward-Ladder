Where Was the Earthquake?

Where was the earthquake? An earthquake sends out two kinds of wave through the ground. The first squeezes and stretches the rock and travels fast; the second shakes it side to side and travels slower. A recording station feels the first, then, some seconds later, the second. The longer the gap, the further away the quake. This page turns the gap into a distance, draws a circle of that radius around each station with 18d’s circles, finds where three circles meet, sees why two stations leave two answers, and sees what a wrong wave speed does to the fix.

Waves at speeds p and s arrive d/p and d/s after the quake, so the gap is d(1/s − 1/p) and d is the gap times ps/(p − s). Each station’s d is a circle around it; two circles cross at two points, and a third circle picks one.

19phys1Two Waves at Two Speeds

In the earth’s crust the fast wave, the push wave, travels about 6 km a second, and the slow wave, the shake wave, about 3.5 km a second. Both leave the quake at the same moment.

From here on, on this page, p = 6 km/s and s = 3.5 km/s are the two speeds, d is a station’s distance from the quake in kilometres, and the gap is the time between the push wave’s arrival and the shake wave’s.

\text{push wave arrives } \tfrac{d}{p} \text{ after the quake} @@ time is distance over speed (5j)
\text{shake wave arrives } \tfrac{d}{s} \text{ after it} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{gap} = \dfrac{d}{s} - \dfrac{d}{p}} @@ the station can measure this without knowing when the quake struck

19phys2Distance From the Gap

Solve the gap for d.

\text{gap} = d\left(\dfrac{1}{s} - \dfrac{1}{p}\right) = d \cdot \dfrac{p - s}{p\,s} @@ 
d = \text{gap} \cdot \dfrac{p\,s}{p - s} = \text{gap} \cdot \dfrac{6 \cdot 3.5}{2.5} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{d \approx 8.4 \text{ km for each second of gap}} @@ the seismologist’s rule of thumb

Arrival-gap technique: a station’s distance from a quake is the gap between the two waves times ps/(p − s), about 8.4 km per second in the crust. A station that records a gap of 6 seconds is about 50 km from the quake. It does not know in which direction.

19phys3Three Circles Meet

Three stations stand at A = (0, 0), B = (100, 0) and C = (0, 80), in kilometres on a map. Their gaps are about 5.95 s, 7.99 s and 7.62 s.

A:\ x^2 + y^2 = 50^2 @@ 5.95 · 8.4 ≈ 50
B:\ (x - 100)^2 + y^2 \approx 67.1^2 @@ 7.99 · 8.4 ≈ 67.1
C:\ x^2 + (y - 80)^2 \approx 64.0^2 @@ 7.62 · 8.4 ≈ 64.0
A - B:\ 200x - 10\,000 = 2\,500 - 4\,500 \Rightarrow x = 40 @@ subtract: the squares cancel, a line
A - C:\ 160y - 6\,400 = 2\,500 - 4\,100 \Rightarrow y = 30 @@ another line
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the quake is at } (40, 30)} @@ check: 40² + 30² = 50²

Subtracting two circles’ statements cancels the squared terms and leaves a straight line (18c) through both crossing points. Two such lines meet at one point, the quake.

19phys4Why Two Stations Aren’t Enough

With only A and B, the quake lies on both circles. The line x = 40 from subtracting them meets circle A where 40² + y² = 50², so y² = 900.

y^2 = 900 \Rightarrow y = 30 \text{ or } y = -30 @@ two square roots (8f)
\htmlClass{new}{(40, 30) \text{ and } (40, -30)} @@ mirror images in the line AB

Two circles cross at two points, mirror images across the line joining their centres, so two stations always leave two candidates. Flipping the map across AB leaves both stations’ distances unchanged, so nothing A and B measure can tell the two apart; a third station off that line breaks the tie.

19phys5Where the Fix Moves When a Speed Is Wrong

The speeds vary with the rock. Suppose the stations used 8 km per second of gap instead of 8.4: every circle shrinks by about 5%.

\text{radii } 47.6,\ 63.9,\ 61.0 @@ each 8/8.4 of before
A - B:\ x \approx 40.9;\ \ A - C:\ y \approx 30.9 @@ the same subtraction
40.9^2 + 30.9^2 \approx 51.3^2 \ne 47.6^2 @@ the point is off circle A
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the three circles no longer meet at one point}} @@ a small triangle instead

With a wrong speed the circles leave a small gap or overlap instead of meeting, and the size of that misfit tells the seismologist the speed is off. Networks with many stations find the speed and the place together: they adjust both until the circles meet as nearly as they can. A seismic centre with dozens of stations places a quake to within a kilometre or two this way within minutes.

Accepted names

The push wave is the P wave (primary) and the shake wave the S wave (secondary); the gap is the S-P interval. The point on the surface above the quake is its epicentre, the point at depth its hypocentre, and the three-circle method trilateration.

Who said it first

Richard Dixon Oldham told P and S waves apart on seismograph records in 1897 and 1900; locating quakes by S-P intervals at several stations became routine in the early twentieth century.
