6·chem · chemistry

Who Gives up Electrons?

Who gives up electrons? Iron left in the rain turns to rust. Bleach is made by passing chlorine into a solution of lye. A flashlight lights while the zinc in its cell wears away. Chemists long saw the first as iron taking in oxygen. Around 1900 they found a better account, which covers all three: in each change, some atoms hand electrons to others. What makes it hard is that nobody sees an electron leave. Worse, electrons are all alike, so nobody could say which electron went where even in principle. What can be said is how many. This page gives each atom a signed whole number from 5b's integers, by a rule that picks, for each bond, which atom takes its electrons. Then it proves that the numbers add up to the charge. That one fact turns “who gives up electrons” into arithmetic, and balancing a change into a least common multiple.

In each bond, give both shared electrons to the atom that grasps harder; split a bond between alike atoms evenly. An atom's oxidation number is the charge it is left with. The numbers in a particle add to its charge. An atom whose number goes up gives up electrons; one whose number goes down takes them. Scale the two halves so that the electrons given equal the electrons taken.

Electrons and charge

An atom is a small centre with electrons round it. Each electron carries one unit of negative charge, written −1. The centre carries one unit of positive charge for each electron of the lone, whole atom, so a lone atom has charge 0. An atom, or a group of atoms held together, that has gained or lost electrons is an ion. Its charge is a whole number of units, an integer in 5b's sense.

From here on, the charge of an ion is written high after its formula, the sign after the size: Fe³⁺ is an iron atom with charge 3 (three electrons short), O²⁻ an oxygen atom with charge −2, and Cl⁻ a chlorine atom with charge −1. A plain e⁻ is one electron.

Measurement shows that charge is never made or lost in a chemical change. Like 5·chem's count of atoms, it is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity, never seen broken in a chemical change. Electrons can move from atom to atom, but the total charge going in is the total coming out.

A rule that picks

In a molecule, two atoms are held together by a bond, which is a pair of shared electrons. Who owns the pair? Not either atom outright. But the atoms do not share equally. Some grasp the shared pair harder than others, and chemists have measured how hard. The grasp of a few atoms, on the usual scale:

That gives 6b's kind of rule: a condition that picks exactly one member of each set. For each bond between unlike atoms, the set is the two atoms, and the rule picks the one that grasps harder. Give that atom both electrons of the bond. Then count what charge each atom is left with.

From here on, the oxidation number of an atom in a particle is the charge it would have if each bond's pair went wholly to the atom that grasps it harder, and each bond between alike atoms were split, one electron each. It is written with its sign always shown: +3, −2, 0.

Between two alike atoms, as in the O–O bond of hydrogen peroxide, the rule cannot pick. The two atoms are as alike as 6b's socks. So the convention does not pick at all: it splits the pair. Where no rule picks a side, the bookkeeping splits the electrons evenly instead of choosing. It works because only the count matters, never which electron.

\text{H–O–H}:\ \text{O takes both pairs} @@ O grasps harder than H (3.4 against 2.2)
\text{O}:\ 6 \text{ of its own},\ 8 \text{ assigned},\ \ 6 - 8 = \htmlClass{new}{-2} @@ a lone O has 6 outer electrons
\text{H}:\ 1 \text{ of its own},\ 0 \text{ assigned},\ \ 1 - 0 = +1 @@ each H
\text{H–O–O–H}:\ \text{each O takes its H pair, splits O–O} @@ the O–O bond has no rule that picks
\text{O}:\ 6 - 7 = \htmlClass{new}{-1} @@ 4 of its own unshared, 2 from H, 1 of the split pair

Only the outer electrons are counted, since the inner ones never move. Here an atom's own count is the number of outer electrons of the lone atom: 1 for H and Na, 6 for O and S, 7 for F and Cl, and so on.

The numbers add up to the charge

Charge-sum technique: the oxidation numbers of the atoms in a particle add up to the particle's charge. Let a particle have atoms 1 to n. Let atom i have ci outer electrons as a lone atom, and let ei be the electrons the bond rule assigns it. Let E be the outer electrons the particle actually holds, and Q its charge.

\text{ox}_i = c_i - e_i @@ the definition, for each atom
Q = \textstyle\sum_i c_i - E @@ the centres carry Σcᵢ units of positive charge beyond the inner electrons; the outer electrons carry E
\textstyle\sum_i e_i = E @@ each electron goes to exactly one atom: a pair whole to one side, or split one and one; separate-piles
\textstyle\sum_i \text{ox}_i = \sum_i c_i - \sum_i e_i @@ sums of differences (5b's laws on ℤ, by induction on n)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_i \text{ox}_i = Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last three lines

So the numbers can be found without drawing a single bond. Chemists keep a short list of working rules, each read off the bond rule for the usual case. An element alone, like Fe or O₂, has 0 everywhere. F is −1. O is −2, except in peroxides, where it is −1, and beside F, where it is +2. H is +1, except beside a metal, where it is −1. Na is +1. These are laws with known exceptions: each exception is a place where the bond rule gives a different count. Then charge-sum finds the one atom left.

\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}:\ 2x + 3(-2) = 0,\ \ x = \htmlClass{new}{+3} @@ rust; charge-sum with Q = 0, and 5g
\mathrm{NaOCl}:\ (+1) + (-2) + x = 0,\ \ x = \htmlClass{new}{+1} @@ the active part of bleach: chlorine at +1
\mathrm{MnO_2}:\ x + 2(-2) = 0,\ \ x = +4 @@ in a flashlight cell
\mathrm{Mn_2O_3}:\ 2x + 3(-2) = 0,\ \ x = +3 @@ what MnO₂ becomes as the cell runs
\mathrm{NH_4^{+}}:\ x + 4(+1) = +1,\ \ x = -3 @@ an ion: Q = +1

Giving and taking

An atom whose oxidation number goes up has given up electrons, one for each step up. That is oxidation. An atom whose number goes down has taken electrons, one for each step down. That is reduction. The atom whose number rises gives up electrons; the atom whose number falls takes them. A change written for one side alone, with its electrons shown, is a half-reaction.

\mathrm{Fe} \to \mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 3e^- @@ iron: 0 to +3, gives up 3
\mathrm{O_2} + 4e^- \to 2\,\mathrm{O^{2-}} @@ oxygen: 0 to −2, two atoms, takes 4

Electrons-balance technique: in a change that keeps its atoms and its charge, the electrons given up equal the electrons taken. By charge-sum, each side's oxidation numbers add up to that side's charge. The charges are the same, by the law. So the rises and the falls, counted atom by atom, add to 0. The rises are the electrons given; the falls are the electrons taken.

So balancing has a second count to meet. Iron gives 3 per atom and oxygen takes 4 per molecule. Both must reach a common multiple, and the least is 5h's lcm(3, 4) = 12.

\text{lcm}(3, 4) = 12 @@ 12 electrons handed over
4\,\mathrm{Fe} \to 4\,\mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 12e^- @@ the iron half, times 4
3\,\mathrm{O_2} + 12e^- \to 6\,\mathrm{O^{2-}} @@ the oxygen half, times 3
\htmlClass{new}{4\,\mathrm{Fe} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 2\,\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}} @@ add, and the 12 electrons cancel. Check: Fe 4 = 4; O 6 = 6; charge 0 = 0

Rust is mostly Fe₂O₃ with water held in it, and the water helps the change along. That is why a nail rusts in Monterey's salt air and barely at all in the dry air of a desert.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change: two sides at once

1a named a second kind of care, the kind that measures how wrong it might be instead of proving. One of its principles is about change. Things do not jump between states, and some changes are two changes at once: what one side loses is the very thing the other side gains, in the same event.

When a change has two sides, describe both at once.

Rust is such a change. Iron's half gives up 12 electrons and oxygen's half takes the same 12. Neither half happens by itself, because an electron given has to land somewhere. The half-reactions are a way of writing the change, not two events. 5·chem's ledger catches a one-sided account: the charge column will not close.

A flashlight lies unused in a drawer for a year, its zinc and its manganese dioxide side by side inside the cell. Why hasn't the zinc given up its electrons?

A flashlight, and bleach

An alkaline cell holds zinc and manganese dioxide, kept apart so that the electrons must go round through the bulb. Zinc grasps weakly and gives; manganese takes.

\mathrm{Zn}:\ 0 \to +2 @@ in ZnO: gives up 2
\mathrm{Mn}:\ +4 \to +3 @@ MnO₂ to Mn₂O₃: takes 1 each
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{Zn} + 2\,\mathrm{MnO_2} \to \mathrm{ZnO} + \mathrm{Mn_2O_3}} @@ two Mn take what one Zn gives. Check: O 4 = 1 + 3

Bleach is made from chlorine and lye. Here one element does both jobs.

\mathrm{Cl_2} + 2\,\mathrm{NaOH} \to \mathrm{NaCl} + \mathrm{NaOCl} + \mathrm{H_2O} @@ atoms: Cl 2, Na 2, O 2, H 2 on each side
\mathrm{Cl}:\ 0 \to -1 \text{ in NaCl} @@ one chlorine atom takes an electron
\mathrm{Cl}:\ 0 \to \htmlClass{new}{+1} \text{ in NaOCl} @@ the other gives one up; Na, O and H do not change

So “who gives up electrons” has a rough answer: whoever grasps more weakly. Metals sit at the weak end of the table, and they are the ones that rust, tarnish and wear away in cells. The table is measured, and its ordering is a law, not a proof.

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

The chemists of 6·chem.4.4 who said a burning metal gives something up were Georg Stahl and his followers, who called the something phlogiston. Antoine Lavoisier weighed the ash, a calx, in the 1770s, found it heavier, and named the gas it takes in oxygen. The idea that oxidation is the loss of electrons came after the electron was found in 1897, through chemists such as Julius Stieglitz around 1908. The grasp table is Linus Pauling's scale of 1932. The alkaline cell was patented by Lewis Urry in the 1950s.

Accepted names

The grasp of an atom is its electronegativity. The oxidation number is also called the oxidation state. A change in which electrons are handed over is a redox reaction, from reduction and oxidation. The atom that takes electrons is the oxidising agent, and the one that gives them the reducing agent. A change in which one element both gives and takes, as chlorine does in making bleach, is a disproportionation. The O–O compounds are peroxides, and NaOCl is sodium hypochlorite.
