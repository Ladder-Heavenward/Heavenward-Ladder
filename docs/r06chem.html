6·chem · chemistry

Who Gives up Electrons?

Who gives up electrons? Iron left in the rain turns to rust. Bleach is made by passing chlorine into a solution of lye. A flashlight lights while the zinc in its cell wears away. Chemists long saw the first as iron taking in oxygen. Around 1900 they found a better account, which covers all three: in each change, some atoms hand electrons to others. What makes it hard is that nobody sees an electron leave. Worse, electrons are all alike, so nobody could say which electron went where even in principle. What can be said is how many. This page gives each atom a signed whole number from 5b's integers, by a technique that picks, for each bond, which atom takes its electrons. Then it proves that the numbers add up to the charge. That one fact turns “who gives up electrons” into arithmetic, and balancing a change into a least common multiple.

In each bond, give both shared electrons to the atom that grasps harder; split a bond between alike atoms evenly. An atom's oxidation number is the charge it is left with. The numbers in a particle add to its charge. An atom whose number goes up gives up electrons; one whose number goes down takes them. Scale the two halves so that the electrons given equal the electrons taken.

6chem1Electrons and Charge

An atom is a small centre with electrons round it. Each electron carries one unit of negative charge, written −1. The centre carries one unit of positive charge for each electron of the lone, whole atom, so a lone atom has charge 0. An atom, or a group of atoms held together, that has gained or lost electrons is an ion. Its charge is a whole number of units, an integer in 5b's sense.

From here on, the charge of an ion is written high after its formula, the sign after the size: Fe³⁺ is an iron atom with charge 3 (three electrons short), O²⁻ an oxygen atom with charge −2, and Cl⁻ a chlorine atom with charge −1. A plain e⁻ is one electron.

Measurement shows that charge is never made or lost in a chemical change. Like 5·chem's count of atoms, it is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity, never seen broken in a chemical change. Electrons can move from atom to atom, but the total charge going in is the total coming out.

6chem2A Technique That Picks

In a molecule, two atoms are held together by a bond, which is a pair of shared electrons. Who owns the pair? Not either atom outright. But the atoms do not share equally. Some grasp the shared pair harder than others, and chemists have measured how hard. The grasp of a few atoms, on the usual scale:

2·chem met this already (2chem7). Beside “is bonded to”, which is symmetric, it set a second relation, “holds the shared part closer than”, which is not. The table says, for each bond, which end that is.

That gives 6b's kind of technique: a condition that picks exactly one member of each set. For each bond between unlike atoms, the set is the two atoms, and the technique picks the one that grasps harder. Give that atom both electrons of the bond. Then count what charge each atom is left with.

From here on, the oxidation number of an atom in a particle is the charge it would have if each bond's pair went wholly to the atom that grasps it harder, and each bond between alike atoms were split, one electron each. It is written with its sign always shown: +3, −2, 0.

Between two alike atoms, as in the O–O bond of hydrogen peroxide, the technique cannot pick. The two atoms are as alike as 6b's socks. So the convention does not pick at all: it splits the pair. Where no technique picks a side, the bookkeeping splits the electrons evenly instead of choosing. It works because only the count matters, never which electron.

\text{H–O–H}:\ \text{O takes both pairs} @@ O grasps harder than H (3.4 against 2.2)
\text{O}:\ 6 \text{ of its own},\ 8 \text{ assigned},\ \ 6 - 8 = \htmlClass{new}{-2} @@ a lone O has 6 outer electrons
\text{H}:\ 1 \text{ of its own},\ 0 \text{ assigned},\ \ 1 - 0 = +1 @@ each H
\text{H–O–O–H}:\ \text{each O takes its H pair, splits O–O} @@ the O–O bond has no technique that picks
\text{O}:\ 6 - 7 = \htmlClass{new}{-1} @@ 4 of its own unshared, 2 from H, 1 of the split pair

Only the outer electrons are counted, since the inner ones never move. Here an atom's own count is the number of outer electrons of the lone atom: 1 for H and Na, 6 for O and S, 7 for F and Cl, and so on.

6chem3The Numbers Add Up to the Charge

Charge-sum technique: the oxidation numbers of the atoms in a particle add up to the particle's charge. Let a particle have atoms 1 to n. Let atom i have ci outer electrons as a lone atom, and let ei be the electrons the bond technique assigns it. Let E be the outer electrons the particle actually holds, and Q its charge.

\text{ox}_i = c_i - e_i @@ the definition, for each atom
Q = \textstyle\sum_i c_i - E @@ the centres carry Σcᵢ units of positive charge beyond the inner electrons; the outer electrons carry E
\textstyle\sum_i e_i = E @@ each electron goes to exactly one atom: a pair whole to one side, or split one and one; separate-piles
\textstyle\sum_i \text{ox}_i = \sum_i c_i - \sum_i e_i @@ sums of differences (5b's laws on ℤ, by induction on n)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_i \text{ox}_i = Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last three lines

So the numbers can be found without drawing a single bond. Chemists keep a short list of working techniques, each read off the bond technique for the usual case. An element alone, like Fe or O₂, has 0 everywhere. F is −1. O is −2, except in peroxides, where it is −1, and beside F, where it is +2. H is +1, except beside a metal, where it is −1. Na is +1. These are laws with known exceptions: each exception is a place where the bond technique gives a different count. Then charge-sum finds the one atom left.

\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}:\ 2x + 3(-2) = 0,\ \ x = \htmlClass{new}{+3} @@ rust; charge-sum with Q = 0, and 5g
\mathrm{NaOCl}:\ (+1) + (-2) + x = 0,\ \ x = \htmlClass{new}{+1} @@ the active part of bleach: chlorine at +1
\mathrm{MnO_2}:\ x + 2(-2) = 0,\ \ x = +4 @@ in a flashlight cell
\mathrm{Mn_2O_3}:\ 2x + 3(-2) = 0,\ \ x = +3 @@ what MnO₂ becomes as the cell runs
\mathrm{NH_4^{+}}:\ x + 4(+1) = +1,\ \ x = -3 @@ an ion: Q = +1

6chem4Giving and Taking

An atom whose oxidation number goes up has given up electrons, one for each step up. That is oxidation. An atom whose number goes down has taken electrons, one for each step down. That is reduction. The atom whose number rises gives up electrons; the atom whose number falls takes them. A change written for one side alone, with its electrons shown, is a half-reaction.

\mathrm{Fe} \to \mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 3e^- @@ iron: 0 to +3, gives up 3
\mathrm{O_2} + 4e^- \to 2\,\mathrm{O^{2-}} @@ oxygen: 0 to −2, two atoms, takes 4

Electrons-balance technique: in a change that keeps its atoms and its charge, the electrons given up equal the electrons taken. By charge-sum, each side's oxidation numbers add up to that side's charge. The charges are the same, by the law. So the rises and the falls, counted atom by atom, add to 0. The rises are the electrons given; the falls are the electrons taken.

So balancing has a second count to meet. Iron gives 3 per atom and oxygen takes 4 per molecule. Both must reach a common multiple, and the least is 5h's lcm(3, 4) = 12.

\text{lcm}(3, 4) = 12 @@ 12 electrons handed over
4\,\mathrm{Fe} \to 4\,\mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 12e^- @@ the iron half, times 4
3\,\mathrm{O_2} + 12e^- \to 6\,\mathrm{O^{2-}} @@ the oxygen half, times 3
\htmlClass{new}{4\,\mathrm{Fe} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 2\,\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}} @@ add, and the 12 electrons cancel. Check: Fe 4 = 4; O 6 = 6; charge 0 = 0

Rust is mostly Fe₂O₃ with water held in it, and the water helps the change along. That is why a nail rusts in Monterey's salt air and barely at all in the dry air of a desert.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change: two sides at once

1a named a second kind of care, the kind that measures how wrong it might be instead of proving. One of its principles is about change. Things do not jump between states, and some changes are two changes at once: what one side loses is the very thing the other side gains, in the same event.

When a change has two sides, describe both at once.

Rust is such a change. Iron's half gives up 12 electrons and oxygen's half takes the same 12. Neither half happens by itself, because an electron given has to land somewhere. The half-reactions are a way of writing the change, not two events. 5·chem's ledger catches a one-sided account: the charge column will not close.

6chem5A Flashlight, and Bleach

An alkaline cell holds zinc and manganese dioxide, kept apart so that the electrons must go round through the bulb. Zinc gives; manganese, already at +4 among oxygen atoms in MnO₂, takes.

\mathrm{Zn}:\ 0 \to +2 @@ in ZnO: gives up 2
\mathrm{Mn}:\ +4 \to +3 @@ MnO₂ to Mn₂O₃: takes 1 each
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{Zn} + 2\,\mathrm{MnO_2} \to \mathrm{ZnO} + \mathrm{Mn_2O_3}} @@ two Mn take what one Zn gives. Check: O 4 = 1 + 3

Bleach is made from chlorine and lye. Here one element does both jobs.

\mathrm{Cl_2} + 2\,\mathrm{NaOH} \to \mathrm{NaCl} + \mathrm{NaOCl} + \mathrm{H_2O} @@ atoms: Cl 2, Na 2, O 2, H 2 on each side
\mathrm{Cl}:\ 0 \to -1 \text{ in NaCl} @@ one chlorine atom takes an electron
\mathrm{Cl}:\ 0 \to \htmlClass{new}{+1} \text{ in NaOCl} @@ the other gives one up; Na, O and H do not change

So “who gives up electrons” has a rough answer when two lone elements meet: whoever grasps more weakly. The flashlight shows where that answer stops. By the table zinc grasps a little more than manganese, yet zinc gives, because the manganese is not alone: it has already given four electrons’ worth of charge to oxygen, and an atom in that state takes one back readily. The rough answer compares atoms; a cell compares what each atom already holds. Metals sit at the weak end of the table, and they are the ones that rust, tarnish and wear away in cells. The table is measured, and its ordering is a law, not a proof.

6chem7A Cell’s Voltage From a Table

Electrons given equal electrons taken (6chem4). In a cell the giving and taking happen in two separate cups joined by a wire, so the electrons must travel through it. Each half has a measured pull for electrons, listed in a table against one agreed half; the cell’s voltage is the taker’s pull less the giver’s.

\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \to \text{Cu}:\ +0.34 \text{ V} @@ copper takes
\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^- \to \text{Zn}:\ -0.76 \text{ V} @@ zinc, lower, gives
E = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10 \text{ V} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the half with the higher pull takes; the cell runs that way on its own}} @@ never double a pull to match electrons

Cell-voltage technique: find both halves in the table; the one with the higher pull takes electrons, and the cell’s voltage is its pull less the other’s, whatever the counts of electrons. Shown on a bench: a zinc strip in zinc sulfate and a copper strip in copper sulfate, joined by a salt bridge, read 1.10 V on a voltmeter, zinc wearing away and copper building up.

6chem8Who Gives, Who Takes

The table of elements (2chem14) sorts the atoms by how they treat an electron. Chemists measure two things for each atom: the energy it costs to pull one electron off, and the energy given out when it takes one more on.

Pulling one off costs more with each step along a row, and less with each step down a column. So the atoms that give an electron most easily stand at the left edge, and lower ones give more easily than higher ones. The atoms that take one most eagerly stand in the last column but one: fluorine, chlorine and those below. The last column does neither: its atoms neither take an electron nor give one up.

Give-and-take technique: an atom far to the left gives, an atom far to the right (the last column apart) takes, and a giver meets a taker more eagerly the farther apart they stand.

6chem2’s grasp scale puts one number on both. An atom that is hard to rob and glad to take has a high grasp. One that is easy to rob and takes little has a low one. Sodium is at 0.9 and chlorine at 3.2.

6chem9Share or Hand Over

Table salt and water are both made by an atom that gives meeting an atom that takes. One is a hard white crystal and the other a liquid. The difference is how far apart the two atoms stand on the grasp scale.

difference in graspwhat happens to the pairexample
under about 0.4shared evenlyC and H, 0.4
from 0.4 to 1.7shared, and held closer by one endO and H, 1.2
well above thathanded over wholeNa and Cl, 2.3

The first two rows are 2·chem’s molecules: a gap filled, a pair shared, and “holds closer than” saying which way it leans. The third row is something else. Sodium does not share its electron with chlorine. It gives it up, and the two are left as Na⁺ and Cl⁻, held together by the pull of unlike charges.

\text{make the two charged atoms}:\ +145 @@ costs energy
\text{let them pull together to } 2.36 \text{ Å}:\ -589 @@ gives energy back
\htmlClass{new}{+145 - 589 = -444} @@ the pull pays the cost four times over

A pull between charges is not a gap. A gap is filled by one partner, and then it is full. A charge pulls on every unlike charge near it. So each Na⁺ in a grain of salt has Cl⁻ on every side, and each Cl⁻ has Na⁺ on every side. Ask 2·chem’s question, “which atoms are joined in one or more steps?”, and the answer is the whole grain. There is no molecule of salt. 22·chem and 32·chem have the crystal.

So NaCl is not 5·chem’s molecular formula. It is a ratio: one sodium for each chlorine. The ratio comes from the charges, and the charges from the columns. An atom of the first column gives one electron, of the second two. An atom of the last column but one takes one, and of the column before it two. The charges must add to nothing.

The last one needs six of each charge, the least number that both 3 and 2 divide (5·chem’s balancing).

Column-charge technique: the formula of a salt is the smallest whole-number ratio in which the charges of its two kinds of atom add to nothing, each charge read from the atom’s column.

Sharing and handing over are two of three. A taker with a taker share: a molecule. A giver with a taker hand over: a salt. A giver with a giver, sodium with sodium or iron with iron, is the third. Neither atom wants the other’s electron and neither holds its own firmly. The outer electrons leave their atoms and belong to the whole piece, moving through it freely. That is a metal, and the loose electrons are why it carries a current, shines, and bends without breaking. Like salt, a metal has no molecules.

6chem10Count It on the Gaps

The gas in a stove is methane, and the flame turns it into carbon dioxide. 2·chem wrote both as gap statements.

6chem2’s technique picks, for each bond, the atom that holds the shared pair closer. A filled gap is one bond. So go through an atom’s gaps one at a time and ask of each: does this atom hold closer than the partner in the gap, or the partner closer than it?

Gap-count technique: for an atom with no charge of its own, the oxidation number is the number of its gaps whose partner holds closer, less the number of its gaps where it holds closer. A gap filled by its own kind counts for neither.

\text{each gap is one shared pair, one electron from each end} @@ 2chem2, 6chem2
\text{partner holds closer}:\ \text{the pair goes to the partner},\ +1 @@ this atom is one electron short
\text{this atom holds closer}:\ -1;\quad \text{alike}:\ 0 @@ one electron over; or the pair is split
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ox} = 0 + (\text{gaps against}) - (\text{gaps for})} \quad \blacksquare @@ the atom began with charge 0
  
\text{methane: C holds closer than H in all four gaps}:\ 0 - 4 = -4 @@ 
\text{carbon dioxide: O holds closer in all four gaps}:\ 4 - 0 = +4 @@ one partner in two gaps is counted in each (2chem4)
\text{water’s oxygen, O}(\text{H}_1, \text{H}_2):\ 0 - 2 = -2 @@ 6chem2’s answer

So in the flame each carbon goes from −4 to +4. It gives up eight electrons, and the four oxygen atoms that burn it take two each.

Between methane and carbon dioxide stand three molecules. Each is the one before with one hydrogen gap changed to an oxygen gap.

moleculecarbon’s gapscountoxidation number
methaneH, H, H, H0 − 4−4

Each change of one gap moves the number by 2. A chemist who sees a carbon gain a bond to oxygen and lose a bond to hydrogen says the carbon was oxidized, and now the count says by how much.

This page’s short list of working techniques is a set of column readings of the table (2chem14). Fluorine stands at the end of its row and holds closer than any partner, so its one gap always counts its way: −1. Oxygen has two gaps and holds closer than everything but fluorine: −2, unless the partner is fluorine or another oxygen. Those are the exceptions the list gives.

Chemists keep a second count beside this one. The oxidation number gives each shared pair whole to the atom that holds it closer. The second count splits every pair, one electron to each end, whoever holds closer. It is the charge an atom would carry if 2chem7’s second relation were ignored and only b were read. In water it gives every atom 0. In the hydroxide of 42·chem2, an oxygen with one hydrogen and one gap unfilled, it gives the oxygen −1, and that is why the minus sign is written on the oxygen. One count asks who holds the pairs. The other asks where a drawing must write its charge.

For an atom that carries a charge by the second count, add that charge: oxidation number = that charge, plus the gaps whose partner holds closer, less the gaps where it holds closer. For the nitrogen of R-NH₃⁺ with R a carbon chain: +1, plus 0, less 4, is −3, the same as before it took the H⁺. Taking up an H⁺ moves no electron from one atom’s keeping to another’s, so it is not oxidation.

The cellar · there if you want it

Who said it first

The chemists of 6·chem.7.4 who said a burning metal gives something up were Georg Stahl and his followers, who called the something phlogiston. Antoine Lavoisier weighed the ash, a calx, in the 1770s, found it heavier, and named the gas it takes in oxygen. The idea that oxidation is the loss of electrons came after the electron was found in 1897, through chemists such as Harry Shipley Fry around 1915. The grasp table is Linus Pauling's scale of 1932. The alkaline cell was patented by Lewis Urry in the 1950s.

Accepted names

The grasp of an atom is its electronegativity. The oxidation number is also called the oxidation state. A change in which electrons are handed over is a redox reaction, from reduction and oxidation. The atom that takes electrons is the oxidising agent, and the one that gives them the reducing agent. A change in which one element both gives and takes, as chlorine does in making bleach, is a disproportionation. The O–O compounds are peroxides, and NaOCl is sodium hypochlorite.

Accepted names: giving, taking, sharing

The energy to pull an electron off an atom is its ionization energy, and the energy given out when it takes one on is its electron affinity. The first column holds the alkali metals, the last but one the halogens, the last the noble gases. A pair shared evenly is a covalent bond, a pair shared and leaning a polar covalent bond, a pair handed over an ionic bond; the bond in a metal is a metallic bond. The second count of 6chem10 is the formal charge. The three molecules between methane and carbon dioxide are methanol, formaldehyde and formic acid.
