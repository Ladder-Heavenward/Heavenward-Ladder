Can You Hear the Shape of a Drum?

Can you hear the shape of a drum? A drumhead tied round its rim rings at certain pitches and no others, and 33g found them for a round drum. Change the shape and the pitches change. So the list of pitches carries information about the shape. How much? In 1911 it was proved that the list tells the area: count the pitches below a high limit, and the count grows in step with the area. A finer count tells the length of the rim, so a drum that sounds exactly like a round drum is a round drum. In 1966 a question with this page’s title was asked in a famous article: does the full list of pitches tell the whole shape? Two years earlier a pair of shapes in sixteen dimensions had been found that ring alike, but drums are flat. The answer for flat drums came in 1992: two drums, each made of seven equal triangles glued in different patterns, ring at exactly the same pitches, and yet one cannot be laid on the other. This page counts the pitches of a rectangle by hand, builds the two drums, and proves in full that every pitch of one is a pitch of the other, by carrying each ringing shape across, triangle by triangle. Some shapes can still be heard: a disc, and since 2009 drums with smooth, highly regular rims and a mirror symmetry. Whether two drums without corners can ring alike is still open.

A drum’s pitches are ck/2π for the k with Δu = −k²u inside and u = 0 on the rim. For a rectangle L × W the k² are π²(m²/L² + n²/W²), and counting lattice points in a quarter ellipse gives N(K) = LWK²/(4π) − O(K); for any drum N(K) ≈ (area)K²/(4π) − (rim)K/(4π). Two drums glued from seven right triangles by the lines and points of a seven-point plane share area 3.5, rim 6 + 3√2 and every pitch: a grid T with three entries ±1 in each row carries the seven pieces of a ringing shape of one to a ringing shape of the other at the same k, and det T = −24 ≠ 0.

sky25.1The Pitches of a Rectangle

A drumhead obeys u_tt = c²Δu with u = 0 on the rim, and 33g looked for shapes that keep their form: u = φ(x, y) times a swing cos(ckt − t₀), with Δφ = −k²φ inside and φ = 0 on the rim. Each such k gives a pitch ck/2π. Call k² a number of the drum, counted as often as there are independent shapes with it. By 33f, shapes with different numbers are perpendicular.

Rectangle-tones technique: on the rectangle [0, L] × [0, W], φ = sin(mπx/L) sin(nπy/W) is a shape for every whole m, n ≥ 1, with number k² = π²(m²/L² + n²/W²). Separation, as 33h did for a plate, gives these; held in trust, by 32’s series in each direction, there are no others.

\partial_x^2 \varphi = -\tfrac{m^2\pi^2}{L^2}\varphi,\ \ \partial_y^2 \varphi = -\tfrac{n^2\pi^2}{W^2}\varphi @@ each sine, twice differentiated
\Delta\varphi = -\pi^2\big(\tfrac{m^2}{L^2} + \tfrac{n^2}{W^2}\big)\varphi @@ add
\varphi = 0 \text{ at } x = 0, L \text{ and } y = 0, W @@ sin of a whole multiple of π
\htmlClass{new}{k^2 = \pi^2(m^2/L^2 + n^2/W^2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rectangle-tones technique

On the unit square the numbers are π² times 2, 5, 5, 8, 10, 10, 13, 13, …: the pitches in ratio √(5/2) ≈ 1.58 and 2 to the lowest, not whole multiples, as 33g found for the disc. The number 5π² comes twice, from (1, 2) and (2, 1), so the square has two independent shapes at that pitch.

sky25.2Counting Pitches in a Quarter Ellipse

Let N(K) count the numbers of a drum up to K², with repeats. For the rectangle, k² ≤ K² says (m/(LK/π))² + (n/(WK/π))² ≤ 1: N(K) counts the whole-number points with m, n ≥ 1 in a quarter ellipse with half-axes LK/π and WK/π, whose area is ¼ π (LK/π)(WK/π) = LWK²/(4π).

Quarter-ellipse technique: for the rectangle L × W, LWK²/(4π) − (L + W)K/π ≤ N(K) ≤ LWK²/(4π).

(m, n) \text{ counted} \Rightarrow [m - 1, m] \times [n - 1, n] \text{ lies in the quarter ellipse} @@ its points have smaller coordinates, so are inside too
\text{the squares do not overlap} \Rightarrow N(K) \le \tfrac{LWK^2}{4\pi} @@ N squares of area 1 inside
(x, y) \text{ in the ellipse, } x, y \ge 1 \Rightarrow (\lfloor x\rfloor, \lfloor y\rfloor) \text{ counted, and its square holds } (x, y) @@ round down: still inside
\text{the rest lies in the strips } 0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le y \le 1:\ \text{area} \le \tfrac{LK}{\pi} + \tfrac{WK}{\pi} @@ each strip is 1 wide and at most one half-axis long
\htmlClass{new}{N(K) \ge \tfrac{LWK^2}{4\pi} - \tfrac{(L + W)K}{\pi}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the quarter-ellipse technique
  
N = 146 @@ counted by script
\tfrac{LWK^2}{4\pi} = \tfrac{2000}{4\pi} = 159.15 @@ the ceiling
159.15 - \tfrac{3\sqrt{1000}}{\pi} = 128.96 @@ the floor
\htmlClass{new}{159.15 - \tfrac{2(L + W)\sqrt{1000}}{4\pi} = 159.15 - 15.10 = 144.06} \quad \blacksquare @@ closer than either: the next footing’s rim term

So N(K)/K² heads to LW/(4π): the count of pitches tells the area. The points left out lie along the two axes, about LK/π + WK/π of them, and only some of those are lost; that is where a term in the length of the rim comes from.

sky25.3What Every Drum Lets You Hear

The same count works for any drum. Stated, proved in 1911 by trapping the drum between unions of small squares: N(K)/K² heads to (area)/(4π) for every drum. A finer count, guessed at once and proved in 1980 for drums with a smooth rim whose bouncing paths are not too often closed, adds a second term: N(K) = (area)K²/(4π) − (rim)K/(4π) plus terms small beside K.

Heard-disc technique: if a drum has exactly the numbers of a disc of radius ρ, it has the disc’s area and rim; and a disc is the only shape with that area and that rim. The first part is the count above, read twice. The second is held in trust: among all shapes of one area, the disc alone has the shortest rim, a result proved in the 19th century and not built on this ladder.

\text{area} = 2,\ \ \text{rim} = 6 @@ the rectangle
K^2 = 1000:\ \ 159.15 - \tfrac{6 \cdot 31.62}{4\pi} = 144.06;\ \ \text{counted } 146 @@ off by 1.3%
K^2 = 10\,000:\ \ 1591.55 - 47.75 = 1543.80;\ \ \text{counted } 1545 @@ off by 0.08%
\text{unit square: rim } 4;\ \ \text{disc of area } 1: \text{ rim } 2\sqrt\pi \approx 3.545 @@ same area, different rims
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the square and the disc of the same area are told apart by their counts}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second term differs by 0.455K/(4π)

So area and rim are heard by every drum, and a few further numbers built from the pitches are known to tell more for smooth rims. None of this tells the whole shape. The question that names this page asks whether everything is heard.

sky25.4Seven Triangles, Glued Two Ways

Take one right triangle with legs 1. Call its first leg the a side, its long side the b side, and its other leg the c side. Lay seven copies in the plane, each the flip of a neighbour across a shared side, as 20c flips; a side of a tile that meets no other tile is rim. The drawings show two such drums, the a sides in teal, the b sides in ink, the c sides in dashed ember.

  • Drum A. Across a sides: 1 with 4, 3 with 6; rim at 2, 5, 7. Across b sides: 2 with 4, 3 with 5; rim at 1, 6, 7. Across c sides: 4 with 5, 6 with 7; rim at 1, 2, 3.
  • Drum B. Across a sides and b sides: the same as A. Across c sides: 1 with 5, 3 with 7; rim at 2, 4, 6.
\text{area} = 7 \cdot \tfrac12 = 3.5 @@ both
\text{rim: } 3 + 3 + 3 \text{ sides};\ \ \text{six legs of } 1 \text{ and three long sides of } \sqrt2 @@ each kind of side is rim on three tiles
6 + 3\sqrt2 \approx 10.243 @@ both
\text{longest straight stretch of rim: A } 2,\ \text{B } 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83 @@ B has two long sides in one line; read off the drawings
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one cannot be laid on the other}} \quad \blacksquare @@ slides, turns and flips keep the lengths of straight stretches

The pattern is not luck. Number seven points and seven lines so that each line holds three points, each point lies on three lines, and any two points share exactly one line: the smallest plane of this kind. The gluing rules of A move the points about, and those of B move the lines in the same way. That shared pattern is what the next footing spends.

sky25.5Carrying a Shape Across

A shape φ on drum A, laid tile by tile onto the one triangle by the flips that made the drum, becomes seven functions u₁, …, u₇ on that triangle. Flips keep Δ, so each uᵢ has Δuᵢ = −k²uᵢ. The rules that make them one smooth shape: across a shared side the two values agree, and the two slopes out through that side add to 0; on a rim side the value is 0. Build seven new functions by the grid T: v₁ = −u₂ + u₄ − u₆, v₂ = −u₁ + u₄ − u₅, v₃ = u₃ + u₄ + u₇, v₄ = u₁ + u₂ − u₃, v₅ = u₂ + u₅ − u₇, v₆ = u₁ + u₆ − u₇, v₇ = −u₃ + u₅ + u₆.

Transplant technique: if u₁, …, u₇ are a shape of drum A with number k², then v₁, …, v₇ are a shape of drum B with the same number; and since det T = −24 ≠ 0, the two drums have exactly the same numbers, each as often.

\text{on a c side of A: } u_4 = u_5,\ u_6 = u_7,\ u_1 = u_2 = u_3 = 0 @@ A’s c rules
v_1 = u_4 - u_6 = u_5 - u_7 = v_5;\ \ v_3 = u_4 + u_7 = u_5 + u_6 = v_7 @@ B joins 1 with 5 and 3 with 7: values agree
v_2 = u_4 - u_5 = 0,\ \ v_4 = 0,\ \ v_6 = u_6 - u_7 = 0 @@ B’s rim at 2, 4, 6
\text{slopes: } s_4 = -s_5,\ s_6 = -s_7 \Rightarrow v_1' = -s_2 + s_4 - s_6 = -(s_2 + s_5 - s_7) = -v_5' @@ slopes add to 0
v_3' = s_3 + s_4 + s_7 = -(-s_3 + s_5 + s_6) = -v_7' @@ the same
\htmlClass{new}{\text{B’s c rules hold}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the a and b sides below
  
\text{a: } u_1 = u_4,\ u_3 = u_6,\ u_2 = u_5 = u_7 = 0:\ \ v_1 = v_4 = u_1 - u_3,\ v_3 = v_6 = u_1 + u_3,\ v_2 = v_5 = v_7 = 0 @@ B’s a rules
\text{a slopes: } v_1' = -s_1 - s_2 + s_3 = -v_4',\ \ v_3' = -s_1 + s_3 + s_7 = -v_6' @@ using s₄ = −s₁, s₆ = −s₃
\text{b: } u_2 = u_4,\ u_3 = u_5,\ u_1 = u_6 = u_7 = 0:\ \ v_2 = v_4 = u_2 - u_3,\ v_3 = v_5 = u_2 + u_3,\ v_1 = v_6 = v_7 = 0 @@ B’s b rules
\text{b slopes: } v_2' = -s_1 - s_2 + s_3 = -v_4',\ \ v_3' = -s_2 + s_3 + s_7 = -v_5' @@ using s₄ = −s₂, s₅ = −s₃
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every rule of B holds, and } \det T = -24} \quad \blacksquare @@ the transplant technique; checked symbol by symbol by script

The rules hold at every point of every side, so the v’s fit into one shape on B with Δ = −k², and it is not 0 because T can be undone. So each number of A is a number of B at least as often. The same argument with T undone goes back, since T’s rules hold read either way. Run on a computer, both drums give numbers 10.15, 14.62, 20.70, … in step.

sky25.6What the Ear Would Hear

Two drums with the same pitches are not yet two drums that sound the same. A struck drum rings with each of its shapes at some strength, and 37d found that two sounds are the same exactly when their recipes agree, the strength at each frequency. The pitches of drum A and drum B agree; the strengths depend on how each is struck.

\text{numbers } 10.15,\ 14.62,\ 20.70 @@ both drums, by script
\text{pitch ratios } \sqrt{14.62/10.15} = 1.200,\ \ \sqrt{20.70/10.15} = 1.428 @@ not whole numbers: 33g3’s tones that are not harmonics
\text{strike A in shape } u \text{ and B in shape } Tu @@ T carries each shape to a shape at the same pitch
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a blend of transplants keeps energy, and then the recipes agree}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the blend found by script, weights with √2 in them

A strike at a single point of A is carried by T to three points of B at once, one on each of three tiles, with signs. So a stick hitting one point of each drum is not covered by the transplant, and nothing forces those two recipes to agree. The honest reading of the 1992 result is narrower than its slogan: the pitches do not tell the shape; whether the whole sound of a stroke does is another question.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞25.oq.1Can two different convex drums, with no notches, ring at exactly the same pitches? Every flat pair known has corners pointing inwards.
Why it is hard: every known flat pair is built from tiles glued in two patterns, and gluing tiles makes notches; a convex pair would need a different idea, and none is known in the plane.
∞25.oq.2Can two different drums with smooth rims, with no corners at all, ring alike? The tiling method needs corners, where the tiles meet.
Why it is hard: for smooth rims the pitches carry far more information, through the lengths of closed bouncing paths, and it is not known whether that information always pins the shape down.
∞25.oq.3In 2009 drums were shown to be heard among drums whose rims are smooth with power-series pieces, have one mirror symmetry and meet a condition on their bouncing paths. Is the mirror needed?
Why it is hard: the proof reads the shape from the pitches term by term along one bouncing path, and the mirror is what removes an ambiguity of left and right that the terms cannot.
∞25.oq.4Since 2022 every ellipse that is nearly a disc is known to be heard among all drums with smooth rims. Is every ellipse heard?
Why it is hard: the proof works by comparing with the disc, where everything is known, and the comparison loses control as the ellipse gets longer.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question is older than its title. In the late 19th century physicists asked what the frequencies of a body say about its shape, and in 1911 a mathematician in Göttingen proved that the count of pitches of a drum grows like its area. He guessed the rim term at once; it took until 1980 to prove it, and then only for rims with a condition on their bouncing paths.

In 1964 a short note pointed out that two shapes in sixteen dimensions, built from two different grids of the kind that ∞21 met, ring alike. In 1966 an article in a monthly journal for a wide readership put the question for drums in the plane, and the title, borrowed from a colleague’s phrase, became one of the most quoted in mathematics. The article itself showed how much can be heard, and asked for more.

For a quarter century the answer was not known. In 1985 a method was found, in Japan, for building pairs of curved surfaces that ring alike from a group that acts in two matching ways. Flat drums were the hard case, since the method needs room to glue. In 1992 three mathematicians in the United States used it to build the first flat pair, and in 1994 a shorter version with the triangles of this page, and the transplant proof, made it something a student can check by hand.

Some said the slogan went too far: the 1992 drums have corners, and a physicist’s drum has a smooth rim, so the question for real drums is open. Others answered that the question as asked was answered, and that the corners are the point: a pitch list cannot see through a notch.

Since then the work has gone the other way, toward shapes that can be heard. Drums with smooth rims made of power-series pieces and two mirror symmetries were shown to be heard in 2000, with one mirror in 2009, and ellipses close to a disc in 2022. What is open now is how far this goes: nobody has found two smooth drums that ring alike, or a proof that none exist.

The accepted names

The numbers of a drum are the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the Laplacian, and the list is its spectrum; drums with the same spectrum are isospectral. The count N(K) is the eigenvalue counting function, and its first term is Weyl’s law; the second term is the Weyl conjecture on the boundary term, proved by Ivrii. The quarter-ellipse technique is a case of the Gauss circle problem’s lattice-point count. The disc’s shortest rim is the isoperimetric inequality. The two drums are the Gordon–Webb–Wolpert pair, here in the seven-triangle form of Buser, Conway, Doyle and Semmler; the seven-point plane is the Fano plane, and the matching actions are those of the group PSL(3, 2) on points and on lines. The transplant technique is transplantation, and the general method is Sunada’s method. Power-series rims are real-analytic boundaries. The question named in the title is Kac’s question.

Who said it first

Hermann Weyl proved the first term in 1911 and guessed the second; Victor Ivrii proved it in 1980. John Milnor gave the sixteen-dimensional tori in 1964, from lattices found by Ernst Witt. Mark Kac wrote “Can one hear the shape of a drum?” in the American Mathematical Monthly (1966); the phrase was Lipman Bers’s. Toshikazu Sunada published his method in 1985. Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert built the first plane pair in 1992. Peter Buser, John Conway, Peter Doyle and Klaus-Dieter Semmler gave the triangle versions and transplantation in 1994. Steve Zelditch proved the analytic results with two mirrors (2000) and one mirror (2009); Hamid Hezari and Zelditch proved the result for ellipses of small eccentricity (2022).

A proof that is not allowed

“The first two hundred numbers of the two drums agree to eight places by computer, so the drums are isospectral.” Not allowed as a proof: a computation of finitely many numbers, to finitely many places, says nothing about the rest. It is strong evidence, and the transplant is the proof.
