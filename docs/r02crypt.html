2·crypt · cryptography

Who May Open What?

Every lock answers one question: who may open what? Rung 2 built sets, the sift, all the subsets, and drawings whose regions are rows, and each of them goes into the answer. The page takes the question in two halves.

First the what, in 2crypt1 to 2crypt5. A term is hidden in a group: the group is said aloud, and the listener does not know which member is meant. A key is a sift: a condition, kept private, that leaves one member. Clues are sifts as well, and they combine. Then rung 2's own tools make the group large. The tree of all the subsets doubles with every member, and a drawing's pieces double with every set. Set trees and diagrams are a field where the code maker can set the listener a problem too long to finish.

Then the who, in 2crypt6 to 2crypt9, with the rule book drawn as an arrow chart in 2crypt8. Permissions are the same field at a gate: the rule is a key, the people let in are a sift, and a listener who can pass for a member of some groups still has to find the right piece.

2crypt1A Term Hidden in a Group

Who may open what? Start with the what. A ledger is kept in the archive of one town. A code maker has to tell a friend which town, and has to say it where a listener can hear. The town is Grayland. Saying “Grayland” gives it away.

So the code maker says a list instead: “It is one of these.”

Call the thing to be kept secret the term, and the set it is said in the group. The term is a member of the group, and nothing marks it out. A listed set has no first member and no favourite (2d8).

What does the listener hold now? One statement: the term, call it z, is in u. Over a listed set, that is an or across the list (2j1):

1c6 called “or” the word for being vague on purpose. This is add an option, done seven times. Each town the code maker adds is one more option, and the listener cannot tell the options apart.

A term hidden in a group: the listener holds the group, and not which member.

The group still tells something. Every town that is not on the list is crossed out. Hiding in a group gives away the group.

Two groups hide nothing. A group of one, {Grayland}, has the term as its only member (2d5). And a list without Grayland on it does not hide Grayland. It is simply not so.

One trouble is left. The friend heard the same list as the listener. So far the friend holds no more than the listener does.

2crypt2A Key Is a Sift

Before any of this, in private, the code maker and the friend agreed on a condition (2e2): “is on salt water, is not on Highway 101, and is in Grays Harbor County.” Let S(x) be “x is on salt water”, H(x) be “x is on Highway 101”, and C(x) be “x is in Grays Harbor County”. Anyone can check these on a map.

The friend takes the group and sifts it (2e5):

One town passes. The friend goes to Grayland.

A key is a sift. The group is said aloud, the condition is kept, and the sift of the one by the other is the secret.

The secret now has two halves, and they travel by different roads: the group in the open today, the condition in private some time before. Rung 2 says what each half is worth alone.

The group alone is 2crypt1: an or across eight towns.

The condition alone is a sift without a bowl (2e13). “The Grays Harbor County town on salt water and off Highway 101” picks out nothing until someone says which towns to sift. A listener who steals the condition and never hears the list has a sieve and nothing to pour through it.

Sifting never adds (2e8). Whatever the condition, what comes out was in the group. A key cannot find a term the group does not hold.

A key has to leave exactly one member. “Is on salt water” is so of Grayland, and it leaves four towns. A condition that is so of the term is not yet a key. It also has to fail for every other member. And a condition that no member passes leaves the empty set (2e10), and the friend goes nowhere.

The same key works on a new group. Next week the code maker says eight other towns, and the old condition sifts the new list. The key was never a town.

2crypt3Clues Sift Too

The listener cannot see the condition. But the listener hears things. Suppose the code maker lets slip: “Bring a boat.” The listener reasons that the town is on salt water, and sifts:

Four towns are left. 1·crypt called a statement about the secret that reaches a listener a leak, and there a leak crossed out rows (1crypt2). Here a leak is a condition, and it sifts the group. It is the same thing in the form of sets.

Then a second slip: “You will not have to leave Grays Harbor County.” So C. By 2i4, sifting by one condition and then by another is sifting by their and, and it leaves the common members of the two sifts (2f8):

Every clue is a sift, and sifts combine by and.

In this group the three conditions cut as finely as conditions can: every town has its own row of T and F in 2crypt2's table. So each condition keeps half of whatever is left. Eight, then four, then two, then one. Three clues like these, and the listener holds the key without ever being told it.

The code maker can use the same technique on purpose, and split the key. One friend is given S. Another is given ¬H ∧ C. The first sifts the group to four towns. The second sifts it to {Elma, Grayland}. Neither knows the town. Together they take the common members, and one is left. (43·crypt splits a secret among more people, with numbers.)

2crypt4The Tree of All the Parts

Eight towns is a small group. A patient listener can visit them all. The code maker needs a larger group, and rung 2 has a tool that makes one out of a short list.

Change the secret. It is no longer one town. Copies of the ledger are kept in several of the towns, and the secret is which ones. So the secret is a part of u, and not a member (2d12). The group it hides in is the set of all the parts of u, 𝒫(u) (2g1).

See it first with three towns: Amanda Park, Brinnon and Chimacum, written A, B and C below. 2g3 listed the parts by keep or drop, one decision for each member, and the decisions make a tree:

Each leaf is a part. Three towns give eight leaves. The secret part {Amanda Park, Chimacum} is one leaf: keep, drop, keep.

Now add a town. Every leaf splits in two (2g7). Four towns give sixteen leaves. For the eight towns of u the tree doubles eight times, to 256 leaves. The code maker wrote eight names.

Each name added costs the code maker one name and the listener a doubling.

The doubling does not have to stop. A list of twenty names can be read out in one breath, and its tree has more than a million leaves. Long before the list fills a page, the tree is past anything that can be searched leaf by leaf.

The key is still a sift. Every sift of u is a part of u (2e8), so a condition picks out a part just as it picked out a member:

The friend with the condition goes straight to the leaf. The listener has the tree.

One caution about trees. Putting the group inside another set, as in {u}, or putting its towns in bags inside bags (2d13), is not this. A merge tips the bags out (2f3), and the work is one step for each bag. A tree protects when the secret is one leaf among its leaves and the listener has to try the leaves.

2crypt5A Diagram: Easy to Check, Hard to Search

Go back to one town, and draw. Let s be the sift of u by S, h the sift by H, and c the sift by C. Draw u as a dashed border and s, h and c as three regions that overlap inside it. The regions cut the border into eight pieces, and each piece is a row (2i1). In this group every piece holds exactly one town. Grayland sits in the piece TFT.

A set expression shades pieces (2i2). So an expression that shades exactly one piece is a key, written as an instruction for shading:

Many expressions shade the same pieces (2i3). The code maker can write the key so that it does not show its piece at a glance:

Now use the diagram as a lock. The code maker puts the expression up where anyone can read it. The gate opens for whoever names a piece that it shades.

The friend was told the piece: TFT. Checking it is reading one row. Is a thing in that piece in s ∪ h? It is in s, so yes. Is it in c ∖ h? It is in c and not in h, so yes. The piece is shaded. That took two short looks, and it would take two short looks however many pieces the diagram had.

The listener has the expression and no piece. With no better idea, the listener tries pieces one after another. Three sets make eight pieces. Each set the code maker draws in doubles the pieces, as each town doubled the tree. With twenty sets there are more than a million pieces, and the expression is a few lines longer.

How did the code maker come by an expression with a shaded piece? By starting from the piece. Show an example is cheap for the one who holds the example (2b7). The code maker picked the piece first and wrote an expression around it. The listener is handed the some without the example, and has to find one.

Easy to check, hard to find. The one who drew the diagram started from the answer.

Set trees and diagrams are a field for this. A few lines from the code maker make a problem that the listener cannot finish by searching. The accepted word for such a problem is intractable.

2crypt6A Gate Sifts People

So far the page has hidden the what. Now the who.

The friend reaches the archive at Grayland, and the ledger is behind a gate. Let p be the set of people who work there. For each of them the gate has to answer one question: may this person open the ledger? A yes is a pair, such as (Evita, ledger), and the rule book is the set of the pairs that get a yes: a relation (2h11).

Pairs written one at a time do not last. So the keeper of the gate names groups. Let g be the guides, b the binders, and t the trainees. Each is a part of p. Then the rule is one condition on membership:

A gate is a sift of people, and its rule is a key.

Draw it as 2crypt5 drew the towns. The three groups are three regions inside p. Every person stands in one piece, the row of their memberships. The rule shades the piece TTF: a guide, a binder, not a trainee. The gate opens for the people standing in a shaded piece. That is what a permission is.

The rule names no person. It reaches whoever is in the groups when it is read (2b6). Put Randy among the binders, and Randy may open the ledger, and nobody looked at the ledger's rule that day.

Taking a permission away is harder than giving one. Suppose (Randy, ledger) was also written into the rule book by hand, long ago. Take Randy out of the binders, and the old pair still says yes. Push the not through (2j8): “not some route gives a yes” is “every route gives a no”.

To let someone in, one route is enough. To keep someone out, every route has to be shut.

2crypt7A Rule That Sifts to One

The rule of a gate names no person (2crypt6). A keeper can use that. Suppose the keeper says, where everyone can hear: “Cowboys may enter.” It sounds like a rule about a kind. Now suppose that only one cowboy ever comes to this gate.

Let w be the set of cowboys, and let v be the set of the people who ever come to the gate. The rule asks x ∈ w. The gate only ever asks it of a member of v. So the people it lets in are a sift of v:

One cowboy ever comes, so this set has one member (2d5). The keeper has let that one person in, and has not said a name.

To say that cowboys can enter, where only one cowboy ever comes, is to say that he can enter. It is a surreptitious sift by a permission.

Set this beside 2crypt2. There the group was said aloud and the condition was kept. Here the condition is said aloud and the group is kept. Who ever comes to the gate is known to the keeper, and the listener was not told. The secret still has two halves, and the listener still holds only one.

What the listener holds is the rule alone, and a rule alone is a sift without a bowl (2e13). From “cowboys may enter” the listener cannot tell whether a crowd was let in or one person. To learn which, the listener has to learn v, and that takes watching the gate. Reading the rule will not do it.

The rule never said “exactly one”. It reaches whoever is in the group when it is read (2b6). A second cowboy who comes is let in as well, and so is a listener who can pass for a cowboy (2crypt9). And if no cowboy comes, the sift is empty (2e10): the rule sounds generous and lets in nobody. The sift holds only for as long as the keeper is right about who comes.

So when a rule names a kind, do not stop at the kind. Ask what set the rule is read on, and sift.

2crypt8The Rule Book as an Arrow Chart

2b19 drew a statement with two gaps as an arrow chart: a dot for each thing in the world, and an arrow for each pair in the extension. “Who may open what” is such a statement. So draw the rule book.

Take a small world: three people, Evita, Randy and Jean, and three papers, the ledger, a map and a roster. Let O(x, y) say “x may open y”. Its extension is the rule book, however the gate came by it:

The who is at the tail of each arrow and the what is at its head. Let f be the set of the papers. Now read two lines in this world, as 2b19 reads any line.

\forall y\,(y \in f \Rightarrow \exists x\,O(x, y)) @@ every paper can be opened by someone: so
\exists x\,\forall y\,(y \in f \Rightarrow O(x, y)) @@ someone can open every paper: not so

On the chart the first line says that every paper has an arrow coming in. The second says that one dot sends an arrow to every paper. Evita has no arrow to the roster, so no dot does.

The signs changed places, and the meaning with them (2b13). A keeper wants the first line to be so, or a paper is lost to everyone. A keeper often wants the second line not to be so. A person who can open everything is the one a listener would most like to pass for.

Count the arrows at each dot. A paper with one arrow coming in depends on one person. A person with arrows going out to everything is one badge away from giving up the whole archive.

The chart also shows what the rule book does not list. Add a second kind of arrow, for “can pass for”. Suppose a listener can pass for Randy:

Nobody drew an arrow from the listener to the map. Follow the two arrows head to tail, and the listener is at the map.

A route is arrows followed head to tail, and a chart shows routes that no one wrote down. This is 2crypt6's rule in a picture. To keep someone out, every route to the paper has to be cut, and the chart is where the routes can be seen.

2b20 gives the keeper a test. To learn whether a set of rules can go wrong, look for one small world where it does. Keep the world small: a few dots are enough to show a paper nobody can open, or a person who opens everything.

2crypt9A Listener Who Can Pass for a Member

The gate cannot see into a person. It learns “x ∈ g” from something shown to it: a badge, a name on a list. A listener who can copy a badge can pass for a member. The listener is in no group, and stands in the piece FFF. Each badge the listener can copy changes one letter of that row.

First give the gate a simpler rule: g ∩ b. The listener copies every badge there is and shows them all. The gate reads TT and opens. No thought was needed. In a rule built only from ∩ and ∪, if a piece is shaded then so is every piece with more T's in its row. More badges never hurt.

A rule that only asks for memberships gives the listener a short road: claim everything.

Now the real rule, (g ∩ b) ∖ t. The listener shows all three badges. The gate reads TTT, and stays shut. One of the badges was the wrong one to show.

So for each badge the listener must decide: show it, or keep it back. That is keep or drop, and the decisions make 2crypt4's tree again. Three groups make eight leaves, and one opens the gate. The gate is a quiet one (1crypt5). It says “No” and nothing more.

The keeper can add groups as the code maker added towns. Each new group is one line in the rule, and it doubles the listener's tree. With twenty groups there are more than a million ways to claim, and every wrong one is a refusal that the keeper hears.

Permissions are a field too. Each group costs the keeper one line and the listener a doubling.

Now suppose the listener can only partly pass. The badges of the guides and the trainees can be copied. The binders are known by sight, and that cannot be copied. Then the listener can change the first and third letters of the row and not the second. The pieces within reach are four:

None of them is TTF. The listener can search for as long as they like, and the gate will not open.

A listener who can partly pass reaches only the pieces that differ from their own in the letters they can copy. The keeper wins outright when no shaded piece is among them. The keeper wins by the length of the search when the shaded pieces are few among many.

The true opener searches for nothing. Evita is a guide and a binder and not a trainee. She is standing in the shaded piece already, and the gate checks her with three looks. It is 2crypt5 again: easy for the one who holds the example, long for the one who has to find it.

2crypt10Too Long to Search Is Not Yet Safe

“Cannot finish by searching” is a claim about one way of working. The listener has others.

Clues still sift. A clue that settles one keep or drop cuts the tree in half. A clue that settles one set of the diagram halves the pieces (1crypt8). Twenty doublings are undone by twenty clues like that. What protects the secret is the leaves still standing after everything that has got out.

An expression can be rewritten. 2i9 drove an expression to its simplest by techs, and the listener may do the same:

Now the piece can be read off with no search at all. Tangle on the page is not hardness. The code maker's hope is only that nobody knows a short road of rewrites for the expression that was chosen.

The group may be small in fact. Twenty names, of which the listener can rule out fifteen by common sense, are a list of five.

And the code maker's own slips from 2crypt2 still count: a group that does not hold the term, or a key that leaves two members.

A gate has its own leaks. A gate that explains its refusal hands over its rule (1crypt5). And every true opener stands in a shaded piece, so every one of them holds the whole key. One of them saying which groups they are in, and which they are not, ends the search.

So anyone judging a lock of this kind works in this order. Count what has leaked. Try to rewrite. Only then count the leaves.

A long search protects only against a listener who has nothing better than searching.

Accepted names

A condition is a predicate. The set of all the parts of a set is its power set. Trying every leaf or every piece in turn is an exhaustive search, also called brute force. Growth by doubling is exponential growth. A problem for which no search is known that stays short as the problem grows is called intractable. Finding a row that makes a compound statement true is the satisfiability problem; finding a piece that a set expression shades is the same problem in the form of sets. A key split so that no holder alone has it is a split key, and the technique is secret sharing. When the term is a person, the group they hide in is called an anonymity set.
