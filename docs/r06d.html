6d · proof

A World Where It Is True, and One Where It Is False

3f showed that the axiom of infinity cannot be proved from what came before it, by building a world where everything earlier is true and the axiom is false. The choosing axiom is in a stranger position. It can be neither proved nor refuted from the other principles, and showing that took two worlds, one for each direction. This step explains both, and the earlier world, with the socks, that showed the way. The great constructions themselves are far beyond the ladder, and the step describes them in words, as 3f described its world. Two pieces are worked by hand. One is a small written-out world in which choice is true by a rule: the idea of the first world, in miniature. The other is the heart of the argument that, in the sock world, endlessly many pairs of socks have no choosing function at all.

To show a statement cannot be proved, build a world where every principle is true and the statement is false. To show it cannot be refuted, build one where it is true. For choice, both worlds exist.

6d1What “independent” Means, Again

3f's argument runs like this. A proof is a statement that names a column of axioms and techs. The techs care nothing for the world they are used in (1e). In any world where the axioms are true, every line of a proof from them is true, and so does its last line. So if there is a world where the axioms are true and a statement is false, there is no proof of that statement from those axioms. 3f called a statement independent of what came before when it is not a technique of it. Choice is independent in both directions: neither it nor its denial is a technique of the other principles. A world in which every axiom of a list is true has the accepted name a model of that list.

\begin{aligned}&\text{assume a proof of choice}\\ &\quad\text{from the other principles}\end{aligned} @@ aiming to refute
\begin{aligned}&\text{choice is true in every}\\ &\quad\text{world where they are true}\end{aligned} @@ the techs care nothing for the world (1e)
\begin{aligned}&\text{in the 1963 world they}\\ &\quad\text{are true, and choice is false}\end{aligned} @@ the world's description (below)
\bot @@ clash
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no proof of choice}} @@ refute
\begin{aligned}&\text{and with the described world, where}\\ &\quad\text{choice is true: } \htmlClass{new}{\text{no proof of its denial}}\end{aligned} @@ the same column, with the denial in choice's place

Which “other principles”? The two worlds were built for the standard list of axioms for sets. It holds the axioms of rungs 2 and 3, together with 4d's replacement, which the ladder names only as history, and with every statement true or false, which is rung 7's. That list holds every principle the ladder has laid down, and more. A world where the longer list is true is a world where the ladder's is true. So those worlds serve the ladder: no proof of choice from the ladder's principles, and no refutation either.

One caution carries over from 3f. Nobody can build a world for these principles inside a column of the ladder's techs. The worlds are built from the principles themselves, and what the techniques say is relative. The first world shows this: if the other principles are free of anything totally false, they stay free of anything totally false with choice added. The second shows the same with its denial added. Whether the principles themselves are free of anything totally false is held in trust here, the question 3f's last question raised.

6d2A Toy: The Written-Out World

Start small, with 3f's layers: V₀ = ∅, and each next layer all the parts of the one before. Their members can be written out to the last brace. Take V₃ = {0, 1, {1}, 2} and the layer above. Every collection you can write down there is finite, so 6c's finite-choice gives it a choosing function by calls. But there is a better way, and it is the idea behind the world where choice is true.

Write the members of V₃ in a fixed listing, say 0, 1, {1}, 2, in the order 3f's keep-or-drop wrote them. Every member of V₄ is a part of V₃, so every member of a member of V₄ is one of these four. Now take the condition “x is in A, and no member of A comes before x in the listing”. In each non-empty A it is so of exactly one member, the one listed earliest. That is because the listing puts the four in order, and a part of the list has one first entry (least-member, on the settled set of places of A's members). So it is a rule, of the boot kind. By Separation the sift by it is a set: a choosing function for any collection of non-empty parts of V₃, at once, with no calls.

\text{listing: } 0,\ 1,\ \{1\},\ 2\ \ \text{at places } 0, 1, 2, 3 @@ a matching between 4 and V₃ (4k's arrangement); place(x) names x's partner
\mathcal A = \{\{1, 2\},\ \{\{1\}, 2\},\ \{0, \{1\}\}\} @@ a collection of non-empty parts of V₃
c(\{1, 2\}) = 1,\ \ c(\{\{1\}, 2\}) = \{1\},\ \ c(\{0, \{1\}\}) = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ the earliest-listed member of each
c = \{(A, x) \in \mathcal A \times \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A : x \in A \land \forall y \in A\,(\text{place}(x) \le \text{place}(y))\} @@ the rule as a sift; one output each, by least-member and 4h's at-most-one least member

In this world the infinity axiom is not true, so it is only a toy: it shows the method, not the result. Change the listing and you get a different choosing function, by a different rule. What matters is that every set of the world sits in one listing, so “earliest” always makes sense.

6d3The Described World, Where Choice Is True

The world of described sets is built in stages, like 3f's layers, with one change. Where 3f took all the parts of the layer below, this world takes only the parts that can be described. Those are the parts sifted from the layer below by a condition that mentions only sets already built. Every set of the world, then, comes with a description, and a description is a string of marks. Strings of marks can be listed in order, the way 4i's numerals can, shorter before longer and then letter by letter. So every set has an earliest description, and the condition “the member of A whose earliest description comes first” is a rule. In the described world, choosing is like choosing shoes.

Two things in that sketch go beyond the ladder. The stages do not stop after every counting number: they go on, past all of them, along a longer run. The recursion that builds stages along such a run is not built here. And checking that every other principle is true in the world is long work. It was done, and the result announced, in 1938.

6d4The Sock World: Socks Made Exact

The other direction came first in a simpler setting. In 1922 a world was built containing things that are not sets, and the ladder has had such things since 2d: a cat is not a set. Its things are the socks of 6b. For each number n there are two socks s_n and s′_n, with the pair K_n = {s_n, s′_n}, and the collection 𝒦 = {K_n : n ∈ ℕ}. The socks are given to be different things, s_n ≠ s′_n, and alike in everything else.

A swap exchanges the two socks of some of the pairs, any pairs you like. It carries every set built from socks along with it: the members of a set are swapped, and their members, all the way down. A swap that touches pair k turns s_k into s′_k and back, and leaves every other sock where it was. Swaps leave every pair K_n in place, as a set, since {s′_n, s_n} = {s_n, s′_n} by same-members. So they leave 𝒦 in place too, and the numbers, which have no socks in them.

This swap is a new use of the word: 0b's swap axiom, swap equals, which writes one name of a thing in place of another, keeps its name and job. In this step the letter w names a swap, and w(x) is what the swap makes of x; it is a name like any other letter.

The sock world keeps only some of the sets built from socks. A set belongs to it when it and its members, and their members, all the way down, each have a support. A support is a finite list of pairs such that every swap touching none of those pairs leaves the set in place. A sock has a support: its own pair. A pair, 𝒦 and every number have the empty support. A choosing function for the first n pairs, picking s_i from K_i for i < n, has a support: the first n pairs, since a swap of any later pair does not touch it. So in the world finite-choice still gives a choosing function, as it must.

It was checked that every principle is true in the world (in the form that allows things that are not sets), and the step does not repeat that check. What it does show, line by line, is the heart of the matter. No-sock-picking technique: in the sock world, 𝒦 has no choosing function.

6d5No Picking Socks

\begin{aligned}&\text{assume } c \text{ is a choosing function}\\ &\quad\text{for } \mathcal K \text{ in the world}\end{aligned} @@ aiming to refute; the some used
c \text{ has a support } E\text{, a finite list of pairs} @@ the world's line
\text{call } k \text{ a number with } K_k \text{ not on } E @@ the numbers of the pairs on E are a finite set; past its greatest member (greatest-member), or 0 if it is empty
\text{let } w \text{ swap } s_k \text{ and } s'_k \text{ only} @@ a swap touching no pair of E
w(c) = c @@ c's support
c(K_k) = s_k \ \lor\ c(K_k) = s'_k @@ c chooses: c(K_k) ∈ {s_k, s′_k}; the listing line, a proved or
\text{case } s_k:\ (K_k, s_k) \in c @@ 4d's value line
\quad (w(K_k), w(s_k)) \in w(c) @@ a swap carries membership, pairs and all, along
\quad (K_k, \htmlClass{new}{s'_k}) \in c @@ w(K_k) = K_k; w(s_k) = s′_k; w(c) = c
\quad s_k = s'_k @@ c is a function: no input has two
\quad \bot @@ clash with s_k ≠ s′_k
\begin{aligned}&\text{case } s'_k\text{: the}\\ &\quad\text{same, with the names turned about}\end{aligned} @@ w also sends s′_k to s_k
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\exists c\,(c \text{ chooses for } \mathcal K \text{ in the world})} \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases; refute

The proof uses exactly what the socks picture said in words. Nothing in the world can tell the socks of a pair apart, because a swap exchanges them and changes nothing that matters. A choosing function would have to tell apart the socks of every pair. Any set of the world can only tell apart the socks of finitely many pairs, the ones on its support. 𝒦 is infinite, so some pair is left over. Notice too what the proof shows about listed choosing. 𝒦 is matched with ℕ: the numbers and 𝒦 are in the world, and the matching n ↦ K_n has the empty support. So even choice restricted to collections matched with ℕ fails in the sock world.

And notice why it does not work for shoes. A swap must keep everything the world is given. Suppose the world is given “s_n is a left shoe”. A swap of s_n and s′_n does not keep it, so it is not one of the world's swaps. And the left-shoe rule (6b's left-shoes) is a set of the world with the empty support.

6d6The 1963 World

The sock world's socks are not sets, and the standard list asks for a world of sets only. In 1963 a way was found to make new sets so alike that nothing in the old world can tell them apart. The world was then cut down to the sets with finite supports, much as the sock world had been. In this 1963 world every other principle is true and choice is false. So choice cannot be proved.

6d7What the Two Worlds Leave You

Like infinity, choice is chosen, not discovered. Neither world is a mistake: in each, every other principle is true. 6b laid the axiom down, as almost all of mathematics does. The cost of doing so is that some sets exist about which nothing more can be said than that they exist. Where a result uses the axiom, careful writers say so, and the ladder will.

Two short facts frame the whole picture. By 6c's only-the-infinite-fails, a world in which choice fails must hold an infinite collection with no choosing function. Every finite collection has one, in every world of the principles. And by 6b, that infinite collection can have no rule: shoes, and settled sets of numbers, have choosing functions in every world.

6d9Running It Backwards

Asked whether a statement follows from the principles, look for a proof and for a world at the same time; finding either ends the search. Asked whether a set belongs to the sock world, look for a finite support: a finite list of pairs such that swaps elsewhere leave it in place. If every finite list misses a pair whose swap moves it, it is not in the world. Asked why a rule works in the described world, find the listing it uses. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The described world is Kurt Gödel’s (1938); the sock world is Abraham Fraenkel’s (1922), built on Bertrand Russell’s socks; the 1963 world is Paul Cohen’s, and his method of making new sets is called forcing.

Gödel stated the result in two sentences: if the axioms of set theory without choice are free from contradiction, they remain so when the axiom of choice is added; the model that shows it consists of the constructible sets.

Gödel, “The consistency of the axiom of choice and of the generalized continuum-hypothesis”, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 24 (1938)

Accepted names

A world in which every axiom of a list is true is a model of that list. The described sets are the constructible sets, and the described world is the constructible universe. The standard list of axioms for sets is Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, ZF, or ZFC with choice. The longer run of stages, past every counting number, is the ordinals. The sock world is the Fraenkel model (with Mostowski, the Fraenkel–Mostowski permutation model).
