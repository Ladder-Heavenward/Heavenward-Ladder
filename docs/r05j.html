5j · technique

Ratio, proportion and units

5c built a fraction as a pair counted up to scaling. A ratio is a statement that uses the same idea to compare two amounts rather than to name one. A proportion is two ratios declared the same; solving one is 5c's cross-multiplying run as an equation. Then the step brings measuring onto the ladder. A quantity is a statement that names a number of units, and the metre, the second, the gram and the litre get their lines, as standard amounts of real things. Units ride along like letters, and changing them is multiplying by a fraction that is 1. That last trick is also the best check in all of science: if the units come out wrong, the working is wrong. The step ends the rung's ordinary rules.

A ratio is a statement that names a pair that means the same when both parts are scaled; two ratios are the same exactly when they cross-multiply to the same thing. A quantity is a statement that names a number times a unit, and units multiply and divide like letters. To change a unit, multiply by one, written as a fraction chosen so that the unwanted unit cancels.

5j1A ratio is a pair counted up to scaling

A drink mixed 3 cups of juice to 2 cups of water tastes the same as one mixed 6 cups to 4, or 30 spoons to 20. What stays the same is not either amount but the comparison, and the comparison survives scaling both parts by one number.

From here on, for rationals p and b, not both 0, the ratio p : b (read “p to b”) is the pair (p, b) with its scalings counted as one. So p : b = c : d means that some rational k ≠ 0 has c = kp and d = kb. Read p : b = c : d as “p is to b as c is to d”. A ratio of three or more parts, p : b : c, is the same with every part scaled by one k.

From here on, a colon between two rationals also means a ratio. 2d's colon inside braces, read “such that”, and the colon in 4d's f : A → B keep their jobs; what stands on each side says which.

“Counted as one” is honest. The scaling relation is reflexive (k = 1), symmetric (use k⁻¹) and transitive (multiply the two ks). So it is a sameness relation in 2g's sense, and a ratio is a class, built by the very trick 5b and 5c used. 5·cb2 comes back to this. For two parts with neither second part 0, the test is 5c's. Ratios-cross-multiply technique: if b ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0, then p : b = c : d exactly when pd = bc, exactly when p/b = c/d.

Two warnings live in the drink. First, 3 : 2 compares a part with a part. The juice is 3 parts out of 3 + 2 = 5, so it is 3/5 of the drink, not 3/2 of it. A ratio of a part to the whole is a fraction; a ratio of a part to a part is not the fraction of anything. Second, the simplest name is found as in 5h, by dividing out the gcd. 18 : 24 = 3 : 4, and 2.5 : 4 = 5 : 8 (k = 2 clears the decimal).

The oldest geometry book that has come down whole gives ratios a whole book of their own, building on an earlier teacher’s work. It opens by calling a ratio a kind of relation, in respect of size, between two amounts of the same kind.

“Of the same kind” matters. A ratio of cups to cups is a pure comparison. Comparing two amounts of different kinds, such as coins to weeks, is a rate, and it carries units; the second half of the step deals with those.

5j2Proportion: two ratios declared the same

From here on, a proportion is an equation of two ratios, p : b = c : d.

The = dividendIn 0b, “the blade is equally sharp and hard” said that how sharp = how hard. A proportion is that statement with numbers in it: one measure against another, the same as a third against a fourth.

If one of the four is unknown, the proportion is a condition, and 5g solves it. By ratios-cross-multiply it is the equation pd = bc, and from there multiplying-keeps-answers finishes. That is why cross-multiplying works: it is the definition of equal fractions, read as a change to both sides.

3 : 8 = x : 20 @@ 3 is to 8 as x is to 20
3 \cdot 20 = 8x @@ the ratios-cross-multiply technique
x = \tfrac{60}{8} = \tfrac{15}{2} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers with 1/8; lowest terms (5h)
\tfrac{15}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{20} = \tfrac{15}{40} = \tfrac38 \;\checkmark @@ check: the new pair cross-multiplies with (3, 8)

Its old accepted name is the rule of three: given three terms of a proportion, find the fourth. It runs much of daily arithmetic. If 4 people need 3 cups of flour, then 6 people need x cups with 4 : 3 = 6 : x, so 4x = 18 and x = 9/2. Sharing in a ratio is the same idea run from the whole. To share 60 coins in the ratio 3 : 2, count parts: 3 + 2 = 5 parts, so one part is 12 coins, and the shares are 36 and 24. Backwards: if the smaller share of a 3 : 2 split is 24, one part is 12 and the whole was 60.

5j3Direct and inverse proportion

From here on, y is directly proportional to x when y = kx for a rational k ≠ 0 that stays fixed, the constant of proportion. And y is inversely proportional to x when xy = k for a fixed rational k ≠ 0.

Directly proportional means y/x = k at every x other than 0: the ratio y : x never changes. Double x and y doubles. The quick test is 0: a direct proportion sends 0 to 0. A card game pays 3 coins to sit down and 2 coins for each hand played: y = 2x + 3. It pays 3 coins for no hands at all, so it is not a proportion. 2 hands pay 7 and 4 hands pay 11, and 11 is not twice 7. What stays fixed there is the step: each extra hand adds 2. Inversely proportional means the product never changes: double one and the other halves. Three painters who take 4 days would take 2 days with six painters, if six could work without getting in each other's way: 3 · 4 = 6 · 2. The right picture sets the three kinds side by side.

One of the first to study motion by measuring it proved this at the opening of that study. A body moving steadily covers, in equal times, lengths that are to each other as its speeds. For a fixed time, how far you go is directly proportional to how fast.

5j4Measuring: a number of units

To measure an amount of something, you pick a standard amount of the same kind. The amount may be how long a rope is, how long a wait lasts, how much stuff is in a bag, or how much a jug holds. You say how many of the standard, and what fraction of one more, the amount comes to. That is a ratio of the same kind, and its answer is a rational.

From here on, a quantity is written as a rational followed by a unit, as in 3 m: the thing measured amounts to that many of the unit. A unit is a statement that names a standard amount of one kind of thing, fixed by agreement. Unit marks are written upright. Each is a name of its unit, not of a rational: the m in 3 m is not a letter standing for a number.

The = dividendIn 0b, “equal parts flour and water” made the part a unit: 1 part = 1 part, whatever a part holds. A unit is that part, fixed by agreement, so 1 m here is 1 m anywhere.

This unit is a standard amount for measuring. 1f's units technique (⊤ and ⊥ as the do-nothings of ∧ and ∨), and 5h's unit fraction keep their names and meanings.

From here on, the front parts kilo (k), centi (c) and milli (m) mean a thousand of, a hundredth of and a thousandth of. So 1 km = 1000 m, 1 cm = 1/100 m, 1 mm = 1/1000 m, 1 kg = 1000 g, 1 mg = 1/1000 g, 1 mL = 1/1000 L. And 1 minute (min) = 60 s, 1 hour (h) = 60 min, 1 day = 24 h. By international agreement, 1 inch (in) = 2.54 cm, and 1 foot (ft) = 12 in.

The mark m now has two jobs among units: before another unit mark it is milli, and alone it is the metre, so mm is a millimetre.

Every real measurement is rounded. A rope “3.2 m long” is a rope whose length, measured as well as the tape allows, rounds to 3.2 m (5h's ≈). The 2 says the tenths were counted and the hundredths were not. The units are fixed by agreement; the numbers in front of them are always somebody's reading. (Temperature is a different sort of quantity: 0 °C is not “no hotness”, and 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. The scale is a rule of the shape y = kx + c, not a proportion.)

From here on, quantities multiply and divide as their numbers do, with their units multiplied and divided alongside, as letters would be. So (3 m)/(2 s) = (3/2) m/s, and (2 m/s)(3 s) = 6 m. The unit m/s is read “metres per second”: from here on, per names dividing by a unit. Quantities may be added only when they carry the same unit: 3 m + 2 m = 5 m, by spread on ℚ; 3 m + 2 s names nothing.

So the speed of a steady motion is the length covered divided by the time taken: 120 km in 2 h is 60 km/h. And like letters, unlike units do not collapse: 3 m + 2 s stays unwritten, the way 3x + 2y does not become 5 of anything.

5j5Changing units is multiplying by one

Converting rests on one observation. Since 1 km = 1000 m names one length twice, the quantity (1000 m)/(1 km) is a length divided by itself. That is 1 (reciprocal on ℚ), and multiplying by 1 changes nothing (times-one on ℚ). It changes the name, the way 6/8 renames 3/4. Its reciprocal, (1 km)/(1000 m), is 1 as well. You choose the one that makes the unwanted unit cancel, numerator against denominator. In the chain below, a stroke through a unit mark shows where it has cancelled; the stroke is a reminder only and claims nothing new. A quantity worth 1 used this way has the accepted name conversion factor.

72\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} @@ a car's speed; we want metres per second
72\,\tfrac{\cancel{\text{km}}}{\text{h}}\cdot\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1000\,\text{m}}{1\,\cancel{\text{km}}}} @@ km in the numerator, so the one goes in with km on its denominator
72\,000\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\cancel{\text{h}}}\cdot\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1\,\cancel{\text{h}}}{3600\,\text{s}}} @@ h in the denominator, so the next one has h in the numerator (1 h = 60 · 60 s)
\tfrac{72\,000}{3600}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 20\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} @@ what is left: 72 000/3600 = 20

The units do the thinking. Had you used (3600 s)/(1 h) by mistake, nothing would cancel. You would be left with m · s/h², a unit that tells you at once you needed the factor's reciprocal. That is the tempting illegal move here, multiplying where you should divide, and you never need to remember which: watch the units. A unit with a square in it needs its factor twice. An acceleration is how much a speed grows each second, so its unit is metres per second per second, m/s/s, written m/s². To turn 9.8 m/s² into km/h², the metres need one factor and the seconds two, since s² is s · s. So 9.8 · (1/1000) · 3600 · 3600 = 127 008 km/h².

5j6Units as a check

In a true equation about measured things, both sides carry the same unit, and every term of a sum carries the same unit. That is the cheapest check there is.

A builder of the mathematics of heat made this a standing test. Every quantity in an equation of physics has its own dimensions, and the terms of one equation cannot be right unless their dimensions agree. He used it to test his own working.

The law for a dropped stone is d = ½ g t². t seconds after letting go it has fallen d metres, and g ≈ 9.8 m/s² is the acceleration of falling.

d = \tfrac12\, g\, t^2 @@ the claim
\text{m} \overset{?}{=} \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\cdot\text{s}^2 @@ put each quantity's unit in its place; the ½ has none
\text{m} = \text{m}\;\checkmark @@ s² cancels: the units agree

Agreeing units do not prove a rule right (the ½ is invisible to them), but disagreeing units prove it wrong. If you misremember the time to reach speed v at acceleration α as t = α/v, its unit is (m/s²)/(m/s) = 1/s, not seconds; t = v/α has (m/s)/(m/s²) = s.

From here on, the average speed of a trip is its total length divided by its total time.

One more illegal move, because it fools nearly everyone. Drive 120 km out at 60 km/h and back at 40 km/h. The average speed is not 50 km/h. Out takes 120/60 = 2 h, back takes 120/40 = 3 h, so it is 240 km/5 h = 48 km/h. You spend longer at the slow speed, so it counts for more. Percents trip the same wire (5h): 50% off and then 20% off is 60% off, not 70%.

5j8Running it backwards

Given a converted answer, recover the chain. If 4 became 240, the factor was 60, so it was minutes to seconds or hours to minutes, and only the units can say which. Given two facts that name one amount twice, make a one of your own. Since 1 in = 2.54 cm, (1 in)/(2.54 cm) is a one, and a metre is 100/2.54 = 5000/127 inches, about 39.37. Given a rule with units, run the check backwards: what combination of h metres and g in m/s² has the unit s²? h/g, or a pure number times it, which is why every rule for the time a stone takes to fall h metres is built from it.

That completes the rung's ordinary rules. You can take away anything from anything and divide anything by anything but 0, and you know why. The integers and the fractions are classes of pairs, and their twelve techniques are proved. You can change both sides of an equation and know which changes keep the answers. You can isolate a letter, solve two equations at once, add and divide fractions, write decimals and round them, expand and factor, and set up a proportion with its units checked.

Now you're a person who can use algebra.

Who said it first

The geometry book is Euclid’s Elements, building on Eudoxus; the student of motion is Galileo; the builder of the mathematics of heat is Joseph Fourier.

Euclid gave ratios a whole book of the Elements, building on Eudoxus, and opens it by calling a ratio a kind of relation, in respect of size, between two amounts of the same kind.

Euclid, Elements V, Definition 3, after Heath (1908)

Galileo, opening his study of motion, proved that a body moving steadily covers, in equal times, lengths that are to each other as its speeds: for a fixed time, how far you go is directly proportional to how fast.

Galileo, Two New Sciences (1638), Third Day, on uniform motion, after Crew and de Salvio (1914)

Fourier made this a principle: every quantity in an equation of physics has its own dimensions, and the terms of one equation cannot be right unless their dimensions agree; he used it as a test of his own working on heat.

Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822)

Accepted names

The constant of proportion is also called the constant of proportionality. A rate given per one unit, such as 60 km/h, is a unit rate. A fraction worth 1 used to change units is a conversion factor. Checking a rule by its units is dimensional analysis.
