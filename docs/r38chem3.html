Why Does a Metal Atom Gather Neighbours?

Why does a metal atom gather neighbours? Claire has two bottles from one metal: iron tablets, pale green, and rust, red-brown. The metals in the middle of the table take several charges, and at each charge they collect a fixed number of neighbours round them. This page counts the charge, the electrons left, and the neighbours, and finds the same count at work in blood.

A metal in the middle of the table has empty shapes, and neighbours with unshared pairs fill them. Count the metal’s charge, its d electrons and two for each neighbour, and the count says what the cluster is and what it can still do.

38chem31One Metal, Several Charges

Claire has two bottles from one metal: iron tablets, pale green, and rust, red-brown. In 6·chem’s count the iron is at +2 in one and +3 in the other. The metals in the middle of the table do this freely, and sodium never does. In water such a metal atom is never bare: it sits in the middle of a cluster of neighbours, and the cluster carries one charge.

Charge-balance technique: the charge of the metal plus the charges of its neighbours is the charge of the whole cluster.

Iron with six waters and a charge of +3 overall: water has no charge, so the iron is +3. Iron with six cyanides and −3 overall: each cyanide is −1, so the iron is +3 again.

38chem32Counting What Is Left

38·chem gave the five d shapes. How many electrons a metal has in them decides almost everything on this page, and it is counted from the table (5chem11): the number of the metal’s column, out of eighteen, less its charge. Nickel in column 10 at +2 has 8. Chromium in column 6 at +3 has 3. Cobalt in column 9 at +3 has 6. Iron in column 8 at +3 has 5. Zinc in column 12 at +2 has 10: every d shape full, which is why zinc compounds are white.

Count technique: the count of d electrons is the column number less the charge.

Iron is in column 8. At +2 it keeps 6 d electrons, at +3 it keeps 5. Five is one electron in each of the five shapes, 38chem9’s spread with nothing paired, and that is a comfortable count: iron left in air and water goes on from +2 to +3, and rust is the +3.

The step between them costs little either way, so iron can take an electron and hand it on, again and again. That is what iron does in the chain of 19·bio, and it is why the body needs it. Iron tablets are the +2 form because the gut takes that one up.

38chem33Neighbours That Bring Both Electrons

A gap (2chem2) is filled by a pair, one electron from each partner. A neighbour of a metal brings both. It is a molecule or ion with an unshared pair (38chem8), and the metal has empty shapes to take the pair into. Six neighbours at the corners of an octahedron is the commonest arrangement. Four is next, at the corners of a tetrahedron or of a flat square. Which of the two a nickel compound has was settled by a magnet (38chem9).

38chem34One Neighbour With Six Hands

A doctor treating lead poisoning gives a molecule that wraps the lead and carries it out of the body. It has six unshared pairs placed so that all six reach one metal atom. Why does the metal let go of six waters for it? Count the loose pieces (38chem23). Before: the metal with its six waters, and one wrapping molecule: two pieces. After: the wrapped metal and six waters: seven. More loose pieces have more ways to be, so the change runs forward even when the new bonds are no stronger than the old.

Many-hands technique: a neighbour that holds a metal at several points displaces neighbours that hold at one, because the change sets more pieces free.

Blood does the same with iron: four of iron’s six places are held by one flat ring.

38chem35Same Neighbours, Different Places

Put four neighbours of one kind and two of another on an octahedron. The two can sit at adjacent corners or at opposite ones, and no turn of the octahedron (20) carries one arrangement to the other: two substances, as with 9chem26’s same side and opposite side. Three and three give two again. On a flat square, two and two give two; on a tetrahedron, two and two give one. That count is how a flat square was told from a tetrahedron before anyone could see either, the argument of 19chem4.

One of the two flat-square arrangements of a platinum compound is a medicine and the other is not.

Arrangement technique: count the arrangements of neighbours that no turn of the shape carries into each other; that is the number of substances with that formula.

38chem36Eighteen, and a Metal That Is Given Back

One s shape, three p shapes and five d shapes make nine, and nine shapes hold eighteen electrons. Add the metal’s d count to two for each neighbour. Nickel at 0 with four carbon monoxides: 10 and 8. Iron at 0 with five: 8 and 10. Chromium at 0 with six: 6 and 12. Each is 18, and each is a stable compound; the count even says how many neighbours each metal takes. A cluster at 16 has one shape empty. It can take one more neighbour, let two neighbours join each other, and release the product, coming back to 16.

That loop is how such a metal speeds a reaction without being used up.

Eighteen technique: a cluster whose d count plus two for each neighbour is 18 is full; one at 16 has room for one more, and a cycle between the two returns the metal unchanged.

38chem37The Ring in Blood

Evita holds up two vials of blood drawn from one arm, bright red from an artery and dark red from a vein. The colour belongs to iron. In blood each iron sits at +2 in the middle of a flat ring that holds four of its six places with four nitrogens, the many-handed neighbour of 38chem34. The protein holds the fifth place from below. The sixth is open, and an oxygen molecule sits there in the lungs and leaves in the tissues. The two colours are the cluster with six neighbours and with five.

Three things follow from the earlier footings. Carbon monoxide brings a pair to the same sixth place and holds it a few hundred times more firmly than oxygen, which is how it kills. Iron pushed to +3 no longer takes oxygen there, so blood has an enzyme whose work is to put it back to +2. And the ring is not special to blood: with magnesium in the middle it is the green of a leaf, and with cobalt it is a vitamin.

Ring technique: a ring that holds four places of a metal leaves two for the work, and what the cluster does is set by which metal, at which charge, and what holds the fifth place.

Accepted names

The middle of the table is the d block, and its members the transition metals. A charge counted this way is an oxidation state. The cluster is a coordination complex, a neighbour a ligand, the number of neighbours the coordination number. A many-handed neighbour is a chelate, and the six-handed one here is EDTA; the ring in blood is haem. Arrangements with adjacent and opposite neighbours are cis and trans, and the platinum medicine is cisplatin. The count of eighteen is the 18-electron rule, and the cycle a catalytic cycle. The ring with iron is haem, the ring itself a porphyrin, the proteins haemoglobin and myoglobin; iron at +3 gives methaemoglobin; the leaf’s is chlorophyll and the vitamin is B12 (its ring differs slightly).
