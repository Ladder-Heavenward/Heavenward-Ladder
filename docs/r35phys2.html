Why Are Big Earthquakes Rare?

Why are big earthquakes rare? Somewhere on earth there is a small earthquake every few minutes, a strong one most weeks, and a great one about once a year. The counts fall by the same factor for each step up in size, all the way from tremors no one feels to the largest ever recorded. This page counts quakes per step of size with 17’s logs, fits the straight line on log paper with 31n, finds how much more energy each step frees, sees that a power law has no typical size, like 35i’s dusts, and finds where the line must bend, because a total has to stay finite (22).

Each step up in magnitude, earthquakes become ten times rarer, so log(count) against magnitude is a straight line of slope −1. Each step frees about 31.6 times the energy, so in energy the count is a power law with no typical size. Each step’s quakes together free about 3.2 times more energy than the step below, a sum that would grow without end, so the line must stop at some largest quake.

35phys22A Straight Line on Log Paper

\log_{10} N(M) = a - b\,M @@ 
b \approx 1:\ \ N \text{ divides by } 10 \text{ per step} @@ fitted by least squares (31n)
\htmlClass{new}{a \approx 8.2:\ \ N(5) \approx 10^{3.2} \approx 1\,600} @@ the world’s line

35phys24No Typical Size: Power Laws and Scale

Put the two lines together, eliminating M: the count is a power of the energy.

N \propto 10^{-M},\ \ E \propto 10^{1.5M} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{N(\text{energy} \ge E) \propto E^{-2/3}} @@ a power law
\dfrac{N(2E)}{N(E)} = 2^{-2/3} \approx 0.63 \text{ at every } E @@ the same ratio at every size

Scale-free count technique: when counts fall by a fixed factor for each fixed factor of size, the count is a power of the size, a straight line on log-log paper, and no size is typical: the pattern looks the same at every scale, like 35i’s dusts. There is no “normal” earthquake, as there is a normal height for a person.

35phys25Where the Line Must Bend: a Total That Has to Stay Finite

Add up the energy freed each year, step by step. Each step up has a tenth as many quakes, each freeing 31.6 times as much.

\text{each step: } \tfrac{1}{10} \times 31.6 \approx 3.16 \text{ times the step below} @@ 
\text{total} = E_5\,(1 + 3.16 + 3.16^2 + \cdots) @@ a geometric sum of ratio above 1 (22)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{grows without end, so the line must stop at some largest quake}} @@

The biggest quakes free most of the energy, and the straight line cannot go on for ever: the earth’s faults are only so long, so the count must fall away below the line at the largest sizes. The largest quake recorded was about magnitude 9.5. A building code sets the quake a site must survive from the line and its bend, reading the chance of a given size each year straight off the plot.

Accepted names

The line is the Gutenberg-Richter law, b its b-value; the magnitude used today is the moment magnitude, and looking the same at every scale is self-similarity. The bend at large sizes is a corner magnitude. The largest recorded quake was in Chile in 1960.

Who said it first

Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter set out the line in the 1940s and 1950s (not yet revisited for the dates).
