1d · proof

If, and exactly when

“If it rains, the street is wet.” That is a promise, and nearly everything proved on the ladder is a promise of this shape. This step gives “if” its mark and its techs: carry a promise through when its condition is met, and make a promise by supposing its condition and keeping it. It sets the ground floor's tech unpack beside an “if”, and shows why the two stay different techs. It proves the earliest techniques about “if”: that promises chain, and that a promise can be turned about with denials. It shows exactly where the turned-about form refuses to turn back, and builds “exactly when” as a promise each way. It ends with the illegal moves that convince more people than any others.

Having “if it rains, the street is wet” and having rain, write that the street is wet. To earn a promise, suppose its condition for a while and reach its result. The if promises nothing when its condition fails; keep that in view and the classic mistakes cannot touch you.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1b (¬, ⊥, suppositions, refute, clash) and 1c (∧, join, keep; ∨ and cases), and on 0c's unpack. 1e writes “every” around an “if”; 1f gives ⇒ its table and shows the table is forced by these techs; 1g is the rulebook for the if family; 1i turns prove-an-if into the commonest proof shape.

1d1The word, then the mark

Stands on 1c1 (and, and its mark).

An “if” has a condition and a promise. “If it rains, the street is wet”: the condition is that it rains; the promise is that the street is wet. The sentence says that whenever the condition is met the promise is kept. It says nothing about days when the condition is not met. On a dry day the street may be wet (a street-cleaning truck went by and hosed it) or dry, and the sentence is not broken either way. The only way to break it is a day when it rains and the street stays dry. Here is the mark:

P ⇒ Q is a statement, made of two. It is called a conditional, or an implication.

Read P ⇒ Q as “if P then Q” or “P implies Q”. P is the hypothesis (the condition) and Q the conclusion (the promise). Those are the names the ladder uses from here on.

1d2Other ways to say the same arrow

Stands on 1d1 (the word, then the mark).

English has other ways of saying the same arrow. This step keeps to “if P then Q”, and takes three of the others one at a time.

“Q whenever P.” “Q whenever P” is “if P then Q” with its halves swapped in the sentence: every time the condition is met, the promise is kept. The same condition and the same promise make the same arrow.

“No P without Q.” The one way to break an if is a day with the condition and without the promise: rain, and a dry street. “No P without Q” forbids exactly that day and nothing else, so it is the same arrow. In marks it is ¬(P ∧ ¬Q): never P and not-Q together.

“P only if Q.” Read it slowly, because it sounds as if it runs from Q to P. It says P never happens except alongside Q: it rains only if the street is wet. That is “no P without Q”, the phrasing just above, so it is the same arrow again, P ⇒ Q. 1g sets it beside the rest of the English phrasings.

None of these says that P makes Q happen. The arrow claims no cause; it claims only that P never comes without Q. “If it is a cat, it is an animal” is a true if, and nothing in it is caused: being a cat does not make anything become an animal.

Long ago, two teachers of logic quarrelled over this very line. One said an “if” is false in one case only: when it starts from something so and ends at something not so. The other wanted more: that it could never start so and end not so, at any time. The ladder's arrow is the first teacher's: an if is false in one case only, the day it rains and the street is dry. The basement tells the rest of the quarrel.

1d3Carry an if through

Stands on 1d1 (the word, then the mark).

Carry an if through: from P and P ⇒ Q, you may write Q. This uses an “if”. You do this all day. If it rains, the street is wet. It is raining. So the street is wet.

Carrying through is the tech everyone uses without noticing. Here it is, named.

It rains. If it rains, the street is wet. So, carrying the if through, the street is wet.

The tech needs both sentences in hand. The if alone gives nothing: it is a promise, and a promise is kept only when its condition is met. The rain alone gives nothing either: without the promise, rain says nothing about streets.

1d4Prove an if

Stands on 1d3 (carry an if through) and 1a3 (a supposition's report).

Prove an if: suppose P; reach Q under that supposition; drop it; conclude P ⇒ Q. This makes an “if”. It is the conclusion 1a drew for its reports, now a tech with a name. The supposition is 1a's, with 1a's rule: once it is dropped, what was said under it is gone, and what remains is the if, not P and not Q.

Proving an if is the tech with the supposition. Suppose the condition; under that supposition, reach the promise by whatever techs you have; drop it. Here the promise is widened with 1c's add-an-option, and the point is to watch the supposition start and end.

Given: if it rains, the street is wet. Suppose, until we drop it, that it rains. Carry the if through: the street is wet. Add an option (1c): the street is wet or the gutter overflows. Now drop the supposition. What we keep is an if: if it rains, then the street is wet or the gutter overflows.

Once the supposition is dropped, you do not have “it rains”, and you do not have “the street is wet or the gutter overflows”. You have only the if that joins them. That is what a promise is: a statement about what follows, not a claim that anything has happened.

1d5A mark and its meaning

Stands on 1d4 (prove an if) and 1b7 (not the same thing as).

The ladder's own marks are unpacked and packed like words. The line that brought in ≠ runs both ways. From x ≠ b you may write ¬(x = b), by unpack. From ¬(x = b) you may write x ≠ b, by pack. 1b did exactly this unpacking and packing.

The mark built at the end of this step, “exactly when”, is a promise each way between two statements. A “from here on” line makes the same two-way traffic between a mark and its meaning. The line makes that traffic by being laid down, not by being proved. That is why unpack stays a tech of its own, beside the if: an if must be earned, and a meaning is laid down.

1d6Promises chain

Stands on 1d4 (prove an if) and 0b7 (chain).

In English: if it rains the street is wet; if the street is wet, Ann wears boots; so if it rains, Ann wears boots. Promises pass along a chain. Its medieval name is the hypothetical syllogism; here it is the chain technique for If.

Chain technique for If: if P then Q, and if Q then R; so if P then R. It needs two ifs, where the first one's promise is the second one's condition. The argument is prove-an-if wrapped around carry-through, used twice. It is this rung's version of 0b's chain technique.

Start from P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R. Suppose, until we drop it, P. Carry the first if through: Q. Carry the second if through: R. Now drop the supposition. What we keep: P ⇒ R. A long chain is the technique made again on its own result, as in 0c.

1d7Turn and deny

Stands on 1d6 (promises chain), 1b5 (refute) and 1a5 (suppositions inside suppositions).

Turn-and-deny technique for If: from “if P then Q”, you may write “if not Q, then not P”. It needs one if. The denials swap places and the arrow turns about. For example: from “if it rains, the street is wet”, you may conclude “if the street is not wet, it did not rain”.

Its argument is the rung's first supposition inside a supposition written out, so here it is in rain and street first. If it rains, the street is wet: that is given. Suppose, until we drop it, that the street is not wet. While that stands, suppose it rains as well. Carry the if through: the street is wet. That clashes with “the street is not wet”: totally false. Drop the inner supposition, by refute: it did not rain. Now drop the outer one. What we keep is an if: if the street is not wet, it did not rain.

Nothing in that argument used what rain or streets are. It is the same for any P and Q. Start from P ⇒ Q. Suppose, until we drop it, ¬Q. While that stands, suppose P as well. Carry the if through: Q. That clashes with ¬Q: totally false. Drop the second supposition, by refute: ¬P. Now drop the first one. What we keep is an if: ¬Q ⇒ ¬P.

1d8Where the turn back stops

Stands on 1d7 (turn and deny) and 1b12 (the step you cannot take).

Now try to turn it back. From ¬Q ⇒ ¬P, can you get P ⇒ Q? Follow the same plan and watch where it stops.

Start from ¬Q ⇒ ¬P. Aiming at Q, suppose P. While that stands, suppose ¬Q as well. Carry the if through: ¬P. That clashes with P: totally false. Drop the second supposition, by refute: ¬¬Q. And there it stops. To reach Q we need a step from ¬¬Q to Q. That is 1b's gap: no tech on this rung takes it.

So the contrapositive follows from the original by techs, and the original does not follow from the contrapositive, not on this rung. They will be made interchangeable on rung 7, where the gap is closed. Until then the licence runs only from the original to the contrapositive, and 1g's rulebook records which way. This is the same gap as 1b's, wearing an arrow. It is worth noticing how naturally the attempt ran into it: nothing was done wrong, the techs simply stop a step short.

1d9Not, as an if

Stands on 1d4 (prove an if) and 1b12 (the step you cannot take).

“Ann is not at home” and “if Ann is at home, then totally false” say the same thing. Not-as-if technique: “not P” and “if P, then the totally false” each give the other. It needs one of the two, and gives the other. A denial is a promise of the totally false. This technique makes exact 1b's hearing of ¬P as “P leads to something totally false”.

One way. Start from ¬P. Suppose P. That clashes with ¬P: totally false. Drop the supposition: P ⇒ ⊥.

The other way. Start from P ⇒ ⊥. Suppose P. Carry the if through: ⊥. Drop the supposition, by refute: ¬P.

So ¬P and P ⇒ ⊥ are interchangeable by techs, in both directions. Refute is nothing but prove-an-if aimed at ⊥, while clash is carry-through aimed at ⊥. The ladder could have built ¬ out of ⇒ and ⊥; it built them separately because “not” comes earlier in speech.

1d10Free ifs

Stands on 1d9 (not, as an if) and 1b10 (when nothing is true, everything is permitted).

Next, a cheap if. Free-if technique: a denied hypothesis, or a conclusion you have, makes any if free. From ¬P you may write P ⇒ Q, for any Q. From Q you may write P ⇒ Q, for any P. You already use the first kind when you say “if pigs can fly, I’ll eat my hat”. The if is safe because its first half is false.

From a denied hypothesis. Start from ¬P. Suppose P. That clashes with ¬P: totally false. Everything is permitted, so Q. Drop the supposition: P ⇒ Q. Any Q at all: “if it rains, the moon is made of cheese”, on a day it does not rain. The Q itself does not leave the supposition: it goes when the supposition is dropped. What leaves is only the if, and that if is empty. To carry it through you would need P, and P is what ¬P refuses.

From a conclusion you have. Start from Q. Suppose P. Q is still in hand. Drop the supposition: P ⇒ Q. Any P at all.

The free ifs sound like tricks and are not. A promise is only ever broken by its condition being met and its promise failing. If the condition cannot be met, or the promise is already kept, nothing can break it, and the techs say so. 1f will show that these techniques are exactly what forces the shape of the ⇒ table.

1d11Exactly when

Stands on 1d4 (prove an if) and 1c2 (join, and keep either part).

“The light is on exactly when the switch is up” promises in both directions. If the switch is up, the light is on. And if the light is on, the switch is up. Here is its mark, defined with 1c's ∧:

P ⇔ Q is a statement, made of two. It is called a biconditional.

No new techs are needed, because the mark is defined in terms of marks that have theirs. To make P ⇔ Q, prove an if each way and join. To use it, keep the direction you need and carry it through.

Using one. Given: the light is on exactly when the switch is up. Given: the switch is up. Unpack ⇔: if the light is on, the switch is up; and if the switch is up, the light is on. Keep the right part (1c): if the switch is up, the light is on. Carry it through: the light is on.

Typing an answer, with no key for these marks? Write the words they are read as: if P then Q in place of P ⇒ Q, and P exactly when Q in place of P ⇔ Q. They mean the same thing, and the ladder reads them the same way.

1d12Making an exactly-when

Stands on 1d11 (exactly when) and 1c3 (and turns about).

Here is the making, written out. An exactly-when comes out of two suppositions, one for each direction, joined after both have been dropped. The first supposes the left side and reaches the right. Dropped, it leaves an if. The second supposes the right side and reaches the left. Dropped, it leaves the other if. Only then are the two ifs joined.

Take (P ∧ Q) ⇔ (Q ∧ P). First direction. Suppose P ∧ Q. Keep left: P. Keep right: Q. Join them the other way about: Q ∧ P. Drop the supposition: (P ∧ Q) ⇒ (Q ∧ P).

Second direction. Suppose Q ∧ P. Keep left: Q. Keep right: P. Join: P ∧ Q. Drop the supposition: (Q ∧ P) ⇒ (P ∧ Q).

Now join the two ifs. Pack ⇔, which is what the mark means: (P ∧ Q) ⇔ (Q ∧ P).

The second supposition cannot use what was said under the first. When the first supposition was dropped, everything said under it went with it (1a's rule), and only the if it made remains. So each direction is earned on its own. That is also why the join waits until the end: the join needs two ifs that both stand, and each stands only once its supposition is dropped. For the light and the switch, each direction would be one promise, proved by supposing its own side.

1d13An exactly-when turns about and chains

Stands on 1d12 (making an exactly-when) and 1d6 (promises chain).

An exactly-when turns about for free: keep right, keep left, join, which is 1c's turn-about technique for And. “The light is on exactly when the switch is up” gives “the switch is up exactly when the light is on”, with no new tech. And it chains: the chain technique for If in each direction, then join. The slides do the chaining.

From now on every “from here on, this mark means…” line may be read as an exactly-when between the mark and its meaning. It is the same two-way traffic that 1b's unpack and pack run on. Unpack is the exactly-when used one way, and pack is the same exactly-when used the other.

1d14Illegal moves

Stands on 1d3 to 1d13.

The if is the connective people most often misuse, and the misuses have names that are centuries old. Each is convicted the same way: a way things could be in which everything given is still true and the conclusion is false. Eight moves look like uses of this step and are not; the list below names all eight. Two of them matter most. Affirming the consequent goes from Q, and if P then Q, to P. Denying the hypothesis goes from not P, and if P then Q, to not Q.

Q,\;\; P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } P\,? @@ affirming the consequent: “the street is wet; if it rained, the street is wet; so it rained.” The street-cleaning truck went by
\neg P,\;\; P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } \neg Q\,? @@ denying the hypothesis: “it did not rain, so the street is not wet.” The street-cleaning truck again
P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } Q \Rightarrow P\,? @@ turning the arrow about without the denials: “if it is a cat it is an animal, so if it is an animal it is a cat.” Rex is an animal and not a cat
P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } P \text{ causes } Q\,? @@ reading a cause into the arrow: “if it is a cat, it is an animal” makes nothing happen. The arrow claims only that P never comes without Q
P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } Q \text{ causes } P\,? @@ reading the cause the other way: a wet street does not make it rain. The arrow claims no cause in either direction
P \Rightarrow Q,\;\text{ so } Q\,? @@ an if is not its conclusion: having the promise is not having the thing promised
\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose } P; \dots Q; \text{ so } P \Rightarrow Q;\\ &\text{and } Q \text{ (from under it)}\,?\end{aligned} @@ a sentence said under the dropped supposition cited after the drop (1a's rule). Outside, you have the if and nothing else
\begin{aligned}&\text{“You can have dessert if you finish}\\ &\quad\text{your dinner”, read as } \Leftrightarrow\,?\end{aligned} @@ an everyday if is often heard as a promise each way. The ladder's ⇒ promises only from hypothesis to conclusion; a ⇔ must be written

The first two illegal moves are the ones to carry with you. Both are convicted by the same street-cleaning truck: a dry day with a wet street. Every given line is so on that day. The street is wet. The promise is kept: it was not broken. It did not rain. And the conclusions “it rained” and “the street is dry” both fail. The if never promised anything about dry days. Both mistakes consist in hearing a promise the if did not make. The picture lets you watch the truck do this.

1d15Running it backwards

Stands on 1d4 (prove an if) and 1a10 (reading the foot).

Wanting “if it rains, the gutter overflows”, suppose rain and go looking for the gutter. The foot's outermost mark is the arrow, so the last move is prove an if, and the first sentence is the supposition of its hypothesis. Everything else happens under that supposition.

Wanting P ⇒ Q, you know the argument's shape before anything else: suppose P, reach Q, drop the supposition. So the search moves under the supposition and asks how to reach Q with P in hand. Wanting Q itself, look for an if you have whose conclusion is Q, and then try to prove its hypothesis. Or look for a chain of ifs leading to Q from something you have. Wanting ¬P, 1b's plan applies. An if with hypothesis P is the usual source of the clash: suppose P, carry through to something denied. Every proof shape in 1i begins with this reading of the goal's outermost mark.

The accepted name

Carry an if through is what medieval logicians called modus ponens, the mode that puts: it puts the hypothesis in and takes the conclusion out.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • P \Rightarrow Q is also written P → Q, and in older books P ⊃ Q. Read it “if P then Q”, or “P implies Q”.
  • P \Leftrightarrow Q is also written P ↔ Q, and said “P if and only if Q”, shortened to “P iff Q”.
  • Three relatives of P \Rightarrow Q have names: its converse Q \Rightarrow P, its inverse \neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q, and its contrapositive \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P. Only the contrapositive follows from it, by this step's turn-and-deny technique for If; the converse and the inverse are the classic illegal moves.

Who said it first

The two teachers were Philo the Dialectician and his teacher Diodorus Cronus; the poet was Callimachus. The ladder’s ⇒ is Philo’s if, and its accepted name is the material conditional.

Philo said an “if” is false in one case only: when it starts from something so and ends at something not so. His teacher Diodorus wanted more, that it could never start so and end not so, at any time. The quarrel grew so loud that the poet Callimachus said even the crows on the rooftops were cawing about which ifs are true.
