1d · proof

If, and exactly when

“If it rains, the street is wet.” That is a promise, and nearly everything proved on the ladder is a promise of this shape. This step gives “if” its mark and its using tech: carry a promise through when its condition is met. It sets 1a’s tech unpack beside an “if”, and shows why the two stay different techs. It shows what a line can do with the promises it holds: carry them through one after another, and find the clash when a promise’s result is denied. And it builds “exactly when” as a promise each way.

Having “if it rains, the street is wet” and having rain, write that the street is wet. The if promises nothing when its condition fails; keep that in view and the classic mistakes cannot touch you.

1d1The word, then the mark

An “if” has a condition and a promise. “If it rains, the street is wet”: the condition is that it rains; the promise is that the street is wet. The if says that whenever the condition is met the promise is kept. It says nothing about days when the condition is not met. On a dry day the street may be wet (a street-cleaning truck went by and hosed it) or dry, and the if is not broken either way. The only way to break it is a day when it rains and the street stays dry. Here is the mark:

P ⇒ Q is a statement, made of two.

Read P ⇒ Q as “if P then Q” or “P implies Q”. P is the hypothesis (the condition) and Q the conclusion (the promise). Those are the names the ladder uses from here on.

1d2Other ways to say the same arrow

English has other ways of saying the same arrow. This step keeps to “if P then Q”, and takes three of the others one at a time.

“Q whenever P.” “Q whenever P” is “if P then Q” with its halves swapped in the sentence: every time the condition is met, the promise is kept. The same condition and the same promise make the same arrow.

“No P without Q.” The one way to break an if is a day with the condition and without the promise: rain, and a dry street. “No P without Q” forbids exactly that day and nothing else, so it is the same arrow. In marks it is ¬(P ∧ ¬Q): never P and not-Q together.

“P only if Q.” Read it slowly, because it sounds as if it runs from Q to P. It says P never happens except alongside Q: it rains only if the street is wet. That is “no P without Q”, the phrasing just above, so it is the same arrow again, P ⇒ Q. 1f sets it beside the rest of the English phrasings.

None of these says that P makes Q happen. The arrow claims no cause; it claims only that P never comes without Q. “If it is a cat, it is an animal” is a true if, and nothing in it is caused: being a cat does not make anything become an animal.

Long ago, two teachers of logic quarrelled over this very line. One said an “if” is false in one case only: when it starts from something so and ends at something not so. The other wanted more: that it could never start so and end not so, at any time. The ladder's arrow is the first teacher's: an if is false in one case only, the day it rains and the street is dry. The basement tells the rest of the quarrel.

1d3Carry an if through

Carry an if through: from P and P ⇒ Q, you may write Q. This uses an “if”, and you do it all day:

It rains. If it rains, the street is wet. So, carrying the if through, the street is wet.

The tech needs both lines in hand. The if alone gives nothing: it is a promise, and a promise is kept only when its condition is met. The rain alone gives nothing either: without the promise, rain says nothing about streets.

Carry-through can be used again on its own result. A line that holds P, P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R reaches Q, and then R: a line runs along a chain of promises as far as the promises reach.

And a line that holds P ⇒ Q, ¬Q and P carries the if through to Q, which clashes with ¬Q: the line equals ⊥. Which of the three to give up is the output a line cannot offer, and waits for proofs. Making a promise of your own, such as P ⇒ R from the two above, comes later.

1d4A mark and its meaning

The ladder's own marks are unpacked and packed like words. The line that brought in ≠ runs both ways, by unpack and by pack, as 1b7 did.

The mark built at the end of this step, “exactly when”, is a promise each way between two statements. A “from here on” line makes the same two-way traffic between a mark and its meaning. The line makes that traffic by being laid down, not by being proved. That is why unpack stays a tech of its own, beside the if: an if must be given or earned, and a meaning is laid down.

1d5Exactly when

“The light is on exactly when the switch is up” promises in both directions. If the switch is up, the light is on. And if the light is on, the switch is up. Here is its mark, defined with 1c's ∧:

P ⇔ Q is a statement, made of two.

No new techs are needed, because the mark is defined in terms of marks that have theirs. To make P ⇔ Q in a line, have an if each way and join. To use it, keep the direction you need and carry it through.

Using one. Given: the light is on exactly when the switch is up. Given: the switch is up. Unpack ⇔: if the light is on, the switch is up; and if the switch is up, the light is on. Keep the right part (1c): if the switch is up, the light is on. Carry it through: the light is on.

No key for these marks? Write if P then Q and P exactly when Q, as 4a17 wrote end.

1d6An exactly-when turns about, and carries along

An exactly-when turns about for free: keep right, keep left, join, which is 1c's turn-about technique for And. “The light is on exactly when the switch is up” gives “the switch is up exactly when the light is on”, with no new tech. And a line can run along exactly-whens: from P ⇔ Q, Q ⇔ R and P, keep the direction you need from each and carry it through, to Q and then R. The slides run it.

From now on every “from here on, this mark means…” line may be read as an exactly-when between the mark and its meaning. That is the two-way traffic of 1d4: unpack is the exactly-when used one way, and pack is the same exactly-when used the other.

1d7Running it backwards

Wanting “Ann wears boots”, look for the promise whose result it is, and go looking for its condition. The foot is a plain statement, so the last move is a carry-through, and the line must come to hold the condition first.

Wanting Q, look for an if in the line whose conclusion is Q, and then try to reach its hypothesis. Or look for a run of ifs leading to Q from something the line holds. Wanting the line to equal ⊥, an if is the usual source of the clash: carry it through to something the line denies. Wanting P ⇒ Q itself, a line on this rung cannot make it; it must be given. Every search begins with this reading of the foot’s outermost mark.

Accepted names

P ⇒ Q is a conditional, or an implication; P ⇔ Q is a biconditional. Carry an if through is what medieval logicians called modus ponens, the mode that puts: it puts the hypothesis in and takes the conclusion out. The chain technique is their hypothetical syllogism.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • P \Rightarrow Q is also written P → Q, and in older books P ⊃ Q. Read it “if P then Q”, or “P implies Q”.
  • P \Leftrightarrow Q is also written P ↔ Q, and said “P if and only if Q”, shortened to “P iff Q”.
  • Three relatives of P \Rightarrow Q have names: its converse Q \Rightarrow P, its inverse \neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q, and its contrapositive \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P. Only the contrapositive follows from it, by 3a15's turn-and-deny technique for If; the converse and the inverse are the classic illegal moves.

Who said it first

The two teachers were Philo the Dialectician and his teacher Diodorus Cronus; the poet was Callimachus. The ladder’s ⇒ is Philo’s if, and its accepted name is the material conditional.

Philo said an “if” is false in one case only: when it starts from something so and ends at something not so. His teacher Diodorus wanted more, that it could never start so and end not so, at any time. The quarrel grew so loud that the poet Callimachus said even the crows on the rooftops were cawing about which ifs are true.

Sextus Empiricus, Against the Logicians II.112–118 and Against the Grammarians I.309; paraphrase
