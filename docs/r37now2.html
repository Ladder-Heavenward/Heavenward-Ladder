37·now2 · research front

What if a machine writes the proof?

What if a machine writes the proof? Every July the strongest school pupils in the world sit two papers of four and a half hours, three problems each, and a perfect score is 42. In 2024 a program scored 28, one point short of the gold line, writing its answers in a calculus that a small checker certified, though it took days on some problems where the pupils had hours. In 2025 two programs scored 35, the gold line, writing in ordinary words within the time, and were marked by the same kind of graders as the pupils. A program had settled a sixty-year-old question in algebra back in 1996, and others have since found proofs no person would have written, some of them far too long for any person to read. What makes the question hard is that rung 37 already answers half of it. Step 37b showed that the proofs from a listed collection of axioms can be listed, so a machine that tries them all in order finds every proof there is. That is useless in practice, and the whole subject is about doing better. This page counts why trying them all is hopeless, says what changes when a guide orders the search, sets out the 2024 and 2025 results and how each was checked, and asks what a proof is for when nobody can read it.

Listing every proof finds every reached statement, but the list is so long that a fast machine would take millions of years to get through the proofs of twenty marks. A guide that orders the search does not change what is found, only how soon, and whatever it finds is certified by the core. In 2024 a guided search wrote contest proofs in a calculus and had them checked; in 2025 programs wrote them in words and had them marked. A proof certifies; it does not always explain.

37now21Trying every proof

37b’s proof-listing technique says that when G can be listed, the proofs from G can be listed one after another. A machine can run down the list, check each with the proof-check technique, and stop when it meets a proof of φ.

Search-finds technique: if G can be listed and G reaches φ, running down the list of proofs finds a proof of φ after finitely many steps; if G does not reach φ, the run never stops.

G \vdash \varphi:\ \text{some proof is at place } k \text{ of the list} @@ the proof-listing technique (37b)
\text{the run checks places } 1, 2, \dots, k \text{ and stops} @@ each check stops (the proof-check technique)
G \nvdash \varphi:\ \text{no place holds a proof, and the run goes on} @@ for PA and the unreached statement U, this happens though U is true (37i)
\text{strings of 20 marks from 16: } 16^{20} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{24} @@ a short proof is longer than this
\htmlClass{new}{10^{9} \text{ checks a second: about } 3.8 \times 10^{7} \text{ years}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1.2 × 10¹⁵ seconds, at 3.16 × 10⁷ seconds a year

So the search is complete and hopeless. A run that has not stopped tells nothing: the proof may be at the next place, or there may be none. Whether some recipe could tell those cases apart ahead of time is the subject of rung 38.

37now22A guide for the search

A person proving something does not try every string of marks. They try the next step that looks promising, and drop it when it leads nowhere. A program can do the same with rules of thumb, and since the 2010s with a guide trained on many proofs: given the statement and the lines so far, it proposes the next line, or ranks the next moves, and the search follows the best first.

Guided-search technique: a guide may order the search in any way at all, and every proof it finds is certified by the core; the guide changes how soon a proof is found, never whether a found proof is right, and a guide that is wrong most of the time costs only time.

\text{guide proposes } \ell_{j+1} \text{ given } \varphi, \ell_1, \dots, \ell_j @@ by any rule, learned or not
\text{core checks } \ell_{j+1} @@ the small-kernel technique (37·now)
\text{rejected or leading nowhere: back up and try the next proposal} @@ the search goes on
\htmlClass{new}{\text{accepted to the end: a proof, whatever the guide was}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the core alone decides

Such a search settled a question in 1996 that had been open since the 1930s: whether three short equations about a joining and a negating operation give all of the algebra of true and false of 7d. A program found the derivation after about eight days of searching. Mathematicians then read it, and it was short enough to read.

37now232024: formal contest proofs

At the 2024 contest, six problems, worth 7 points each, were turned by people into formal statements in a checker’s calculus. The statement-read technique applies: those translations were the part that had to be read. A program, a guided search with a guide trained by having it prove and check millions of formal statements, then searched for proofs, and a second program did the geometry.

\text{solved: two algebra, one number, one geometry} @@ 4 problems of 6
4 \times 7 = 28 \text{ of } 42 @@ full marks on each, given by the usual graders
\text{gold line: } 29 @@ set by the pupils’ scores that year
\htmlClass{new}{28:\ \text{silver, one point short}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two counting problems unsolved

The proofs were checked by a core, so their correctness did not rest on the graders. But the time did not match: the pupils had nine hours for six problems, and the program took minutes on some and up to three days on others. The two problems it did not solve were the counting ones, where a formal statement is hard to write and the next step hard to guess.

37now242025: proofs in words

At the 2025 contest at least two programs read the problems as the pupils did, in words, and wrote proofs in words, within the time limit. Their answers were marked by graders as pupils’ answers are. Each solved five of the six and scored 35, which was the gold line. At least one other program reached gold with formal proofs checked by a core.

Two-checks technique: a proof in a calculus is certified by the core and needs only its statement read; a proof in words is certified by readers, and needs the same trust as any person’s proof, no less and no more.

\text{2024: formal, checked by a core} @@ the small-kernel technique
\text{2025: in words, marked by graders} @@ as a pupil’s answer is
\text{a reader can miss a gap; a core cannot} @@ the statement-read technique still applies to the formal kind
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the two kinds need different trust}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the words are faster to write, and to get wrong

A program writing in words can write a proof that looks right and is not, as a person can. The best results join the two: a proof in words, turned into lines, checked by a core.

37now25What a proof is for

In 2016 a search settled whether the numbers from 1 on can be coloured red and blue so that no three of the same colour satisfy x² + y² = z². They can up to 7824, and not up to 7825. The proof is a record of the search, about two hundred million million bytes, checked by a separate small checker. Nobody will read it, as nobody will write out 37m’s code of a proof.

3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2,\ \ 5^2 + 12^2 = 13^2 @@ 5 is in two triples
\text{colour } 3, 4 \text{ red}:\ 5 \text{ blue} @@ else 3, 4, 5 are one colour
5 \text{ blue}:\ 12, 13 \text{ not both blue} @@ else 5, 12, 13 are one colour
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each choice forces others, through thousands of triples}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the search followed every forced chain

A proof does two jobs. It makes a statement certain, and it shows why the statement holds, so that the reader can do the next thing. A checked record does the first job fully and the second not at all. A short proof found by a machine, like the 1996 one, can do both. Mathematicians now argue about how much of the second job they are willing to give up, and nobody yet knows whether programs will find proofs that explain.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The contest is the International Mathematical Olympiad. The search-finds technique says the set of statements PA reaches is computably enumerable; rung 38 says what that means. A guided search with a learned guide is neural theorem proving. The 1996 question was the Robbins problem, whether Robbins algebras are Boolean algebras, and the 2016 colouring is the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem, settled by a SAT solver.

Who said it first

Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw’s Logic Theorist proved results of Principia Mathematica by guided search in 1956. Herbert Robbins posed the question about his equations in the 1930s; William McCune’s program EQP found the proof in October 1996. Marijn Heule, Oliver Kullmann and Victor Marek settled the colouring in 2016. In 2024 Google DeepMind’s AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 scored 28 at the Olympiad, working in Lean. In 2025 a Gemini Deep Think model and an experimental OpenAI model each scored 35 in natural language, and Harmonic’s Aristotle reported a gold score with Lean proofs.

A proof that is not allowed

“The program found a proof of φ in a second; the search-finds technique would take millions of years; so the program must have skipped the checking.” Not allowed: the search-finds technique bounds a blind search. A guided one may reach the right place at once, and the core checks the lines it gives, which takes no longer than the lines are.
