26j · technique

Why 1/(1 + x²) has radius 1

Rung twenty three found that the endless sum for one over one plus x squared, one minus x squared plus x to the fourth and so on, settles only between minus one and one. That is strange. The function itself is smooth on the whole line. It never climbs, never breaks, never even comes close to trouble. So why does its sum stop at one? The line cannot answer, because the trouble is not on the line. In the plane, one plus z squared is zero at i and at minus i, and both sit at distance one from the centre. A power sum settles on discs, not on stretches of the line, and its disc can grow only until it meets a point where the function fails. This step proves that, and turns it into a way to read off how far a sum reaches without writing a single piece of it.

A power sum about a point settles on a disc in the plane. Where the function has a complex slope, the disc reaches at least that far. Where the function has a pole, the disc must stop. So the reach is the distance to the nearest trouble in the plane, even when the line shows none.

26j1The plane’s sum is the line’s sum

23c measured how far a power sum reaches along the line: its reach R, with the sum settling in size where |x − w| < R and wandering where |x − w| > R. 26a1 noted that the same holds in the plane: a power sum settles in size on a disc |z − w| < R and wanders outside it. The reach on the line and the radius of the disc are one number, since the sizes |uₖ||z − w|ᵏ depend only on |z − w|.

Plane-reach technique: let w be real, and let f have a complex slope at every z with |z − w| < R, with f real on the floor; then rung 23’s power sum of f about w reaches at least R, and equals f on that whole disc.

f(z) = \textstyle\sum u_k (z - w)^k \text{ for } |z - w| < R @@ the rebuilt-in-the-plane technique (26g2)
f(x) = \textstyle\sum u_k (x - w)^k \text{ for real } x,\ |x - w| < R @@ the part of the disc that lies on the floor
u_k = \tfrac{f^{(k)}(w)}{k!},\ \text{slopes taken along the floor} @@ the smooth-sum technique (23e3) for the sum on the floor
\text{so } \textstyle\sum u_k(x - w)^k \text{ is rung 23's sum of } f \text{ about } w @@ the same pieces, one by one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{its reach is at least } R} \quad \blacksquare @@ it settles at every z of the disc (26g2), so at every real x with |x − w| < R
  
1 + z^2 = 0 \iff z = i \text{ or } z = -i @@ (z − i)(z + i) = z² + 1; a product is 0 only if a factor is
\tfrac{1}{1+z^2} \text{ has a complex slope at every } z \ne \pm i @@ the plane-slope technique (26d1): a quotient
|z| < 1 \Rightarrow z \ne \pm i @@ |±i| = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sum } 1 - x^2 + x^4 - \cdots \text{ reaches at least } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plane-reach technique with w = 0, R = 1

Nothing on the floor stops the disc. The disc is stopped by i and −i, which the floor never sees.

26j2A pole stops the sum

The plane-reach technique gives a floor for the reach. A pole gives a ceiling.

Pole-wall technique: if the power sum S(z) = Σ uₖ(z − w)ᵏ equals f at every z with |z − w| < d, and f has a pole at a point p with |p − w| = d, then the reach of S is at most d. No power sum can be continued past a pole.

\text{suppose the reach } R' > d @@ for contradiction
S \text{ has a complex slope, so is continuous, on } |z - w| < R' @@ term by term (23e3, in the plane); the slope-means-no-gap technique (13b)
z_n = w + (1 - \tfrac1n)(p - w):\ |z_n - w| < d,\ z_n \to p @@ points on the segment from w to p, inside the smaller disc
S(z_n) = f(z_n) \to S(p) @@ S equals f there; S is continuous at p, since |p − w| = d < R′
f(z) = \tfrac{g(z)}{(z - p)^m},\ g(p) \ne 0 \Rightarrow |f(z_n)| \text{ passes every ceiling} @@ a pole of order m (26h1): |g(zₙ)| heads to |g(p)| > 0, and |zₙ − p|ᵐ to 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the reach of } S \text{ is at most } d} \quad \blacksquare @@ a list that heads to S(p) cannot pass every ceiling

So when f has a complex slope on the disc of radius d and a pole on its rim, the reach is exactly d: the plane-reach technique gives at least d, the pole-wall technique at most d.

\cos z = 0 \iff z = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + \pi k @@ e^(2iz) = −1 (26i3) and the whole-turn technique (26b3)
\tan z = \tfrac{\sin z}{\cos z} \text{ has a complex slope on } |z| < \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ the plane-slope technique (26d1)
\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1 \ne 0,\ \cos' \tfrac{\pi}{2} = -1 \ne 0 @@ a pole of order 1 at π/2 (26h1): (z − π/2) tan z heads to −1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sum of } \tan \text{ about } 0 \text{ reaches exactly } \tfrac{\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ plane-reach and pole-wall

26j3The nearest trouble

For a quotient of polynomials the two techniques meet, and the reach can be read off from the roots of the bottom.

Nearest-trouble technique: let f = p/q with p and q real polynomials sharing no root in ℂ, and w real with q(w) ≠ 0; then the power sum of f about w reaches exactly to the nearest root of q in the plane.

q \text{ has finitely many roots in } \mathbb{C};\ d = \text{the least } |r - w| @@ the root technique (25d4); q(w) ≠ 0, so d > 0
f \text{ has a complex slope at every } z \text{ with } |z - w| < d @@ the plane-slope technique (26d1): q(z) ≠ 0 there
\text{reach} \ge d @@ the plane-reach technique (26j1)
q = (z - r)^m q_1,\ q_1(r) \ne 0,\ p(r) \ne 0 @@ r a nearest root, repeated m times (25d4); p and q share no root
f = \tfrac{p/q_1}{(z - r)^m}:\ \text{a pole of order } m \text{ at } r @@ p/q₁ has a slope at r and is not 0 there (26h1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reach} = d} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pole-wall technique (26j2), with S equal to f on |z − w| < d (26j1)
  
\tfrac{1}{1+x^2} \text{ about } 1:\ |1 - i| = |1 + i| = \sqrt2 @@ roots ±i
\tfrac{1}{1 - x^2} \text{ about } 0:\ |1| = |-1| = 1 @@ roots ±1, on the floor
\tfrac{1}{1 - x + x^2} \text{ about } 0:\ \big|\tfrac{1 \pm i\sqrt3}{2}\big| = 1 @@ the quadratic formula in ℂ; no root on the floor
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reaches: } \sqrt2,\ 1,\ 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nearest-trouble technique

1/(1 + x²) and 1/(1 − x²) both have sums reaching 1 about 0. On the line the second blows up at ±1 and the first is calm everywhere. In the plane they look alike: each has two troubles at distance 1. The line sees only the troubles that happen to sit on it. A sum, which lives on discs, sees them all.

Moving the centre moves the disc. About w = 3, the sum of 1/(1 + x²) reaches √10, because √(3² + 1²) is the distance from 3 to ±i. No centre on the floor ever gets a reach less than 1.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The reach is the radius of convergence. The plane-reach and pole-wall techniques together say that the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of a function holomorphic near w is the distance from w to the nearest singularity, when the function cannot be continued past it. The nearest-trouble technique is that statement for rational functions. The pieces of the sum of x/(eˣ − 1) are Bₖ/k!, where the Bₖ are the Bernoulli numbers.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy found in 1831 that a power series converges in the largest disc in which its function stays holomorphic. Jacob Bernoulli’s Ars conjectandi, printed in 1713, introduced the Bernoulli numbers for the sums of powers 1ᵏ + 2ᵏ + ⋯ + nᵏ.
