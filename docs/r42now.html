42·now · research front

Do Primes Keep Coming Close Together?

Do primes keep coming close together? Eleven and thirteen are two apart, and so are 101 and 103, and a million has more than eight thousand such pairs below it. Everyone who looks at a table of primes guesses the pairs go on for ever, and nobody has proved it. For a long time nobody could prove even that primes come within any fixed distance of each other again and again: maybe the gaps, which grow on average, grow without limit for every pair. In the spring of 2013 a lecturer who had spent years outside the main stream of the subject sent in a paper proving that some gap under seventy million happens endlessly often. Within weeks a group working in public, on a website anyone could read and post to, had cut the number in stages; a younger worker found a simpler method that autumn; and the group brought the number to 246 the next year. That is where it stands. This page shows why the average gap grows, why a clock forbids some patterns and allows others, how a pattern of numbers with no forbidden hours turns into a bound on gaps, and why the methods that reached 246 cannot by themselves reach 2.

Gaps between primes grow on average like ln x, and some are as long as you like. A pattern of offsets can hold primes all at once again and again only if, for every prime p, it misses an hour of the clock of p. In 2013 it was proved that every such pattern of enough numbers holds at least two primes endlessly often, so gaps no wider than the pattern recur; the width came down from 70 million to 246.

42now1Gaps Grow, and Some Are Long

42g held in trust that π(x) is about x / ln x, so on average the primes near x are about ln x apart. Below a million there are 78 498 primes, an average gap of 12.7, and ln 10⁶ is 13.8. Averages say nothing about the shortest gaps, and a gap can be as long as you like.

Long-gap technique: for every n ≥ 2, the n − 1 numbers n! + 2, n! + 3, …, n! + n are none of them prime.

2 \le j \le n \Rightarrow j \mid n! @@ j is one of the factors of n!
j \mid n! + j @@ and j divides j
1 < j < n! + j @@ a divisor that is neither 1 nor the number
\htmlClass{new}{n! + j \text{ is not prime for } j = 2, \ldots, n} \quad \blacksquare @@ a run of n − 1

With n = 7: 5042 to 5047 are six numbers in a row, none prime. The true runs come far sooner: the first gap of 114 is after 492 113, well below a million, and the factorial run of that length starts near 114!, a number of 187 digits. So long gaps are certain and the factorial is a clumsy witness. The question is the other end: do short gaps keep coming?

42now2Patterns a Clock Forbids

Take offsets h₁ < h₂ < ⋯ < h_k, a pattern, and ask whether n + h₁, …, n + h_k can all be prime for endlessly many n. For the pattern 0, 2, 4 the answer is no: on the clock of 3 the three numbers n, n + 2, n + 4 sit at three different hours, so one of them is at hour 0 and is divisible by 3. Only 3, 5, 7 escape, by being 3 itself.

Covered-hours technique: if a pattern fills every hour of the clock of some prime p, then n + h₁, …, n + h_k are all prime for at most finitely many n.

\{h_i \bmod p\} = \text{every hour of } \mathbb{Z}/p @@ the pattern covers the clock of p
\text{for each } n,\ \text{some } i \text{ has } n + h_i \equiv 0 \pmod p @@ the hour −n is among the hᵢ
p \mid n + h_i,\ \ \text{so } n + h_i \text{ is prime only if it is } p @@ a prime with the divisor p is p
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all prime only for } n + h_i = p:\ \text{ at most } k \text{ values of } n} \quad \blacksquare @@ one for each i

A pattern that misses at least one hour of every prime clock is called admissible; only clocks of primes p ≤ k can be covered, since k offsets cannot fill more than k hours. So the test is finite. 0, 2 misses hour 1 on the clock of 2 and hour 1 on the clock of 3: admissible. 0, 2, 6 is admissible; 0, 2, 4 is not. The guess, held by nearly everyone and proved for no pattern of two or more, is that every admissible pattern is all prime endlessly often, with a count that fair shares (42g) predict.

42now3Patterns From Large Primes

A pattern of k numbers is easy to make admissible: take primes bigger than k.

Big-primes technique: any k different primes, each larger than k, form an admissible pattern.

p \le k:\ \ \text{no } h_i \text{ is } \equiv 0 \pmod p @@ each hᵢ is a prime larger than k ≥ p, so p does not divide it
\text{the hour } 0 \text{ of } \mathbb{Z}/p \text{ is missed} @@ for every p ≤ k
p > k:\ \ k \text{ offsets fill at most } k < p \text{ hours} @@ some hour is missed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no prime clock is covered}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pattern is admissible

With k = 3: 5, 7, 11, width 6. The first proof of bounded gaps, in 2013, took k = 3 500 000 and the 3.5 million primes from 3 500 017 on. The last of them is 63 374 611, so the width of the pattern is 59 874 594, under seventy million. That is where the first bound came from: it is the width of an admissible pattern, not a measured gap. Narrower patterns of the same size exist and were found by search.

42now4From Patterns to Gaps

What was proved in 2013, and sharpened in 2014, is this, held in trust here: for k at least 50, every admissible pattern of k offsets has, for endlessly many n, at least two of n + h₁, …, n + h_k prime. The proof weighs each n by how likely it is that many of the n + hᵢ are prime, using fair shares in the hours of many clocks at once (42g, 42c), and shows the weighted count of primes beats one per n.

Width-bound technique: if every admissible pattern of k offsets holds two primes endlessly often, then the gap h_k − h₁ of the narrowest admissible pattern of k offsets is reached or beaten by gaps between primes endlessly often.

\text{fix an admissible pattern of } k,\ \text{ width } H = h_k - h_1 @@ the narrowest known
n + h_i,\ n + h_j \text{ both prime, endlessly often} @@ held in trust: the result of 2013–2014
|(n + h_j) - (n + h_i)| \le H @@ both lie in the window from n + h₁ to n + h_k
\text{the primes between them, if any, give a gap smaller still} @@ consecutive primes in the window
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a gap} \le H \text{ between consecutive primes, endlessly often}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for each such n, and the n go on for ever

The record is H = 246, the narrowest admissible pattern of 50 offsets, found by computer search. The route there: 70 million in May 2013; 4680 by the summer, by a public group tightening every step; 600 that November by a new, simpler weighing that also gave three, four, any number of primes within a bounded window; and 246 in 2014 by the group with the new weighing. If a stronger form of fair shares, believed and unproved, holds, the same argument gives 12, and a stronger form still gives 6. It cannot give 2: a weighing of this kind cannot tell numbers with an even count of prime factors from those with an odd count, and twin primes need exactly that. Primes two apart remain open.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Patterns are prime k-tuples, and the admissible ones those that cover no residue system. The guess that every admissible pattern is all prime endlessly often is the Hardy–Littlewood k-tuple conjecture, and its case 0, 2 the twin prime conjecture. The result of 2013 is bounded gaps between primes, written lim inf (p_(n+1) − p_n) < ∞. The weighing is the Selberg sieve as used by Goldston, Pintz and Yıldırım, refined by Zhang and Maynard; the stronger form of fair shares is the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, and the obstacle at 6 is the parity problem of sieve theory.

Who said it first

The k-tuple guess is in G. H. Hardy and J. E. Littlewood (1923). Daniel Goldston, János Pintz and Cem Yıldırım showed in 2005 that gaps are endlessly often far smaller than the average, and bounded if the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture holds. Yitang Zhang proved the bound of 70 million in a paper received by the Annals of Mathematics in April 2013, while a lecturer at the University of New Hampshire. Terence Tao proposed the public project Polymath8, which reached 4680. James Maynard, and independently Tao, found the simpler weighing in November 2013 and got 600; Polymath8b reached 246 in 2014. The long-gap technique is classical; gaps much longer than ln x, endlessly often, are due to Erik Westzynthius (1931) and later Robert Rankin (1938), with the current form by Kevin Ford, Ben Green, Sergei Konyagin, Maynard and Tao (2014–2018).

A proof that is not allowed

“There are endlessly many primes, and they cannot all be far apart, since the gaps average ln x, so a fixed gap recurs.” Not allowed: an average that grows does not stop every gap from growing too. Gaps could grow like ln x for every pair and keep the average. What forbids that is the result of 2013, and nothing short of it.
