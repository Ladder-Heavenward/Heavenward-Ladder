Why Does a River Suddenly Jump?

Why does a river suddenly jump? Below a weir or a dam’s spillway, water races down thin and fast, then all at once piles up into a churning step and flows on deep and slow. The step stands in one place, roaring. This page writes the energy of flowing water as depth plus a speed term, finds that one flow can run at two depths with the same energy, finds the depth between them where the energy is least with 14h, finds the height of the jump from momentum (14·phys) and the energy it destroys, and ends with the chance of a big flood over the years a channel must serve.

Per metre of width, a flow q at depth y moves at v = q/y and has energy y + v²/2g per unit weight. That is least at the critical depth (q²/g)^(1/3); every other energy has a fast shallow depth and a slow deep one. A jump from fast to slow keeps momentum, not energy, which sets its height and the energy lost.

20eng21Flowing Water’s Energy: Depth Plus a Speed Term

Randy is checking the channel below a small dam. Water flows at q = 2 cubic metres per second for each metre of the channel’s width. At depth y, it moves at v = q/y.

From here on, on this page, the energy of the flow, measured as a height, is E = y + v²/(2g), with g ≈ 9.81 m/s²: the depth, plus the height the water could climb if its speed turned to height (14·phys).

v = \dfrac{q}{y} @@ the same flow through any depth
\htmlClass{new}{E(y) = y + \dfrac{q^2}{2g\,y^2}} @@ depth plus a speed term

20eng22One Flow, Two Depths

For large y the depth term wins and E grows; for small y the speed term q²/(2gy²) wins and E grows without bound. In between, E dips to a least value. So any energy above the least is reached at two depths.

At E = 2.57 m, the same flow can run 0.3 m deep and fast, or 2.53 m deep and slow. Water racing off a spillway is on the fast branch; water in the pool downstream is on the slow one.

20eng23The Critical Depth as the Least Energy

Find the bottom of E(y) by setting its slope to 0 (14h).

E\prime(y) = 1 - \dfrac{q^2}{g\,y^3} = 0 @@ power technique (13d)
y_c = \left(\dfrac{q^2}{g}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\dfrac{4}{9.81}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.74 \text{ m} @@ the critical depth
E(y_c) = y_c + \tfrac{1}{2}y_c = 1.5\,y_c \approx 1.11 \text{ m} @@ the speed term is half the depth there
\htmlClass{new}{v_c = \sqrt{g\,y_c}} @@ the speed of a small wave on water that deep

Least-energy depth technique: a flow q per metre of width has least energy at the critical depth (q²/g)^(1/3), where it moves exactly at the speed of a small surface wave; shallower flows are fast, deeper flows slow. Engineers build a short hump or narrowing in a channel to force the flow through its critical depth, then read q from the depth there: a measuring flume.

20eng24The Jump: Momentum Kept, Energy Lost

In the jump the water churns, so energy is not kept. Momentum is (14·phys): the change in the water’s momentum each second equals the difference in the pushes of the water on either side, ½ρgy² per metre of width (15·eng).

\rho q\,(v_2 - v_1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g\,(y_1^2 - y_2^2) @@ momentum kept
y_2 = \dfrac{y_1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 + 8F^2} - 1\right),\ \ F = \dfrac{v_1}{\sqrt{g y_1}} @@ solving the quadratic (8i)
F = \dfrac{6.67}{1.72} \approx 3.89:\ \ y_2 \approx 0.15\,(11.04 - 1) \approx 1.51 \text{ m} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{E_1 - E_2 \approx 2.57 - 1.60 = 0.97 \text{ m}} @@ over a third of the energy, gone to churning

A jump keeps momentum and loses energy, so it lands not at the slow depth with the same energy (2.53 m) but at a shallower one (1.51 m). Dam builders want this: a concrete basin below a spillway holds the jump in place so the water’s energy is spent on churning there, not on scouring the riverbed downstream. A model basin in a hydraulics lab, run at scale, shows the jump standing exactly where these numbers put it.

20eng25A Hundred-Year Flood: Its Chance Over n Years

A spillway basin must handle a big flood. A hundred-year flood is one big enough that, in any one year, the chance of a flood at least that big is 1 in 100. It does not come once a century on a schedule.

\text{chance of none in one year} = 0.99 @@ 
\text{none in } n \text{ years} = 0.99^n @@ years independent: chances multiply (7·eng)
n = 30:\ 1 - 0.99^{30} \approx 0.26 @@ about one chance in four
\htmlClass{new}{n = 100:\ 1 - 0.99^{100} \approx 0.63} @@ not certain, even in a century

The chance of at least one hundred-year flood in n years is 1 − 0.99ⁿ: about one in four over a 30-year mortgage, and about two in three over a century. That is why dams that must not fail are built for floods far rarer than once in a hundred years. A county engineer explains a flood map to homeowners with exactly this count.

Accepted names

E is the specific energy; the fast and slow branches are supercritical and subcritical flow, and the two depths with one energy are alternate depths. F is the Froude number. The jump’s two depths are conjugate or sequent depths, and the formula for y₂ is the Bélanger equation. A hundred-year flood has a return period of 100 years.

Who said it first

Jean-Baptiste Bélanger derived the jump from momentum in 1828 and 1838; Boris Bakhmeteff set out specific energy and the critical depth for channels in 1912.
