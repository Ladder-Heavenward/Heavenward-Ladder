15·eng · engineering

How hard does water push on a dam?

How hard does water push on a dam? Shasta Dam, north of Redding, holds back a lake more than five hundred feet deep. Before a single bucket of its concrete was poured, its builders had to know the push of that lake, and where on the wall it would act. Guessing high wastes concrete by the million tons. Guessing low drowns a valley. The question is hard because the water does not push the same everywhere. Near the surface it barely presses. At the bottom it presses harder than fifteen times the air. No single pressure times the wall gives the answer. This page cuts the wall into thin strips, one depth at a time, and traps the push between rung 15's lower and upper sums. The push turns out to be an area. Then the page finds where the whole push acts, and why every dam on the coast is thicker at the bottom than at the top.

In still water the pressure grows evenly with depth. On a thin strip of wall it is nearly the same all across, so the strip's push is about the pressure times the strip's area, and the strips add up to a strip sum. The sums close on one number: the wall's width times the area under the pressure, down the depth. That is half the bottom pressure, spread over the whole wall. The push above any level grows like the square of the depth, and its turning effect like the cube, so the wall must thicken as it goes down. The whole push acts one third of the way up from the bottom.

The words

Shasta Dam, finished in 1945, is a concrete dam on the Sacramento River. By the figures of the agency that runs it, it stands 602 ft high, and the water at the dam is 522.5 ft deep when the lake is full: about 159.3 m. Its top is 30 ft thick, and its base 543 ft.

From here on, on this page, y is a depth below the water's surface, in metres, and p(y) the water's pressure there, in pascals (15·chem). ρ (rho) is the water's density, its mass per cubic metre: about 1000 kg for fresh water, 1025 kg for sea water. g ≈ 9.8 m/s², as in 12·phys. H is the water's depth at the wall, and W the width of wall that the water touches, along the dam.

Pressure-with-depth law: in still water, p(y) = ρgy, above the pressure of the air. In still water the pressure grows evenly with depth: each metre down adds the weight of one more metre of water above each square metre. It presses the same way in every direction at one depth. Both are laws, measured since the 1580s; they fail where the water moves fast. Air presses on both faces of a dam alike, so this page leaves it out.

15·chem found the air above a square metre at sea level weighs about 101 kN. Water is about 800 times as dense as air near the ground, so at Shasta's bed the pressure is 9800 × 159.3 ≈ 1.56 × 10⁶ Pa: about 15.4 atmospheres.

The push, in thin strips

Take a strip of wall W wide, from depth y to depth y + Δ. Its area is WΔ (15a's box). The pressure on it lies between ρgy at its top edge and ρg(y + Δ) at its bottom edge. So its push lies between ρgWy · Δ and ρgW(y + Δ) · Δ. That is a premise of physics: more pressure on every part of a strip pushes harder.

Cut the depth [0, H] into n equal pieces, of width Δ = H/n. Add the least pushes, and add the greatest. These are 15a's lower and upper sums of the function ρgWy.

\text{lower}_n = \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \rho g W\,(i\Delta)\,\Delta = \rho g W \Delta^2 \tfrac{(n - 1)n}{2} = \rho g W H^2\,\tfrac{n - 1}{2n} @@ sums-are-linear, doubled-sum (15g); Δ = H/n
\text{upper}_n = \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho g W\,(i\Delta)\,\Delta = \rho g W H^2\,\tfrac{n + 1}{2n} @@ the same, one piece on
\text{lower}_n \le F \le \text{upper}_n \text{ for every } n @@ the premise, strip by strip, added (areas-add for the boxes)
\textstyle\int_0^H \rho g W y\,dy = \rho g W \tfrac{H^2}{2} @@ areas-scale (15e); 15h's area under x
\htmlClass{new}{F = \tfrac12 \rho g W H^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ exactly-one-between (15c); the pieces need not be equal, every partition traps F

Push-is-an-area technique. The push of still water on a wall is the wall's width times the area under the pressure, taken down the depth. For a wall W wide and water H deep, F = ½ρgWH². That is the pressure at half the depth, ½ρgH, spread over the whole wall, WH. Double the depth and the push grows four times.

F = \tfrac12 \cdot 9800 \cdot 1 \cdot 159.3^2 \approx 1.24 \times 10^8 \text{ N} @@ for each metre of width at the deepest part, W = 1
\approx \text{the weight of } 12\,700 \text{ tonnes} @@ divide by g: 1.24 × 10⁸ / 9.8 kg
p(H) = 9800 \cdot 159.3 \approx 1.56 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa} @@ the pressure at the bed; the average over the wall is half of it

The whole dam is 3460 ft along its top, but the valley narrows toward the bed, so most of that length holds back shallower water. The push per metre at the deepest part is the honest number. A full total needs W to change with depth; 15·eng.4.2 asks for one.

Why a dam is thicker at the bottom

Stand at a level y below the surface. Everything the water pushes above that level must pass down through the dam at that level, into the concrete below. Per metre of width, that push is ½ρgy², and it grows like the square of the depth. Its turning effect about that level grows faster still: like the cube, as the next section shows. The concrete at depth 159 m carries the push of all the water above it; the concrete near the top carries almost none.

A dam is thicker at the bottom because the push it must carry grows faster than the depth. Shasta's thickness goes from 30 ft at the top to 543 ft at the base. Guess that it grows evenly between them. Then a one-metre slice of the dam has area 15d's way, the average thickness times the height: about 87.3 × 183.5 ≈ 16 000 m². At 2400 kg of concrete per cubic metre it weighs about 3.8 × 10⁸ N, about three times the push. A dam of this kind holds by its weight. Its accepted name is a gravity dam.

Where the whole push acts

A single force at some height h above the bed, equal to the whole push, would have the turning effect F · h about the bed (12·phys). The strips have turning effects too: the strip at depth y sits H − y above the bed. Their turning effects add, and are trapped by lower and upper sums the same way. Choose h so the single force turns the wall exactly as the strips do.

\text{turning effect of the strips} = \textstyle\int_0^H \rho g W\,y\,(H - y)\,dy @@ the strips’ sums trap it; continuous-is-integrable (15f), exactly-one-between (15c)
= \rho g W\Big(H\textstyle\int_0^H y\,dy - \int_0^H y^2\,dy\Big) @@ areas-add, areas-scale (15e)
= \rho g W\big(\tfrac{H^3}{2} - \tfrac{H^3}{3}\big) = \tfrac16 \rho g W H^3 @@ 15h: the areas under x and under x²
F h = \tfrac12 \rho g W H^2 \cdot h = \tfrac16 \rho g W H^3 @@ the single force turns the wall the same
\htmlClass{new}{h = \tfrac H3} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiplying-keeps-answers: divide by ½ρgWH², which is not 0

Centre-of-push technique. The whole push of still water on a wall acts one third of the way up from the bottom. Not at the middle: the deep strips push hardest, so the single force sits low. At Shasta it acts about 53 m above the bed, and its turning effect about the bed is about 6.6 × 10⁹ N·m per metre of wall. Its accepted name is the centre of pressure.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, trapped

12e set beside halving a principle of the second kind of care: when a thing cannot be pinned to a dot, give the stretch it stays inside, and say how wide. The strip sums are that stretch. With n strips the lower and upper sums differ by ρgWH²/n, so they trap the push to within a share 1/n of it either way.

Be rigorous about the trap, not the dot.

For Shasta, with 10 strips the push per metre is trapped between about 1.12 × 10⁸ N and 1.37 × 10⁸ N. With 100 strips the trap is ten times narrower. The integral is the dot, and it is exact only for still water of one density. The trap is what a hand calculation in 1938 could promise.

Ten strips give Shasta's push per metre to within about ten percent either way. Would you pour the concrete on that?

Running it backwards

Handed the push per metre on a wall of fresh water, the depth is H = √(2F/(ρg)): a push of 490 000 N per metre means water 10 m deep. Handed the height where the push acts, the depth is three times it. Handed the pressure at the bed, the depth is p/(ρg).

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

Simon Stevin, in De Beghinselen des Waterwichts (1586), found the push of water on a wall by thin strips, trapping it between too little and too much, and saw that it depends on depth alone. He found that the push on the bottom of a vessel can far exceed the weight of the water in it; this has the accepted name the hydrostatic paradox. Blaise Pascal is said to have burst a barrel with a tall thin tube of water in 1646; the story may be told better than it happened. Shasta Dam was designed and built by the United States Bureau of Reclamation, from 1938 to 1945; the figures on this page are the Bureau’s.

Stevin had the motto “Wonder en is gheen wonder”: what looks like a wonder is no wonder, once it is worked out.

Simon Stevin, on the title page of De Beghinselen der Weeghconst (1586); paraphrase

In 15·eng.4.4, the paradox is Stevin’s; the barrel, Pascal’s.

Accepted names

The pressure of still water is hydrostatic pressure; ρgy is gauge pressure, the pressure above the air’s. The push on a wall is the hydrostatic force or thrust, and the point where it acts the centre of pressure. A dam that holds by its weight is a gravity dam.
