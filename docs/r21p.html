21p · technique

Related rates

When two lengths are locked together by a shape, they cannot change on their own. If one moves, the other must move too, and at a speed the shape decides. A ladder against a wall, a balloon watched from the ground, a tank filling with water: each is a shape that ties one rate to another. This step shows how to find the one you cannot see from the one you can, and what it means when the answer comes out impossible.

Lengths tied together by a shape have speeds tied together by that same shape. Take the slope in time of the tie itself, and the one speed gives the other. When a speed comes out endless, the shape has stopped holding.

21p1Take the slope of the tie

Tied-rates technique: if x(t) and y(t) stay tied by an equation for every t, take the slope in t of both sides; the result ties x′ to y′. Put in the numbers for the instant only after the slopes are taken.

x(t)^2 + y(t)^2 = L^2 @@ foot x from the wall, top y up it: the right-triangle technique
2x\,x' + 2y\,y' = 0 @@ slope in t of each side; the chain rule on each square
\htmlClass{new}{y' = -\tfrac{x}{y}\,x'} \quad \blacksquare @@ wherever y ≠ 0
x' > 0 \Rightarrow y' < 0 @@ the foot slides out, the top comes down

21p2Rates through an angle

Angle-rate technique: when an angle θ is tied to lengths through tan, cos or sin, take the slope of the tie: (tan θ)′ = sec² θ · θ′. Answers come in radians per unit of time.

\tan\theta = \tfrac{h}{d} @@ tan is the steepness (21c), d fixed
\sec^2\theta\,\theta' = \tfrac{h'}{d} @@ slope in t; the chain rule
\htmlClass{new}{\theta' = \frac{d\,h'}{d^2 + h^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ sec² θ = 1 + h²/d²

21p3Rates through a volume

Volume-rate technique: write the volume in the one length you want, using the shape to remove the others; then its slope in t ties the filling rate to that length’s rate.

r = \tfrac{R}{H}h @@ similar triangles: the water’s top radius at depth h
V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h = \tfrac{\pi R^2}{3H^2}h^3 @@ the cone (21o1), r removed
\htmlClass{new}{V' = \tfrac{\pi R^2}{H^2}h^2\,h'} \quad \blacksquare @@ slope in t
h' = \tfrac{H^2 V'}{\pi R^2 h^2} @@ at a steady pour, the level slows as it rises

21p4When the answer is impossible

Broken-tie technique: if a rate comes out endless, or faster than anything can move, then the tie it came from has stopped holding before that instant. The equations are right; the picture they describe has ended.

y' = -\tfrac{x}{y}\,x' \to -\infty \text{ as } y \to 0 @@ x → L and x′ stays fixed
\text{no ladder top falls endlessly fast} @@ suppose the tie held to the floor
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the top leaves the wall before } y = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ refutation (4e): the tie x² + y² = L² with the top on the wall fails

The accepted names

These are related rates problems; the tie is the constraint, and taking the slope in t of both sides is implicit differentiation with respect to time.

Who said it first

In 21p.4.5, the sliding ladder has been a textbook exercise since the nineteenth century. Paul Scholten and Andrew Simoson, “The falling ladder paradox”, College Mathematics Journal 27 (1996), showed that a real ladder’s top leaves the wall before it reaches the floor.
