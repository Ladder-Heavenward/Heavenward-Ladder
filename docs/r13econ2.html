Who Pays for the Smoke?

Who pays for the smoke? A brickworks sells its bricks and pays for its clay, its fuel and its workers. Its smoke settles on the washing and in the lungs of the town, and nobody sends the works a bill for that. So the works makes bricks as if the smoke cost nothing. 13·econ said one more adds to profit exactly when it brings in more than it costs. This page counts the cost of one more brick to the town as well as to the works, finds that the works makes too many, fixes it with a charge on each pallet, and then turns to a park that everyone enjoys and no one wants to pay for.

Write the cost of one more to the one who decides, and the cost of one more to everyone. The decider stops where the price meets their own cost of one more; everyone would stop where it meets the whole cost. A charge equal to the cost that falls on others moves the first stop to the second. For a thing everyone shares, add up what one more is worth to each person, and compare the total with its cost.

13econ21The Words

The works sells bricks by the pallet at 10 dollars. After q pallets in a day, the next costs the works 2 + q dollars: the kiln runs hotter and the overtime grows. Each pallet also puts out smoke that costs the town 4 dollars, in cleaning and in doctor’s bills.

the next pallet, after q
price10
cost to the works2 + q
cost to the town4
cost to everyone6 + q

From here on, on this page, the cost to the works is the own cost, the cost to the town is the cost to others, and the two together are the whole cost.

13econ22What the Works Chooses

The works adds a pallet while it brings in more than it costs the works, by 13·econ’s one-more test. It never sees the smoke.

\text{one more pays while } 2 + q < 10 @@ the one-more test (13econ5), own cost only
q < 8 @@ take 2 from both sides (5g)
q = 8:\ 2 + 8 = 10 @@ the ninth pallet brings in what it costs: no gain
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the works makes } 8 \text{ pallets}} @@ it stops where the price meets its own cost

Each of those 8 pallets earned the works something. Whether each was worth making depends on who is counting.

13econ23What the Town Would Choose

Count the smoke too. A pallet is worth making, for everyone together, when it brings in more than its whole cost.

\text{one more pays everyone while } 6 + q < 10 @@ the whole cost
q < 4 @@ take 6 from both sides
\text{pallets } 5 \text{ to } 8:\ \text{whole cost } 10, 11, 12, 13 @@ each costs everyone at least what it brings in
\htmlClass{new}{4 \text{ pallets, not } 8} @@ the works makes twice what everyone would choose

Smoke technique: when a decider’s one more puts a cost on others, the decider stops where the price meets the own cost, past the point where it meets the whole cost. Too much is made, and the last pallets cost the town more than they earn the works.

13econ24A Charge on Each Pallet

The town charges the works 4 dollars a pallet, exactly the smoke’s cost to others. The works’ own cost of the next pallet becomes 2 + q + 4.

\text{own cost with the charge: } 6 + q @@ the charge is paid on every pallet
6 + q < 10 \iff q < 4 @@ the same test as the town’s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the works makes } 4} @@ it now stops where everyone would

Charge technique: a charge on each unit equal to its cost to others makes the decider’s own cost the whole cost, so the decider stops where everyone would. The town does not have to tell the works how much to make. It only has to make the works see the smoke.

13econ25The Park Nobody Pays For

Now the other way round. Three households share a green. One more tree costs 30 dollars, and each household would gladly pay 12 for it: shade, birds, a view. No household will buy it alone, since 12 < 30. Together the tree is worth 36 to them.

\text{worth to one household: } 12 < 30 @@ alone, nobody buys it
\text{worth to all three: } 12 + 12 + 12 = 36 @@ everyone enjoys the same tree
36 > 30 @@ so the tree is worth its cost
\htmlClass{new}{\text{add what one more is worth to each, then compare}} @@ the shared-thing test

Shared-thing technique: for a thing everyone enjoys at once, one more is worth making when the sum of what it is worth to each person is more than its cost. Each household hopes the others will pay, since it enjoys the tree either way. Left to each alone, too few trees are planted, the mirror of too many bricks.

Accepted names

A cost a decider puts on others is a negative externality; the own cost is the private marginal cost and the whole cost the social marginal cost. A charge equal to the cost on others is a Pigouvian tax. A thing everyone enjoys at once, from which no one can be kept out, is a public good; hoping others pay is free riding, and the shared-thing test is the Samuelson condition.

Who said it first

Arthur Pigou proposed the charge in The Economics of Welfare, 1920. Paul Samuelson wrote the shared-thing test in “The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure”, 1954.
