16d · proof

Substitution

16c turned every rule for slopes into a rule for areas, at least in principle: find a function with the right slope. Rung 13's chain rule is the rule for slopes of a function inside a function. Its slope is the outside's slope, taken at the inside, times the inside's slope. Read backwards, any integrand of that shape has an antiderivative at once. This step proves the backwards reading, with the ends of the integral moved to where the inside sends them. It also sets up a bookkeeping that does the moving almost by itself.

Name the inside function by a new letter. By the chain rule, the area under the outside taken at the inside, times the inside’s slope, is the area under the outside alone between the inside’s values at the two ends. The new letter’s small change is the inside’s slope times the old letter’s small change, and the ends move with the new letter.

16d1The chain rule, backwards

Stands on 13e (the chain rule), 16b (antiderivative-exists) and 16c (area-is-a-change).

Substitution technique for areas: let g have a slope at every point of [v, w], with g′ continuous there, and let f be continuous on an interval J that holds every value of g on [v, w]. Then ∫ᵥʷ f(g(x)) g′(x) dx = ∫_{g(v)}^{g(w)} f(r) dr.

\text{call } F \text{ an antiderivative of } f \text{ on } J @@ antiderivative-exists (16b)
(F \circ g)'(x) = F'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) @@ chain for slopes (13e), with the inside r = g(x)
f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \text{ is continuous on } [v, w] @@ 11c for the chain f(g(x)); 11b for the product; g′ continuous by hypothesis
\textstyle\int_v^w f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = F(g(w)) - F(g(v)) @@ area-is-a-change (16c) on [v, w], with G = F ∘ g
\textstyle\int_{g(v)}^{g(w)} f(r)\,dr = F(g(w)) - F(g(v)) @@ area-is-a-change on the interval between g(v) and g(w), inside J; if g(w) < g(v), 15e's convention turns both sides over
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_v^w f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(v)}^{g(w)} f(r)\,dr} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines have the same right side

Without ends, the same lines say: ∫ f(g(x)) g′(x) dx = F(g(x)) + C. Its accepted name is integration by substitution, or the change of variables; another is u-substitution, after the letter often used for the inside. On this ladder u is 11a's step, so the inside keeps 13e's name, r.

16d2The bookkeeping in d's

Stands on 16d1 and 13e (dy/dx as one mark).

From here on, in a substitution, the line r = g(x), dr = g′(x) dx is a reminder of substitution for areas, not a statement about numbers. It says that wherever g′(x) dx sits next to a function of g(x), it may be traded for dr, and the ends traded for the values of r there.

13e read dy/dx as one mark, not a fraction. The line dr = g′(x) dx looks like that mark multiplied out. It is not: no number dr or dx has been defined. But substitution for areas says the trade it suggests always gives the right answer, under its hypotheses. That is why these marks have lasted.

\textstyle\int_0^1 2x(x^2 + 1)^3\,dx:\ \ r = x^2 + 1,\ dr = 2x\,dx @@ x = 0 gives r = 1; x = 1 gives r = 2
= \textstyle\int_1^2 r^3\,dr = \tfrac{r^4}{4}\Big|_1^2 = \tfrac{16 - 1}{4} = \tfrac{15}{4} @@ substitution for areas; area-is-a-change with power for slopes
\textstyle\int_0^4 x\sqrt{x^2 + 9}\,dx:\ \ r = x^2 + 9,\ dr = 2x\,dx,\ x\,dx = \tfrac12 dr @@ areas-scale: a constant factor moves across; r runs from 9 to 25
= \tfrac12 \textstyle\int_9^{25} r^{1/2}\,dr = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 r^{3/2}\Big|_9^{25} = \tfrac13(125 - 27) = \tfrac{98}{3} @@ fraction-power for slopes (13h)
\textstyle\int_0^1 (1 - x)^5\,dx:\ \ r = 1 - x,\ dr = -dx @@ r runs from 1 down to 0
= \textstyle\int_1^0 r^5\,(-dr) = \int_0^1 r^5\,dr = \tfrac16 @@ areas-scale, then 15e's convention for the reversed ends

Straight-inside technique: if F′ = f and k ≠ 0, then ∫ f(kx + m) dx = (1/k) F(kx + m) + C. It is substitution for areas with r = kx + m and dr = k dx, divided through by k.

16d3Odd and even

Stands on 16d2.

From here on, f is even when f(−x) = f(x) for every x in its domain, and odd when f(−x) = −f(x). The names come from the powers: x² and x⁴ are even, x and x³ odd.

Odd-and-even-areas technique: if f is continuous on [−p, p], then ∫₋ₚᵖ f = 0 when f is odd, and ∫₋ₚᵖ f = 2∫₀ᵖ f when f is even.

16d4Why the halves cancel or double

Stands on 16d3 and 15e (interval-cut-in-two).

\textstyle\int_{-p}^{0} f(x)\,dx:\ \ r = -x,\ dr = -dx @@ x = −p gives r = p; x = 0 gives r = 0
= \textstyle\int_p^0 f(-r)\,(-dr) = \int_0^p f(-r)\,dr @@ substitution for areas with g(x) = −x; areas-scale; the convention
\text{odd: } \textstyle\int_0^p f(-r)\,dr = -\int_0^p f,\ \ \text{so } \int_{-p}^{p} f = -\int_0^p f + \int_0^p f = 0 @@ areas-scale; split-in-any-order (16a)
\text{even: } \textstyle\int_0^p f(-r)\,dr = \int_0^p f,\ \ \text{so } \int_{-p}^{p} f = 2\int_0^p f \quad \blacksquare @@ split-in-any-order

Who said it first

Leibniz wrote to a correspondent that the art of finding is helped most by signs that express the inmost nature of a thing briefly and as if painted, for then the labour of thinking is wonderfully reduced.

Leibniz, letter to Tschirnhaus (1678); paraphrase

The d’s, and the bookkeeping with them, are Leibniz’s; the dotted letters are Newton’s. In 16d.4.5, the three students were Charles Babbage, John Herschel and George Peacock, and their society was the Analytical Society.
