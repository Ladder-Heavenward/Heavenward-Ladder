Why Does Walking Downhill Find the Bottom?

Why does walking downhill find the bottom? Rung 28 proved that on a bowl the walk downhill reaches the bottom, and that on any smooth surface it reaches a place where the uphill vector is small, which may be a bottom, a poor hollow or a saddle. The error of a learning machine is no bowl. Yet machines with millions of knobs are trained by this walk, and it brings their error on the examples to nearly 0. One reason is now proved. If the slope is large wherever the error is, then every step pays off a fixed share of the height, and a bowl is not needed for that. A machine made of pieces in a row gets such a slope when it has more knobs than answers asked of it. What is proved covers machines far wider, and steps far shorter, than the ones in use; what is seen in use, since 2021, breaks a rule of rung 28 and still works.

A step of 1/L lowers f by at least |∇f|²/(2L). The slope pays at rate μ where ½|∇f|² ≥ μ·f; then f(w_K) ≤ (1 − μ/L)ᴷ f(w₀). Two learners in a row, f = ½(uv − 1)²: a saddle at (0, 0), bottoms on uv = 1, and ½|∇f|² = (u² + v²)·f. In general f = ½|F(w) − y|², ∇f = Jᵀ(F(w) − y), and the slope pays at the least stretch of JJᵀ.

inf83.1The Slope Pays for the Height

Let f be an error: a smooth function of the knobs w that is never below 0. The small-slope technique of 28·now showed that if f never bends more sharply than L, one step of size 1/L against the uphill vector lowers f by at least |∇f|²/(2L). That alone finds a small slope, not a low place. The slope pays at rate μ at a point w when ½|∇f(w)|² ≥ μ·f(w): the higher the error, the steeper the ground.

Slope-pays technique: if f never bends more sharply than L, and the slope pays at rate μ at every point of a walk downhill with steps 1/L, then f(wK) ≤ (1 − μ/L)ᴷ·f(w₀).

f(w_{k+1}) \le f(w_k) - \tfrac{1}{2L}\,|\nabla f(w_k)|^2 @@ the small-slope technique (28·now), its third line
\tfrac12|\nabla f(w_k)|^2 \ge \mu\,f(w_k) @@ the slope pays at wₖ
f(w_{k+1}) \le \big(1 - \tfrac{\mu}{L}\big)\,f(w_k) @@ put the second line in the first
\htmlClass{new}{f(w_K) \le \big(1 - \tfrac{\mu}{L}\big)^K f(w_0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on K (4c)

Nothing in the proof asks for a bowl. A bowl ½(λ₁x₁² + ⋯ + λₙxₙ²) is one case: its uphill vector has square Σλᵢ²xᵢ², at least λ₁·Σλᵢxᵢ², so its slope pays at rate λ₁, and with L = λₙ the factor per step is 1 − λ₁/λₙ, the number 28o found for the distance to the bottom with steps of 1/λₙ. Where the slope pays there is no poor hollow and no saddle, since a flat point has ½|∇f|² = 0, and so f = 0. The whole question is moved into one inequality.

inf83.2Two Learners in a Row

The smallest machine made of pieces is two of ∞4’s one-knob learners in a row: the first multiplies its input by v, the second by u. Feed it 1 and ask for 1. The answer is uv, and the error is f(u, v) = ½(uv − 1)².

Two-knob technique: for f(u, v) = ½(uv − 1)², the flat points are (0, 0) and the points with uv = 1; (0, 0) is a saddle; the points with uv = 1 are lowest points, of height 0; and ½|∇f|² = (u² + v²)·f everywhere.

\nabla f = \big((uv - 1)\,v,\ (uv - 1)\,u\big) @@ the one-knob technique (28a) and the chain rule
\nabla f = 0 \iff uv = 1 \ \text{or}\ u = v = 0 @@ a product is 0 exactly when a factor is
f(s, s) = \tfrac12(s^2 - 1)^2 < \tfrac12 < \tfrac12(s^2 + 1)^2 = f(s, -s) @@ for 0 < s < 1: down one way from f(0, 0) = ½ and up another, a saddle (28l)
uv = 1:\quad f = 0 \le f(u', v') \text{ for every } (u', v') @@ a square is never below 0
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12|\nabla f|^2 = \tfrac12(uv - 1)^2(v^2 + u^2) = (u^2 + v^2)\,f} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first line, squared and added

This f is no bowl. Along the line u = v it has a bottom at each of (1, 1) and (−1, −1) and a hump between them, and its lowest points are not one point but two whole curves. Yet the slope pays at rate μ wherever u² + v² ≥ μ, which is everywhere but near the saddle, where both knobs are small. From (1.5, 0.2) with steps of 0.2 the error falls from 0.245 to under a millionth in ten steps. From (0.1, 0.05), beside the saddle, with steps of 0.25, it is still 0.21 after ten steps, and a few millionths after twenty.

Read it with arrows. The error is a bowl in the answer, ½(b − 1)². The answer is two straight maps in a row. Each piece is as simple as a piece can be, and the surface over the knobs is bent all the same: the bending comes from the composing. By ∞4 the slopes of learners in a row compose as the learners do, so the walk never needs the surface whole. It asks each piece for its own slope and passes the result back along the row.

inf83.3More Knobs Than Answers

Now any machine. It has n knobs w, and on m examples it gives a list of m answers, F(w). The answers wanted are the list y, and the error is f(w) = ½|F(w) − y|². Write J for the slope grid of F at w, as in 28b: m rows, one for each answer, and n columns, one for each knob.

Wide-grid technique: ∇f = Jᵀ(F(w) − y); and if every stretch of JJᵀ at w is at least μ, the slope pays at rate μ at w.

f = \tfrac12|r|^2,\quad r = F(w) - y @@ the misses, one for each example
\nabla f = J^{\mathsf T} r @@ maps in a row (28c): the slope row of ½|r|² is rᵀ, then J
|\nabla f|^2 = r^{\mathsf T} J J^{\mathsf T} r @@ |x|² = xᵀx
r^{\mathsf T} J J^{\mathsf T} r \ge \mu\,|r|^2 @@ the axes-form technique (31e): a form is at least its least stretch times |r|²
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12|\nabla f|^2 \ge \mu \cdot \tfrac12|r|^2 = \mu\,f} \quad \blacksquare @@ halve both sides

JJᵀ is an m by m grid. Its stretches can all be above 0 only if Jᵀ loses no list of misses but 0, and then the n columns of J build every list of m numbers, which needs n ≥ m by the exchange technique of 27b. So the slope can pay in this way only when the machine has at least as many knobs as answers asked of it. For the two learners in a row, m = 1 and n = 2, J is the row (v, u), and JJᵀ = u² + v²: the last footing again.

This turns an old worry round. A machine with more knobs than examples has a surface with no single bottom, and that was thought to make the walk harder. It is what lets the slope pay. With the slope-pays technique, a walk that stays where every stretch of JJᵀ is at least μ reaches error 0 at the rate (1 − μ/L)ᴷ. What must still be shown, for a given machine, is that the walk does stay there.

inf83.4What Is Proved and What Is Seen

  • The condition that the slope pays, and the rate that follows, were given in 1963. In 2016 it was shown to be weaker than the other conditions then used to prove such rates, and to need no bowl.
  • From 2018 it was proved for networks made wide enough, with far more knobs than examples, that the slope grid hardly moves during the walk, and that the walk reaches error 0 on the examples.
  • In 2020 this was read as above: the surface of such a machine is not a bowl even close to its bottoms, and the slope pays on most of the space of knobs, though not all.
  • In 2021 came a thing seen, not proved. Networks trained with a fixed step η sharpen as they train: the sharpest bend of the surface rises until it sits just above 2/η, and stays there. The error then goes up and down from step to step, and down over the long run.

The last entry is outside the proofs of this page and of rung 28. The step-size technique of 28o says that on a bowl a walk with η above 2 over the sharpest bend flies off. The surfaces of working networks are not bowls, and something holds the walk at that edge without letting it go.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The inequality that ties slope to height dates from 1963. In 2016 it was taken up again and shown to cover more cases than the conditions then in use.

From 2018 proofs came that the walk reaches error 0 for very wide networks, on surfaces that are not bowls, and in 2020 the inequality of 1963 was offered as the reason.

In 2021 careful runs showed that the steps used in practice are too long for the proofs: the walk sits at the edge where rung 28 says a bowl would throw it off.

The accepted names

The walk downhill is gradient descent; the uphill vector is the gradient, and the step η is the learning rate. “Never bends more sharply than L” is L-smoothness. “The slope pays at rate μ” is the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality, or PL condition; the rate (1 − μ/L)ᴷ is linear convergence. A bowl is a strongly convex quadratic. A poor hollow is a spurious local minimum. Two learners in a row are a deep linear network of depth two, or a matrix factorization of size one. J is the Jacobian, JJᵀ the tangent kernel, a machine with more knobs than answers is over-parameterized, and the condition on most of the space is called PL*. The sharpest bend is the sharpness, the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian; training with it at 2/η is the edge of stability, and its rise is progressive sharpening. Which bottom the walk reaches is the question of implicit bias, and answering well on new examples is generalization.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy described the walk in 1847. Boris Polyak, and Stanisław Łojasiewicz, gave the inequality in 1963. Hamed Karimi, Julie Nutini and Mark Schmidt, “Linear convergence of gradient and proximal-gradient methods under the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition” (2016). Arthur Jacot, Franck Gabriel and Clément Hongler on the tangent kernel of wide networks (2018); Simon Du and co-authors, “Gradient descent finds global minima of deep neural networks”. Chaoyue Liu, Libin Zhu and Mikhail Belkin, “Loss landscapes and optimization in over-parameterized non-linear systems and neural networks” (2020). Jeremy Cohen, Simran Kaur, Yuanzhi Li, Zico Kolter and Ameet Talwalkar, “Gradient descent on neural networks typically occurs at the edge of stability” (2021).
