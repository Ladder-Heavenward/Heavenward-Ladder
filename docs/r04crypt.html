4·crypt · cryptography

Can a shifted alphabet keep a secret?

Can a shifted alphabet keep a secret? As long as messages have been carried by people who could be caught, senders have wanted words that only the right reader can read. The oldest trick on record moves every letter a fixed number of places along the alphabet: the letter a is written as d, b as e, and so on. The reader who knows the number slides back and reads. What makes the question hard is that a secret is not something you can check on the page. It is a question about a reader who does not know the number, and about what that reader can do. This page turns letters into the numbers 0 to 25, so that the slide is 4j's division with remainder. It proves every slide can be undone, and counts the keys. Then it counts the work an enemy must do to try them all.

Number the letters from 0 to 25, the letter a as 0 and z as 25. To shift by a key, add the key and keep the remainder by 26. To undo it, shift by whatever brings the key up to 26. There are 26 shifts, and one of them changes nothing, so an enemy who tries all 25 others is sure to find the message.

The class · read it through, then work the picture and the slides

Letters as numbers

From here on, on this page, the letters of the alphabet name numbers, in order: a names 0, b names 1, c names 2, and so on to z, which names 25. So a letter is a number less than 26, a member of the set 26.

A message is a list of letters, 4i's list with letters for entries. Spaces and stops are left as they are. A message is written in small letters, and its secret form in capitals, so the two are never confused: salinas is a message, VDOLQDV a secret text.

Counting on, and starting again past z

From here on, on this page, for a number k and a letter p, shiftk(p) names the remainder of p + k by 26. Division-with-remainder gives it, and one-split makes it the only one. The number k is the key.

Shifting a message shifts each letter, one at a time. With key 3, s is 18, and 18 + 3 = 21, which is v. Most letters just count on. The letters near the end run past z, and the remainder sends them back to the start.

\text{s} = 18,\ \ 18 + 3 = 26 \cdot 0 + 21 @@ the remainder is 21: v
\text{a} = 0,\ \ 0 + 3 = 26 \cdot 0 + 3 @@ d
\text{y} = 24,\ \ 24 + 3 = 27 = 26 \cdot 1 + \htmlClass{new}{1} @@ past z: the remainder is 1, which is b
\text{shift}_3(\text{yolo}) = \text{BROR} @@ one letter at a time
\text{shift}_3(\text{salinas}) = \text{VDOLQDV} @@ the same, for each of the seven letters

To shift a letter by k, count on k places, and past z start again at the letter a. A remainder by 26 is always less than 26, so the answer is always a letter.

Every shift can be undone

Shifting twice is shifting once. Shifts-add technique: shifting by k and then by m is shifting by the remainder of k + m by 26. Let p be a letter. Let k and m be numbers. Let r be the remainder of k + m by 26.

p + k = 26q + s,\ \ s \lt 26 @@ division-with-remainder; s = shift_k(p)
s + m = 26q' + t,\ \ t \lt 26 @@ division-with-remainder; t = shift_m(s)
k + m = 26q'' + r,\ \ r \lt 26 @@ division-with-remainder; r as named
p + r = 26q''' + u,\ \ u \lt 26 @@ division-with-remainder; u = shift_r(p)
p + k + m = 26q + s + m = 26(q + q') + t @@ the first two lines; regroup, and the spread technique
p + k + m = p + r + 26q'' = 26(q''' + q'') + u @@ the third and fourth lines, the same way
\htmlClass{new}{t = u} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-split, with base 26: one number has one remainder

Undoing a shift is one more shift. Key-back technique: the key back from k is the j with k + j = 26. For every letter p, shiftj(shiftk(p)) = p. Here k is less than 26, so less-is-adding gives such a j.

\text{shift}_j(\text{shift}_k(p)) = \text{shift}_r(p) @@ shifts-add, with r the remainder of k + j by 26
26 = 26 \cdot 1 + 0,\ \ \text{so } r = 0 @@ k + j = 26; one-split
p + 0 = 26 \cdot 0 + p,\ \ p \lt 26 @@ p is a letter
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shift}_j(\text{shift}_k(p)) = p} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-split: the remainder of p by 26 is p

So the key back from 3 is 23, and shift23(VDOLQDV) = salinas. The key back from 13 is 13 itself: the same shift locks and unlocks. Key-back also says that a shift is a matching of the letters with themselves, in 4k's sense. Two letters never land on the same letter, since shifting both back would give back two different letters from one. And every letter c is landed on, by shiftj(c). So a shift loses nothing. The reader who knows k reads the message exactly.

How many keys?

Key 0 changes nothing: shift0(p) = p. Key 26 changes nothing either, and key 29 does what key 3 does, since only the remainder of the key by 26 matters (shifts-add with m = 0). Two keys below 26 give two different shifts, since shiftk(0) = k. So there are exactly 26 shifts, and one of them leaves the message as it was. A shift has 25 keys that change anything.

Now take the enemy's side. You have a secret text and know a shift made it, but not the key. You do not need the key. Try every key back, from 1 to 25, and read. Here is a secret text from Castroville, where many of the country's artichokes are grown.

\text{DUWLFKRNHV} @@ the secret text
\text{shift}_1:\ \text{evxmglsoiw} @@ not a word
\text{shift}_2:\ \text{fwynhmtpjx} @@ not a word
\text{shift}_{22}:\ \text{zqshbgnjdr} @@ keys back 3 to 22 read no word either
\text{shift}_{23}:\ \htmlClass{new}{\text{artichokes}} @@ a word: the key was 3, since 3 + 23 = 26
\text{shift}_{24},\ \text{shift}_{25}:\ \text{bsujdiplft},\ \text{ctvkejqmgu} @@ not words; only one key back reads

By hand, a key takes a minute or so, and all 25 take under thirty minutes. A computer does them faster than you can blink. An enemy who knows a shift was used has only 25 keys to try, and trying them all always finds the message. The lock is the number of keys, and 25 is too small to hide behind.

A bigger lock, and why size is not enough

A shift is one kind of matching of the alphabet with itself. Any matching can serve as a key: write the alphabet in a scrambled order, and send each letter to the one below it. Such a key is an arrangement of the 26 letters, so by 4k's arrangements technique there are 26! of them.

26! = 26 \cdot 25 \cdot 24 \cdots 2 \cdot 1 @@ arrangements: 26 choices for a's partner, then 25, and so on
= \htmlClass{new}{403\,291\,461\,126\,605\,635\,584\,000\,000} @@ a number of 27 digits
1\,000\,000\,000 \text{ keys a second} @@ a fast computer
\text{about } 12\,800 \text{ million years} @@ to try them all; the 25 shifts take less time than a blink

That is a lock no one can open by trying keys. Yet a scrambled alphabet is broken every week by people doing puzzles in newspapers. They do not try keys. They count letters, and the box below says why that works. A big number of keys is needed for a lock, and it is not enough.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting

Nobody can say which letter comes next in a sentence. Yet count the letters of a long stretch of ordinary English, and the tallies make a pile with a steady shape. In 1000 letters, e comes about 120 to 130 times, t about 90, the letters a and o about 80 each, and z perhaps once. A shift moves the whole pile along by the key and keeps its shape. So in a long secret text made by a shift, the most common letter is very likely e, moved on. One count replaces 25 tries.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it.

The count is not a proof that any one message reads this way, and a short message can break the pattern. A long one almost never does. The shape of the pile is what the counting gives you.

A secret text of 60 letters, made by a shift, holds X nine times and no other letter more than five times. Which key back would you try first?

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

If he had anything secret to say, he wrote it in cipher, changing the order of the letters so that not a word could be made out. Whoever wishes to read it must put the fourth letter of the alphabet, d, for the letter a, and so with the others.

Suetonius, Life of the Deified Julius, 56 (about 121); paraphrase

The general of 4·crypt.4.5 is Julius Caesar. Suetonius says Augustus used a shift of one. Counting letters to break a cipher was described first by al-Kindi, in Baghdad, in the ninth century. In 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote that a military cipher must not need to be secret, and must be able to fall into enemy hands without harm. A shift by 13, which undoes itself, was used on the early internet from about 1982 to hide the answers to riddles and the ends of stories.

Accepted names

A shift is a Caesar cipher, and the shift by 13 is ROT13. The message is the plaintext and the secret text the ciphertext. Writing the secret is encryption, and reading it with the key is decryption. Trying every key is a brute-force attack, and the set of all keys is the key space. A scrambled alphabet is a substitution cipher, and breaking one by counting letters is frequency analysis. Taking remainders by 26 is arithmetic modulo 26. Kerckhoffs's rule is Kerckhoffs's principle, and its opposite is called security through obscurity.

\text{so } 25 \cdot 25 = 625 \text{ keys to try} @@ false: still 25
