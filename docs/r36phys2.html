How Does a Scanner See a Slice From Shadows?

How does a scanner see a slice from shadows? An X-ray picture is a shadow: everything the rays cross is piled into one grey. Yet a hospital scanner, spinning an X-ray source round a patient, produces a clean picture of one thin slice, with every organ in its place. It never photographs the slice. It works it out from hundreds of shadows. This page adds up what a ray crosses as 15’s area, solves four pixels from four rays with 27l, sees why smearing the shadows back blurs, finds that each shadow is one line through the slice’s frequencies with 32e, and sharpens by filtering before smearing back, as 32i did.

Each ray’s dimming, read as a log, is the sum of what it crossed. Many rays at many angles give many sums; enough of them pin down every pixel. Smearing each shadow back along its rays rebuilds a blurred slice. Each shadow’s frequencies are the slice’s frequencies along one line through the centre, so the blur is a known weighting, and filtering each shadow first undoes it.

36phys21A Shadow Adds Up What the Ray Crosses

Evita runs a hospital scanner. A thin beam of X-rays passes through the body. Each millimetre of tissue removes a fixed share of what reaches it, with a share μ that is larger for bone than for soft tissue.

I = I_0\,e^{-\int \mu\,dx} @@ fixed share per millimetre (17j)
\htmlClass{new}{-\ln\dfrac{I}{I_0} = \int \mu\,dx} @@ the log of the dimming is the sum along the ray (15)

From here on, on this page, a shadow is the list of these sums for all the parallel rays at one angle, and the slice is the unknown μ at every point of it.

36phys22Four Pixels, Four Rays: Solve

Shrink the slice to four squares, a and b on top, c and d below. Rays along the rows, along the columns and along one diagonal give five sums.

a + b = 5,\ \ c + d = 9,\ \ a + c = 6,\ \ b + d = 8,\ \ a + d = 7 @@ the five readings
d = 7 - a,\ \ c = 9 - d = 2 + a,\ \ a + c = 6:\ 2a + 2 = 6 @@ elimination (27l)
\htmlClass{new}{a = 2,\ b = 3,\ c = 4,\ d = 5} @@ check: b + d = 8

Rows and columns alone would not do: their four sums agree in one way (rows add to what columns add to), leaving one unknown loose. The diagonal ray settles it. A real slice of 512 by 512 pixels needs hundreds of thousands of rays at hundreds of angles, too many for elimination.

36phys23Smearing the Shadows Back Blurs

A quick rebuild: for each angle, spread each ray’s sum evenly back along its path, and add over all angles. Every ray through a bright spot puts some brightness there, but also everywhere else along its path.

\text{at distance } r \text{ from the point: brightness} \propto \dfrac{1}{r} @@ rays from every angle cross there
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a sharp point comes back as a star with a halo fading as } 1/r} @@ a blur

36phys24Each Shadow Is One Line Through the Slice’s Frequencies

Break a shadow into frequencies (32e). Those frequencies are exactly the slice’s own two-dimensional frequencies along one line through the centre, at the shadow’s angle. Shadows at all angles fill the plane of frequencies like spokes of a wheel.

\text{frequencies of the shadow at angle } \theta = \text{slice frequencies on the spoke at } \theta @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spokes crowd near the centre: low frequencies are counted too often, by a factor } \propto 1/|k|} @@ the blur, read in frequencies

Slice technique: each shadow’s frequencies are the slice’s along one spoke through the centre, so enough shadows pin down every frequency; smearing back overcounts the low ones by 1/|k|, and multiplying each shadow’s frequencies by |k| first undoes it.

36phys25Filter, Then Smear Back

Multiplying frequencies by |k| is a filter (32i): in the shadow itself, it is a convolution with a small kernel that sharpens edges and gives each bright line slightly dark flanks. Filter each shadow, then smear back.

\text{for each angle: filter the shadow (multiply its frequencies by } |k|) @@ 32i
\text{smear each filtered shadow back, and add} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the dark flanks cancel the } 1/r \text{ halo: the point comes back sharp}} @@

A scanner sharpens each shadow by a fixed filter and smears it back; the halos cancel, and the slice comes out sharp. Every morning Evita scans a test cylinder holding rods of known materials at known places; the rebuilt slice shows each rod at its place, with the brightness its material should give, before any patient is scanned.

Accepted names

The scanner does computed tomography, CT; a shadow is a projection, and the whole set the Radon transform. Smearing back is back-projection, and filtering first filtered back-projection. The spoke picture is the Fourier slice theorem; μ is the attenuation coefficient, and the dimming law Beer-Lambert.

Who said it first

Johann Radon showed in 1917 that a function is fixed by its line sums; Godfrey Hounsfield built the first scanner and shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in medicine with Allan Cormack (not yet revisited for the dates).
