21·cb18 · newsflash for 18a

A city is two turns

18a named points in space by three numbers: across, along and up. On a globe there is a better way, the way every map already does it. Turn round from a starting line to face the city, then tilt up from the equator to reach it. Two turns name every place on Earth. With them you can find how far apart two cities are, both straight through the ground and along the surface, and see why a flight between them bends on a flat map.

A point on a ball is two turns: round from a starting line, then up from the equator. The angle between two cities, seen from the centre, gives their distance along the surface. The shortest-looking line on a flat map is not the route a plane flies.

21·cb18.1Two turns name a point

Two-turns technique: the point on the sphere of radius 1 reached by turning θ round from the starting line and φ up from the equator is (cos φ cos θ, cos φ sin θ, sin φ). Tilting up by φ puts you sin φ high and cos φ out from the axis; then turn that reach round by θ.

\text{up: } \sin\varphi,\ \ \text{out from the axis: } \cos\varphi @@ in the upright plane through the point, 21b
\text{across, along} = (\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\ \cos\varphi\sin\theta) @@ turn and reach (21r1), with reach cos φ
\htmlClass{new}{P = (\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\ \cos\varphi\sin\theta,\ \sin\varphi)} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: cos² φ (cos² θ + sin² θ) + sin² φ = 1 (18a, 21c)

From here on, θ is the longitude and φ the latitude. On a sphere of radius R, the point is R times this one.

21·cb18.2Through the ground, and along it

City-distance technique: the angle γ between two cities, seen from the centre, has cos γ = cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos(θ₁ − θ₂) + sin φ₁ sin φ₂; the surface route along the circle through both and the centre is Rγ long, and the straight line through the ground is 2R sin(γ/2).

P \cdot Q = \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2) + \sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 @@ multiply coordinates and add
= \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) + \sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 = \cos\gamma @@ differences (21e2); the opening is the cosine (21b5), |P| = |Q| = 1
|P - Q|^2 = 2 - 2\cos\gamma = 4\sin^2\tfrac{\gamma}{2} @@ the dot technique; half arcs (21k2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{surface } R\gamma,\ \ \text{through the ground } 2R\sin\tfrac{\gamma}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ arcs on any circle (21j3), on the circle through P, Q and the centre

From here on, a circle on the sphere whose centre is the sphere’s centre is a great circle. The equator is one; so is every line of longitude with its twin across the pole.

21·cb18.3Why the route bends on a flat map

Two cities on one line of latitude are joined by that line, a circle of radius R cos φ. The great circle through them is shorter, and it bends toward the pole.

\text{along the latitude: } R\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} \cdot \pi = \tfrac{\pi R}{\sqrt2} \approx 2.22R @@ arcs on any circle (21j3), radius R cos φ
\cos\gamma = \cos^2\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cos\pi + \sin^2\tfrac{\pi}{4} = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 0 @@ city-distance
\htmlClass{new}{\text{over the pole: } R\gamma = \tfrac{\pi R}{2} \approx 1.57R} \quad \blacksquare @@ a shorter route, through the pole

A flat map that draws latitudes as straight lines draws the great circle as a curve bowed toward the pole. The plane flies the curve.

21·cb18.4A spiral staircase

Climb while you turn: t ↦ (cos t, sin t, ct). Seen from above it is a circle, seen from the side it is a wave, and seen from a slant it is a wave that can loop. One turn has length 2π√(1 + c²).

\text{from above: } (\cos t, \sin t) @@ drop the height: the circle (21b)
\text{from the side: } (\sin t,\ ct) @@ drop the first coordinate: a wave standing up (21i)
\text{speed} = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t + c^2} = \sqrt{1 + c^2} @@ distance in space is the plane’s used twice (18a), speed as in 18i
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one turn: } 2\pi\sqrt{1 + c^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the length is the area under the speed (18i)

From here on, this curve is a helix.

The accepted names

The two turns are spherical coordinates (longitude and latitude, here measured from the equator). The surface distance along a great circle is the great-circle distance; the formula for cos γ is the spherical law of cosines. The staircase is a circular helix.

Who said it first

In 21·cb18.5.5, the first map is Gerardus Mercator’s (1569); the second is Arno Peters’s (1973), whose equal-area projection had been drawn by James Gall in 1855. That no flat map keeps every distance follows from Carl Friedrich Gauss’s Theorema Egregium (1827).
