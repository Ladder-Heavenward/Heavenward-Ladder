How Many Seats Should an Airline Sell?

How many seats should an airline sell? A regional jet that flies from Seattle to Medford in the morning has seventy-six seats. Not everyone who buys a ticket turns up. A meeting moves, a child is sick, a traveller takes an earlier flight. If the airline sells exactly seventy-six tickets, the plane leaves most mornings with empty seats that could have been sold. If it sells more, now and then more people turn up than there are seats, and someone is bumped: put on a later flight and paid for the trouble. Airlines have sold more tickets than seats for as long as they have flown, and the question of how many more is a real one, asked every day for every flight. What makes it hard is that no one knows who will come. Each traveller shows or does not by chance, and the count who show wobbles from day to day. The answer sets one cost against another: the fare lost on an empty seat against the price of a bumped passenger. This page counts who shows with rung thirty’s chance functions, finds the exact number of tickets that makes the average cost least, sees why a bigger plane can sell a bigger share of extra seats, sees what goes wrong when travellers come in groups, and asks what promise an airline can honestly make about bumping.

Each ticket holder shows with the same chance, independently of the others. Then the count who show has a chance function that is the chance function of one traveller multiplied by itself once for each ticket, and from it come the chance of every count, the average and the spread. Now ask what one more ticket does. If its holder stays home, nothing changes. If its holder shows, then either there was an empty seat, and the airline gains a fare, or there was not, and someone is bumped. So one more ticket lowers the average cost exactly while the chance that the plane is already full is below the fare over the fare plus the cost of a bump. That chance rises as tickets are added, so there is one best number. For a big plane the count who show has a spread that grows only like the square root of the tickets, so a bigger plane can overbook by a bigger share. Families and groups who travel together make the spread wider, and the same number of tickets bumps more often. And the airline’s promise about bumping is a rule with a stated chance of failing, which is only as good as its estimate of who shows.

30logi1Who Shows

Sell n tickets. Let Bᵢ be 1 if the holder of ticket i shows and 0 if not, with P(Bᵢ = 1) = p and q = 1 − p, each traveller independent of the others. The count who show is S = B₁ + ⋯ + B_n. One traveller’s chance function (30i) is E[s^{Bᵢ}] = q + ps.

G_S(s) = (q + ps)^n @@ the sums-multiply technique (30i): independent, so the chance functions multiply
(q + ps)^n = \textstyle\sum_k \binom nk p^k q^{n-k} s^k @@ the binomial-sum technique (23h)
P(S = k) = \binom nk p^k q^{n-k} @@ the chances-from-G technique (30i): the coefficient of s^k
E\,S = G_S'(1) = np(q + p)^{n-1} = np @@ the average-from-G technique (30i)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Var} S = npq,\ \ \text{width } \sqrt{npq}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g): each Bᵢ has spread E Bᵢ² − p² = p − p² = pq

No new technique is needed; this is 30i’s read-back of n coins, with its average and spread worked out. If n ticket holders each show with chance p, independently, the count who show takes the value k with chance C(n, k)p^k q^{n−k}, with average np and width √(npq). Horizon Air flies the Embraer 175, with 76 seats, between Seattle and Medford. Take p = 0.92: about 8 travellers in 100 not showing, a share of the size airlines report for such routes. Sell 80 tickets. Then the average count is 73.6, the width 2.43, and the chance that 77 or more show, so that someone is bumped, is 10.9%.

30logi2One More Ticket

Set c seats, a fare f lost on each empty seat, and a cost b for each passenger bumped: payment, a seat on a later flight, a hotel, goodwill. The average cost of selling n tickets is K(n) = f·E[(c − S_n)⁺] + b·E[(S_n − c)⁺], where x⁺ is x when x > 0 and 0 otherwise. Selling ticket n + 1 adds one traveller B, independent of S_n.

S_{n+1} = S_n + B @@ B shows with chance p, independently of S_n
B = 0:\ \text{no change};\ \ B = 1,\ S_n < c:\ -f;\ \ B = 1,\ S_n \ge c:\ +b @@ an empty seat filled, or one more bumped
K(n+1) - K(n) = p\big(b\,P(S_n \ge c) - f\,P(S_n < c)\big) @@ the whole-chance and independent techniques (30f)
= p\big((f + b)\,P(S_n \ge c) - f\big) @@ P(S_n < c) = 1 − P(S_n ≥ c)
P(S_n \ge c) \text{ rises with } n @@ S_{n+1} ≥ S_n always
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sell ticket } n + 1 \text{ exactly while } P(S_n \ge c) < \tfrac{f}{f + b}} \quad \blacksquare @@ once the cost rises it keeps rising

One-more-ticket technique: one more ticket lowers the average cost exactly while the chance that the tickets already sold fill the plane is below f/(f + b), so the best number is the first n at which that chance reaches f/(f + b). Take a fare of $150 and a bump cost of $800, both figures chosen for this page. Then f/(f + b) = 0.158. By the count of 30·logi.1, P(S₇₉ ≥ 76) = 0.115, below the line, and P(S₈₀ ≥ 76) = 0.223, above it. So sell 80.

The costs: 76 tickets, $912 a flight on average, all of it in empty seats. 79 tickets, $550. 80 tickets, $513, with a bump on about one flight in nine. 81 tickets, $570. Selling four extra seats on each of a few thousand flights a year is worth over a million dollars.

30logi3A Bigger Plane

Alaska Airlines flies the Boeing 737-900ER, with 178 seats, between Seattle and San Francisco. The count who show is a sum of many independent parts, so by the central limit technique (30j) it is close to a bell with average np and width √(npq). The one-more-ticket rule asks for the n at which P(S_n ≥ 178) reaches 0.158, and on the bell that is about one width: np + √(npq) ≈ 177.5.

np = 189 \times 0.92 = 173.88,\ \ \sqrt{npq} = 3.73 @@ the count of 30·logi.1
P(S \ge 178) \approx P\big(Z > \tfrac{177.5 - 173.88}{3.73}\big) = P(Z > 0.97) = 0.166 @@ the central limit technique; half a seat for the step from 177 to 178
P(S_{188} \ge 178) = 0.107,\ \ P(S_{189} \ge 178) = 0.166 @@ exact, by the count of 30·logi.1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sell } 189:\ 11 \text{ extra, } 6.2\% \text{ of the seats; on 76 seats, } 4 \text{ extra, } 5.3\%} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-more-ticket technique

The count who show wobbles like the square root of the tickets, so the bigger the plane, the bigger the share of extra tickets it can sell at the same cost of bumping. The average no-shows grow like n; their wobble only like √n.

30logi4Travellers Who Come Together

30·logi.1 asked for independent travellers. A couple on one booking shows together or not at all; so does a family, or a group of four on a business trip. Sell n tickets as n/g groups of g, each group showing whole with chance p, independently.

S = g\,(C_1 + \cdots + C_{n/g}),\ \ C_j \text{ is } 1 \text{ if group } j \text{ shows} @@ each group counts g seats
E\,S = g \cdot \tfrac ng \cdot p = np @@ the average-adds technique (30g): unchanged
\operatorname{Var} S = g^2 \cdot \tfrac ng \cdot pq = g\,npq @@ the spread of g C is g² times its spread; the groups are independent
\htmlClass{new}{\text{groups of } g \text{ leave the average as it was and make the width } \sqrt g \text{ times as large}} \quad \blacksquare @@ by the spread-of-a-sum technique the extra is the co-spread inside each group

Groups-widen technique: travellers who show together leave the average count unchanged and widen it by √g, so the same number of tickets bumps more often. With 80 tickets on the 76-seat jet, all in pairs, someone is bumped only if 39 or 40 pairs show: chance 15.9%, up from 10.9%. In groups of four, only if all 20 groups show: 18.9%. A late connecting flight is a group too: everyone on it misses together.

30logi5A Promise With a Known Exception Rate

An airline may choose not the cheapest number but a promise: bump someone on no more than 5% of flights. By the count of 30·logi.1, 79 tickets bump on 4.3% of flights and 80 on 10.9%. So the promise means 79 tickets, at $550 a flight against $513: the promise costs about $38 a flight.

But p is not known. It is estimated from past flights, as the share who showed, and by 30q’s sample-average technique that share has width √(p(1 − p)/n). From 2000 past tickets on the route, 92% of whom showed, the estimate has width √(0.92 × 0.08/2000) = 0.006. Within a width or two of 0.92 the chance of a bump changes a great deal.

p = 0.90:\ \ 3.5\% @@ the count of 30·logi.1
p = 0.91:\ \ 6.3\% @@ 
p = 0.92:\ \ 10.9\% @@ 
p = 0.93:\ \ 18.1\% @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a width of } 0.01 \text{ in } p \text{ moves the bump chance by about half}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the count who show moves by 0.8 of a seat, a third of its width

A promise about bumping is a rule with a stated exception rate, and the rate is only as good as the estimate of who shows. Monday mornings to Medford and holiday flights to San Francisco have different p, and the airline keeps a separate estimate for each.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The airline does not know who will come, and it does not pretend to. It sells 79 tickets and states that on about 1 flight in 23 someone will be bumped. That is a law with a known exception rate: a rule about real things that comes with how often it fails. It is not weakened by the flights on which it fails; it predicted them.

A rule chosen against chance should state how often it fails, and how well that rate is known.

The rate itself is measured, so it carries a width. The honest statement is the rule, the exception rate, and how well the rate is known: 79 tickets, a bump on about 4% of flights, if 92% show, as they have on 2000 past tickets. When a new season changes who flies, the count of bumped flights is the check, and the rule is redrawn.

Over a summer, the 79-ticket rule on the Seattle to Medford flight bumped someone on 9 of 90 flights. It promised about 4 in 100. What would you check first?

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Selling more tickets than seats is overbooking. The count who show, with chances C(n, k)p^k q^{n−k}, has the binomial distribution. Putting a passenger on a later flight against their will is involuntary denied boarding; when passengers agree, for a voucher, it is voluntary. The one-more-ticket rule, with its line f/(f + b), is the critical ratio, the same rule as for a newspaper seller deciding how many papers to stock (the newsvendor problem). Groups who show together make the count overdispersed.

Who said it first

Martin Beckmann wrote down a model of overbooking as a choice between two costs in 1958, and Marvin Rothstein built one for an airline in 1971. The newsvendor rule goes back to Francis Edgeworth’s question of 1888 about how much cash a bank should keep against withdrawals. In the United States, rules on paying bumped passengers followed a lawsuit by the consumer advocate Ralph Nader, bumped from a flight in 1972, which reached the Supreme Court in 1976. The Department of Transportation publishes the rates of bumping for each large airline. In 30·logi.7.5, some airlines have announced that they no longer overbook, among them Southwest in 2017.
