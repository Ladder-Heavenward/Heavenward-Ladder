39d · proof

A One-Way Valve

The newsflash on the complex rung met the diode, a sandwich of two kinds of silicon whose current grows by equal factors for equal steps of push, and found it shut below about half a volt and wide open just above. This step takes that valve and asks what logic it can do on its own. Two valves feeding one resistor make an either gate: the output follows whichever input is higher. Two valves hanging from a resistor to the supply make a both gate: the output follows whichever input is lower. So both and either come cheaply. Then the step proves what valves cannot do, and the proof is the interesting part. In any network of valves and resistors, raising an input never lowers anything, so no such network can turn a one into a zero. And no node moves further than the inputs moved, so a weak one never comes out stronger: each stage loses a little, and after a few stages the one is gone. Something with a supply of its own is needed, and the next step builds it.

A diode passes current one way, exponentially, and backwards almost none. Two diodes and a resistor make either, or both. In a network of diodes and resistors, raising an input never lowers a node, so there is no not; and no node moves further than an input, so a weak level is never restored.

39d1Forward and Back

26·then1 laid down the measured law of a silicon diode, I = Iₛ(e^(V/Vₜ) − 1), with Iₛ about 10⁻¹² A and Vₜ ≈ 0.0259 V at room warmth, and proved the sixty-millivolt technique for V above 0. The difference here is the other side: V below 0.

Reverse-leak technique: pushed backwards, a diode passes less than Iₛ, however hard it is pushed.

V < 0 \Rightarrow 0 < e^{V/V_T} < 1 @@ exp is above 0 everywhere and rises (17e), and exp 0 = 1
-1 < e^{V/V_T} - 1 < 0 @@ take 1 from each part
-I_s < I < 0 @@ multiply by Iₛ > 0 (5g)
V = -5 \text{ V}:\ \ I \approx -10^{-12} \text{ A};\ \ V = 0.6 \text{ V}:\ \ I \approx 0.012 \text{ A} @@ the law, worked by machine
\htmlClass{new}{\text{forward at } 0.6 \text{ V passes about ten thousand million times more than backward at } 5 \text{ V}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0.012 against 10⁻¹²

So a diode is a switch read by the sign of the push: closed one way, open the other. It is a switch nobody works. Its state is set by the voltages around it, which is what makes it a valve and not yet a gate that can be steered. Its accepted plain name is a rectifier, from the Latin rectus, straight, for the way it turns a socket’s back and forth into one-way flow.

39d2Either and Both From Valves

Read 5 V as 1 and 0 V as 0. For either: each input feeds a diode, the diodes join at the output, and a 1 kΩ resistor runs from the output to ground. For both: each input meets a diode pointing toward it, the diodes join at the output, and a resistor runs from the output up to the 5 V supply.

Diode-gate technique: the either gate’s output sits one diode drop below the higher input, and the both gate’s output one drop above the lower input; read against a threshold, they are 39a’s greater and lesser.

\text{either, inputs } 5, 0:\ \ 5 - V_d = 1000\,I,\ \ I = I_s(e^{V_d/V_T} - 1) @@ the higher input’s diode is forward; the loop law (7·phys)
V_d \approx 0.57 \text{ V},\ \ \text{output} \approx 4.43 \text{ V} @@ solved by machine; the other diode is pushed backwards and passes less than Iₛ
\text{both, inputs } 5, 0:\ \ \text{the diode to the } 0 \text{ V input is forward} @@ the supply pushes current out through it
\text{output} \approx 0 + 0.6 \text{ V} \text{, read as } 0 @@ the lower input wins
\htmlClass{new}{\text{either} \approx \max(x, y) - V_d,\ \ \text{both} \approx \min(x, y) + V_d} \quad \blacksquare @@ the or-as-greater technique (39a2), and the and as the lesser

On {0, 1} the lesser of x and y is xy, so the both gate is 39a1’s product, read off a wire. The gates are cheap and they work. What they do to the level is the problem: 4.43 V is not 5 V.

39d3No Not From Valves

A network of diodes and resistors, with fixed supplies and some inputs, settles where the current law holds at every inner node: what flows in flows out. Each part’s current is a rising function of the voltage across it, V/R for a resistor and the diode’s law for a diode, and every inner node is joined by some path to a supply or an input.

Rising-network technique: in a network of diodes and resistors, raising inputs and lowering none never lowers the voltage of any node.

\text{some node falls; let } m \text{ fall the most, by } \delta > 0 @@ suppose it, and take the largest fall (4h: the finitely many nodes have a largest)
\text{each neighbour } j \text{ of } m \text{ falls by at most } \delta @@ inner nodes by the choice of m; inputs rose; supplies did not move
V_m - V_j \text{ does not rise, so the current from } m \text{ to } j \text{ does not rise} @@ each current is a rising function of the voltage across it
\text{the currents out of } m \text{ add to } 0 \text{ before and after} @@ the current law (7·phys)
\text{so none changed, and each neighbour fell by exactly } \delta @@ a sum of parts that did not rise, unchanged: each part unchanged; strictly rising functions
\bot @@ carry it along a path from m to a supply or an input, which fell by at most 0, not δ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no node falls}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition is refuted (1b)

A not would have to send its output down when its input goes up. The rising-network technique forbids it. So valves make both and either and nothing that turns a 1 into a 0. 39f will show that both and either alone miss every table with a not in it, and 4·comp’s not gate needs a part this network does not have.

39d4No Restoring From Valves

The same argument, run on the largest rise in place of the largest fall, gives a second fact.

No-gain technique: in a network of diodes and resistors, if no input rises by more than Δ, no node rises by more than Δ. The node with the largest rise, if above Δ, has every neighbour rising by less or as much, so no current out of it falls, so none changes, and the walk to an input or supply ends in ⊥.

5 \text{ V} \to 4.43 \to 3.86 \to 3.29 \to 2.73 \text{ V} @@ each stage with a 1 kΩ load, solved by machine: each drop near 0.57 V
\text{reading threshold } 2.5 \text{ V} @@ halfway, as 39b4
\text{after four stages the margin is } 0.23 \text{ V} @@ from 2.5 V at the start
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a fifth stage reads the } 1 \text{ as } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ about 2.2 V: below the threshold

A gate that restored a weak level would have to move its output further than its input moved, near the threshold: a slope above 1. The no-gain technique says valves never do. So noise that slips in is never squeezed out again, and every stage spends margin. 39b4 priced margin in spreads of noise; a chain of valves spends it by the stage. The cure is a part with its own supply, steered by the signal, and 39e builds it.

The accepted names

The diode’s law is the Shockley diode equation; Iₛ is the saturation current and Vₜ = kT/q the thermal voltage. The either and both gates are diode–resistor logic (DRL), an OR gate and an AND gate. A network whose outputs rise with its inputs computes a monotone function; the rising-network technique is a maximum principle for resistive networks, like the one for still water on rung 33. That no passive network has a slope above 1 is the statement that a passive network has no power gain.

Who said it first

Ferdinand Braun found that a metal point on a crystal passes current one way in 1874, and crystal detectors were the first radio receivers. John Ambrose Fleming’s valve of 1904, a heated wire and a plate in a vacuum, was the first diode tube. William Shockley derived the junction diode’s law in 1949. Diode logic was used beside valves in machines of the late 1940s and 1950s, including the SEAC (1950), with valves added to restore the levels.

A proof that is not allowed

“A diode backwards passes no current, so it is an open switch, and a diode forwards is a closed one. So a network of diodes is a network of switches, and 4·comp built a not from switches.” Not allowed: the switches of a relay are worked by another wire. A diode’s state is set by the voltages on its own two ends, which is why the rising-network technique covers it.
