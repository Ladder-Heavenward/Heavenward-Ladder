How Much Does One Vote Weigh?

How much does one vote weigh? A committee has four blocs, A, B, C and D, holding 4, 3, 2 and 1 votes, and a motion passes with 6 of the 10. It looks as if A has four tenths of the say and D one tenth. But a bloc has a say only when its votes change the outcome. This page writes “the motion passes” as an or of ands of the blocs’ letters, calls a bloc pivotal when flipping its letter flips the whole statement, counts every group of blocs with 4k’s counting, and finds that power is not the share of votes: one bloc with 2 votes weighs as much as one with 3, and a bloc can hold votes and no power at all.

Let each bloc’s letter be true when it votes yes. “The motion passes” is true exactly when the yes votes reach 6: write it as an or of the smallest groups that reach 6. In each passing group, a bloc is pivotal if changing its letter to false makes the statement false. A bloc’s power is its share of all the pivotal places.

6econ21A Committee of Blocs

The committee has four blocs. Each bloc votes as one, yes or no. A motion passes when the yes votes add up to 6 or more.

From here on, on this page, the letter A stands for “bloc A votes yes”, and the same for B, C and D. A group is a set of blocs, the ones voting yes; it passes when its votes add to at least 6.

There are 16 groups, one for each way of choosing yes or no for each of the four blocs (4k: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2), from the empty group to all four.

6econ22“The Motion Passes” as an Or of Ands

Find the groups that pass with nobody to spare: take any bloc out and the group fails. A with B makes 7, A with C makes 6, and B, C and D together make 6. Every other passing group holds one of these three.

A \land B:\ 4 + 3 = 7 @@ passes
A \land C:\ 4 + 2 = 6 @@ passes
B \land C \land D:\ 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 @@ passes
\htmlClass{new}{\text{passes} \;\Leftrightarrow\; (A \land B) \lor (A \land C) \lor (B \land C \land D)} @@ an or of ands, as in rung 1

The statement has a column (1e): 16 rows, one per group, with 1 where the motion passes. Seven rows have a 1: AB, AC, BCD, ABC, ABD, ACD and ABCD.

6econ23A Pivotal Bloc Flips the Statement

In a passing group, a bloc matters if its leaving would sink the motion. In rung 1’s terms: keep every other letter as it is, flip this bloc’s letter from true to false, and see whether the statement goes from true to false.

Pivot technique: a bloc is pivotal in a passing group when flipping its letter, with every other letter held, flips the passing statement from true to false.

\text{group } \{A, B, D\}:\ 4 + 3 + 1 = 8 @@ passes
\text{flip } A:\ 3 + 1 = 4 @@ fails: A is pivotal
\text{flip } B:\ 4 + 1 = 5 @@ fails: B is pivotal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flip } D:\ 4 + 3 = 7} @@ still passes: D is not

\htmlClass{new}{\text{power: } A\ \tfrac{5}{12},\ B\ \tfrac{3}{12},\ C\ \tfrac{3}{12},\ D\ \tfrac{1}{12}} @@ a ratio of counts (5j)

Pivot-count technique: a bloc’s power is the number of passing groups in which it is pivotal, over all the pivotal places of all the blocs. Votes go 4, 3, 2, 1; power goes 5, 3, 3, 1. C, with 2 votes, has exactly the power of B, with 3: every group B can rescue, C can rescue too.

6econ25Votes and No Power

Now give the committee different votes: A and B 4 each, C 2 and D 1, with 8 needed out of 11. The only way to reach 8 is A and B together: without both, the most is 4 + 2 + 1 = 7.

\text{passes} \;\Leftrightarrow\; A \land B @@ every passing group holds A and B
\text{flip } C \text{ in any passing group: } A \land B \text{ still true} @@ C never flips the statement
\text{the same for } D @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{power: } A\ \tfrac{1}{2},\ B\ \tfrac{1}{2},\ C\ 0,\ D\ 0} @@ three votes between them, and no say

A bloc whose letter does not appear in the passing statement, once it is written as an or of the smallest passing groups, has no power, whatever its votes. A committee’s secretary can find such a bloc in minutes by writing the passing statement out, and a committee setting its votes can check every bloc this way before it adopts them.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Blocs voting with different weights make a weighted voting game, and the votes needed are the quota. A group is a coalition, a passing group with nobody to spare is a minimal winning coalition, and a pivotal bloc is a critical or swing voter. Power as the share of pivotal places is the Banzhaf power index, and a bloc with no power is a dummy.

Who said it first

John Banzhaf set out the count in 1965, in “Weighted voting doesn’t work”, using a county board with weighted votes where some members held votes and no power. Lloyd Shapley and Martin Shubik had published a related count, by orders of voting, in 1954.
