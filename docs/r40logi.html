40·logi · logistics

What is the cheapest way to ship?

What is the cheapest way to ship? A freight company has two ports, three cities that want containers, a map of roads, two bridges over a river and a warehouse to build by a deadline. Each of these is a question about a network, and this rung has already answered each one in the abstract. This page puts numbers on them. It finds the shortest road from Seattle to Boise, and the nearest warehouse to every town in one run. It finds the most traffic that can cross the Columbia River north out of Portland, and why widening the wrong road adds nothing. It finds the cheapest plan for moving 700 containers from Seattle and Oakland to three cities, proves that no plan is cheaper, and reads off what a container is worth at each port. And it finds the chain of tasks that sets how long a project must take, and how long every other task may slip.

Shortest routes come from the nearest-first technique; one extra start serves every warehouse at once. The most a network carries is its smallest cut, so only widening a link of every smallest cut helps. The cheapest shipping plan is a best corner, its used routes form a tree, and prices at the ports prove it cheapest. A project takes as long as its longest chain of tasks.

40·logi.1The shortest road, and the nearest warehouse

Roads between six towns, in rounded road km: Seattle to Ellensburg 175, Ellensburg to Yakima 55, Yakima to Pendleton 215, Seattle to Portland 280, Portland to Pendleton 340, Pendleton to Boise 340. Every length is at least 0, so the nearest-first technique of 40k applies: settle the nearest unsettled town, then lower the tentative distances of its neighbours.

\text{settle Seattle } 0;\ \ \text{Ellensburg } 175,\ \text{Portland } 280 @@ tentative
\text{settle Ellensburg } 175;\ \ \text{Yakima } 175 + 55 = 230 @@ 
\text{settle Yakima } 230;\ \ \text{Pendleton } 230 + 215 = 445 @@ 
\text{settle Portland } 280;\ \ \text{Pendleton stays } 445 < 280 + 340 @@ 620 is longer
\text{settle Pendleton } 445;\ \ \text{Boise } 445 + 340 = 785 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Seattle to Boise: } 785 \text{ km, by Ellensburg, Yakima and Pendleton}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nearest-first technique (40k)

When snow shuts the pass between Seattle and Ellensburg, the link goes and the run gives 280 + 340 + 340 = 960 km by Portland. A freight planner keeps the same map and changes the lengths: km for fuel, hours for drivers, dollars for tolls. The technique does not care which, so long as no length is below 0.

Shared-start technique: add one new dot with a link of length 0 to every warehouse; one nearest-first run from it gives every town its distance to the nearest warehouse.

\text{a path from the new dot starts with one link of length } 0 \text{ to a warehouse } h @@ the new dot links only to warehouses
\text{its length is the length of the rest, a path from } h @@ adding 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shortest from the new dot} = \min_h \text{shortest from } h} \quad \blacksquare @@

With warehouses in Seattle and Boise, one run gives Portland 280 and Yakima 230 from Seattle, and Pendleton 340 from Boise. Running once per warehouse would cost one run each; the shared start costs one run.

40·logi.2The river cut

North out of Portland every truck crosses the Columbia River, on the I-5 bridge with 3 lanes each way or the I-205 bridge with 4. A freeway lane carries at most about 2000 vehicles an hour. Make the roads a flow network: Portland is s, Vancouver on the north bank is t, and each road has the capacity of its lanes.

\text{the cut: south bank on one side, north bank on the other} @@ every route from s to t crosses it
\text{capacity} = (3 + 4) \cdot 2000 = 14\,000 \text{ vehicles an hour} @@ the two bridges northbound
\text{the roads to the bridges carry more: } 4 \text{ and } 5 \text{ lanes, } 18\,000 @@ a larger cut
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the most that can cross} = 14\,000 \text{ an hour}} \quad \blacksquare @@ if a flow of 14 000 exists, the bottleneck technique (40f) says no cut is smaller

Untouched-cut technique: widening links that leave some smallest cut untouched cannot raise the largest flow.

\text{flow} \le \text{that cut’s capacity} @@ the flow-under-cut technique (40f)
\text{its capacity is unchanged} @@ none of its links was widened
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the largest flow is unchanged}} \quad \blacksquare @@ it was already equal to that cut

So adding a lane to the approach roads, which are not on the river cut, moves nothing more across. Adding a lane to one bridge raises the cut to 16 000, and the flow rises only if no other cut is now smaller. A planner finds the smallest cut first, from the reachable dots of the bottleneck technique, and spends on its links.

40·logi.3The cheapest shipping plan

Seattle has 300 containers and Oakland 400. Portland wants 250, Sacramento 300 and Boise 150. The cost of a route is its road km, one container at a time: Seattle to Portland 280, to Sacramento 1210, to Boise 810; Oakland to Portland 1020, to Sacramento 130, to Boise 1050. Let x be the containers on each route. Each port ships what it has, each city gets what it wants, every x is at least 0, and the total km is to be least. That is a linear program, as in 40g, with six unknowns.

\text{Seattle: } 250 \to \text{Portland},\ 50 \to \text{Boise} @@ 300 containers
\text{Oakland: } 300 \to \text{Sacramento},\ 100 \to \text{Boise} @@ 400 containers
250 \cdot 280 + 50 \cdot 810 + 300 \cdot 130 + 100 \cdot 1050 @@ the total km
\htmlClass{new}{= 70\,000 + 40\,500 + 39\,000 + 105\,000 = 254\,500 \text{ container-km}} @@ the next footing proves no plan is cheaper

It uses 4 routes, which is the 2 ports plus the 3 cities less 1, and those routes join the five places into a tree: Portland, Seattle, Boise, Oakland, Sacramento in a path. That is no accident.

Tree-corner technique: at a corner of the shipping plans, the routes in use contain no cycle; so when supplies and wants are whole numbers, every corner ships whole numbers.

\text{a cycle of used routes alternates port, city, port, city} @@ links join ports to cities only: two-sided (40a)
\text{add } t \text{ and take } t \text{ in turn round it} @@ the cycle is even, so this closes up
\text{each port and city gains } t \text{ and loses } t @@ supplies and wants still met
\text{the plan is the middle of the plans for } +t \text{ and } -t @@ both allowed for small t, since used routes carry more than 0
\text{so a plan with a cycle of used routes is not a corner} @@ a corner is the middle of no two other plans
\text{a forest has a leaf: one route, which must carry that place’s whole amount} @@ 40b
\htmlClass{new}{\text{peel leaves: each amount is a whole number less whole numbers}} \quad \blacksquare @@

By the tight-corner technique of 40g a corner is best, so a best plan in whole containers exists, and no rounding is needed. Peeling the tree above gives the plan back: Portland is a leaf, so Seattle to Portland carries 250; Sacramento is a leaf, so Oakland to Sacramento carries 300; Boise takes the rest from each port.

40·logi.4What a container is worth at each port

Give each port i a price qᵢ and each city j a price pⱼ, read as the worth of one container there. The twin of the shipping program (40g) asks for prices with pⱼ − qᵢ at most the cost of the route from i to j, and the most total worth Σ pⱼ · want − Σ qᵢ · supply.

Route-price technique: a shipping plan is cheapest if there are prices with pⱼ − qᵢ at most the cost on every route, and equal to it on every route in use.

\textstyle\sum_{i,j} c_{ij} x_{ij} \ge \sum_{i,j} (p_j - q_i)\, x_{ij} @@ any plan: c ≥ p − q and x ≥ 0
= \textstyle\sum_j p_j \cdot \text{want}_j - \sum_i q_i \cdot \text{supply}_i @@ sum the x into each city and out of each port
\text{equality for the given plan} @@ c = p − q wherever x > 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no plan costs less than the given one}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the price-bound technique, for shipping
  
q_{\text{Oak}} = 0,\ \ p_{\text{Sac}} = 130,\ \ p_{\text{Boi}} = 1050 @@ equal on Oakland’s two used routes
q_{\text{Sea}} = 1050 - 810 = 240,\ \ p_{\text{Por}} = 240 + 280 = 520 @@ equal on Seattle’s two used routes
\text{unused: } p_{\text{Sac}} - q_{\text{Sea}} = -110 \le 1210,\ \ p_{\text{Por}} - q_{\text{Oak}} = 520 \le 1020 @@ the two unused routes pass
\htmlClass{new}{520 \cdot 250 + 130 \cdot 300 + 1050 \cdot 150 - 240 \cdot 300 = 254\,500} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-prices technique (40g): the two totals meet

The prices say more than that the plan is best. A container that starts in Seattle instead of Oakland is worth q_Sea − q_Oak = 240 km less to ship: run the program with 301 and 399 and the total drops to 254 260. A port with a higher price is a port to grow. Oakland to Portland passes with 500 km to spare, so a road there would have to cut its cost by more than 500 before any container used it.

40·logi.5The longest chain of tasks

A warehouse is built in seven tasks, in days: survey S 2; dig B 3, after S; order the frame C 4; raise the frame D 5, after B and C; roof E 2, after D; wire F 3, after D; finish G 2, after E and F. Make each task a dot with a link to each task that must wait for it. The graph is one-way with no cycle, so by the forward-order technique of 40l it has a forward order, and the earliest start of each task is the latest finish of the tasks before it.

S: 0,\ \ B: 2,\ \ C: 0 @@ S and C wait for nothing
D: \max(2 + 3,\ 0 + 4) = 5 @@ B finishes at 5, C at 4
E: 5 + 5 = 10,\ \ F: 10 @@ after D
G: \max(10 + 2,\ 10 + 3) = 13 @@ F finishes later
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the warehouse is done at } 13 + 2 = 15 \text{ days}} @@ the longest chain S, B, D, F, G is 2 + 3 + 5 + 3 + 2

Run the same pass backwards from day 15 to find the latest start that does not delay the end. The slack of a task is its latest start less its earliest.

Slack-time technique: a task can start up to its slack late without moving the end, and the tasks of slack 0 contain a longest chain.

\text{latest: } G\ 13,\ F\ 10,\ E\ 11,\ D\ 5,\ B\ 2,\ C\ 1,\ S\ 0 @@ each: the least latest start of what waits, less its own days
\text{slack: } C\ 1 - 0 = 1,\ \ E\ 11 - 10 = 1,\ \text{ the rest } 0 @@ 
\text{a slack-0 task has a slack-0 task right before it, finishing just in time} @@ else it could start later
\htmlClass{new}{\text{following them back from } G \text{ gives the chain } S, B, D, F, G} \quad \blacksquare @@ a longest chain

Longest paths through every dot are among the questions of 39h with no known fast method, on graphs in general. Here the graph has no cycle, and the forward order makes the longest path as easy as the shortest. A manager shortens the project only by shortening a task of slack 0; buying a faster roofer saves nothing.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The nearest-first technique is Dijkstra's algorithm, and the shared start is a super-source. The river cut is the max-flow min-cut theorem in use; the untouched-cut technique is why only critical arcs repay widening. The cheapest shipping plan is the transportation problem; its corners are basic feasible solutions, whose used routes form a spanning tree, and its whole corners come from total unimodularity. The route prices are dual variables, or shadow prices, and the route-price technique is complementary slackness for the transportation problem. The longest chain of tasks is the critical path, and the forward and backward passes are the critical path method.

Who said it first

Frank Lauren Hitchcock set the transportation problem in 1941 and Tjalling Koopmans studied it for wartime shipping; Leonid Kantorovich had priced resources in 1939, and the two shared the 1975 economics prize. The critical path method was built by Morgan Walker and James Kelley in 1957 for plant maintenance, and its cousin PERT the next year for the Polaris missile programme. Edsger Dijkstra's method is of 1959; Lester Ford and Delbert Fulkerson's flows of 1956 began from a question about the rail network of eastern Europe.

A proof that is not allowed

“Boise gets its containers from Seattle, the cheaper port for it, Sacramento from Oakland, and Portland from Seattle. Each city is served as cheaply as it can be, so the plan is cheapest.” Not allowed: Seattle has 300 containers and that plan asks it for 400. A plan cheapest city by city may not be a plan at all. The proof that a plan is cheapest is a set of prices, checked on every route.
