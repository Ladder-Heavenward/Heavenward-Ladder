How Should You Bid at an Auction?

How should you bid at an auction? Bid too much and you overpay; bid too little and you lose a thing you wanted. The answer depends on the rules. This page sets out four common rules, finds that under one of them you should bid exactly what the thing is worth to you, and under another you should bid less. Then it averages over every pair of bidders, with 16’s areas, and finds that the two rules bring the seller the same price on average. Last, it shows why the winner of a guess is often the one who guessed too high.

Name what the thing is worth to each bidder and what each pays under the rule. Compare bidding your worth with bidding more or less, case by case. Where the price is your own bid, write your average gain as a function of your bid and set its slope to 0. Average the price over every pair of worths as an area, and compare the rules.

16econ21Four Ways to Sell

Evita has a painting to sell, and two bidders, Terry and Mike. To Terry it is worth v, to Mike w, measured in thousands of dollars and lying somewhere from 0 to 1. Each knows only their own worth. Evita can sell it in four common ways.

rulehow it runsthe winner pays
risingthe price climbs until one bidder drops outthe price where the other dropped out
fallingthe price falls until one bidder calls “mine”the price at the call
sealed, first priceeach writes one bid; the higher winstheir own bid
sealed, second priceeach writes one bid; the higher winsthe other’s bid

From here on, on this page, a bidder’s gain is their worth less the price when they win, and 0 when they lose. In the rising auction a bidder stays in until the price reaches their worth, so the winner pays the other’s worth, as in the second-price rule. In the falling auction a bidder must call before seeing anyone else, as in the first-price rule.

16econ22Second Price: Bid Your Worth

Under the second-price rule, Terry writes a bid b. Let r be Mike’s bid. Terry wins when b > r and then pays r, not b. Compare b = v with any other b, case by case on r.

r < v,\ r < b:\ \text{wins either way, pays } r @@ the same gain, v − r
r > v,\ r > b:\ \text{loses either way} @@ the same gain, 0
b < r < v:\ \text{a low bid loses a gain of } v - r > 0 @@ shading can only cost
v < r < b:\ \text{a high bid wins and pays } r > v @@ overbidding can only cost
\htmlClass{new}{b = v \text{ is never worse than any other bid}} @@ whatever Mike does

Second-price technique: when the winner pays the other’s bid, bidding your own worth is never worse than any other bid, whatever the others do. Your bid decides only whether you win, never what you pay.

16econ23First Price: Bid Less

Under the first-price rule, the winner pays their own bid, so bidding your worth gains nothing even when you win. Say Mike bids half his worth, w/2, and w is equally likely to lie anywhere from 0 to 1, so the chance it lies in a stretch is that stretch’s length. Terry bids b, no more than ½.

\text{Terry wins} \iff w/2 < b \iff w < 2b @@ the chance is the length 2b
G(b) = (v - b) \cdot 2b = 2vb - 2b^2 @@ average gain: what a win brings, times its chance
G\prime(b) = 2v - 4b @@ slopes of a sum (13)
2v - 4b = 0 @@ the flat-top technique (14a)
\htmlClass{new}{b = v/2} @@ Terry bids half his worth too

Mike’s half was a guess, and the answer is half again, so the pair is stuck in 7·econ’s sense: each bid is best against the other. First-price technique: when the winner pays their own bid, each bidder shades below their worth; with two bidders and worths spread evenly, by half.

16econ24Which Rule Pays Evita More?

Under the first-price rule Evita gets half the higher worth; under the second-price rule she gets the lower worth in full. Neither is larger every time. Average over every pair (v, w), each equally likely, using 15f’s average value one bidder at a time.

\text{fix } v:\ \textstyle\int_0^1 \min(v, w)\,dw = \int_0^v w\,dw + \int_v^1 v\,dw @@ split the area at w = v (16a)
= \tfrac{v^2}{2} + v(1 - v) = v - \tfrac{v^2}{2} @@ area is a change (16c)
\textstyle\int_0^1 \left(v - \tfrac{v^2}{2}\right) dv = \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 @@ average over v (15f)
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac13} @@ the second-price average
  
\min + \max = v + w @@ one is the lower, one the higher
\text{average max} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac23 @@ averages add (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac13} @@ the first-price average

Equal-average technique: with these bidders, the first-price rule and the second-price rule bring the seller the same average price, 1/3. One rule asks for the truth and charges the second bid; the other charges the winner’s own bid, which the winner has shaded by just enough.

16econ25The Winner Guessed Too High

Some things are worth the same to everyone, and no one knows how much: the oil under a field, a crop not yet grown. Each bidder makes a guess, some too high and some too low, right on average as in 6·stat. The highest guess wins.

\text{guesses } 8,\ 10,\ 12 @@ right on average: 30/3 = 10
\text{each bids its guess; } 12 \text{ wins} @@ the highest guess, not the average one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the winner pays } 12 \text{ for } 10} @@ winning picks the guess that was too high

Winning is news: it says every other bidder guessed lower. When the thing is worth the same to all and each only guesses it, bid below your guess, and further below when there are more bidders. With more guessers, the highest guess lies further above the truth.

16econ27A Reserve Price

In a second-price sale the winner pays the other bid (16econ22). A seller can also set a reserve: no sale below it, and a lone bid above it pays the reserve. Sometimes the item goes unsold; but when one bidder is high and the other low, the seller is paid the reserve, not the low bid.

\text{no reserve: average take} = \tfrac13 @@ the average lower worth
\text{reserve } r:\ 2r^2(1 - r) + (1 - r)^2\big(r + \tfrac{1 - r}{3}\big) @@ one above, or both above
r = \tfrac12:\ \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac{5}{12} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{5}{12} > \tfrac13 \text{ although one sale in four fails}} @@

Reserve technique: a reserve trades the chance of no sale against a higher price when only one bidder is keen; with worths spread evenly up to 1, a reserve of ½ is best, whatever the number of bidders. Shown in a run: a hundred thousand simulated sales between two bidders take on average 0.333 with no reserve and 0.417 with a reserve of ½, as the areas predict.

Accepted names

The rising auction is the English auction, the falling one the Dutch auction. A sealed second-price auction is a Vickrey auction, and bidding your worth there is a dominant strategy. Shading under the first-price rule is bid shading, and the stuck pair of bids is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium. The equal averages are a case of revenue equivalence. A thing worth the same to all is a common value, and paying too much for it is the winner’s curse.

Who said it first

William Vickrey worked out the second-price rule and the equal averages in “Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders”, 1961.
