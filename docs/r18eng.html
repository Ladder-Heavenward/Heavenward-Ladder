Why Are Bridges Built From Triangles?

Why are bridges built from triangles? Look at a railway bridge, a crane's arm or the frame under a roof, and you see triangles, rarely a four-sided shape on its own. Carpenters knew the trick long before anyone could say why it worked: nail a slanting board across a gate, and it stops sagging. The question became urgent in the nineteenth century, when railways needed long, light bridges and some of the first ones fell. What makes it hard is that a frame is many bars at once, each pushing or pulling on the others, and a guess about one bar changes all the rest. This page shows, with 18f's three-sides technique, that a triangle cannot change its shape without changing a side, while a four-sided frame can. Then it finds the force in every bar, one joint at a time, from triangles similar to the bars themselves.

Three bars fix a triangle. A frame built from a triangle, adding each new joint with two bars, keeps its shape, with three fewer bars than twice the joints. A bar under tension pulls on each end along its own length: its force is a scaled copy of the bar. At each pin the forces add to (0, 0).

18eng1A Frame of Bars and Pins

A frame is a set of joints, points of the plane, and a set of bars, each joining two joints. A bar keeps its length. A pin lets the bars at a joint turn freely there. A frame keeps its shape if every placement of its joints with the same bar lengths is congruent to the first: the whole frame may be moved, but not bent. Its accepted name is a rigid frame; a frame built this way to carry loads is a truss.

Rigid-triangle technique: a triangle of three bars cannot change its shape without changing a side. If two placements of a triangle of bars have the same three bar lengths, then they are congruent. It is 18f's three-sides technique, carried out on bars.

Four bars in a ring do not keep their shape. Put A = (0, 0) and B = (1, 0), with every bar of length 1. For any p with −1 < p < 1, put D = (p, √(1 − p²)) and C = B + (D − A).

d(A, B) = 1,\ \ d(A, D) = \sqrt{p^2 + 1 - p^2} = 1 @@ distance-laws (18a)
d(B, C) = d(A, D) = 1,\ \ d(D, C) = d(A, B) = 1 @@ C − B = D − A and C − D = B − A: the parallelogram technique (18f)
d(A, C) = \sqrt{(1 + p)^2 + 1 - p^2} = \sqrt{2 + 2p} @@ the diagonal changes as p does
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{every bar keeps its length, and the shape }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{changes}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ at p = 0 a square; near p = ±1 nearly flat

Its accepted name is racking. A gate racks, a bookcase without a back racks, and a barn whose walls have no braces leans more each year. Add one bar from A to C. Its length must stay √2, so 2 + 2p = 2 and p = 0. The square is two triangles now, and it keeps its shape.

18eng2Building Frames That Keep Their Shape

Add-a-joint technique: a frame grown from a triangle, one joint and two bars at a time, keeps its shape. Start from a triangle of bars. Add a new joint, joined by two new bars to two joints already placed, and not in line with them. Repeat. Then the frame cannot move except as a whole, and it has b = 2j − 3 bars for j joints.

\text{the first triangle keeps its shape} @@ rigid-triangle; fix it in place, and only the rest can move
\text{a new joint } X \text{ with bars to } P \text{ and } Q:\ d(X, P) = r,\ d(X, Q) = s @@ the two bar lengths
X \text{ is on two circles with different centres} @@ two-circles-meet (18d): at most two such points, a positive distance apart
\begin{aligned}&\text{a coordinate of } X \text{ takes}\\ &\quad\text{at most two values as the frame moves}\end{aligned} @@ the points are fixed once P and Q are
\text{so it cannot change at all} @@ in-between-value (12a): a continuous coordinate that changed would pass through every value between
\htmlClass{new}{b = 3 + 2(j - 3) = 2j - 3} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction (4c) on the joints added: three bars, then two for each joint past three

The count goes the other way too, as a rough guide. Each joint has two coordinates, so j joints have 2j. Moving the whole frame uses 3 of them: slide across, slide up, turn. Each bar fixes one length. So a frame with fewer than 2j − 3 bars always has a way to move. A frame with 2j − 3 bars may still move, if they are badly placed.

18eng3The Force in a Bar

Two measured laws carry forces into the plane. A force has a size and a direction, and two forces on one pin add as 18a1’s displacements add: their parts across and their parts up add separately. A pin at rest has its forces adding to (0, 0). Their accepted names are the parallelogram of forces and equilibrium. A bar pinned at both ends, and loaded only at its pins, pushes or pulls only along its own length. A pull is a tension, and a push is a compression, written here as a negative tension.

Bar-force technique: the force a bar puts on its end is a scaled copy of the bar itself. Let a bar run from P to Q, with length ℓ and tension T. Then the force it puts on P is (T/ℓ)(Q − P). Its part across is T · (run/ℓ), and its part up is T · (rise/ℓ). So the force, its two parts, and the bar's run and rise make similar triangles, with scale factor T/ℓ.

\text{a pull of size } |T| \text{ along the bar, toward } Q @@ the bar law
u = \tfrac{1}{\ell}(Q - P),\ \ |u| = 1 @@ distance-laws: |tu| = |t| |u| with t = 1/ℓ
\htmlClass{new}{F = T\,u = \tfrac{T}{\ell}(Q - P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the force of size |T| along u; T < 0 pushes P away from Q
\text{the run and rise of } F \text{ are } \tfrac{T}{\ell} \text{ times the bar's} @@ part-of-the-way (18a), or parallel-cut (18f) with t = T/ℓ: similar triangles

18eng4A Roof Frame, One Joint at a Time

Joint technique: at every pin, the pushes and pulls add to nothing. Write it as two equations at each joint, one for the parts across and one for the parts up. Solve joint by joint, starting where only two bars are unknown. Its accepted name is the method of joints.

A hay barn near Lodi has roof frames of two rafters and a tie. The span is 8 m and the rise 2 m: A = (0, 0), B = (8, 0), peak C = (4, 2). Each rafter is √(4² + 2²) = √20 ≈ 4.47 m. Put a load of 10 kN at the peak, from the roof above. The walls push A and B up, with 5 kN each by the balance of the frame.

\text{at } C:\ \ T_{AC}\,\tfrac{(-4, -2)}{\sqrt{20}} + T_{BC}\,\tfrac{(4, -2)}{\sqrt{20}} + (0, -10) = (0, 0) @@ bar-force for each rafter; the load; the joint technique
T_{AC} = T_{BC},\ \ -\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{20}}\,T_{AC} = 10 \Rightarrow T_{AC} = -\tfrac{10\sqrt{20}}{4} \approx -11.2 \text{ kN} @@ the parts across cancel; the parts up carry the load: both rafters in compression
\text{at } A:\ \ T_{AC}\,\tfrac{(4, 2)}{\sqrt{20}} + T_{AB}\,(1, 0) + (0, 5) = (0, 0) @@ the rafter, the tie, the wall
T_{AB} = -\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{20}}\,T_{AC} = 10 \text{ kN} @@ the parts across: the tie pulls, in tension
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\text{tie}}{\text{half the load}} = \tfrac{\text{half the span}}{\text{rise}} = \tfrac{4}{2}} @@ similar-triangles (18f): the force triangle at A is the rafter's run-and-rise triangle, scaled

So the tie pulls with P · s/(2h), for a load P at the peak, half-span s and rise h. A flat roof needs a strong tie: halve the rise, and the tie's pull doubles. Many houses in the Central Valley built since the 1960s have roofs carried by factory-made wooden frames of this kind, joined by toothed steel plates.

18eng5A Bridge of Triangles

A small footbridge truss has joints A = (0, 0), C = (8, 0), E = (16, 0) along the bottom and B = (4, 3), D = (12, 3) along the top, in metres. Its seven bars are AB, BC, CD, DE along the slants, AC, CE along the bottom and BD along the top. Every slant is a 3-4-5 triangle: run 4, rise 3, length 5. A load of 60 kN hangs at C. A holds the bridge by a pin; E rests on a roller that can only push up.

j = 5,\ b = 7 = 2 \cdot 5 - 3 @@ grown from ABC by D (bars to B, C) and E (to C, D): add-a-joint
\text{supports}: 30 \text{ kN up at } A \text{ and at } E @@ the balance of the whole bridge; the load is in the middle
\text{at } A:\ \ T_{AB}\,(\tfrac45, \tfrac35) + T_{AC}\,(1, 0) + (0, 30) = (0, 0) @@ bar-force; the joint technique
T_{AB} = -50,\ \ T_{AC} = 40 @@ up: 3/5 T_AB = −30; across: T_AC = −4/5 T_AB
\text{at } B:\ \ T_{AB}\,(-\tfrac45, -\tfrac35) + T_{BC}\,(\tfrac45, -\tfrac35) + T_{BD}\,(1, 0) = (0, 0) @@ the bars from B to A, to C, to D
T_{BC} = 50,\ \ T_{BD} = -80 @@ up: 30 − 3/5 T_BC = 0; across: 40 + 40 + T_BD = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{slants } \mp 50,\ \text{bottom } +40,\ \text{top } -80 \text{ kN}} @@ D and E by the same steps; C checks: 3/5 (50 + 50) = 60

The top bar is squeezed hardest and the bottom bars pulled, just as a beam's top face is squeezed and its bottom face stretched in 16·eng. A truss is a beam with its middle taken out. In a beam the wood near the middle of the depth barely works. In a truss every bar works at its full push or pull, and the slants carry the load from the bottom to the top.

In Sacramento, the I Street Bridge of 1911 carries trains and cars across the river on steel trusses of this kind, and the lattice arm of a building crane is the same idea stood on end.

Who said it first

Whipple set out to find, for every member of a bridge truss, the strain it must bear, by resolving the weights at each joint along the members that meet there, so that each member could be made just strong enough for its work.

Squire Whipple, A Work on Bridge Building (1847); paraphrase

Simon Stevin's wreath of balls on a slope (1586) led him to the triangle of forces. Herman Haupt's General Theory of Bridge Construction (1851) did for American practice what Whipple began. James Clerk Maxwell gave the counting rule in 1864. In 18·eng.7.4, the engineer of 1880 is Heinrich Manderla, whose prize essay on secondary stresses settled how to compute them; the American preference for pins lasted until about 1900.

Accepted names: truss; chord (the top and bottom bars), web (the slants and uprights); the Warren truss (James Warren and Willoughby Monzani, 1848), the Pratt truss (Thomas and Caleb Pratt, 1844), the Howe truss (William Howe, 1840); a two-force member (a bar loaded only at its pins); statically determinate (b = 2j − 3, with the joint technique enough) and statically indeterminate (more bars than that); infinitesimal flexibility, for 18·eng.7.2; the rough guide that a frame with fewer than 2j − 3 bars can move is Maxwell's rule.
