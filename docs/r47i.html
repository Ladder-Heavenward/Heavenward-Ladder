The Table of a Group

Every representation of a finite group splits into whole ones, and the characters of whole ones are perpendicular (47·cb27). How many whole representations are there, and how big? Let the group act on its own members. That one representation holds every whole representation, each as many times as its dimension, so the squares of the dimensions add to the size of the group. And there are exactly as many whole representations as families of members (47h). This step proves both counts and fills in the full table of the shuffles of three things.

The group acting on functions on itself has character |G| at e and 0 elsewhere; each whole V appears dim V times, so Σ (dim V)² = |G|. Characters take one value on each family, and span every function that does: the number of whole representations is the number of families. For the shuffles of three: dimensions 1, 1, 2, and 1 + 1 + 4 = 6.

The squares count and the family count, with perpendicularity, often fix the whole table without building a single representation; chemists read from such tables how a molecule can vibrate and which lines its light shows.

Accepted names

The group acting on itself is the regular representation; families are conjugacy classes; functions constant on them are class functions; the table is the character table. Plain, sign and flat are the trivial, sign and standard representations of S₃.

Who said it first

Georg Frobenius invented characters in 1896, prompted by a letter from Richard Dedekind; William Burnside and Issai Schur extended the theory by 1905, and Burnside used it in 1904 to prove that groups of size pᵃqᵇ are solvable.
