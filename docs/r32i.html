32i · technique

Convolution and Filters by Hand

Roll two dice at a game night in Bellingham and add them. Seven is the likeliest total, and two and twelve the rarest, and the shape of the chances is a tent. Multiply out two polynomials and the coefficients of the answer come from the same kind of sum: every way of pairing a term of one with a term of the other that lands on a given power. Both are blends, done on lists instead of on functions. This step does blends by hand. Then it reads a blend the way 32d taught, one turning at a time. A smoothing, a tone control, the treble knob on an amplifier: each is a blend, and each simply multiplies every turning by a number of its own. Write down that number for every speed and you know everything the filter does. The last footing builds a filter that runs one reading at a time, the kind a thermometer or a phone uses, and finds its number for each speed by feeding it a single turning.

The coefficients of a product of polynomials are the blend of the coefficient lists, and the chances of a sum of independent counts are the blend of their chances. A filter that blends multiplies each turning by its own number, the weight’s coefficient at that speed: the size is the gain and the turn is the shift. A filter that runs one reading at a time, mixing each new reading with its last output, has, for each speed, the share it takes from each new reading, divided by one minus the share it keeps times a backward turning.

32i1Blending Lists

For two lists a₀, a₁, a₂, … and b₀, b₁, b₂, …, the blend a * b is the list whose kth entry is a₀b_k + a₁b_(k−1) + ⋯ + a_kb₀: every pairing whose places add to k.

List-blend technique: the coefficients of a product of polynomials are the blend of their coefficient lists; so the chances of a sum of two independent whole-number counts are the blend of their chance lists.

\big(\textstyle\sum_j a_jz^j\big)\big(\sum_m b_mz^m\big) = \sum_{j, m} a_jb_mz^{j + m} @@ multiply out term by term (5)
\text{the coefficient of } z^k = \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{k} a_jb_{k-j} @@ gather the pairings with j + m = k
G_{X+Y} = G_XG_Y @@ the sums-multiply technique (30i)
\htmlClass{new}{P(X + Y = k) = \textstyle\sum_j P(X = j)\,P(Y = k - j)} \quad \blacksquare @@ read off the coefficients, by the chances-from-G technique (30i)
  
\text{one die: } \tfrac16(z + z^2 + \cdots + z^6) @@ its chance function
\text{two dice: } \tfrac{1}{36}(z + \cdots + z^6)^2 @@ the list-blend technique
\tfrac{1}{36}(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1) \text{ for totals } 2, \ldots, 12 @@ each total counts the pairings that make it
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{total } 7) = \tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16,\ \ P(\text{total } 2) = \tfrac1{36}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tent

Blend the tent with a third die and the shape rounds off. Blend again and again and it heads to the bell: 32·stat asks why, with turnings.

32i2A Filter Is a Number for Every Speed

A filter that blends a signal with a fixed weight g sends each turning eₙ to cₙ(g)eₙ (32d1). Write H(n) = cₙ(g).

Gain-per-turning technique: a blend multiplies each turning by its own number H(n); the size |H(n)| is the gain at that speed, and the turn of H(n) is the shift the turning suffers.

(e_n * g)(x) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{in(x - t)}g(t)\,dt = e^{inx}\,c_n(g) @@ the blend of one turning
H(n) = |H(n)|\,e^{-i\phi_n} @@ out-and-round form (21·cb20, 26a)
\htmlClass{new}{e_n * g = |H(n)|\,e^{in(x - \phi_n/n)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turning comes out scaled by |H(n)| and slid by φₙ/n
  
H(n) = \tfrac{\sin nh}{nh} \text{ for the average over } [x - h, x + h] @@ 32d1’s example
h = \tfrac\pi3:\ \ H(1) = \tfrac{\sin(\pi/3)}{\pi/3} \approx 0.827,\ \ H(3) = \tfrac{\sin\pi}{\pi} = 0 @@ the window holds a whole wave of speed 3
\htmlClass{new}{q * g = \tfrac4\pi\big(0.827\sin x + 0\cdot\tfrac{\sin 3x}{3} + H(5)\tfrac{\sin 5x}{5} + \cdots\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the square recipe (32h1), each term multiplied

The average’s numbers are all real, so it shifts nothing; it only scales, and it wipes out the speeds whose waves fit the window exactly. The treble knob on an amplifier is a filter whose gain falls for high speeds; a hum filter has a gain of 0 at the mains hum of 60 turns a second.

32i3A Filter That Runs One Reading at a Time

A thermometer on a buoy off Newport reads every minute, xₖ at minute k. Its display shows yₖ = (1 − β)y_(k−1) + β xₖ, for a fixed β in (0, 1): each new display is mostly the old one, with a share β of the new reading.

Stepped-filter technique: the running filter yₖ = (1 − β)y_(k−1) + β xₖ sends the stepped turning e^(iωk) to H(ω)e^(iωk), with H(ω) = β / (1 − (1 − β)e^(−iω)).

y_k = \beta\textstyle\sum_{j \ge 0}(1 - \beta)^j\,x_{k - j} @@ unroll the rule (9·cb4); a blend of the readings with the list β(1 − β)ʲ
x_k = e^{i\omega k}:\ \ y_k = \beta\,e^{i\omega k}\textstyle\sum_{j \ge 0}\big((1 - \beta)e^{-i\omega}\big)^j @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
\textstyle\sum_{j \ge 0}\big((1 - \beta)e^{-i\omega}\big)^j = \tfrac{1}{1 - (1 - \beta)e^{-i\omega}} @@ the geometric sum (15i), of size 1 − β < 1
\htmlClass{new}{y_k = H(\omega)\,e^{i\omega k},\ \ H(\omega) = \tfrac{\beta}{1 - (1 - \beta)e^{-i\omega}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turning comes out a turning

With β = 0.1: H(0) = 1, so a steady temperature shows unchanged. At ω = π/10, a wiggle of period 20 minutes, |H| ≈ 0.32 and the display lags by about 63°. At ω = π, a reading that flips every minute, |H| = 0.1/1.9 ≈ 0.053: the jitter is cut to a twentieth.

The accepted names

The blend of lists is discrete convolution, the list-blend technique the convolution of coefficient sequences (and, for chances, of probability mass functions). H(n), as a function of the speed, is the filter’s frequency response; |H| is the gain, or magnitude response, and the turn its phase response. The running filter is a first-order infinite impulse response filter, or exponential moving average; the window average is a moving average, or boxcar filter. A filter that needs only the past is causal, one that needs the future non-causal, and the shift-free average is zero-phase.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre counted the totals of several dice by multiplying polynomials in 1730. The running average was used by Robert Brown for stock control in the 1950s and is called exponential smoothing. Stephen Butterworth designed the flattest filters in 1930, and the hum filter of the 1930s telephone exchanges is the ancestor of every notch filter.

A proof that is not allowed

“The running filter has gain β at every speed, since each reading enters with weight β.” Not allowed: each reading enters again and again, with weights β(1 − β)ʲ, and the shares add as a geometric sum whose total depends on the speed. At ω = 0 they add to 1, not β.
