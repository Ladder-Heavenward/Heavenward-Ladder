26g · proof

The Rim Fixes the Inside

A function with a complex slope on a disc is fixed inside the disc by its values on the rim alone. Know it round a circle, and you know it at every point inside, by one integral. From that one fact a flood follows. Such a function is a power sum, so it has slopes of every order, and rung 23’s catch, a smooth function that refuses its sum, cannot happen here. A function with a complex slope everywhere that stays bounded must be constant. And the root technique of rung 25 drops out in two lines.

A function with a complex slope on a disc is fixed inside by its values on the rim, and is a power sum there. One that has a slope everywhere and stays bounded is constant. So every polynomial has a root, again.

26g1The Rim Formula

Rim technique: if f has a complex slope on an open disc holding the circle C: |ζ − w| = r and its inside, then for every z with |z − w| < r, f(z) = (1/2πi) ∮_C f(ζ)/(ζ − z) dζ. Here ζ (little zeta) means the point that runs round C.

g(\zeta) = \frac{f(\zeta) - f(z)}{\zeta - z},\ \ g(z) = f'(z) @@ continuous on the disc, with a slope at every ζ but z (26d1)
\oint_C g = 0 \Rightarrow \oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta = f(z)\oint_C \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta-z} @@ the disc-undoing technique, one point excepted (26f2)
\frac{1}{\zeta-z} = \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(z-w)^k}{(\zeta-w)^{k+1}} @@ the geometric sum, with |z − w|/|ζ − w| = |z − w|/r < 1
\text{settles everywhere at once on } C @@ the at-once comparison (23d4): pieces at most (|z − w|/r)ᵏ/r
\oint_C \frac{d\zeta}{\zeta-z} = \sum_k (z-w)^k\oint_C \frac{d\zeta}{(\zeta-w)^{k+1}} = 2\pi i @@ term by term (23e1); circle powers (26e3): only k = 0 survives
\htmlClass{new}{f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta} \quad \blacksquare

26g2Every Such Function Is a Power Sum

Rebuilt-in-the-plane technique: if f has a complex slope on the open disc |z − w| < R, then f(z) = Σ uₖ(z − w)ᵏ there, with uₖ = (1/2πi) ∮ f(ζ)/(ζ − w)ᵏ⁺¹ dζ round any circle |ζ − w| = r < R; and |uₖ| ≤ M/rᵏ, where M is the largest |f| on that circle.

f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint f(\zeta)\sum_k\frac{(z-w)^k}{(\zeta-w)^{k+1}}\,d\zeta @@ the rim technique (26g1); the geometric sum of its proof
= \sum_k (z-w)^k\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-w)^{k+1}}\,d\zeta @@ settles everywhere at once on the circle, since f is bounded there: term by term (23e1)
|u_k| \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{r^{k+1}}\cdot 2\pi r = \frac{M}{r^k} @@ the path fence (26e1)
\htmlClass{new}{f = \textstyle\sum u_k(z-w)^k \text{ on } |z-w| < R} \quad \blacksquare @@ any r < R will do, so the sum holds on the whole disc

So one complex slope gives slopes of every order, by 23e’s smooth-sum technique: uₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(w)/k!. Rung 23 found a real function, e^(−1/x²), smooth and not its sum. In the plane that cannot happen. And the reach of the sum is at least the distance to the nearest point where f fails, which is why 1/(1 + x²) reaches only 1 on the line: in the plane it fails at ±i.

26g3Bounded Everywhere Means Constant

Bounded-constant technique: if f has a complex slope at every z in ℂ and |f(z)| ≤ M for all z, then f is constant. So, again, every polynomial of degree n ≥ 1 has a root.

f = \textstyle\sum u_k z^k \text{ on all of } \mathbb{C} @@ 26g2, with every R
|u_k| \le M/r^k \text{ for every } r > 0 @@ 26g2
k \ge 1:\ u_k = 0 @@ M/rᵏ heads to 0 as r grows
\htmlClass{new}{f = u_0} \quad \blacksquare
  
p \text{ has no root} \Rightarrow 1/p \text{ has a slope everywhere, and } |1/p| \le \max\big(2/(|u_n|R^n),\ \max_{|z|\le R}|1/p|\big) @@ the plane-slope technique (26d1); the far-out technique (25d1); the lowest-size technique (25d2)
1/p \text{ constant, so } p \text{ constant: a contradiction} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bounded-constant technique

And the largest size of f on a closed disc is reached on its rim: by the rim technique at the centre, |f(w)| ≤ the largest |f| on any circle round w. A function with a complex slope cannot have a peak of size inside a disc unless it is flat.

The accepted names

The rim technique is Cauchy’s integral formula. The rebuilt-in-the-plane technique says holomorphic functions are analytic, with Cauchy’s estimates |uₖ| ≤ M/rᵏ. The bounded-constant technique is Liouville’s theorem. The largest size on the rim is the maximum modulus principle; the ladder says largest size, since its word for |z| is size.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave the integral formula in 1831. Joseph Liouville stated the bounded-constant theorem in his lectures of 1847, and Cauchy had proved it in 1844. The two-line proof that every polynomial has a root is the standard one in every course on the subject.
