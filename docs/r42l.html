42l · technique

Discrete Logarithms

Rung seventeen built a function that turns multiplying into adding, the log. A prime clock has one too. Every unit is a power of a full hand, and the count of steps to reach it turns products into sums, on the clock of one less than the prime. On the line the log is easy to compute, by tables or by a few terms of a sum. On a clock nobody knows a fast way. Raising to a power is quick, by squaring; going back, from the hour to the power, seems to need a search, and the best searches known grow far faster than the number of digits. In 1976 two people saw that this one-way street lets strangers agree on a secret while everyone listens. Each picks a power in private, sends the full hand raised to it, and raises what arrives to their own power. Both land on the same hour, and a listener who saw both messages would have to take a log to follow. The step finds logs by hand, shows the best simple search, and shows when the clock gives its logs away.

With a full hand g, every unit is g to one power on the clock of p − 1, its clock log, and logs turn products into sums. Two people send g to secret powers and each raises what arrives to their own: the same hour. Finding a log by a table of small steps and a stride takes about the root of p products, and when p − 1 has only small primes the log splits clock by clock.

42l1The Log on a Clock

Let [g] be a full hand of the clock of a prime p. By 42e every unit [a] is [g]ᵏ for exactly one k from 0 to p − 2. From here on, that k is the clock log of a to the base g, written log_g a. It is the same mark as 17’s log, given a further job: on a clock, with a full hand named as its base, it names a number from 0 to p − 2. On the line it keeps its old meaning.

Clock-log technique: log_g(ab) ≡ log_g a + log_g b (mod p − 1), and log_g(aᵐ) ≡ m log_g a (mod p − 1). For [g]^(j + k) = [g]ʲ[g]ᵏ, and exponents live on the clock of p − 1 (the exponent-clock technique, 42d3).

2^0, \ldots, 2^{11} \equiv 1, 2, 4, 8, 3, 6, 12, 11, 9, 5, 10, 7 \pmod{13} @@ [2] is a full hand (42e4)
\log_2 3 = 4,\ \ \log_2 5 = 9 @@ read off the list
\log_2 15 \equiv 4 + 9 = 13 \equiv 1 \pmod{12} @@ the clock-log technique
\htmlClass{new}{15 \equiv 2 = 2^1 \pmod{13}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the check

The list was built by multiplying by 2 eleven times. To read a log from it, the whole list was needed. For a clock of six hundred digits the list would have about 10⁶⁰⁰ entries.

42l2A Secret Agreed in Public

Two people choose a prime p and a full hand g in public. Each picks a power in private, a and b. The first sends A = gᵃ, the second B = gᵇ, both on the clock of p.

Shared-secret technique: Bᵃ ≡ Aᵇ (mod p), so both reach the hour g^(ab) without either sending it. Both are g^(ab), by power-of-a-power (4g) carried to the clock.

a = 6:\ \ A = 5^6 \equiv 8 \pmod{23} @@ the first sends 8
b = 15:\ \ B = 5^{15} \equiv 19 \pmod{23} @@ the second sends 19
19^6 \equiv 2 \pmod{23} @@ the first raises B to a
8^{15} \equiv 2 \pmod{23} @@ the second raises A to b
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the shared hour is } 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shared-secret technique

A listener sees 23, 5, 8 and 19. To reach 2 the obvious road is a clock log: log₅ 8 = 6. Whether some road avoids the log is not known. Nobody has found one.

42l3A Table of Steps and Strides

Trying k = 0, 1, 2, … finds a log in up to p − 1 products. A table does better. Write m for the least whole number with m² ≥ p − 1. Every k from 0 to p − 2 is mi + j with j below m and i below m, by division with remainder (4j7).

Step-and-stride technique: to find log_g a, list g⁰, …, g^(m − 1), then compute a, a·g^(−m), a·g^(−2m), …; the first of these found in the list, a·g^(−mi) = gʲ, gives log_g a = mi + j, after at most about 2m products.

m = 5:\ \ 5^0, \ldots, 5^4 \equiv 1, 5, 2, 10, 4 @@ 5² = 25 ≥ 22: the small steps
5^5 \equiv 20,\ \ [20]^{-1} = [15] @@ 20 · 15 = 300 = 13 · 23 + 1 (42b2): one stride back
i = 0:\ 8 \text{ not in the list};\ \ i = 1:\ 8 \cdot 15 = 120 \equiv 5 @@ 5 = 5¹ is in the list
8 \cdot 5^{-5} \equiv 5^1 \Rightarrow 8 \equiv 5^{5 + 1} @@ multiply back by 5⁵
\htmlClass{new}{\log_5 8 = 6} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first’s private power, found

About 2√p products, against p. For a clock of six hundred digits, √p has three hundred: still far past any machine. The best methods known for prime clocks are much faster than that, and still slower than any power of the number of digits.

42l4When the Clock Gives Its Logs Away

Logs live on the clock of p − 1. If p − 1 is a product of small prime powers, the remainder technique splits a log into small logs.

Split-log technique: if p − 1 = uv with u and v sharing nothing, then log_g a on the clock of u is the log of a^v to the base g^v, which has order u, and likewise for v; the two readings are put back by the remainder technique. For (g^v)^(x) = (gˣ)^v = a^v exactly when x is the log of a, read on the clock of u, by the power-order technique (42e1).

36 = 4 \cdot 9 @@ p − 1 split into clocks that share nothing
2^9 \equiv 31,\ \ 10^9 \equiv 1:\ \ 31^{x} \equiv 1 \Rightarrow x \equiv 0 \pmod 4 @@ [31] has order 4
2^4 = 16,\ \ 10^4 \equiv 10:\ \ 16^x \equiv 10 @@ [16] has order 9
16^1, \ldots, 16^6 \equiv 16, 34, 26, 9, 33, 10:\ \ x \equiv 6 \pmod 9 @@ at most nine tries
\htmlClass{new}{x \equiv 0 \pmod 4,\ x \equiv 6 \pmod 9 \Rightarrow \log_2 10 = 24} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remainder technique (42c2); 2²⁴ ≡ 10 on the clock of 37

So a clock for secrets must have p − 1 with a large prime factor; the usual choice is p = 2q + 1 with q prime too. Even then the exchange has a weak point that is not arithmetic. A listener who can change messages can stand in the middle, agree one secret with each side, and pass everything on. Each side must sign what it sends, by 42i’s turned keys, so the other knows who sent it.

The accepted names

The clock log is the discrete logarithm, or index, of a to the base g. The shared-secret technique is the Diffie–Hellman key exchange, and the lock of 42l.6.1 is ElGamal encryption (1985). The step-and-stride technique is Shanks’s baby-step giant-step algorithm (1971); the split-log technique is the Pohlig–Hellman algorithm (1978). Primes p = 2q + 1 with q prime are safe primes, and q a Sophie Germain prime. The listener in the middle is a man-in-the-middle attack. Computing g^(ab) from gᵃ and gᵇ is the computational Diffie–Hellman problem. Recording now to read later is “harvest now, decrypt later”.

Who said it first

Gauss tabulated indices in the Disquisitiones (1801). Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published the exchange in “New Directions in Cryptography” (1976), building on Ralph Merkle’s puzzles; Malcolm Williamson had found it at GCHQ in 1974, unpublished until 1997. Taher ElGamal gave the lock and a signature in 1985. Peter Shor’s quantum algorithm for discrete logs and factoring is from 1994.

A proof that is not allowed

“log₅ 8 on the clock of 23 is the real log ln 8 / ln 5 ≈ 1.29, rounded to 1.” Not allowed: the clock log is a count of steps round the clock, and it has nothing to do with the size of 8. 5¹ = 5, not 8; the clock log is 6.
