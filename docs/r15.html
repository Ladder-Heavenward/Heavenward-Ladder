Rung 15 · theory

Measuring what is under a curve

Rung 14 ended with a question it could not answer: given a slope at every input, what was the function? It could only say that any two answers differ by a constant. This rung turns to a question that looks unrelated. Draw the graph of a function that is never negative, over a closed interval. How much room is there under it? For a box with straight sides the answer is the width times the height, and that much is taken as the starting point. For anything curved, no rule from earlier rungs gives a number at all. This rung builds one. Cut the interval into pieces, stand a box on each piece that fits under the graph, and add them: that is too small. Stand a box on each piece that reaches over the graph, and add them: that is too big. Suppose exactly one number sits above every too-small total and below every too-big one. Then that number is the area. In this section we'll teach you what it means to say that area is squeezed between every estimate, and what you can do because you know it.

Counting squares under a curve on graph paper is something you could do as a child. Squeezing the true area between two kinds of estimate is something you can do now. The difference is the distance you have climbed.

Under-estimates come from boxes inside, over-estimates from boxes outside. Cutting finer only tightens both. Every under-estimate sits below every over-estimate. When the gap can be made as small as you like, one number is left between them, and that number is the area.

What it means to say it

The region under a graph is a set of points of the plane: those (x, y) with x in the interval and y between 0 and f(x). Asking for its area is asking for a single number that measures it. Rung 9 gave the plane, and nothing since has said how big a set of points is. So area has to be built, and the building needs a few starting rules that anyone would accept. A box has area width times height. A region that sits inside another has no more area. Two regions that overlap at most along a line have areas that add.

Those rules already pin the number down, in the sense that matters. A heap of boxes that fits inside the region must have no more area than the region. A heap that covers it must have at least as much. So whatever the area is, it is at least every under-estimate and at most every over-estimate. The claim of this rung is that, for every continuous function on a closed interval, these estimates leave room for exactly one number. That number is the area, by definition, and the rules force it.

An estimate is a sum. Each under-estimate is the width of a piece times the least height of the graph on it, added up over the pieces. Each over-estimate uses the greatest height. So area is built from sums, and the techniques of this rung are ways of adding up many things at once.

Where it comes from

About 370 BCE, a Greek mathematician had a way of proving that two magnitudes are equal without ever saying what either one is. Show that neither can be less, by fitting figures inside, and neither can be more, by fitting figures outside. A century later another used it to find the area of the region cut off the graph of y = x² by a straight line: four thirds of a certain figure drawn inside it. His method of discovery treated a region as a heap of lines and weighed them on a balance. It was lost for two thousand years; the manuscript was found under a prayer book in 1906.

In 1635 the heap of lines was made into a method, and its maker was attacked for it. Around 1640 the area under xⁿ was found for every whole n, by cutting the interval into pieces whose widths shrink in a fixed ratio. In 1656 the same results were guessed from patterns in sums. In 1675 one of the inventors of the calculus wrote ∫ for the sum, an S drawn long. In 1823 the area for a continuous function was defined as a limit of sums, and in 1854 for any function for which that limit exists. The version this rung uses, with the least and greatest height on each piece, was given in 1875.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the one before.

First, cut and estimate. A partition of [u, v] is a finite list of cut points from u to v. On each piece, the least and greatest values of f give a box under the graph and a box over it. Their totals are the lower sum and the upper sum for that partition. For one partition, the lower sum is never more than the upper sum.

Second, every under-estimate is below every over-estimate. Add a cut point, and the lower sum can only rise, the upper sum only fall, since the least value on a smaller piece is at least the least on the larger one. Two partitions have a common one, with every cut point of both. So the lower sum of one is at most the lower sum of the common partition. That is at most its upper sum, which is at most the upper sum of the other.

Third, the best estimates. The lower sums are bounded above, by any upper sum, so rung 8's no-holes gives them a least upper bound. The upper sums have a greatest lower bound. The first is at most the second. When they are the same number, f is called integrable, and the number is written with the long S, ∫. It is the area.

Fourth, areas add, scale and split. Lower and upper sums behave well under adding functions, multiplying by a number, and cutting the interval in two, so the areas do too. A function that lies below another has the smaller area.

Fifth, every continuous function on a closed interval is integrable. A continuous function cannot vary much on a short enough piece, and on a closed interval one length of piece works everywhere at once. Cut finer than that length, and the boxes over and the boxes under differ by as little as you like.

What follows

Areas under x, x² and x³ come out of sums of whole numbers, of their squares and of their cubes, and those sums have short formulas. Areas under any power come out of sums whose terms multiply by a fixed number each time. Those sums have a short formula too, which also gives a meaning to some sums that never end. Strip sums (Riemann's), with any height from each piece, land between the lower and upper sums. So they approximate the area as closely as the squeeze does. And the science page reads the pressure of a gas as a function of its volume, and asks for the room under that curve.

What you can do because you know it

You can say exactly what the area under a graph is, and prove that a given number is it. You can compute such areas for polynomials by hand. You can bound an area from above and below with a few boxes, and say how many boxes it takes to get within a given error. You can add up long lists of numbers with Σ by formulas, and prove the formulas. You can add up an endless list whose terms shrink by a fixed factor. And you can prove that some functions have no area at all.

This is the first area you have measured, of very many. Nobody has reached the edge of what can be added up.

And 15·eng asks how hard water pushes on a dam, trapping the push between lower and upper sums of thin strips. It carries a box on the cloud, trapped. And 15·phys asks where a plate balances, cutting it into thin upright strips so that its balance point comes out as one area shared by another.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down for numbers. Three rules about area are taken as the starting point, and the rest is defined from them. No-holes is spent twice. Once it gives the lower sums a least upper bound. Once, in 15f, it shows that a single length of piece serves a continuous function on the whole closed interval. Rung 12's highest-and-lowest technique is used on every piece, so the least and greatest heights are reached. The squeeze proves that the area exists, and in 15d and the techniques it gives the number. It does not, on its own, give a quick way to find it. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that area is squeezed between every estimate, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Eudoxus of Cnidus, about 370 BCE, had the way of proving two magnitudes equal by fitting figures inside and outside; its accepted name is the method of exhaustion. Archimedes, a century later, found the area cut off the parabola: four thirds of a triangle drawn inside it. His Method, which weighed a region as a heap of lines, was found under a prayer book in 1906.

Bonaventura Cavalieri, in 1635, made the heap of lines into a method, and was attacked for it by Paul Guldin. Pierre de Fermat, around 1640, found the area under xⁿ for every whole n by cutting the interval into pieces whose widths shrink in a fixed ratio. John Wallis, in 1656, guessed the same results from patterns in sums. Gottfried Leibniz, in 1675, wrote ∫ for the sum, an S drawn long. Augustin-Louis Cauchy, in 1823, defined the area for a continuous function as a limit of sums, and Bernhard Riemann, in 1854, for any function for which that limit exists. The version of Riemann’s idea this rung uses, with the least and greatest height on each piece, was given by Gaston Darboux in 1875.

Accepted names: strip sums are Riemann sums; the lower and upper sums are Darboux sums; an integrable function is Riemann integrable.
