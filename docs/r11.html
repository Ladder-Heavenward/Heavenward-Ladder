Rung 11 · theory

Drawing without lifting the pen

Rung 10 built the limit by refusing to look at the input itself. Where a function is headed at an input was a fact about that input’s neighbours, never about the input itself, and the game left it out on purpose. That was the right refusal: it let the square of the input less one, over the input less one, head to 2 at 1, where it has no output at all. This rung puts the input back. A function is continuous at an input when where it is headed and where it is are the same: when it lands where it was headed.

You have drawn a line without lifting the pen since you could hold one. Turning that into something a proof can use is the work of this rung, and it is work you are ready for.

Look where a function is headed at an input, and look where it is. When the two are the same, the function is continuous there, and every limit at that input is found by substituting.

What it means to say it

Take three functions, each looked at near the input 1. The first is x². It heads to 1 at 1 (10a's square-limit), and its value at 1 is 1. The second is 9a's H(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1), on every real but 1. It heads to 2 at 1, and it has no value there. The third is H with a stray value put in by a separate case, 3 at 1, as 10a did. It heads to 2, and its value is 3. Rung 10 could not tell these three apart in any way that mattered: the game never looks at 1. This rung looks. The first lands where it was headed. The second has nowhere to land. The third lands somewhere else.

That is the whole definition. A function is continuous at an input w when its limit at w exists and is its value at w: lim_{x→w} f(x) = f(w). Unpacked, it makes three demands at once, and each can fail on its own. There must be a value, f(w). There must be a limit. And they must be the same. A hole fails the first, a jump between two sides fails the second, a stray value fails the third.

Read in the game, the definition becomes simpler than the limit, not harder. The target is now the value itself, and the input w no longer needs to be excused, because at w the output is exactly on target. So the definition can be played with nothing left out. For every gap around f(w) there is a nearness around w where every input, w included, has its output in the gap.

The title uses a word the ladder has not used before. A curve is the graph of a function on an interval: its set of pairs, 9e's picture of two orders. No shape is meant, and nothing about a curve is claimed that is not a claim about pairs of reals. 11a gives the word its line. “Where a curve is headed is where it is” then says exactly the definition: at each input, the limit is the value.

Where it comes from

For most of the history of the calculus, “continuous” meant something like “given by one formula”, or “drawn without lifting the pen”. Neither survives inspection. One formula can hide a break, as |x|/x does at 0; two formulas can meet without one, as 11e shows. The modern idea arrived in two short passages a few years apart, both early in the century before last.

A priest who taught religion at the university in Prague wrote a short paper in 1817. Its purpose was to prove, without any appeal to pictures, a fact the ladder reaches on rung 12. To do it he first had to say what continuity is. He said it in terms of a difference that can be made smaller than any given amount. Hardly anyone read it for half a century.

A lecturer at the École Polytechnique in Paris put almost the same idea into his course of analysis in 1821, the same course that gave rung 10 its limits. His words were about small increases, and they sound vague until you read them as the game. In his words, a function stays continuous between two bounds when, between them, an infinitely small increase of the input always produces an infinitely small increase of the function itself.

Read “infinitely small” as “as small as you like”, and the lecturer's sentence is the definition of this rung. The challenger names a gap around the value, and you answer with a nearness around the input.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things. First, the definition itself. A function is continuous at an input when it lands where it was headed: its limit there is its value. The definition has forms for one side, for a set of inputs, and for an interval whose ends belong to it.

Second, sums and products. If two functions are continuous at an input, so are their sum and their product. The proof is rung 10's: the limit techniques, with the values standing where the limits stood. The quotient comes too, provided the bottom's value at the input is not 0.

Third, continuous after continuous. Do one continuous function, then another, and the result is continuous. The proof is a chain of three gaps. The challenger names a gap for the final output. The outer function says how near its own input must be: a gap in the middle. The inner function, challenged with that middle gap, says how near x must be. The middle gap needs a name, and gets a third Greek letter, introduced on 11c with its line.

Fourth, every polynomial is continuous, at every real. Two bricks and two joins build it: a constant and x itself are continuous, and sums and products keep continuity. The proof runs by induction on the degree, writing a polynomial as its constant term plus x times a polynomial of degree one less.

Fifth, a place where a function's formula changes need not be a break. |x| is −x on the left of 0 and x on the right. The two pieces arrive at 0 at the same height, 0, and |x| is continuous there. Yet on the left each step across lowers the output, and on the right each step raises it. 11e names such a place a joint: the left and right pieces arrive at the same height, but need not climb at the same rate.

What follows

The quotient principle settles a question rung 9 raised. 1/x is continuous at every input it has, so it is continuous wherever it is defined. 0 is not one of its inputs, and continuity is never asked at a real that is not an input. Words matter here, and the rung is careful with them: 1/x is continuous, and it has a break at 0, of the endless kind that 11f names.

Continuity can also be tested by sequences. If a function is continuous at w, then every sequence of its inputs heading to w is carried to a sequence of outputs heading to the value at w. That proof is short and costs nothing. The converse is true too, and costs something real. To prove it, one must pick a bad input from each of endlessly many shrinking windows at once, with no rule for which. Making those picks is a move cited to rung 6's choosing axiom, and the proof also cites rung 7's either-way principle. 11e says exactly where each one enters, and why the either-way principle alone would not be enough.

What you can do because you know it

You can substitute. For a continuous function, the limit at an input is the value there. So the hardest limits of rung 10 become one line wherever the function is continuous, and you know exactly when that line is licensed. You can build continuous functions from smaller ones, and know the result is continuous without playing a single round of the game. You can carry a limit inside a continuous function: if the inside heads to M and the outside is continuous at M, the whole heads to the outside's value at M.

And you can find the breaks. 11f is the field guide. For a function built from formulas, the techniques of 11b to 11e leave only a few places where a break can hide: where a bottom is 0, where a formula changes, at the edge of a root's domain. At each, three numbers decide the matter: the limit from the left, the limit from the right, and the value. The kind of break, removable, a jump, or endless, says what can be done about it. A removable break can be mended by choosing one value. A jump or an endless break cannot.

Then 11·chem heats a pan of ice. Its temperature, as a function of the heat put in, is continuous. It is made of pieces that climb at different rates and meet at joints. At the melting and the boiling the temperature stops climbing, while the heat goes into changing ice to water and water to steam. Read the same measurements the other way, heat held against temperature, and the function jumps at the melting point and the boiling point, by amounts every chemist knows. One experiment, the two kinds of place this rung is about.

That is a first step onto the continuous. You are just getting started; so is everyone else who works there.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down for the definition or for its first four principles: everything stands on rung 10's limits, rung 8's line and rung 9's functions. One split, “the input is w or it is not”, is a comparison of reals. It is licensed by rung 8's three-way-order on ℝ, and so by rung 7's either-way principle. The converse of the sequence test is the one place the rung leans on the choosing axiom, and the page that proves it says so on the line where it happens. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that a function lands where it was headed, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

The priest in Prague was Bernard Bolzano, and his paper of 1817 proved what is now called the intermediate value theorem. The lecturer in Paris was Augustin-Louis Cauchy, in his Cours d’analyse (1821).

Bolzano says that a function varies continuously at a value of its input when the difference between its output there and its output at a nearby input can be made smaller than any given quantity, provided the nearby input is taken close enough.

Bolzano, Rein analytischer Beweis des Lehrsatzes … (Prague, 1817), preface

Cauchy says that a function stays continuous between two bounds when, between them, an infinitely small increase of the input always produces an infinitely small increase of the function itself.

Cauchy, Cours d'analyse de l'École royale polytechnique (1821), ch. II, §2
