17c · proof

ln(xy) = ln x + ln y

This is the step the rung is named for. The area under one over the input, from one to a product, is the area from one to the first factor plus the area from one to the second. The proof takes three lines, from 17b's slope and rung 14's rule that same slopes mean the same shape lifted. Quotients and powers follow, and with them every log technique that a table of logs was ever used for.

Hold the second factor fixed. The logarithm of the product and the logarithm of the first factor have the same slope, so they differ by a constant, and setting the first factor to one shows the constant is the logarithm of the second. Then the logarithm of a reciprocal is the negative, the logarithm of a quotient is a difference, and the logarithm of a fractional power is that power times the logarithm.

17c1Products

Log-of-a-product technique: for all x, y > 0, ln(xy) = ln x + ln y.

\text{fix } y \gt 0;\ \ g(x) = \ln(xy) \text{ for } x \gt 0 @@ xy > 0, so g has a value
g'(x) = \tfrac{1}{xy}\cdot y = \tfrac1x = \ln'(x) @@ chain for slopes (13e), the inside xy has slope y; slope-of-log (17b)
g(x) = \ln x + C \text{ on } (0, \infty) @@ same-slopes (14e): same slopes on an interval, same shape lifted
x = 1:\ \ \ln y = 0 + C @@ ln 1 = 0 (17a)
\htmlClass{new}{\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y} \quad \blacksquare @@ C = ln y, for each y

The survey gave the same fact by a picture. The strip from y to xy is y times as wide as the strip from 1 to x. At matching points it is y times lower. So the two have the same area. The proof above does not use the picture. The picture in this step shows it.

17c2Quotients and powers

Log-of-a-quotient technique: ln(1/y) = −ln y and ln(x/y) = ln x − ln y, for x, y > 0.

0 = \ln 1 = \ln\big(y \cdot \tfrac1y\big) = \ln y + \ln\tfrac1y @@ log-of-a-product with x = 1/y
\ln\tfrac1y = -\ln y @@ subtraction (5b): take ln y from both sides
\htmlClass{new}{\ln\tfrac{x}{y} = \ln x + \ln\tfrac1y = \ln x - \ln y} \quad \blacksquare @@ log-of-a-product with 1/y in place of y

Log-of-a-power technique: for x > 0 and every rational r, ln(xʳ) = r ln x.

h(x) = \ln(x^r),\ \ x \gt 0 @@ xʳ > 0 for rational r (8i, fraction-exponent)
h'(x) = \tfrac{1}{x^r}\cdot r x^{r-1} = \tfrac{r}{x} @@ chain for slopes; fraction-power for slopes (13h) gives the inside's slope
(r \ln x)' = \tfrac{r}{x} @@ slope-of-log, a constant times
h(x) = r\ln x + C;\ \ x = 1 \Rightarrow C = 0 @@ same-slopes; 1ʳ = 1 and ln 1 = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\ln(x^r) = r \ln x} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every rational r

The London professor of the survey's visit of 1615 wrote that he proposed a change: the logarithm of 1 should be 0, and that of ten a power of ten. The maker said he had long had the same thought, but had been too ill to redo the tables. Logs scaled so that the log of ten is 1 have the accepted name common logarithms.

Who said it first

Briggs wrote that, on his visit to Napier, he proposed that the logarithm of 1 be 0 and that of ten be a power of ten, and that Napier said he had long had the same thought, but had been too ill to redo the tables.

Henry Briggs, Arithmetica logarithmica (1624), preface; paraphrase

The professor is Henry Briggs, and the first table-maker John Napier. Logs to base ten are still called Briggsian, or common, logarithms.

Accepted names

The log techniques (log-of-a-product, log-of-a-quotient, log-of-a-power) are the laws of logarithms. Logs with the log of ten equal to 1 are the common logarithms.
