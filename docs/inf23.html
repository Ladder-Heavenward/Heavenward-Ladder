What Does a Failed Division Leave Behind?

What does a failed division leave behind? Rung 47 found that over a ring, tensoring can lose things: a row of maps that was exact stopped being exact at its start. The loss is not noise. It is a new object, and it can be computed. Rows of maps in which each map’s reach is the next map’s loss are the tool; the snake is a map that appears between two such rows, found by chasing members; tensoring a clock with a clock loses exactly a clock of their greatest common divisor; and the ways to stack one clock on another can be counted, two for the clock of 2 on itself. One question about stacking, asked for endless groups, cannot be settled by the axioms of sets, as the size of the line could not. Since 2020 a new foundation for doing this with spaces has been built, and a hard result in it was checked by machine.

Exact: reach of each map = loss of the next. 0 → A → B → C → 0 exact gives |B| = |A|·|C|. Snake: two exact rows with maps down give a map from the loss of the right map to the leftover of the left map. ℤ/m ⊗ ℤ/n = ℤ/gcd(m, n), and the part lost when tensoring 0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ/m → 0 with ℤ/n is also ℤ/gcd(m, n). Stacks of ℤ/2 on ℤ/2: ℤ/4 (with a carry) and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2 (without). Whether every A whose stacks under ℤ all split has a basis is not settled by the axioms (1974).

inf23.1Exact Rows

47h3 called a row of maps A → B → C exact at B when what the first reaches is exactly what the second loses. A row 0 → A → B → C → 0, exact at every place, says three things: A goes into B one-to-one, B reaches all of C, and what is sent to 0 in C is exactly the copy of A. Take groups that add, and the clock of 4 between two clocks of 2.

Short-row technique: if 0 → A → B → C → 0 is exact and the groups are finite, then |B| = |A| · |C|.

\mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}/4,\ \ 1 \mapsto 2 @@ doubling: one-to-one, reaching {0, 2}
\mathbb{Z}/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2,\ \ x \mapsto x \bmod 2 @@ reaches all of ℤ/2; loses {0, 2}
\text{reach of the first} = \{0, 2\} = \text{loss of the second} @@ exact in the middle
\htmlClass{new}{|B| = 4 = 2 \cdot 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ B splits into classes of A, one class for each member of C; <a href="r27c.html#eba55b">27c2</a>’s count for spaces

inf23.2The Snake

Take two exact rows 0 → A → B → C → 0 and 0 → A′ → B′ → C′ → 0, and maps α, β, γ down from each place to the one below, so that going across then down is going down then across. Some members of C are sent to 0 by γ; some members of A′ are not reached by α.

Snake technique: there is a map δ from what γ loses to what α leaves unreached in A′: lift c to b in B, push b down by β, and the result comes from some a′ in A′; δ(c) is the class of a′.

c \in \mathrm{loss}(\gamma),\ \ b \mapsto c @@ B reaches all of C
\beta(b) \mapsto \gamma(c) = 0 \text{ in } C' \Rightarrow \beta(b) \text{ comes from one } a' \in A' @@ exactness of the lower row at B′
b + \text{(image of } a \in A) \text{ gives } a' + \alpha(a) @@ another lift of c differs by something from A
\htmlClass{new}{\delta(c) = a' \text{ mod the reach of } \alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ the choice of lift washes out

inf23.3What Tensoring Loses

47h3 tensored 0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ/2 → 0 with ℤ/2 and watched the first map, once one-to-one, lose everything. Do this with any two clocks. Tensor 0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ/m → 0, the first map multiplying by m, with ℤ/n. The ℤ’s become ℤ/n, and multiplying by m on ℤ/n is no longer one-to-one.

Clock-loss technique: ℤ/m ⊗ ℤ/n is a clock of g = gcd(m, n), and the part that multiplying by m loses on ℤ/n is also a clock of g.

\mathbb{Z}/6 \xrightarrow{\times 4} \mathbb{Z}/6 \to \mathbb{Z}/4 \otimes \mathbb{Z}/6 \to 0 @@ tensoring keeps the end of the row exact
\text{reach of } \times 4 = \{0, 4, 2\} \Rightarrow \mathbb{Z}/4 \otimes \mathbb{Z}/6 = \mathbb{Z}/6 / \{0, 2, 4\} = \mathbb{Z}/2 @@ what is left over
\text{loss of } \times 4 = \{x : 4x \equiv 0 \bmod 6\} = \{0, 3\} @@ the new object at the start
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both are clocks of } 2 = \gcd(4, 6)} \quad \blacksquare @@ <a href="r04j.html#7403ee">4j11</a>’s greatest common divisor

For coprime clocks, gcd 1, both are nothing: 47h3’s ℤ/2 ⊗ ℤ/3 = 0, and nothing is lost either. The lost part has a name of its own and turns up wherever tensoring is used; it measures how far a row of maps is from staying exact.

inf23.4Stacking One Clock on Another

A stack of C on A is an exact row 0 → A → E → C → 0: E holds a copy of A, and its classes are C. Two stacks are the same when a matching of the middles fits both rows. The plain stack is E = A × C; a stack splits when C can be sent back into E, undoing the last map.

Two-stacks technique: there are exactly two stacks of ℤ/2 on ℤ/2: the clock of 4, which does not split, and ℤ/2 × ℤ/2, which does.

E = \{(a, b)\},\ \ (a, b) + (a', b') = (a + a' + c(b, b'),\ b + b') @@ a list of two bits; c is a carry, 0 or 1
c(1, 1) = 1:\ (0, 1) + (0, 1) = (1, 0) \ne 0 \Rightarrow E = \mathbb{Z}/4 @@ the carry of adding in base 2
c = 0:\ \text{every member added to itself is } 0 \Rightarrow E = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2 @@ no carry
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two stacks; } \mathbb{Z}/4 \text{ does not split}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its only member of order 2 is 2, already in the copy of A

Every group of 4 members is one of the two, so there are no more. The ways to stack, counted this way, form a group of their own, here with two members; it is the second object a failed splitting leaves behind.

inf23.5A Question the Axioms Leave Open

Turn to endless groups. Call A splitting when every stack of A on ℤ splits. A group with a basis, a copy of ℤ for each of its members of a list, is splitting. Is every splitting group one with a basis? A clock is not even a candidate.

No-split technique: the stack 0 → ℤ → ℤ → ℤ/2 → 0, doubling then taking the remainder, does not split; so ℤ/2 is not splitting.

s : \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z},\ \ s(1) = k @@ suppose a map back
2k = s(1 + 1) = s(0) = 0 \Rightarrow k = 0 @@ s keeps adding
s(1) = 0 \mapsto 0 \ne 1 @@ the remainder of 0 is not 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no map back: the stack does not split}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ⊥

For groups without such members of finite order, the question stood until 1974, when it was shown that the axioms of sets neither prove nor refute that every splitting group has a basis: by 35f3’s reasoning, one world of the axioms says yes and another says no. Within the new foundation of the next section, one natural reading of the question is answered yes from the axioms alone, and a 2023 paper found another reading left open again.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1974 a logician showed that whether every endless group whose stacks on ℤ all split must have a basis is settled neither way by the axioms of sets, in one of the best-known techniques of its kind outside logic.

In December 2020 a mathematician posed a challenge: check by machine a hard result of the new theory of spaces that he and a colleague had built, a technique on which much of that theory rested. A team finished the check in July 2022.

In the new theory one natural reading of the 1974 question was answered yes from the axioms alone by its two founders; in 2023 three mathematicians reported this and showed that another reading, for a lighter kind of space, is again left open by the axioms.

The accepted names

An exact row 0 → A → B → C → 0 is a short exact sequence; what a map loses is its kernel, what it leaves unreached its cokernel. The snake technique is the snake lemma, δ the connecting homomorphism, and chasing members is diagram chasing. The part lost by tensoring is Tor, Tor(ℤ/m, ℤ/n) = ℤ/gcd(m, n). A stack is an extension; the group of extensions is Ext, Ext(ℤ/2, ℤ/2) = ℤ/2; the carry is a 2-cocycle. A splitting group is a Whitehead group, a group with a basis is free abelian, and the 1974 question is Whitehead’s problem. The new theory of spaces is condensed mathematics, the machine check the Liquid Tensor Experiment, done in the proof checker Lean. The subject of the page is homological algebra.
