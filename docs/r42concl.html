42·∎ · conclusion

Arithmetic works on a clock

Whole numbers taken by their remainders keep every rule of plus and times. An hour undoes exactly when it shares nothing with the clock, so prime clocks are fields. Clocks that share nothing read together as one. Powers cycle within the count of units, a prime clock has a full hand, squares obey a swap law, and the primes are endless and thin.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on r42g is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 42 asked what is left of arithmetic when only remainders count. On the clock of n, sums and products carry over and obey every rule of plus and times; an hour can be undone exactly when it shares nothing with n, so a prime clock is a field; clocks of sizes that share nothing read together are one clock; every unit to the power of the count of units is 1, and a prime clock has a hand that reaches every unit; squares on prime clocks obey the square-swap technique; and the primes are endless, with Σ 1/p passing every bound and ζ a product over them.

42a built the clock and carried the rules over. 42b found the undo and the field. 42c read several clocks at once. 42d made powers cycle, and 42e found the full hand. 42f sorted the squares and proved the swap. 42g counted the primes. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

Let n ≥ 1. For integers m, b, m ≡ b (mod n) means n ∣ m − b; it is an equivalence, ℤ/n has exactly the n classes [0], …, [n − 1], and [m] + [b] = [m + b], [m][b] = [mb] are well defined and obey every rule of plus and times that holds in ℤ. The class [a] has an inverse exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1, the inverse is unique, the units form a group, and ℤ/n is a field exactly when n is prime. If gcd(m, n) = 1, sending the hour of x on the clock of mn to its hours on the clocks of m and n is a matching of ℤ/mn with ℤ/m × ℤ/n that keeps sums and products.

If gcd(a, n) = 1 and c(n) = n ∏(1 − 1/p) over the primes p ∣ n, then a^c(n) ≡ 1 (mod n); for p prime, (ℤ/p)ˣ has an element of order p − 1, and exactly c(p − 1) of them. For an odd prime p and p ∤ a, a^((p − 1)/2) ≡ (a ∕ p), and for different odd primes p and q, (p ∕ q)(q ∕ p) = (−1)^(((p − 1)/2)((q − 1)/2)). For x ≥ 3, Σ_(p ≤ x) 1/p ≥ ln ln x − 1, and for s > 1, ζ(s) = ∏_p 1/(1 − p^(−s)).

So the rung’s claim holds: arithmetic works on a clock, with every rule of plus and times and with laws of its own about undoing, powers, squares and primes. Held in trust, and not part of the claim: that π(x) divided by x/ln x settles on 1, and that each unit hour gets its fair share of primes (42g).
