42·∎ · conclusion

Counting Alone Says How Things Connect

The links at the dots add to twice the links. Trees have n − 1 links and number nⁿ⁻²; tours of every link need no odd dots or two; flat graphs have V − E + F = 2 and five colours. Matchings, flows, best plans and games are settled by counts that meet: neighbours, cuts and prices. Large enough disorder holds order, and an average proves some disorder of a given size has none.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 42j is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 42 asked what counting alone can say about things joined in pairs. The links at the dots add to twice the links, and from that and counts like it come the shape of trees, the tours of every link, the limits of flat drawings, the matchings, the flows, the best plans and the values of games; and counting forces order in every large enough disorder, while an average proves some disorder of a given size has none. The price is that a count often says what exists without saying how to find it.

42a counted the ends of links and found two-sided graphs. 42b counted the links and the kinds of trees. 42c found when a tour of every link exists, and a round of every dot. 42d counted dots, links and faces of flat drawings and coloured them in five. 42e matched by counting neighbours, 42f carried flows across cuts, 42g met best plans with prices, and 42h gave every game a value. 42i crowded boxes and counted by overlaps, and 42j found order that must appear. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

In every finite graph the links at the dots add to twice the number of links, and the graph is two-sided exactly when it has no cycle of odd length. A tree on n dots has n − 1 links, any two of one piece, no cycle and n − 1 links give the third, every graph in one piece has a spanning tree, and there are nⁿ⁻² trees on n named dots. A graph in one piece has a tour of every link exactly when no dot or exactly two dots have an odd count, and one with n ≥ 3 dots, each linked to at least n/2, has a round of every dot. A flat drawing in one piece has V − E + F = 2; a flat graph with V ≥ 3 has at most 3V − 6 links, a dot with at most five, and five colours, and K₅ and K₃,₃ are not flat.

On a two-sided graph with finite side X, some matching covers X exactly when |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every S ⊆ X, and the most links in a matching equals the fewest dots in a cover. In a network with finite capacities the largest flow equals the smallest cut, and with whole capacities it can be taken whole. A linear program with an allowed plan, whose twin has covering prices, has a best plan whose value equals the least price total, and every finite game of gain against loss has a value. More than n · k things in n boxes put more than k in some box; counting by overlaps gives the derangements Dₙ, the whole number nearest n!/e. For all s, t ≥ 2, R(s, t) exists and R(s, t) ≤ R(s − 1, t) + R(s, t − 1), with R(3, 3) = 6; a graph on n dots with no triangle has at most n²/4 links; and if 2^(k/2) ≥ n, with k ≥ 3, some colouring of Kₙ has no one-colour group of k.

So the rung’s claim holds: counting the links at the dots, the dots, links and faces, the neighbours of each group, the room across each cut and the ways things fall into boxes settles what paths, tours, drawings, colourings, matchings, flows, plans and orderly groups exist. Held in trust: that a closed curve drawn without crossing itself splits the sheet in two, on 42d.
