22d · proof

A sum is where its partial sums settle

You cannot add endlessly many numbers one at a time; you would never finish. What you can do is add the first few, then the first few more, and watch the running total. If the running totals settle, the place they settle is the total of the endless sum. That one sentence turns every question about endless sums into a question about lists, and the last three steps answered those. This step draws the first two lessons from it. The pieces you add must shrink toward nothing, or the total cannot settle. And shrinking is not enough: the pieces one, a half, a third, a quarter and so on shrink toward nothing, yet their running total climbs past any ceiling you name.

An endless sum has a total when its running totals settle, and the total is where they settle. The pieces must shrink toward nothing. But pieces that shrink can still add up to more than any number you name.

22d1Running totals

Rung 15 made the definition already. For a sequence u₁, u₂, u₃, …, the running totals are sₙ = u₁ + u₂ + ⋯ + uₙ. The endless sum Σ uₙ has a total S when sₙ → S, and then Σ uₙ = S.

From here on, the endless sum Σ uₙ settles when its running totals settle, and wanders when they wander. Accepted names: the series converges or diverges; sₙ is its n-th partial sum.

Partial-sum technique: every question about an endless sum is a question about the sequence of its running totals. So 22a, 22b and 22c apply at once. A sum of terms that are never negative has climbing running totals. By the climb technique it settles exactly when its running totals have a ceiling.

This is where 15i's geometric sums came from: for |r| < 1, the running totals of 1 + r + r² + ⋯ are (1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r), and they settle at 1/(1 − r).

22d2The pieces must shrink

Shrink technique: if Σ uₙ settles, then uₙ → 0. The piece uₙ is the difference of two running totals, and both head to the same total.

u_n = s_n - s_{n-1} @@ running totals differ by one piece
s_n \to S,\ \ s_{n-1} \to S @@ the same sequence, one place on
\htmlClass{new}{u_n \to S - S = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ limits of differences (10, read on sequences)

Read it the other way round and it becomes a test. If the pieces do not head to 0, the sum wanders. 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + ⋯ has pieces ±1, so it has no total. Its running totals are 1, 0, 1, 0, …, and 22b1 shows two threads settling apart.

22d3Shrinking is not enough

Grouping technique: 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ¼ + ⋯ passes every ceiling, although its pieces head to 0. Group the pieces in blocks that double in length. Each block adds at least a half.

\tfrac13 + \tfrac14 \ge \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 @@ the block of 2: each piece at least the last
\tfrac15 + \cdots + \tfrac18 \ge 4 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac12 @@ the block of 4
\tfrac{1}{2^{k-1}+1} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{2^k} \ge 2^{k-1} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^k} = \tfrac12 @@ the block of 2ᵏ⁻¹, for every k ≥ 1
s_{2^k} \ge 1 + \tfrac{k}{2} @@ induction (4c) over the blocks
\htmlClass{new}{s_n \text{ passes every ceiling: } \sum \tfrac1n \text{ wanders}} \quad \blacksquare @@ counting passes any real (8): pick k > 2β

The sum wanders, but slowly. To pass 10 it needs more than twelve thousand pieces. A computer adding a million pieces a second would watch it creep past 30 only after weeks. Watching cannot tell you it wanders. The grouping proves it.

22d4Sums that add, and tails

Sum-of-sums technique: if Σ uₙ = A and Σ vₙ = B, then Σ (uₙ + vₙ) = A + B and Σ c·uₙ = c·A. Running totals add and scale, and limits of sequences do too.

Tail technique: dropping or changing finitely many pieces never changes whether a sum settles. It only shifts the total by a fixed amount. What decides settling is the tail: the pieces from some place on.

Crowd technique for sums: Σ uₙ settles exactly when, for every ε > 0, every block of pieces far enough out adds to less than ε in size. That is 22c2 for the running totals, since a block uₘ₊₁ + ⋯ + uₙ is sₙ − sₘ. It is the form used in the next three steps.

s_n - s_m = u_{m+1} + \cdots + u_n @@ a block of pieces
\text{settles} \Leftrightarrow (s_n) \text{ crowds} @@ 22c1 and 22c2
\htmlClass{new}{\Leftrightarrow\ \forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall n > m \ge N:\ |u_{m+1} + \cdots + u_n| < \varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ ﹀ “crowds” (22c2)

The accepted names

An endless sum is a series; its running totals are partial sums. The shrink technique read as a test is the n-th term test (or divergence test). 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ is the harmonic series, and the grouping technique is the Cauchy condensation idea in its first form. The crowd technique for sums is the Cauchy criterion for series.

Who said it first

Nicole Oresme proved the harmonic series wanders by the doubling blocks around 1350, three centuries before calculus. Pietro Mengoli (1650) and Johann and Jacob Bernoulli (1689) proved it again by other routes. Cauchy (1821) made the running totals the definition of an endless sum. In 22d.4.5, the runner is Zeno of Elea’s (the dichotomy, about 450 BC), and the answer about endless halves that are only possible is Aristotle’s (Physics, book 6). The modern reply by sums was argued by Bertrand Russell and Adolf Grünbaum; Max Black (1951) and James Thomson (1954) answered that a total does not show an endless task completed.
