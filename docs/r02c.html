Claims Built in Layers

2b gave every and some, and rung 1 gave not, and, or and if. This step puts them together. A statement with a gap can be joined, denied and bound as often as you like, and the result is still one statement. The work is to build such a statement in layers, to say plainly what each sign reaches, and to take it apart in a proof one layer at a time.

Build from the inside out; take apart from the outside in. The last mark put on is the first one used.

2c1Joining Statements With a Gap

Rung 1’s joining words work on statements with a gap as well as on whole statements. Let C(x) be “x is a cat”, G(x) “x is grey” and A(x) “x is asleep”. Then C(x) ∧ G(x) ∧ ¬A(x) is “x is a cat, and grey, and not asleep”. It still has one gap, marked by x in three places, and one name fills all three: put t in, and it says t is a cat, grey, and not asleep.

So a joined statement is a statement with a gap in 0a4’s sense, and every and some bind it as 2b1 bound P(x). ∃x (C(x) ∧ G(x) ∧ ¬A(x)) says some grey cat is not asleep. ∀x ((C(x) ∧ G(x)) ⇒ A(x)) says every grey cat is asleep.

2c2The Things a Gap May Take

The things a gap may take, all together, are called the domain. Every and some speak of the domain and of nothing outside it. Let B(x) be “x barks”. ∀x B(x), “everything barks”, is not so when the domain is the animals on a farm. When the domain is the farm’s dogs, it may well be so.

So a statement with every or some is not settled until its domain is. Say the domain first, as the sceptic’s game in 2b13 listed its five people before anyone picked.

There are two ways to speak only of cats. Make the domain the cats, and write ∀x A(x). Or keep a wide domain and say “cat” inside, as 2b10 did: ∀x (C(x) ⇒ A(x)). Of cats, the two say the same. The second is the one to use when one statement must speak of cats and dogs at once.

A domain is a gathering of things. 2d gives gatherings their own marks, as sets; until then, the domain is said in words.

2c3Built in Layers

A statement of many parts is built one mark at a time, and the last mark put on is its main mark. Build “every cat is grey or asleep”. Start from C(x), G(x) and A(x). Join the last two with or: G(x) ∨ A(x). Put the if in front: C(x) ⇒ (G(x) ∨ A(x)). Bind the gap: ∀x (C(x) ⇒ (G(x) ∨ A(x))). The ∀ went on last, so it is the main mark.

The main mark says what kind of statement the whole is. This one is an every, whatever lies inside it. Inside the every the main mark is ⇒, and inside the if’s result it is ∨. Brackets, as in 1b2, show which mark sits outside which.

2c4What a Sign Reaches

A ∀ or an ∃ reaches only as far as its brackets. In ∀x (C(x) ⇒ A(x)) the ∀ grabs the x in both places. In ∀x C(x) ⇒ A(x) it reaches only C(x), and the x in A(x) is a gap no sign has grabbed. That is not a statement about every cat. It is an if with a gap still open, waiting for a name.

Reach changes the meaning even when every gap is grabbed. Let D(x) be “x is a dog”. ∃x C(x) ∧ ∃x D(x) says there is a cat and there is a dog. Its two x’s are grabbed by different signs, and they may be different things. ∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x)) says one thing is both a cat and a dog. The first is so on most farms; the second on none.

When every gap is grabbed by a sign or filled by a name, the statement says something that is so or not so. While a gap is open, it does not.

2c5Saying It in Marks

To put a sentence into marks, find its main mark first, then work inward. “Every grey cat is asleep” is an every, of the kind grey cat: ∀x ((C(x) ∧ G(x)) ⇒ A(x)). “No cat is a dog” is a not in front of a some, ¬∃x (C(x) ∧ D(x)), or an every with a not inside, ∀x (C(x) ⇒ ¬D(x)). 2b15 showed what cannot stand beside each; 3a’s boxes take you from either one to the other.

Let U(x) be “x purrs”. “Only cats purr” runs the other way from how it sounds: whatever purrs is a cat, ∀x (U(x) ⇒ C(x)). “Some cat is not asleep” puts the not inside, on the asleep alone: ∃x (C(x) ∧ ¬A(x)).

English hides the main mark, and the marks show it. When a sentence can be read two ways, as 2b14’s “every student read a book” can, write both readings in marks, and the difference is in plain sight.

2c6A Proof Through the Layers

Use a given by its main mark first. Make a goal by its main mark last. Each mark has its techs: 2b’s for every and some, 1c’s for and and or, 1d’s for if. Take the givens apart from the outside in, and build the goal from the inside out.

Let M(x) be “x is on the mat”. Given: every cat is grey and asleep, ∀x (C(x) ⇒ (G(x) ∧ A(x))); and some cat is on the mat, ∃x (C(x) ∧ M(x)). Goal: something on the mat is asleep, ∃x (M(x) ∧ A(x)).

Use the some: call such a thing b, a fresh name, with C(b) ∧ M(b) said of it. Keep left: C(b). Keep right: M(b). Use the every with b: C(b) ⇒ (G(b) ∧ A(b)). Carry the if through with C(b): G(b) ∧ A(b). Keep right: A(b). Join: M(b) ∧ A(b). Show an example, b: ∃x (M(x) ∧ A(x)). That does not mention b, so it comes back to the margin.

The givens came apart outermost first: the some, then the and inside it; the every, then the if inside it, then the and. The goal was made innermost first: the and, then the some around it. The some was used before the every, so that b was fresh when it was named.

Accepted names

Statements built this way are the language of first-order logic, also called predicate logic. A statement with a gap is a predicate; every and some are quantifiers; the main mark is the main connective, or main logical operator. The things a gap may take are the domain, or universe of discourse, and putting a sentence into marks is symbolization. The open textbook forall x teaches this language under these names.

Two languages

The marks are one language, the one under study; the English around them is another, the one the study is written in. The accepted names are object language and metalanguage. A habit keeps them apart: when a sentence is about a mark, quote the mark. ‘P’ is a letter; P is so or not so. And a letter that stands for any statement at all, as A does in “from A and B, A”, belongs to the English about the marks, not to the marks; forall x prints such letters in a script face for that reason.
