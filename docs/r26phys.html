How Does a Radio Tune in One Station?

How does a radio tune in one station? The wire in a radio’s aerial is struck by every station at once. In the San Francisco Bay Area that is dozens of them, each pushing the electrons back and forth at its own pace, hundreds of thousands of times a second. They all arrive mixed in one small voltage. Turn the dial, and one of them comes out loud and the rest fall silent. What makes this hard is that no part of the circuit looks at the station. A coil and a store of charge, wired together, simply let one pace through far better than the others. Why one pace, and how sharply? This page answers with rung twenty-six’s growth that turns. A voltage that swings back and forth is the shadow of a point going round, and once it is written that way the coil and the store become complex numbers that add, and the station the circuit favours can be read off with a pencil. On the way it settles what rung seven left open about the 120 volts in a wall outlet.

A voltage that swings at a steady pace is the real part of a complex number turning at that pace. For such a voltage, a resistor, a coil and a store of charge each pass a current in fixed proportion to the voltage, and the proportion is a complex number: its size says how much current, and its turn says how far the current runs behind or ahead. Wired in a row, these numbers add. The coil’s and the store’s cancel at exactly one pace, and there the current is largest. How sharply the circuit favours that pace is set by the resistance: a small one makes a narrow band, a large one a wide band. A spring pushed at its own pace swings the same way.

26phys1120 Volts That Reach 170

7·phys said that the current in a house changes direction 60 times a second, and called 120 V the steady voltage that would warm the kettle at the same rate. Here is why. The voltage in the wall is V(t) = V₀ cos ωt, with ω = 2π · 60 a second. A resistor R turns V²/R into heat at each instant.

P(t) = \tfrac{V_0^2}{R}\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac{V_0^2}{2R}\big(1 + \cos 2\omega t\big) @@ the double technique (21d): cos² A = (1 + cos 2A)/2
\int_0^{2\pi/\omega} \cos 2\omega t\,dt = \Big[\tfrac{\sin 2\omega t}{2\omega}\Big]_0^{2\pi/\omega} = 0 @@ area and slope undo each other (16c)
\text{average power over one period} = \tfrac{V_0^2}{2R} = \tfrac{(V_0/\sqrt2)^2}{R} @@ the area over one period, divided by the period
\htmlClass{new}{120 \text{ V steady} \iff V_0 = 120\sqrt2 \approx 170 \text{ V}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same warmth as a steady V₀/√2

Steady-stand-in technique: a voltage swinging between +V₀ and −V₀ warms a resistor as a steady V₀/√2 would, so a 120 V outlet swings to about 170 V each way. The 1500 W kettle of 7·phys draws 1500 W on average, and 3000 W at each peak, 120 times a second.

26phys2A Coil and a Store as Complex Numbers

V₀ cos ωt is the real part of V₀e^(iωt), a point going round a circle at pace ω (26a). The three parts of a circuit obey rules with real numbers in front: a resistor V = RI (7·phys), a coil V = LI′, a store of charge I = CV′ (24·then.2). So, as on 26·cb24, a complex solution’s real part is a real solution. Try a complex current I = I₀e^(iωt).

I' = i\omega\,I @@ the complex-rate technique (26·cb24.1), with r = iω
\text{coil}:\ V = L I' = i\omega L\,I @@ the coil’s rule
\text{store}:\ I = C V' = i\omega C\,V \Rightarrow V = \tfrac{1}{i\omega C}\,I = -\tfrac{i}{\omega C}\,I @@ the store’s rule; 1/i = −i
\htmlClass{new}{V = Z I:\ \ Z_R = R,\ \ Z_L = i\omega L,\ \ Z_C = -\tfrac{i}{\omega C}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same shape as V = RI, with a complex number for R

From here on, Z is the impedance. Impedance technique: for a voltage turning at pace ω, a resistor, a coil and a store each pass a current with V = ZI, where Z is R, iωL or −i/(ωC); the size of Z is the ratio of the swings, and the turn of Z is how far the current runs behind. A coil’s Z points straight up: its current runs a quarter turn behind its voltage. A store’s points straight down: its current runs a quarter turn ahead.

26phys3Impedances in a Row Add

Wire a resistor, a coil and a store in a row. The same current runs through all three, and their voltages add to the supply’s, by 7·phys’s loop law. So their impedances add.

V = Z_R I + Z_L I + Z_C I = \big(R + i(\omega L - \tfrac{1}{\omega C})\big)\,I @@ the loop law; one current
I_0 = \tfrac{V_0}{Z},\ \ |I_0| = \tfrac{V_0}{|Z|} @@ the size of a quotient is the quotient of the sizes (25c)
\text{the turn of } I_0 = -\text{the turn of } Z @@ out and round (21·cb20): dividing subtracts turns
\htmlClass{new}{R = 30,\ L = 0.1,\ C = 10^{-4}:\ \ Z \approx 30 + 11.2i,\ \ |Z| \approx 32.0 \ \Omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ ωL ≈ 37.7, 1/(ωC) ≈ 26.5, at 60 Hz

Row technique: in a row, impedances add as complex numbers, and the current is the voltage over the total. In that circuit on a 120 V outlet, the current is 120/32.0 ≈ 3.75 A, running 20.4° behind the voltage. The coil’s 37.7 Ω and the store’s 26.5 Ω do not add to 64 Ω. They point opposite ways, and mostly cancel.

26phys4The Pace That Gets Through

Every station pushes on the aerial at once, each at its own pace. The circuit passes each one by the size of its own Z. The size is smallest where the coil and the store cancel.

|Z|^2 = R^2 + \big(\omega L - \tfrac{1}{\omega C}\big)^2 \ge R^2 @@ the size of a complex number (25c)
|Z| = R \iff \omega L = \tfrac{1}{\omega C} \iff \omega = \omega_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} @@ the up part is 0
\htmlClass{new}{f_0 = \tfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}:\ \text{the current is largest, } V_0/R, \text{ and in step}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turn of Z is 0 there

Resonance technique: a coil and a store in a row pass the pace 1/√(LC) best, with only the resistance in the way. Left alone, the same circuit obeys Lq″ + Rq′ + q/C = 0 for its charge q: 26·cb24’s damped spring, with 2b = R/L and ω² = 1/(LC). It rings at nearly the pace it favours.

L = 240\ \mu\text{H},\ \ C \text{ from } 30 \text{ to } 365 \text{ pF} @@ a coil on the aerial rod, and a store turned by the dial
f_0 \text{ from } 538 \text{ to } 1876 \text{ kHz} @@ the resonance technique, at each end: the AM band is 540 to 1700 kHz
680 \text{ kHz}: C \approx 228 \text{ pF};\ \ 740: 193;\ \ 810: 161 @@ C = 1/((2πf)²L)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the dial turns } C,\ \text{and } C \text{ picks the station}} @@ KNBR, KCBS and KGO, San Francisco

26phys5How Sharp the Tuning Is

A station one channel over is only 10 kHz away: AM stations in the Americas are spaced 10 kHz apart. How much of it gets through? Near ω₀ the up part of Z is nearly a straight line in ω.

X(\omega) = \omega L - \tfrac{1}{\omega C},\ \ X'(\omega_0) = L + \tfrac{1}{\omega_0^2C} = 2L @@ the up part; 1/(ω₀²C) = L at resonance
X(\omega) \approx 2L(\omega - \omega_0) @@ the tangent line (13k), near ω₀
\big(\tfrac{R}{|Z|}\big)^2 = \tfrac{1}{1 + (X/R)^2} \ge \tfrac12 \iff |X| \le R @@ the share of the power passed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{half the power or more for } |\omega - \omega_0| \le \tfrac{R}{2L},\ \text{a band } \tfrac{R}{L} \text{ wide}} \quad \blacksquare @@ write Q = ω₀L/R: the band is ω₀/Q

Sharpness technique: a tuned circuit passes at least half the power of every pace within ω₀/(2Q) of ω₀, where Q = ω₀L/R; the smaller the resistance, the narrower the band. At 680 kHz with R = 20.5 Ω, Q ≈ 50 and the band is 13.6 kHz wide. The station at 690 kHz still gets 32% of its power through; the one at 740 kHz gets about 1.4%.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, tuned

A station is not one pace. Its voice and music push its signal out to about 5 kHz on either side, so the station is a band about 10 kHz wide. A circuit that passed only 680 kHz exactly would pass no sound at all. The tuning has to hear a band, and the question is only how wide.

A tuned circuit hears a band, not a single pace; choose its width, and say it.

Too wide and the neighbours leak in. Too narrow and the station’s own high notes are cut. One coil and one store cannot do both well, which is why a real set shifts every station down to one fixed pace and tunes there again with several circuits in a row.

A listener in Santa Rosa wants KNBR at 680 kHz, with a strong station at 690 kHz on the dial beside it. Which Q would you choose, and what does the listener lose by it?

26phys6A Spring Pushed at Its Own Pace

Push a damped spring with a force that swings: y″ + 2by′ + ω₀²y = F cos ωt. Try y = Ye^(iωt) for the complex push Fe^(iωt), as for the circuit.

(-\omega^2 + 2ib\omega + \omega_0^2)\,Y = F @@ the complex-rate technique, twice
|Y| = \tfrac{F}{|\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2ib\omega|} = \tfrac{F}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4b^2\omega^2}} @@ the size of a quotient (25c)
\omega = \omega_0:\ \ |Y| = \tfrac{F}{2b\omega_0} @@ the real part is 0
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{|Y(\omega_0)|}{|Y(0)|} = \tfrac{F/(2b\omega_0)}{F/\omega_0^2} = \tfrac{\omega_0}{2b}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against a steady push of the same size

Pushed-swing technique: a damped spring pushed at its own pace swings ω₀/(2b) times as far as a steady push of the same size would hold it. A playground swing 2.4 m long has ω₀ = √(9.8/2.4) ≈ 2.02 a second. With a light brake, b = 0.05 a second, a push in step builds a swing twenty times larger than the same push held still. The circuit’s Q is the same number: ω₀/(2b) with 2b = R/L.

The accepted names

The steady stand-in V₀/√2 is the root-mean-square voltage. Z is the impedance, its up part X the reactance, and V₀e^(iωt) with its fixed complex number a phasor. A coil and a store in a row tuned to one pace are a series LC (or RLC) circuit, the pace 1/√(LC) its resonant frequency, and Q its quality factor; the band where half the power or more gets through is the bandwidth. Shifting every station down to one fixed pace (455 kHz) is the superheterodyne receiver. The pushed spring is the driven damped oscillator.

Who said it first

Oliver Heaviside named impedance in 1886. Arthur Kennelly and Charles Proteus Steinmetz put complex numbers to work on swinging currents in 1893. Heinrich Hertz used a tuned spark circuit to catch radio waves in 1887, and Oliver Lodge patented tuning to one pace in 1897. Charles Herrold broadcast voice and music from San Jose from 1909, a station that grew into KCBS. Edwin Armstrong invented the superheterodyne in 1918. In 26·phys.8.5, the bridge fell on 7 November 1940; K. Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan (1991) argued that textbooks had called it resonance wrongly, and that it was self-excited flutter.
