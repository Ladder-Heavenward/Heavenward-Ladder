41b · proof

Undoing on a clock

On a clock, adding can always be undone: go back the same number of hours. Multiplying is another matter. On the clock of twelve, five times five is one, so five undoes itself. Two has no undo at all, since every multiple of two is an even hour and one is odd. The last step found that the hours that cannot be cancelled are the ones that share a divisor with the clock. This step finds that the others can be undone, and finds the undo by a road the ladder built on rung four: the oldest algorithm, run backwards. The undoable hours multiply among themselves and form a group. And on a clock whose size is prime, every hour but twelve o’clock is undoable, so dividing works there as it does in the fractions, with a finite set of hours.

An hour can be undone exactly when it shares nothing with the clock, and the gcd algorithm run backwards finds the undo. The undoable hours form a group under times. A clock whose size is a prime is a field: every hour but zero has an undo.

41b1Which hours have an undo

4j13 found, with plus only, numbers x and y with u · x = b · y + gcd(u, b) or the other way round. It had to allow either side, since it had no taking away. In ℤ the two sides fold into one.

Gcd-as-a-sum technique: for any integers a and n, not both 0, there are integers x and y with ax + ny = gcd(a, n). For counting numbers it is 4j13 with the multiple taken across: u · x − b · y = g, or u · (−x) + b · y = g. A negative a or n changes only signs, since gcd(−a, n) = gcd(a, n).

From here on, an undo of the hour [a] on the clock of n is an hour [x] with [a][x] = [1]. Undo-exactly-when technique: the hour [a] has an undo on the clock of n exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1, and then it has only one.

\gcd(a, n) = 1:\ \ ax + ny = 1 @@ the gcd-as-a-sum technique
ax \equiv 1 \pmod n @@ n ∣ ny, and ax − 1 = −ny
\htmlClass{new}{[a][x] = [1]} @@ ︿ undo
[a][x] = [1] \Rightarrow ax - 1 = nk \Rightarrow ax + n(-k) = 1 @@ the other way: ﹀ ≡
d \mid a,\ d \mid n \Rightarrow d \mid 1 @@ divides-a-multiple and divides-a-sum (4j2): every common divisor divides 1
[a][x] = [a][x'] = [1] \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{[x] = [x']} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clock-cancel technique (41a4)

So the hours that cannot be cancelled cannot be undone, and the ones that can be cancelled can be undone. The same line, ax + ny = 1, says both.

41b2Finding the undo

The undo is found by 4j12’s algorithm, then 4j13’s reading from the foot up, now with taking away. On the clock of 43, find the undo of [17].

43 = 17 \cdot 2 + 9,\ \ 17 = 9 \cdot 1 + 8,\ \ 9 = 8 \cdot 1 + 1 @@ the gcd algorithm (4j12): gcd(43, 17) = 1
1 = 9 - 8 @@ the last line, taken across
\phantom{1} = 9 - (17 - 9) = 2 \cdot 9 - 17 @@ the middle line, 8 = 17 − 9
\phantom{1} = 2(43 - 2 \cdot 17) - 17 = 2 \cdot 43 - 5 \cdot 17 @@ the first line, 9 = 43 − 2 · 17
17 \cdot (-5) \equiv 1 \pmod{43} @@ 43 ∣ 2 · 43
\htmlClass{new}{[17]^{-1} = [-5] = [38]} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 17 · 38 = 646 = 15 · 43 + 1

From here on, [a]⁻¹ names the undo of [a], when it has one: 19a’s mark for an undo in a group, and 41b3 shows the undoable hours are a group. It is not 5b’s reciprocal: 1/17 is no integer, and [17]⁻¹ is the hour [38].

Each round of the algorithm shrinks the pair, and 4j12 ended within a few rounds. How few is a question for 41h, where the count of rounds decides whether a machine can do this for numbers with hundreds of digits.

41b3The undoable hours form a group

From here on, (ℤ/n)ˣ names the set of hours of the clock of n that have an undo: the raised cross is part of the mark, and it marks “under times”. An hour with an undo is a unit, from the Latin unitas, oneness: a unit times its undo is one.

Units-group technique: the units of the clock of n form a group under times. A product of units is a unit: by 19c, [b]⁻¹[a]⁻¹ undoes [a][b]. [1] is a unit and does nothing. Regroup comes from ℤ by 41a3. Each unit has its undo in the set, since [a] undoes [a]⁻¹. That is 19a’s three demands, met.

(\mathbb Z/12)^\times = \{[1], [5], [7], [11]\} @@ gcd with 12 is 1 for these four only (undo-exactly-when)
[5]^2 = [25] = [1],\ \ [7]^2 = [49] = [1],\ \ [11]^2 = [121] = [1] @@ each is its own undo
(\mathbb Z/10)^\times = \{[1], [3], [7], [9]\} @@ the same test
[3][7] = [21] = [1],\ \ [9]^2 = [81] = [1] @@ 3 and 7 undo each other
\htmlClass{new}{[3]^2 = [9],\ [3]^3 = [7],\ [3]^4 = [1]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the powers of [3] walk through all four units of the clock of 10

Both groups have four members, and they are not alike. In the clock of 12 every unit done twice does nothing. In the clock of 10 one unit, [3], has all four as its powers. Which clocks have such a unit is 41e’s question.

41b4A prime clock is a field

Prime-clock-field technique: the clock of n is a field exactly when n is prime.

p \text{ prime},\ \ 0 \lt a \lt p \Rightarrow \gcd(a, p) = 1 @@ the gcd divides p, so it is 1 or p (4j9); p ∤ a
\text{every hour but } [0] \text{ has an undo} @@ the undo-exactly-when technique
[0] \ne [1] @@ p ∤ 1, since p > 1
\htmlClass{new}{\mathbb Z/p \text{ is a field}} @@ the rules of plus and times come along (41a3); undoes for times; 0 ≠ 1
n = jk,\ \ 1 \lt j, k \lt n \Rightarrow [j][k] = [0] @@ not prime: 41a4’s two hours that multiply to [0]
\htmlClass{new}{[j] \text{ has no undo, so } \mathbb Z/n \text{ is no field}} \quad \blacksquare @@ [k] = [j]⁻¹[j][k] = [0] would follow, against 0 < k < n

In a field, ax = b has exactly one answer when a is not 0: x = a⁻¹b. On the clock of 43, 17x ≡ 5 has x ≡ 38 · 5 = 190 ≡ 18, and 17 · 18 = 306 = 7 · 43 + 5. The field with two members of 5c.4.3 is the clock of 2. So for each prime p there is a field with p members, and the fractions, the reals and 25’s ℂ are no longer the only fields the ladder can compute in.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The gcd-as-a-sum technique is Bézout’s identity (Bézout’s lemma), and running the algorithm backwards is the extended Euclidean algorithm. The undo [a]⁻¹ is the inverse of a modulo n, or modular inverse. (ℤ/n)ˣ is the group of units, or multiplicative group, of ℤ/nℤ. The prime-clock-field technique says ℤ/pℤ is a field, written 𝔽_p or GF(p). The value of (p − 1)! in 41b.6.1 is Wilson’s theorem. The greatest gap of 41b.6.4 is the Frobenius number, and its formula for two coins is Sylvester’s.

Who said it first

Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac solved ax − by = 1 by the algorithm in 1624; Étienne Bézout proved the like statement for polynomials in 1779. The value of (p − 1)! was known to Ibn al-Haytham around 1000, announced by Edward Waring for his student John Wilson in 1770, and first proved by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1771. James Joseph Sylvester found the two-coin formula in 1884. Évariste Galois built fields with a prime power of members in 1830, the subject of 42g.

A proof that is not allowed

“On the clock of 12, [5][5] = [1] and [7][7] = [1], so every unit is its own undo, on every clock.” Not allowed: two cases are not every case. On the clock of 10, [3][3] = [9], and the undo of [3] is [7].
