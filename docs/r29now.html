Can We Hear Space Bend?

Can we hear space bend? A hundred years ago a new account of gravity said that mass bends the ruler of space and time, and that a pair of heavy bodies whirling round each other should send ripples through that ruler, spreading out at the speed of light. For decades no one was sure the ripples were real and not a trick of the coordinates, and everyone agreed they would be far too small ever to catch. What makes the question hard is size. A ripple from a collision across the universe stretches a length of four kilometres by far less than the width of a proton, and then squeezes it back, a few hundred times a second. On 14 September 2015 two instruments, one in Washington State and one in Louisiana, caught exactly such a ripple, rising in pitch like a bird’s chirp. This page builds what that needs from rung twenty-nine’s ruler. A clock higher up runs faster, and the satellites that tell your phone where it is must be corrected for it, by about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day. A passing ripple stretches one arm of an instrument and squeezes the other. The way its pitch climbs gives the masses that made it. It ends with what has been seen since, and what is still open.

Near a mass, the time part of the ruler is shrunk by the energy of place, so a clock lower down ticks slower, and a clock higher up gains about the drop in that energy over the square of light’s speed, every second. For a satellite twenty thousand kilometres up, the height wins over its speed, and its clock gains about thirty-eight millionths of a second a day, which light covers in about eleven kilometres. A ripple of the ruler adds a little to the length along one direction and takes as much away along the one across it, so light timed along two arms set across each other comes back out of step by the ripple’s size times the arm. Two bodies circling each other lose energy to the ripples, and the pitch climbs by a law whose power makes one stretched power of the pitch fall in a straight line with time. Two readings of the pitch, and the time between them, give the masses. What is open is the source of a slow hum of ripples found in the timing of spinning stars, and whether the ruler holds where gravity is strongest.

29now1Higher Clocks Run Faster

Far from any mass, 29·phys’s ruler holds: ds² = c²dt² − dx² − dy² − dz². Near a mass M, write Φ = −GM/r for the energy of place of one kilogram at distance r from its centre: the work-energy technique (16·phys) gives it from the pull GM/r². A measured law, predicted in 1915 and checked many times since, says how the ruler changes, while Φ/c² is small: ds² = (1 + 2Φ/c²)c²dt² − (1 − 2Φ/c²)(dx² + dy² + dz²). A clock reads the ruler along its own path: c²dτ² = ds².

d\tau^2 = \big(1 + \tfrac{2\Phi}{c^2}\big)\,dt^2 @@ a clock at rest: dx = dy = dz = 0
d\tau = \big(1 + \tfrac{\Phi}{c^2} + \cdots\big)\,dt @@ the binomial-sum technique (23h), power ½
\tfrac{d\tau_2}{d\tau_1} = 1 + \tfrac{\Phi_2 - \Phi_1}{c^2} + \cdots @@ two clocks at rest, one higher; divide, and keep the first-order terms
\htmlClass{new}{\Phi_2 - \Phi_1 \approx gh:\ \text{the higher clock gains } \tfrac{gh}{c^2} \text{ of each second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ near the ground Φ rises by g ≈ 9.81 J/kg each metre (16·phys)

Height-clock technique: of two clocks at rest, the higher gains (Φ₂ − Φ₁)/c² of each second, near the ground gh/c², so time runs faster upstairs. A clock on top of the Space Needle, 184 m up, gains 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ of each second: 1.7 billionths of a second a day.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a measurement picks the geometry

Nothing in logic says that time near the earth must run at one rate everywhere. The flat ruler of 29·phys is free of contradiction, and so is the bent one above. They differ in what clocks at different heights should read, and only a measurement can choose. In 1959 gamma rays sent up a tower 22.5 m tall came out shifted in pitch by the predicted 2.5 parts in 10¹⁵. In 2010, clocks that count the swings of light compared readings with one raised by a third of a metre, and found the gain.

The bend in the ruler near the earth is measured, not assumed: state the clocks, the heights, and how small a departure they could have seen.

So the geometry of space and time near the earth is a measured fact, known to within what the clocks can see. Each new clock tests it again, at a finer scale, and the bent ruler has passed every test so far.

A clock is driven from Seattle up to Paradise on Mount Rainier, 1646 m up, left for a day, and brought back. By the height-clock technique it should return about 15 billionths of a second ahead. What else would you check before reading that as the ruler’s bend?

29now2Thirty-Eight Millionths of a Second a Day

27·nav’s satellites fly 26 560 km from the earth’s centre. Their clocks are higher than ours, which makes them gain, and they are moving, which makes them lose. The earth’s GM is 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s², and its radius about 6371 km.

\tfrac{GM}{c^2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{6371 \text{ km}} - \tfrac{1}{26\,560 \text{ km}}\Big) = 5.29 \times 10^{-10} @@ the height-clock technique, from the ground to the orbit
v^2 = \tfrac{GM}{r} = (3874 \text{ m/s})^2,\ \ \tfrac{v^2}{2c^2} = 0.83 \times 10^{-10} @@ the light-clock technique, for the speed of a circular orbit
(5.29 - 0.83) \times 10^{-10} \times 86\,400 \text{ s} = 38.5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ s} @@ the gain less the loss, over one day
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a GPS clock gains about } 38 \text{ microseconds a day}} \quad \blacksquare @@ light covers 11.5 km in that time

Orbit-clock technique: a clock in orbit gains (GM/c²)(1/R − 1/r) of each second for its height and loses v²/(2c²) for its speed; for GPS the gain wins, by about 38 microseconds a day. So the satellites’ clocks are built to run slow on the ground, at 10.22999999543 MHz in place of 10.23 MHz, and in orbit they tick at 10.23.

This settles 27·nav.6.5 in part. A gain shared by every satellite shifts every ρᵢ by the same amount, and the clock-unknown technique’s b takes it all: the fix itself would barely move. But the gain is not quite shared. The orbits are slightly stretched, so each satellite climbs and falls, and its gain swings: a stretch of 2% swings it by about 46 billionths of a second, 14 m. And the satellites’ time would drift from the earth’s by 38 microseconds a day, ruining every clock that sets itself by GPS. Receivers correct for both.

29now3A Ripple in the Ruler

Far from its source, a ripple of the ruler passing along z changes it to ds² = c²dt² − (1 + h)dx² − (1 − h)dy² − dz², where h rises and falls a few hundred times a second and is far smaller than 1. Lengths along x grow by a share h/2, and lengths along y shrink by as much. Two arms of length L, one along x and one along y, each with a mirror at the end, measure it with light, for which ds = 0.

c^2dt^2 = (1 + h)\,dx^2 @@ light along the x arm: ds = 0
c\,dt = \sqrt{1 + h}\;dx = \big(1 + \tfrac h2 + \cdots\big)\,dx @@ the binomial-sum technique (23h)
\text{out and back along } x:\ \tfrac{2L}{c}\big(1 + \tfrac h2\big);\ \ \text{along } y:\ \tfrac{2L}{c}\big(1 - \tfrac h2\big) @@ the ruler-length technique (29d); h barely changes during one trip
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the arms differ by } hL \text{ as light measures them}} \quad \blacksquare @@ c times the difference of the trips, halved

Arm-stretch technique: a passing ripple of size h makes two arms of length L set across each other differ by hL, as light measures them, one growing while the other shrinks. The instruments that caught the 2015 ripple have arms of 4 km. At its strongest the ripple had h ≈ 10⁻²¹, so the arms differed by 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m: about a four-hundredth of a proton’s width, 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. The light runs back and forth about 280 times in each arm before the two beams are laid over each other, and how far their waves fall out of step reads the difference.

One instrument is at Hanford in Washington State, the other at Livingston in Louisiana, 3002 km apart. The ripple reached Livingston 6.9 thousandths of a second first. Light would take at most 10.0 thousandths of a second between them, so the ripple came from a direction about 46° from the line joining them: a ring on the sky.

29now4The Chirp Gives the Masses

Two black holes circling each other lose energy to ripples, draw closer and circle faster. The ripple’s frequency f is twice the circling rate, and climbs. The new account of gravity gives a law, held in trust here: f′ = k f^{11/3}, with k = (96/5) π^{8/3} (GM/c³)^{5/3}, where M = (m₁m₂)^{3/5}/(m₁ + m₂)^{1/5} is a mass built from the two.

f' = k\,f^{11/3} @@ the law, held in trust
\big(f^{-8/3}\big)' = -\tfrac83 f^{-11/3}f' = -\tfrac83 k @@ the chain technique of rung 13, with the real power rule (17g)
f^{-8/3}(t) = \tfrac83 k\,(t_c - t) @@ a constant slope gives a straight line (14d); at t_c the frequency has no bound: the merger
k = \frac{f_1^{-8/3} - f_2^{-8/3}}{\tfrac83\,(t_2 - t_1)} @@ two readings of the frequency
\htmlClass{new}{M = \tfrac{c^3}{G}\Big(\tfrac{5k}{96\,\pi^{8/3}}\Big)^{3/5}} \quad \blacksquare @@ undo k’s formula

Chirp technique: two readings of the climbing frequency and the time between them give k, and k gives the mass M of the pair. The published trace of the 2015 ripple climbs from 35 Hz to 150 Hz in roughly 0.17 to 0.2 s. With 0.17 s, k ≈ 1.65 × 10⁻⁴ and M ≈ 30 times the sun’s mass; with 0.2 s, 27. The full fit of every cycle gave about 30. No stars weigh that much and fit so close: two black holes. The fit gave about 36 and 29 suns, merging into one of 62. Three suns’ worth of mass went into the ripple in a fraction of a second: by the mass-energy technique, about 5.4 × 10⁴⁷ J.

29now5What Is Proved, and What Is Open

Ripples in the ruler of spacetime have been heard, and they move at the speed of light; what makes the slowest of them, and whether the ruler holds where gravity is strongest, are open.

  • Predicted, 1916. Ripples of the ruler, from the new account of gravity. For forty years it was argued whether they were real or only a trick of the coordinates, until a short argument in 1957 showed that a ripple could heat a rod by rubbing beads along it: it carries energy.
  • Measured, 1974 onward. A pair of dead stars, one of them flashing like a lighthouse, was found circling ever closer, at the rate ripples would carry the energy away, to within a fraction of a percent.
  • Heard, 2015. The ripple of this page, from two black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away.
  • Heard and seen, 2017. Two neutron stars merging: the ripple, then 1.7 s later a flash of gamma rays, after 130 million years of travel. So ripples move at the speed of light to within a few parts in 10¹⁶.
  • Found, 2023. The timing of dozens of spinning stars across our galaxy drifts together, as a slow hum of ripples with periods of years would make it drift.
  • Open. What makes that hum: pairs of giant black holes at the hearts of merging galaxies, or something from the early universe.
  • Open. How fast the universe is growing. Ripples measure the distance to their source directly, and two other ways of measuring disagree.
  • Open. Whether the ruler of the new account holds where gravity is strongest, at the moment two black holes merge. Every test so far has passed. Instruments in space, with arms millions of kilometres long, are planned for the 2030s.

The accepted names

The new account of gravity is general relativity. Φ is the Newtonian potential, the bent ruler the weak-field metric, and a clock’s gain with height the gravitational time dilation, or gravitational redshift. The ripples are gravitational waves, h is the strain, and its two shapes are polarizations. The instruments are laser interferometers; the two in the United States are LIGO, and the 2015 signal is GW150914. The rising frequency is a chirp, M the chirp mass, and t_c the time of coalescence. The flashing dead star is a pulsar; the slow hum is the nanohertz gravitational-wave background, found by pulsar timing arrays. Measuring distance by the ripple is a standard siren.

Who said it first

Albert Einstein predicted the waves in 1916, and with Nathan Rosen nearly withdrew them in 1936. Felix Pirani and Richard Feynman’s bead argument, at the Chapel Hill conference of 1957, settled that they carry energy. Robert Pound and Glen Rebka measured the shift up a tower at Harvard in 1959; James Chin-Wen Chou and colleagues raised an optical clock by 33 cm in 2010. Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found the pulsar pair in 1974. LIGO was founded by Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Ronald Drever, led to completion by Barry Barish; the detection of 14 September 2015 was announced on 11 February 2016, and Weiss, Barish and Thorne shared the Nobel prize in 2017. The neutron-star merger GW170817 was seen on 17 August 2017. The North American, European, Indian, Chinese and Australian pulsar timing arrays announced their evidence in June 2023. In 29·now.7.5, Joseph Weber reported coincidences between his aluminium bars at Maryland and near Chicago in 1969; by the mid-1970s groups led by Richard Garwin, Tony Tyson and others had found nothing, and the dispute shaped how LIGO later stated its false alarm rate, less than once in 200 000 years for the 2015 signal.
