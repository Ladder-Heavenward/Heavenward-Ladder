23h · technique

Polynomials that hug a curve, by hand

The proof steps of this rung showed that the growth function, the sine, the cosine and the logarithm are endless sums of powers. This step puts those sums to work by hand. First it moves them to any centre you like, so a value near a point you know well comes out in two or three pieces. Then it finds the sum for any power of one plus x, a whole power or a fraction or a negative one, which is how roots were computed for centuries. Last, it builds new sums out of old ones: by putting something else in for x, by taking slopes and areas one piece at a time, and by multiplying two sums together. Because a function has only one such sum about a point, any honest way of building it gives the right one.

Known sums move to any centre. Any power of one plus x is an endless sum that reaches one unit. New sums come from old ones by putting in, adding, taking slopes and areas, and multiplying, and they are always the right ones.

23h1Any centre

Stands on 23b2 (eˣ, sin wnd cos wre their sums), 23e4 (the sum for ln(1 + x)), 23e3 (the smooth-sum technique), 17e (adding becomes multiplying), 21d (the sum formulas) and 17c (the log of a product).

Recentring technique: to find the sum of f about w, write x = w + h and turn f(w + h) into sums in h that you know; the result is the sum of f about w, and it reaches as far as those sums do. It is the matching sum, and no other, by the smooth-sum technique (23e3): one total near w has only one power sum about w.

e^{x} = e^{w}e^{h} = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0} \tfrac{e^w}{k!}(x-w)^k @@ adding becomes multiplying (17e); the sum for eʰ (23b2) reaches everywhere
\sin x = \sin w\cos h + \cos w\sin h @@ the sum formula (21d), with h = x − a
\sin x = \sin w\big(1 - \tfrac{h^2}{2!} + \cdots\big) + \cos w\big(h - \tfrac{h^3}{3!} + \cdots\big) @@ the sums for cos wnd sin (23b2); add them piece by piece (22d)
\ln x = \ln w + \ln\big(1 + 	frac{h}{w}\big) = \ln w + \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 1} \tfrac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k\,w^k}(x-w)^k @@ the log of a product (17c); h/a for x in the sum for ln(1 + x) (23e4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sum for } \ln x 	ext{ about } w 	ext{ has reach } w, \text{ and holds for } 0 < x \le 2w} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum for ln(1 + x) holds for −1 < h/a ≤ 1

By hand: 31° is π/6 + π/180, and sin π/6 = ½, cos π/6 = √3/2. Two pieces past the first give sin 31° ≈ ½ + (√3/2)h − ¼h², with h = π/180, which is 0.5150388. The true value is 0.5150381. The error ceiling of 23b2, with every slope at most 1, puts the error below h³/6, under 0.0000009. A centre you know well, near the input, does most of the work.

23h2Any power of 1 + x

Stands on 23a1 (the slopes of a power), 17g (the real power rule), 23c3 (the reach-by-ratio technique), 23e3 (the term-by-term technique), 13c (the product rule), 14d (zero slope means flat) and 4j (the two-term power).

From here on, for any real α and whole k ≥ 1, = α(α − 1)⋯(α − k + 1)/k!, and = 1. Read it "α choose k". When α is a whole number it is 4j's count of choices. Accepted name: the general binomial coefficient.

Binomial-sum technique: for every real α and every x with |x| < 1, (1 + x)^α = Σₖ (α choose k) xᵏ. When α is a whole number 0, 1, 2, …, the pieces stop after x^α and this is 4j's two-term power, true for every x. Otherwise the sum is endless, and the proof shows its total is the power without ever bounding a slope.

f(x) = (1+x)^\alpha,\ \ f^{(k)}(x) = \alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-k+1)(1+x)^{\alpha-k} @@ the real power rule (17g), k times, with the chain rule
c_k = \tfrac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!} = \binom{\alpha}{k} @@ the matching sum (23a2)
\tfrac{|c_{k+1}|}{|c_k|} = \tfrac{|\alpha - k|}{k+1} \to 1 @@ a is not a whole number, so no cₖ is 0
S(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0} \binom{\alpha}{k}x^k \text{ settles for } |x| < 1 @@ the reach-by-ratio technique (23c3): R = 1
(k+1)\binom{\alpha}{k+1} = (\alpha-k)\binom{\alpha}{k} @@ the recipe for a choose k, one factor more
(1+x)S'(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0}\Big[(k+1)\binom{\alpha}{k+1} + k\binom{\alpha}{k}\Big]x^k = \alpha\,S(x) @@ slopes term by term (23e3); gather the pieces with xᵏ; the line above
g(x) = S(x)(1+x)^{-\alpha},\ \ g'(x) = (1+x)^{-\alpha-1}\big[(1+x)S'(x) - aS(x)\big] = 0 @@ the product rule (13c) and the real power rule (17g)
g(x) = g(0) = S(0) = 1 \text{ on } (-1, 1) @@ zero slope means flat (14d)
\htmlClass{new}{(1+x)^\alpha = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0}\binom{\alpha}{k}x^k \text{ for } |x| < 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply by (1 + x)^α
  
\sqrt{1+x} = 1 + \tfrac{x}{2} - \tfrac{x^2}{8} + \tfrac{x^3}{16} - \tfrac{5x^4}{128} + \cdots @@ α = ½
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}} = 1 - \tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac{3x^2}{8} - \tfrac{5x^3}{16} + \cdots @@ α = −½
\tfrac{1}{1+x} = 1 - x + x^2 - x^3 + \cdots @@ α = −1: the geometric sum (15i), as it must be
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{(1+x)^2} = 1 - 2x + 3x^2 - 4x^3 + \cdots} @@ α = −2: a choose k is (−1)ᵏ(k + 1)

By hand: √26 = 5√(1 + 1/25). Four pieces give 5(1 + 0.02 − 0.0002 + 0.000004) = 5.09902, against √26 = 5.0990195. The trick is always the same: pull out a power you know, so that what is left is 1 plus something small.

23h3New sums from old

Stands on 23e3 (the term-by-term and smooth-sum techniques), 22d (the sum-of-sums technique), 22g1 (the bracket), 17c (the log of a quotient) and 23h1.

Building technique: put cxᵐ in for x, multiply by a power of x, add, or take slopes and areas term by term, and the result is the sum of the new function wherever every sum it was built from settles. No slope of the new function needs to be found. The smooth-sum technique (23e3) says a function has only one power sum about a point, so a sum built honestly is the matching sum.

e^{-x^2} = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0} \tfrac{(-x^2)^k}{k!} = 1 - x^2 + \tfrac{x^4}{2!} - \tfrac{x^6}{3!} + \cdots @@ −x² for x in the sum for eˣ (23b2); it reaches everywhere
\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx = 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac1{10} - \tfrac1{42} + \tfrac1{216} - \tfrac1{1320} + \tfrac1{9360} - \cdots @@ areas term by term (23e3): the piece with x²ᵏ gives (−1)ᵏ/(k!(2k + 1))
0.74672 < \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx < 0.74684 @@ the bracket (22g1), after six pieces and after seven
\ln\tfrac{1+x}{1-x} = \ln(1+x) - \ln(1-x) = 2\big(x + \tfrac{x^3}{3} + \tfrac{x^5}{5} + \cdots\big) @@ the log of a quotient (17c); −x for x in 23e4; subtract piece by piece (22d); |x| < 1
\htmlClass{new}{\ln 2 = 2\big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac{1}{3 \cdot 3^3} + \tfrac{1}{5 \cdot 3^5} + \cdots\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ x = ⅓ makes (1 + x)/(1 − x) = 2

The curve e^(−x²) has no antiderivative among the functions built so far, so 16c cannot find its area. The sum finds it in a few lines: 0.74682, to five places. And ln 2 from 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ⋯ creeps; the new sum gains about one place for every piece, since each piece is near a ninth of the one before. 23i counts the pieces.

Read the other way, a built sum hands you slopes for free. The piece with x⁶ in e^(x²) is x⁶/3!, and the smooth-sum technique says it is f⁽⁶⁾(0)x⁶/6!. So f⁽⁶⁾(0) = 6!/3! = 120, with no slope taken at all.

23h4Multiplying sums

Stands on 23c2 (inside the reach a sum settles in size), 22d1 (running totals), 22h1 and 10c (limits multiply), 23h3.

Product technique: where Σ uₖxᵏ and Σ vₖxᵏ both settle in size, their totals multiply to Σ cₖxᵏ, with cₖ = u₀vₖ + u₁vₖ₋₁ + ⋯ + uₖv₀. Multiply as for polynomials, and gather every pair of powers that adds to k.

p_j = a_jx^j,\ q_l = b_lx^l;\ \ A_N = \textstyle\sum_{j \le N} p_j,\ B_N = \sum_{l \le N} q_l,\ P_N = \sum_{k \le N} c_kx^k @@ running totals (22d1)
P_N = \textstyle\sum_{j + l \le N} p_jq_l,\ \ A_NB_N = \sum_{j \le N,\ l \le N} p_jq_l @@ cₖxᵏ gathers the pairs with j + l = k
A_NB_N - P_N = \textstyle\sum p_jq_l \text{ over } j \le N,\ l \le N,\ j + l > N @@ the pairs in one and not the other
j + l > N \Rightarrow j > \tfrac N2 \text{ or } l > \tfrac N2 @@ else j + l ≤ N
|A_NB_N - P_N| \le \Big(\textstyle\sum_{j > N/2} |p_j|\Big)\Big(\sum_l |q_l|\Big) + \Big(\sum_j |p_j|\Big)\Big(\sum_{l > N/2} |q_l|\Big) @@ every such pair is counted at least once; all in size
\text{both tails} \to 0 @@ both sums settle in size (23c2), and a tail is the total less a running total
\htmlClass{new}{P_N \to AB} \quad \blacksquare @@ A_NB_N → AB, limits multiply (10c, 22h1)
  
e^x\sin x = (1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \tfrac{x^3}{6} + \cdots)(x - \tfrac{x^3}{6} + \tfrac{x^5}{120} - \cdots) @@ both reach everywhere
= x + x^2 + \big(\tfrac12 - \tfrac16\big)x^3 + \big(\tfrac16 - \tfrac16\big)x^4 + \big(\tfrac1{24} - \tfrac1{12} + \tfrac1{120}\big)x^5 + \cdots @@ gather the pairs for each power
= x + x^2 + \tfrac{x^3}{3} - \tfrac{x^5}{30} + \cdots @@ at x = 0.1: 0.110333, against eˣ sin x = 0.1103330
\sin x\cos x = x - \tfrac{2}{3}x^3 + \tfrac{2}{15}x^5 - \cdots @@ the same gathering
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12\sin 2x = x - \tfrac{(2x)^3}{2 \cdot 3!} + \tfrac{(2x)^5}{2 \cdot 5!} - \cdots = x - \tfrac23x^3 + \tfrac{2}{15}x^5 - \cdots} \quad \blacksquare @@ 2x for x (23b2); it agrees, as sin 2A = 2 sin A cos A (21d) says it must

Dividing is harder. 1/cos x is smooth near 0, but its sum stops reaching at π/2, where cos is 0, though the sums for sin and cos reach everywhere. A quotient's sum can be found piece by piece, from (1/cos x) · cos x = 1, but how far it reaches has to be found separately.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The binomial-sum technique is the binomial series (the binomial theorem for any exponent); a choose k is the generalised binomial coefficient. Moving a sum to a new centre is expanding about a point. The building technique is the algebra of power series; the product technique is the Cauchy product of two series. The area under e^(−x²) is, up to a factor, the error function, and the sum for arcsin is Newton's.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton found the binomial series in the winter of 1664 to 1665, by extending John Wallis's patterns, and described it in two letters of 1676 sent through Henry Oldenburg to Gottfried Leibniz. Leonhard Euler gave an argument in the 1770s that took the settling for granted. Niels Henrik Abel published the first full proof in 1826, for every power and at the edges too. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the product rule for sums settling in size in 1821, and Franz Mertens showed in 1875 that one of the two settling in size is enough.
