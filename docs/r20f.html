20f · proof

Every isometry is at most three flips

20c named three kinds of move, and 20e built slides and turns out of flips. Are there other isometries, ones no one has thought of? This step says no. A point is fixed by its distances to three corners of a triangle, so an isometry is fixed by where it sends three corners. Three flips, chosen one corner at a time, can send them anywhere they are allowed to go. The same construction proves Euclid’s tests for congruent triangles from the moves.

Given three points not on one line, any point is fixed by its distances to them, and an isometry by where it sends them. Flip the first point to its place, then the second, then the third, each flip keeping what is already placed. So every isometry is at most three flips.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 20e (two flips), 20d (congruence), 18d (equally far from two points), 18f (triangles by hand). 20g counts the flips by parity.

20f1Three points fix a point

Stands on 18d2 (the points equally far from two points).

Three-anchors technique: if A, B, C are not on one line, a point is fixed by its distances to A, B and C. Two different points X and Y with the same three distances would put A, B and C all on the one line of points equally far from X and Y.

d(X, A) = d(Y, A),\ \ d(X, B) = d(Y, B),\ \ d(X, C) = d(Y, C) @@ suppose
X \ne Y \Rightarrow A, B, C \in \{P : d(P, X) = d(P, Y)\} @@ each is equally far from X and Y
\{P : d(P, X) = d(P, Y)\} \text{ is a line} @@ 18d2, since X ≠ Y
\htmlClass{new}{X = Y} \quad \blacksquare @@ A, B, C are not on one line: proof by contradiction (7e)

20f2An isometry is fixed by three points

Stands on 20f1 and 20b (the group).

Three-points-decide technique: two isometries that agree at three points not on one line are the same move. If f(A) = A, f(B) = B and f(C) = C, every X has f(X) at the same three distances as X, so f(X) = X. For two isometries f and g agreeing at A, B, C, apply this to g⁻¹ ∘ f.

f(A) = A,\ f(B) = B,\ f(C) = C @@ suppose
d(f(X), A) = d(f(X), f(A)) = d(X, A) @@ f keeps distances; likewise for B and C
f(X) = X \text{ for every } X @@ three-anchors
g^{-1} \circ f \text{ keeps } A, B, C \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{f = g} \quad \blacksquare @@ g⁻¹ ∘ f is an isometry (20b), so it does nothing

20f3Three flips are enough

Stands on 20f2, 20c3 (flips) and 18d2.

Three-flips technique: every isometry is at most three flips. Choose A, B, C not on one line. Flip A onto f(A). Then flip the new B onto f(B), across a line that keeps f(A). Then the new C onto f(C), across a line that keeps f(A) and f(B).

F_1:\ \text{across the line equally far from } A \text{ and } f(A) @@ do-nothing if f(A) = A; now F₁(A) = f(A)
F_2:\ \text{across the line equally far from } F_1(B) \text{ and } f(B) @@ do-nothing if they agree
d(f(A), F_1(B)) = d(A, B) = d(f(A), f(B)) @@ so f(A) is on F₂’s line, and F₂ keeps it
F_3:\ \text{the same for } F_2F_1(C) \text{ and } f(C) @@ its line holds f(A) and f(B), by the same line of distances
F_3F_2F_1 = f \text{ at } A, B, C \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{f = F_3 \circ F_2 \circ F_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ three-points-decide

This also settles a question 20a left open. Every flip is onto, so any function from the plane to itself that keeps distances is a move: the proof never used onto, only distances.

20f4Euclid’s tests, earned from the moves

Stands on 20f3, 20d4 (congruence) and 18f (triangles by hand).

Three-sides-congruent technique: two triangles with their three sides equal in pairs are congruent: some isometry sends one onto the other. Run 20f3 with f(A), f(B), f(C) replaced by the corners of the second triangle. Each line of distances in the proof needs only that the sides agree, and then the three flips carry the corners, and with them the segments (20a2), onto the second triangle.

AB = A'B',\ AC = A'C',\ \angle A = \angle A' \Rightarrow BC = B'C' @@ two-sides-and-the-angle (18f), then three-sides-congruent
\angle A = \angle A',\ \angle B = \angle B',\ AB = A'B' @@ similar-triangles (18f) gives ratios equal to AB/A′B′ = 1, so all three sides agree
\angle A = \angle A',\ \angle B = \angle B' \Rightarrow \text{similar} @@ stretch by k = A′B′/AB (a similarity), then the three sides agree
\htmlClass{new}{\text{side-angle-side, angle-side-angle, side-side-side; and angle-angle for similar}} \quad \blacksquare

Euclid proved the first by laying one triangle on another, and had to hold the move in trust (20a.4.5). Here the move is built.

The accepted names

The three-flips technique is often stated as: every isometry of the plane is a product of at most three reflections. The tests are SSS, SAS, ASA and AA; side-side-angle is the ambiguous case (21h meets it again).

Who said it first

In 20f.4.5, the book is David Hilbert's Grundlagen der Geometrie (1899), in which a form of side-angle-side is axiom III.5.
