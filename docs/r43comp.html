How Can Strangers Share a Secret?

How can strangers share a secret? A phone in a café opens a page from a bank it has never met, over a radio link anyone in the room can hear, and in a fraction of a second the two agree a key nobody listening can find. Then every word between them is locked with that key, each packet carries a tag that shows it was not changed on the way, and the bank shows a signed note that it is the bank. Four tools do the work. A lock with one key shared by both sides is fast, and needs a long stream of key that looks random from a short seed. A function easy to run and hard to run backwards lets the clock powers of 43i and 43l agree a key in public. A short fingerprint of a long file lets a signature cover the file. A tag made with the shared key shows the message is whole. This page builds each from the clocks of the rung, proves what can be proved, and is plain about what is only believed.

A shared key needs a stream that looks random; a generator that is a straight map on a clock gives itself away in three outputs. Taking square roots on the clock of pq is exactly as hard as splitting pq. A fingerprint built from two clock powers can be matched only by someone who finds a clock log. A tag on a prime clock with a key used once can be forged only by luck, one chance in p. And a signature must go on a fingerprint, or two signatures multiply into a third.

43comp1A Stream of Key From a Seed

9·crypt showed a pad as long as the message hides everything, and its short-key technique that a short key stretched by a fixed rule cannot hide everything. Every fast lock with a shared key stretches a short seed into a long stream anyway, and asks only that the stream look random to anyone without the seed: that no quick test can tell it from coin tosses better than by guessing. A stream like that is called pseudorandom.

The easiest stretch is a straight map on a clock: xₙ₊₁ ≡ a xₙ + c (mod p), with the seed x₀ and the numbers a and c the key. It is useless for secrets.

Three-outputs technique: on a prime clock, three outputs in a row x₀, x₁, x₂ of xₙ₊₁ ≡ a xₙ + c, with x₁ ≢ x₀, give a and c, and so every later output.

x_1 \equiv ax_0 + c,\ \ x_2 \equiv ax_1 + c \pmod p @@ the rule, twice
x_2 - x_1 \equiv a(x_1 - x_0) @@ subtract: c is gone
a \equiv (x_2 - x_1)(x_1 - x_0)^{-1} @@ x₁ − x₀ is a unit of the prime clock (43b)
\htmlClass{new}{c \equiv x_1 - ax_0,\ \text{ then } x_3, x_4, \ldots \text{ follow}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rule run on

On the clock of 101 a stream reads 3, 56, 48. Then x₂ − x₁ ≡ −8 ≡ 93, x₁ − x₀ = 53, and 53⁻¹ ≡ 61, since 53 · 61 = 3233 = 32 · 101 + 1. So a ≡ 93 · 61 ≡ 17 and c ≡ 56 − 51 = 5, and the next output is 17 · 48 + 5 ≡ 13. The streams in use are built from many rounds of mixing, not from one straight map, and nobody has proved any of them pseudorandom. Whether a pseudorandom stretch exists at all is open; a proof would also settle whether one-way functions exist, the subject of the next footing.

43comp2Easy One Way, Hard the Other

A one-way function is quick to run and slow to run backwards on almost every input. Two from this rung are believed one-way: multiplying two large primes, and a clock power gˣ on a prime clock, both quick by square-and-multiply (43h). Nobody has proved that any function is one-way. What can be proved is that two hard-looking jobs are the same job. Squaring on the clock of n = pq is quick. Taking square roots there is exactly as hard as splitting n.

Root-gives-factor technique: for n = pq with p and q different odd primes, anyone who can find a square root on the clock of n of any square can split n, with a chance at least one half at each try.

x \text{ a unit chosen at random},\ \ s \equiv x^2 \pmod n @@ only the square is handed over
s \text{ has four roots: } \pm x \text{ on each of the clocks of } p \text{ and } q @@ the two-clock technique (43c) and few-roots (43e): two roots on each prime clock
y \text{ returned, and } y \not\equiv \pm x \text{ with chance } \tfrac12 @@ the four roots look alike from s, so y does not depend on which x was chosen
y^2 \equiv x^2:\ \ n \mid (y - x)(y + x),\ \ n \nmid y - x,\ n \nmid y + x @@ y is x on one clock and −x on the other
\htmlClass{new}{\gcd(y - x, n) \in \{p, q\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gcd by halving remainders (43h3); each try fails with chance at most ½

On the clock of 77, pick x = 10: s ≡ 100 ≡ 23. The roots of 23 are 10, 32, 45 and 67. If the root-finder returns 32, then gcd(32 − 10, 77) = gcd(22, 77) = 11, and 77 = 7 · 11. If it returns 67 ≡ −10, try again. So a lock whose opening is taking square roots on the clock of pq is exactly as safe as pq is hard to split, a promise the two-key technique of 43i does not have.

43comp3A Fingerprint Nobody Can Match

22·crypt showed every short fingerprint of long files has matches, so only finding one can be made hard, and that a random search finds one after about the square root of the number of fingerprints. Here is a fingerprint where finding a match is provably as hard as a clock log. Take a prime q with p = 2q + 1 prime too, g of order q on the clock of p, and h = g^b with b known to nobody. The fingerprint of a pair (x₁, x₂) of hours of the clock of q is H(x₁, x₂) = g^(x₁) h^(x₂) on the clock of p: q² inputs, q outputs.

Collision-gives-log technique: any two different pairs with the same fingerprint g^(x₁)h^(x₂) give b, the clock log of h to the base g.

g^{x_1}h^{x_2} \equiv g^{y_1}h^{y_2} \pmod p @@ a match
g^{x_1 - y_1} \equiv h^{y_2 - x_2} = g^{b(y_2 - x_2)} @@ divide; h = g^b
x_1 - y_1 \equiv b(y_2 - x_2) \pmod q @@ exponents live on the clock of the order q (43d3)
y_2 \ne x_2:\ \text{ else } g^{x_1 - y_1} \equiv 1 \text{ and the pairs agree} @@ the pairs differ
\htmlClass{new}{b \equiv (x_1 - y_1)(y_2 - x_2)^{-1} \pmod q} \quad \blacksquare @@ q is prime, so y₂ − x₂ is a unit (43b)

Small: q = 11, p = 23, g = 2, whose powers are 2, 4, 8, 16, 9, 18, 13, 3, 6, 12, 1, order 11. Let h = 13. Then H(1, 0) = 2, and H(5, 1) = 9 · 13 = 117 ≡ 2. The match gives b ≡ (1 − 5)(1 − 0)⁻¹ ≡ −4 ≡ 7, and 2⁷ = 128 ≡ 13. The fingerprints used every day, such as the one in each web page’s lock, are fast mixers with no such proof; their safety is that years of public attack have found no match.

43comp4A Tag That Shows a Message Is Whole

A lock keeps a message from being read; it does not keep it from being changed. Two sides who share a key add a tag to each message, made from the message and the key, and the receiver remakes the tag and compares. The question is the chance that someone who has seen one message and its tag can make another message with a right tag. On a prime clock, with a key used once, the answer is exact.

One-time-tag technique: with a key (a, b) chosen at random from the hours of the clock of a prime p and the tag t ≡ am + b, someone who sees one message and its tag makes a right tag for any other message with chance exactly 1/p.

t \equiv am + b \pmod p \text{ seen} @@ p keys fit it: each a, with b ≡ t − am
m' \ne m,\ \text{ a guess } t' @@ the forger’s try
am + b \equiv t,\ \ am' + b \equiv t' @@ two straight conditions on (a, b)
a \equiv (t - t')(m - m')^{-1},\ \ b \equiv t - am @@ m − m′ is a unit (43b): exactly one key fits both
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one key in } p \text{ gives } t':\ \text{ chance } 1/p} \quad \blacksquare @@ the p keys that fit t were all equally likely

With p = 101, key (37, 58) and message 20, the tag is 37 · 20 + 58 = 798 ≡ 91. A forger sees (20, 91) and wants a tag for 21. Every one of the 101 tags is the right one for exactly one key that fits what was seen, so the guess is right one time in 101. With a clock of 2¹²⁸ hours the chance is 1 in 3.4 × 10³⁸. Tags in use reuse one key for many messages and are built from fingerprints of the key and the message together; they stand on belief, and this one on proof.

43comp5Signing a Fingerprint

43i signs m by s ≡ m^d on the clock of n. Two faults come with signing m itself. A long file is many hours, one power each. And signatures multiply: (m₁m₂)^d ≡ m₁^d m₂^d, so two signatures give a third the signer never made. With 43i’s n = 3233 and d = 2753, the signature on 2 is 1027 and on 3 is 1857, and 1027 · 1857 ≡ 2902, the signature on 6. Both faults go if the signer signs a fingerprint H(m) in place of m.

Sign-the-fingerprint technique: a signature on H(m) passes for every m′ with H(m′) = H(m), so signing fingerprints is as safe as the signature itself and the hardness of finding a match, and no safer.

s \equiv H(m)^d,\ \ \text{check: } s^e \equiv H(m) @@ the turned-key technique on the fingerprint
H(m') = H(m) \Rightarrow s^e \equiv H(m') @@ the check sees only H
\text{a forger of } m' \text{ needs } s' \text{ with } s'^e \equiv H(m') @@ a new fingerprint needs a new power
H(m_1)H(m_2) \ne H(m_1m_2) \text{ in general} @@ a fingerprint keeps no products, so the multiplying trick finds no target
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a forgery is a match of } H \text{ or a power the key alone gives}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two ways in

The signature also closes the gap 43l left. Two strangers who agree a key by the shared-secret technique cannot tell whether a listener in the middle agreed one with each of them. If the bank signs its half, gᵇ, with a key whose public part the phone already trusts, the listener cannot put her own in its place. That trust comes from a chain of signed notes ending at a few public keys built into the phone.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A lock with one shared key is a symmetric cipher, and one that adds a stretched stream to the message is a stream cipher; the stretcher is a pseudorandom generator. The straight map on a clock is a linear congruential generator. A one-way function is the same name in every text. The root-gives-factor technique is Rabin’s reduction (1979), and the lock it guards is the Rabin cryptosystem. The fingerprint g^(x₁)h^(x₂) is the Chaum–van Heijst–Pfitzmann hash (1991), and a fingerprint no one can match is collision resistant. A tag is a message authentication code, or MAC; the one-time tag is the Carter–Wegman construction (1979–1981) in its simplest form, and tags in use are HMAC and its kin. Signing a fingerprint is hash-and-sign. The listener in the middle is the man-in-the-middle attack, and the chain of signed notes is a public key infrastructure.

Who said it first

Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman asked for one-way functions with a way back in 1976. Michael Rabin linked square roots to splitting in 1979. Larry Carter and Mark Wegman gave the tags with a proved chance in 1979 and 1981. David Chaum, Eugène van Heijst and Birgit Pfitzmann gave the fingerprint from clock logs in 1991. Joan Boyar showed in 1989 how fast the straight map gives itself away, even when only part of each output is shown.

A proof that is not allowed

“Finding square roots on the clock of n is hard, because finding them on the clock of a prime p is hard.” Not allowed: on a prime clock roots are quick, and for p ≡ 3 (mod 4) the root of s is s^((p + 1)/4), one power. The hardness lives in not knowing p and q; the root-gives-factor technique says exactly that.
