How Much Colour Is in the Glass?

How much colour is in the glass? Hold a glass of grape juice up to a window. Some of the light gets through and some does not, and the colour you see is what is left. A chemist turns that into a measurement: shine light of one wavelength through a sample, and compare what comes out with what went in. The less gets through, the more of the coloured substance is dissolved. This page builds the rule that links the two, from rung 17's exponential. Then it asks what happens when the sample sits in a round tube, so that each ray crosses a different length of liquid. Each path is a chord of a circle, and 18d already knows its length.

Absorbance counts powers of ten in how much the light is dimmed. In a dilute solution, absorbance is in proportion to the path the light takes and to the concentration. Across a round tube, a ray that misses the centre crosses a path twice the square root of the radius squared less its distance from the centre squared. Several paths read together give less than their average absorbance.

Light in, light out

An instrument shines light of one wavelength into a sample. Call the amount of light going in I₀ and the amount coming out I. Each is measured in the same unit, so only their ratio matters. The fraction let through, T = I/I₀, has the accepted name transmittance. The absorbance is A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = −log₁₀ T. The instrument has the accepted name spectrophotometer.

Why a log? Because of what happens when two samples stand in a row. If the first lets through a fraction T₁ and the second T₂, together they let through T₁T₂. Fractions let through multiply, so absorbances add. That is 17c's log-of-a-product, for log₁₀ (17g's log-base-p).

T = 0.50 \Rightarrow A = -\log_{10} 0.50 \approx 0.301 @@ half the light through
\text{two such samples in a row}:\ T = 0.25,\ \ A \approx 0.602 @@ the fractions multiply; the absorbances add
A = 2 \Rightarrow T = 10^{-2} = 0.01 @@ one percent through: undo the log (17h)

The thin-layer law

Now let the light travel a distance x into a liquid holding one coloured substance at concentration c, in mol/L. Call what is left of the light I(x). Measurement shows a regularity, for a dilute solution and one wavelength. Each thin layer takes away the same small fraction of whatever light reaches it. That fraction is in proportion to the layer's thickness and to c. In marks: I′(x) = −kcI(x), for a number k that belongs to the substance and the wavelength. Thin-layer law: in a clear, dilute solution, light of one wavelength fades at a rate in proportion to how much light is left and to how much colour is dissolved. It is a law: measured, with a range. Its range is set out in the box below.

Absorbance technique: if the thin-layer law is true along a path of length ℓ, then the absorbance is A = εℓc, with ε = k / ln 10. The number ε has the accepted name the molar absorption coefficient, in L/(mol·cm).

I'(x) = -kc\,I(x) @@ the thin-layer law, for x from 0 to ℓ
I(x) = I_0\,e^{-kcx} @@ proportional-slope (17f), with the constant −kc; I(0) = I₀
\tfrac{I_0}{I(\ell)} = e^{kc\ell} @@ real-exponent: 1/e^(−y) = e^y
\log_{10}\tfrac{I_0}{I(\ell)} = \tfrac{kc\ell}{\ln 10} @@ log-base-p: log₁₀ y = ln y / ln 10, and ln e^y = y
\htmlClass{new}{A = \varepsilon \ell c},\ \ \varepsilon = \tfrac{k}{\ln 10} \quad \blacksquare @@ name the constant

So the absorbance is a straight line in c through 0, and a straight line in ℓ through 0. Double the path or double the colour, and the absorbance doubles. The light let through does not halve: it is squared, since T = 10−A.

\text{NADH at } 340 \text{ nm}:\ \varepsilon = 6220 \text{ L/(mol·cm)} @@ a molecule every living cell uses to carry energy; its absorbance is how many enzyme tests are read
A = 0.311,\ \ell = 1.00 \text{ cm}:\ \ c = \tfrac{0.311}{6220} = 5.0 \times 10^{-5} \text{ mol/L} @@ absorbance technique, solved for c: 50 µmol/L
\text{permanganate at } 525 \text{ nm}:\ \varepsilon \approx 2400 \text{ L/(mol·cm)} @@ the deep purple of KMnO₄
1.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ mol/L},\ 1 \text{ cm}:\ A = 0.24,\ T \approx 0.58;\ \ 2 \text{ cm}:\ A = 0.48,\ T \approx 0.33 @@ A doubles; T is squared, 0.58² ≈ 0.33

Reading a well

The Salinas Valley grows much of the country's lettuce, and decades of fertilizer have reached its groundwater as nitrate. Many wells there test above the drinking-water limit of 10 mg/L of nitrate nitrogen. A common test turns the nitrate into a pink dye and reads its absorbance near 543 nm. The lab does not look up ε. It reads standards of known strength in the same cell, draws the line through them, and reads the unknown off the line. That line is the absorbance technique, with εℓ measured all at once; its accepted name is a calibration curve.

Every reading is taken against a blank: the same cell, filled with the same water and the same dye-making chemicals, but no nitrate. The glass walls, the water and the chemicals all take some light. The blank's reading is set to A = 0, so what is left is the nitrate's share. That works because absorbances add.

\begin{aligned}&\text{standards } 0,\ 2,\ 5,\ 10 \text{ mg/L read } 0.000,\\ &\quad \ 0.081,\ 0.203,\ 0.405\end{aligned} @@ a straight line through 0, slope 0.0405 per mg/L
\text{a well reads } 0.567 \Rightarrow \tfrac{0.567}{0.0405} = 14 \text{ mg/L} @@ above the limit of 10

The path across a round tube

A square cell has flat, parallel walls, 1.00 cm apart inside. Every ray that comes in straight crosses exactly 1.00 cm of liquid. Older classroom instruments, and many field kits, take round tubes instead. Seen from above, the inside of the tube is a circle of radius r. A ray is a line. It passes the centre at some distance d, and the liquid it crosses is a chord.

Chord technique: a ray that passes the centre of a round tube of inner radius r at distance d crosses a path ℓ(d) = 2√(r² − d²) of liquid when d < r, and none when d ≥ r.

\begin{aligned}&\text{the ray is a line at}\\ &\quad\text{distance } d \text{ from the centre } O\end{aligned} @@ the tube's inside, from above, is the circle of radius r about O
d \lt r:\ \text{it meets the circle at } F \pm \sqrt{r^2 - d^2}\,e @@ line-meets-circle (18d), with F the foot of the perpendicular and |e| = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\ell(d) = 2\sqrt{r^2 - d^2}} @@ distance-laws: the two points differ by 2√(r² − d²) e
d = r:\ \text{one point};\ \ d \gt r:\ \text{none} \quad \blacksquare @@ line-meets-circle: a tangent line crosses no liquid, and a ray past it misses

The longest path is the one through the centre, 2r. A ray that slips a little off centre loses very little. Small-slip technique: for a slip of u = d/r, with 0 ≤ u ≤ 1, the path falls short of 2r by a fraction between u²/2 and u².

\tfrac{\ell(d)}{2r} = \sqrt{1 - u^2} @@ chord technique; take r out of the root
\big(1 - \tfrac{u^2}{2}\big)^2 = 1 - u^2 + \tfrac{u^4}{4} \ge 1 - u^2 @@ square-of-a-difference (5i); square-never-negative
\sqrt{1 - u^2} \le 1 - \tfrac{u^2}{2} @@ powers-keep-order (8f), both sides not negative
s = 1 - u^2 \in [0, 1] \Rightarrow s^2 \le s \Rightarrow s \le \sqrt{s} @@ multiply s ≤ 1 by s ≥ 0; then powers-keep-order
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{u^2}{2} \le 1 - \tfrac{\ell(d)}{2r} \le u^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines, taken from 1
  
r = 0.60,\ d = 0.06:\ \ \ell = 2\sqrt{0.36 - 0.0036} \approx 1.194 \text{ cm} @@ a slip of a tenth of the radius: half a percent short
r = 0.60,\ d = 0.30:\ \ \ell = 2\sqrt{0.27} \approx 1.039 \text{ cm} @@ a slip of half the radius: 13% short

Many paths, one reading

A real beam is not one ray. It is a band of rays, each crossing its own chord, and the detector adds up the light they let through. It cannot add absorbances. So what does it read? Take the simplest case: two rays carrying equal light, with absorbances A₁ and A₂ along their paths.

Two-ray technique: two rays read together give an absorbance no more than the average of their own, and equal to it only when the two are equal. In marks: −log₁₀((10−A₁ + 10−A₂)/2) ≤ (A₁ + A₂)/2.

u = 10^{-A_1},\ v = 10^{-A_2}:\ \ \text{light out} = \tfrac{u + v}{2} @@ each ray carries half the light in; the detector adds what comes out
(\sqrt u - \sqrt v)^2 \ge 0 \Rightarrow \tfrac{u + v}{2} \ge \sqrt{uv} @@ square-never-negative; square-of-a-difference
\sqrt{uv} = 10^{-\frac{A_1 + A_2}{2}} @@ real-exponent: powers of 10 multiply by adding exponents
\htmlClass{new}{A_{\text{read}} = -\log_{10}\tfrac{u + v}{2} \le \tfrac{A_1 + A_2}{2}} @@ log₁₀ rises (log-base-p), so −log₁₀ turns order about
\text{equal} \iff \sqrt u = \sqrt v \iff A_1 = A_2 \quad \blacksquare @@ a square is 0 only at 0; 10ˣ is one-to-one
  
\text{paths } 1.20 \text{ and } 0.72 \text{ cm, average } 0.96 @@ chord technique
\varepsilon c = 0.25 \text{ per cm}:\ \ A_{\text{read}} \approx 0.236 \text{ against } 0.240 @@ 2% low
\varepsilon c = 1 \text{ per cm}:\ \ A_{\text{read}} \approx 0.897 \text{ against } 0.960 @@ 7% low
\varepsilon c = 3 \text{ per cm}:\ \ A_{\text{read}} \approx 2.45 \text{ against } 2.88 @@ 15% low: the darker the sample, the more the short path wins

So a round tube with a wide beam does not give a straight line in c. At low c the reading sits close to the line. As c grows, the reading falls further below it, and never above. The two-ray technique says which way the error runs before a single sample is read.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The thin-layer law is a measured rule, and it comes with its range. It is true for dilute solutions, roughly below 0.01 mol/L. It is true for light of one wavelength, in a clear liquid that scatters nothing, with every ray crossing the same path. Past each edge it fails, and in a known direction. Crowded molecules change one another's colour. A band of wavelengths, or a band of paths, mixes strong and weak absorbers, and the two-ray technique shows that the mix reads low. Above about A = 2, so little light gets through that stray light inside the instrument swamps it, and the reading flattens.

State the rule, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

Nearly every way this law fails makes the reading fall below the straight line. A chemist who sees a reading above the line looks for a mistake, not a new law.

A sample reads A = 0.50, and the same sample made twice as strong reads 1.00. Made ten times as strong, it reads 3.4. Which way did the reading leave the line, and would you change the law or the sample?

Who said it first

Bouguer reported that light loses equal fractions of itself in passing through equal thicknesses of the same transparent matter, so that the losses go on in a progression of multiplying, not of adding.

Pierre Bouguer, Essai d’optique sur la gradation de la lumière (1729); paraphrase

In 18·chem.4.4, the scientist of 1729 is Pierre Bouguer, the one of 1760 is Johann Heinrich Lambert, in his Photometria, and the one of 1852 is August Beer. Many books now say the Beer–Lambert law, some the Bouguer–Lambert–Beer law.

Accepted names: the absorbance technique is the Beer–Lambert law.
