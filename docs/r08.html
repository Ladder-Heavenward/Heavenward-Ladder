Rung 8 · theory

Drawing lines

Rung 5 built the fractions and left them in a strange state. They are crowded: between any two there is a third. And they are full of holes. 5e split all the fractions into a lower part and an upper part with no fraction at the split. The split sat where a number multiplied by itself would give 2. This rung fills every such hole with one idea. Then it proves the property that the rest of the ladder leans on: every collection of numbers that has something above it all has a least such thing. In this section we teach you what it means to say that the line has no holes, and what you can do because you know it.

You have met this number before, on a calculator that printed a row of digits and stopped. You knew the digits went on. Now you can build what they go on toward. This is less “you know this” and more “you can do this”: everything here is made from fractions you already own.

A number is the set of fractions below it. Order is being a part of. The least thing above a whole heap of numbers is just all their fractions merged into one set.

What it means to say the line has no holes

Start with what went wrong. On 5e you proved that no fraction multiplied by itself gives 2 (the no-fraction-squares-to-2 technique). You also proved that among the positive fractions whose square is below 2 there is no greatest (the no-greatest-below technique). So sort every fraction into two parts. The lower part holds those that are negative or square to less than 2. The upper part holds the rest. Then every fraction lands on one side, and everything in the lower part is below everything in the upper part. The lower part has no greatest member, and the upper part has no least. The split is clean, and at the split there is nothing. That is what 5e called a hole. There are endlessly many others: one wherever a number would have to square to 3, or to 5, or multiply by itself three times to give 2.

A collection of numbers has no holes when every split of it into a lower part and an upper part, everything below everything, has a number of the collection standing exactly at the split. The claim of this rung is that there is such a collection. It contains the fractions, with their adding, multiplying and order unchanged, and it has no holes. Its members are the real numbers. The ladder will call the collection the line, because that is what everyone calls it. 8a says in so many words what that name does and does not mean. It is a word for an ordered set, and its pictures are pictures of order, left for less and right for greater. No shape is meant. Shapes wait for geometry, many rungs up.

Where it comes from

In the autumn of 1858 a man was teaching the calculus at the polytechnic in Zürich. He found that he could not prove, from anything he had, facts his students were asked to hold in trust. He traced the trouble to the numbers themselves. Everyone pictured them along a line and assumed the line had no gaps. But nobody had said what having no gaps meant, let alone proved it. His answer, published in 1872, has two halves. First, say exactly what it means: every splitting has a number at the split. Second, notice that the fractions fail this, and turn the failure into a construction. If a splitting of the fractions has no fraction at it, make a new number out of the splitting itself.

The ladder takes the second half and makes it exact with tools you already own. A splitting is fixed by its lower part, so let the lower part be the number. A real number will be a set of fractions with four properties, the shape of a lower part. It has a member. It does not have every fraction. It is closed downward. And it has no greatest member. Such a set is called a cut. Every fraction gives one, the set of fractions below it. The hole of 5e gives one too, and it is the cut of no fraction at all. The set of all cuts is sifted from the set of all subsets of the fractions, by rung 2's tools, and nothing new has to be assumed. A real number is a set of fractions. A fraction is a class of pairs of integers. An integer is a class of pairs of counting numbers. A counting number is a set built from the empty set. The whole tower stands on rung 2.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things, in this order. A real number is a cut, and one real is at most another when its fractions are among the other's: order is being a part of, rung 2's ⊆. Any two reals can be compared: one is at most the other. The proof splits on whether the first is a part of the second, which nobody may be able to check. So it uses rung 7's either-way principle, and 8b shows that the comparison and that principle are equally strong. Anything with an upper bound has a least upper bound: take the union of the heap, one set, and the union is a cut, the least one above them all. Counting eventually passes any real. And two has a square root on the line: a real number whose square is exactly 2.

Between the third and the fifth sits the arithmetic. To say “the square of this real is 2”, you need to multiply reals. To say “counting passes it” in the useful form, you need to subtract them. So 8d defines adding, negatives, multiplying and reciprocals of cuts, each proved to give a cut. Then it proves 5c's twelve techniques for the reals. Some reduce at once to the rules for fractions, and some need care with signs. Rung 5 said that whatever it proved from those twelve techniques alone would be true in any collection obeying them; 8d is where that promise is collected. The reals then obey the twelve techniques with no holes, and 8d proves it.

What you can do because you know it

You can name numbers that fractions could only approach: the number whose square is 2, the one whose square is 3, the one of which twelve, multiplied together, give 2. Each is the least upper bound of the fractions that fall short of it, and each exists because no-holes says so. You can compare any two of them, add, subtract, multiply and divide them, with every rule you learned for fractions. You can solve every equation of the form tx² + bx + c = 0 that has an answer, and say exactly when it has none. You can measure how far apart two numbers are. So you can say how near 7/5 comes to the number whose square is 2, which 5e had to leave as a question of size. You can read an endless decimal as the name of one real number, and know which endless decimals name fractions: exactly those that end in a repeating block.

The technique pages make the hands fast. 8g is inequalities: moving terms across <, the one tech that turns the order about, and charts of signs, kept as tables. 8h is absolute value, the size of a number without its sign. With it comes distance on the line, defined for the line alone, and the inequality that keeps its old geometric name. 8i is roots and powers with fractions in the high place. Then comes the rewriting called completing the square, and from it the recipe that solves every quadratic, the quadratic formula. 8·phys aims all of this at the world. It gives powers of ten below one, and the scientific way of writing very large and very small sizes. It gives how many figures a measurement may honestly claim, and the art of estimating an answer before you know how to find it. The two call backs complete old foreclosures. 8·cb5 returns to 5e's holes and to 5g's endless division. 8·cb6 returns to 6·phys, where your bass was tuned with numbers measured off a tuner, and computes them.

That is where the line starts for you. Nobody has finished finding out what the reals hold, and you have only just arrived.

And 8·comp asks why a computer finds 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3: one tenth has no ending numeral in twos, so the machine keeps a nearby fraction, and 8h's distance technique bounds how far its sums can stray. It carries a box on the cloud, stored.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down: no axiom on this rung. Every real is built from the fractions by sifting and the power set, and every principle is proved. The price is paid elsewhere. Rung 7's either-way principle is used in the very first comparison of two reals. It is used again in the proof that counting passes them. So the reals of this ladder are classical. A constructive reader, of the kind 7a described, builds the reals differently and finds that two of them need not compare. You will see exactly where the either-way principle enters, line by line, and 8b proves that it cannot be avoided for the comparison. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say the line has no holes. You will know why the fractions had them, and what you can do on a line that has none.

Who said it first

The one idea that fills every hole is Richard Dedekind’s. The man teaching at the polytechnic in Zürich in 1858 was Dedekind, and the answer published in 1872 is his Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen.

Dedekind wrote that he expected most readers to be disappointed to learn that the secret of a line without gaps lay in so ordinary a remark: that when a line is split into two parts, everything of the one to the left of everything of the other, exactly one place of it does the splitting. The remark is plain. Turning it into a definition is the whole idea.

Dedekind, Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen (1872), §III; in English as Continuity and Irrational Numbers, trans. W. W. Beman (1901)

Accepted names: a cut is a Dedekind cut; the either-way principle is the law of excluded middle; “anything with an upper bound has a least upper bound” is the least-upper-bound property.
