Is a Distance a Kind of Arrow?

Is a distance a kind of arrow? Put a number on each pair of points and ask only that going by way of a third point costs at least the direct number. That is a category in which the arrows are numbers and running in a row is adding, so the detour rule of rung 34 is composing, and a map that does not stretch is a map of such categories. Rung 47’s arrows-into technique becomes the fact that a point is fixed by its distances to all the others. And a finite space gets a number, its magnitude, that counts how many points it effectively has: two points close together count as about one, far apart as two. Watching that count as the space is scaled up recovers the dimension of rung 35. Magnitude was defined from 2008 to 2013, and computed for balls of odd dimension in 2018. The magnitude of a disc in the plane was still open in the sources checked.

Read d(x, y) as an arrow x → y worth a number: the detour rule d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) is composing, d(x, x) = 0 the do-nothing. Maps of such categories are short maps, d(f x, f y) ≤ d(x, y). x ↦ d(−, x) keeps distances exactly. For a finite space with Zij = e−d(i,j), a weighting has Zw = (1, …, 1) and the magnitude is Σ wᵢ: 2/(1 + e−d) for two points at distance d.

inf9.1Distances as Arrows

34a1 gave a distance four rules: never negative, 0 exactly between a point and itself, the same both ways, and a detour never shortens: d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z). Now read d(x, y) as the one arrow from x to y, carrying a number. Running two arrows in a row adds their numbers, and the rule demands only that the direct arrow cost no more than the sum.

Detour-is-composing technique: the detour rule and d(x, x) = 0 are exactly 47a1’s demands, running in a row and the do-nothing arrow, for a category whose arrows carry numbers and in which “at most” stands for “is”.

\text{arrows } y \to z \text{ then } x \to y \text{ give } x \to z @@ running in a row
d(x, z) \le d(y, z) + d(x, y) @@ the composite costs no more than the two: the detour rule
0 \ge d(x, x) @@ the do-nothing arrow costs nothing
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a distance is a category with arrows worth numbers}} \quad \blacksquare @@ brackets cannot matter, since adding numbers ignores them

The other two rules are extra. Drop “the same both ways” and a one-way street gives a category too; drop “0 only for x itself” and two points can be at distance 0 without being one. The arrows keep only composing and the do-nothing.

inf9.2Maps That Do Not Stretch

A map of such categories must send each arrow x → y to an arrow f(x) → f(y) worth no more: d(f(x), f(y)) ≤ d(x, y). Such a map is short: it never stretches a distance.

Short-row technique: short maps in a row are short, and the do-nothing map is short; so spaces with a distance and short maps form a category.

d(g(f(x)), g(f(y))) \le d(f(x), f(y)) @@ g is short, at the points f(x), f(y)
d(f(x), f(y)) \le d(x, y) @@ f is short
\htmlClass{new}{d(g f x,\ g f y) \le d(x, y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two in a row; and id gives d(x, y) ≤ d(x, y)

Reading the plane’s point (x₁, x₂) as x₁ on the line is short for the straight distance, since |x₁ − y₁| ≤ √((x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)²). Doubling, x ↦ 2x on the line, is continuous but not short.

inf9.3A Point Is Its Distances

47g2’s arrows-into technique fixed a thing by the arrows into it. Here the arrows into x are the numbers d(z, x), one for each z. Send x to the function d(−, x) on the space, and measure two such functions by their largest gap, as 24c measured functions.

Distances-to technique: the largest gap between d(−, x) and d(−, y) is exactly d(x, y): a point is placed, distances and all, by its distances to every point.

|d(z, x) - d(z, y)| \le d(x, y) \text{ for every } z @@ 34a1’s back-triangle technique
z = y:\ |d(y, x) - 0| = d(x, y) @@ the gap is reached at y
\htmlClass{new}{\sup_z |d(z, x) - d(z, y)| = d(x, y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a bound that is reached is the largest

So every space with a distance sits inside a space of functions without any distance changing. Taking the limits there of rows that bunch up fills the space’s holes: that is how the rationals are filled out to the line, said with arrows. The filling is told here, not proved.

inf9.4How Many Points, Effectively

For a finite space with points 1, …, n, make the grid Z with Zij = e−d(i,j): 1 on the diagonal, near 1 for close points, near 0 for far ones. A weighting is a list w with Zw = (1, …, 1). The magnitude is Σ wᵢ.

Two-point technique: two points at distance d have magnitude 2/(1 + e−d); three points each at distance d from the others have magnitude 3/(1 + 2e−d).

Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & q \\ q & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ q = e^{-d} @@ two points
w_1 + q\,w_2 = 1,\ \ q\,w_1 + w_2 = 1 \Rightarrow w_1 = w_2 = \tfrac{1}{1 + q} @@ subtract: (1 − q)(w₁ − w₂) = 0, and q < 1 for d > 0
\text{three points: } w_i + 2q\,w_i = 1 \Rightarrow w_i = \tfrac{1}{1 + 2q} @@ by symmetry each row reads the same
\htmlClass{new}{|\{a, b\}| = \tfrac{2}{1 + e^{-d}},\quad |\{a, b, c\}| = \tfrac{3}{1 + 2e^{-d}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the weights

At d = 1 two points have magnitude about 1.46. Close together they count as nearly one point; far apart, nearly two.

inf9.5Scaling Up

Scale a space by t: multiply every distance by t. The magnitude changes with t, and its change says something about the space.

Scale-count technique: for two points, and for three at equal distances, the magnitude of the space scaled by t runs from 1 as t → 0 to the number of points as t → ∞.

\tfrac{2}{1 + e^{-td}} \to \tfrac{2}{1 + 1} = 1 \ \text{ as } t \to 0 @@ e⁰ = 1: all points at one place
\tfrac{2}{1 + e^{-td}} \to 2 \ \text{ as } t \to \infty @@ e^{−td} → 0
\tfrac{3}{1 + 2e^{-td}} \to 1 \text{ and } \to 3 @@ the same for three
\htmlClass{new}{\text{from 1 point to all the points}} \quad \blacksquare @@ magnitude counts points at the scale they are seen

For shapes, the growth of magnitude with t gives dimension. A segment of length L has magnitude 1 + L/2, so scaled by t it grows like t: dimension 1, as rung 35’s box count found by covering with smaller and smaller boxes. For shapes in higher dimensions the growth goes as t to the dimension. These are told here, from the sources, not proved.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1973 a category theorist observed that a space with a distance is a category whose arrows are numbers, with adding as running in a row, so that short maps, completion and many facts about distances become facts about categories of this kind.

From 2008 a mathematician defined a count for finite categories that agrees with the Euler count of rung 34 for shapes, and carried it to spaces with a distance, where it became the magnitude. Ecologists had already met the same number: in measuring how many species a place effectively has, with near relatives counted as partly the same.

Magnitude was computed for many spaces: for a segment, for odd-dimensional balls in 2015-2018, and asymptotically for others. In 2017 a homology was defined whose counts add up to the magnitude, and it has since been studied for graphs and for spaces.

The accepted names

A category with arrows worth numbers is a Lawvere metric space, a category enriched in [0, ∞]; short maps are 1-Lipschitz or non-expansive; x ↦ d(−, x) is the Yoneda embedding (here, the Kuratowski embedding), and the filling is the Cauchy completion. The magnitude is due to Leinster; the grid Z is the similarity matrix; the growth exponent is the Minkowski dimension, recovered by magnitude. Magnitude homology is a categorification of magnitude.

Who said it first

F. William Lawvere, “Metric spaces, generalized logic, and closed categories” (1973). Tom Leinster, the Euler characteristic of a category (2008) and “The magnitude of metric spaces” (2013); Tom Leinster and Simon Willerton, asymptotic magnitude (2013). Juan Antonio Barceló and Anthony Carbery, magnitude of odd balls (2015 preprint; 2018); Simon Willerton (2017-2020). Richard Hepworth and Simon Willerton, magnitude homology of graphs (2017); Tom Leinster and Michael Shulman, magnitude homology of metric spaces (2017).
