introduction

How to read the ladder

Two directions at once

The ladder always goes in two directions. It tries to make the concepts of mathematics more legible in English. And it tries to show the techniques in the more rigorous writing system of mathematics.

It works in pairs the same way. It works the small case and the large case. It works the series and the arrangement. It works the plan and the behaviour. None of these is ever finished, and none is ever worked alone.

The ladder is for everyone, and a page that is too long for you is the right length for someone else. Skim what you already have, and slow down where you don’t.

Why it is worth it

Everything is possible to guess. That's not a contradiction, that's math. It sounds confusing. Don't be put off by a little confusion, confusion is a sign you've found something interesting. Mathematicians seek confusing things and learn what it takes to intuit the truth.
Math can tell you x is 2 and 2 so x is 4 because it's obvious, but it's only obvious because of a long history of unraveling the confusion at the edge of observable reality and knitting something true and intuitive from it. From here we train your intuition, and you wouldn't believe how far it goes if you were just told. You have to see it to believe it.

Sometimes the confusion can be daunting, but look at it this way: math is already the most challenging course you'll have year on year, but if you climb the ladder, you'll quickly find that greater effort means greater height.

If you take the ladder to the limit, you'll climb alongside the people who actually wrote the books on the subject, and find they are beginners too, a little further up a ladder with no top. There are few enough influential figures, writing each in their own ways, that their innovations stay with us, but each of them have their quirks and you will too. Unnoticed quirks or deliberate style are normal to develop over the course of your climb, there's no one way to write anything. Don't let it slip, inconsistency and quirks are not the same thing. Your peers can't tell inconsistency from being sloppy, or being wrong, and their style is to assume the latter.

The ladder teaches the guesswork and the technique, not what has been observed. The annexes show the techniques at work in the real world, in physics, chemistry, biology and more, but they do not hand you the observations themselves. For those you still need a science lab.
Don't be fooled: the ladder says everything plainly, everything examined, but if you think you know what is being said, be bold, push through.

The ladder is for every learner and every student, and those words never stop applying to you.

Two words for where a line comes from

A line you start from is an axiom. It says how things are, it is laid down, and everything above it rests on it. It is earned from above: take it away, and the machine breaks. A line you build is a technique, earned from below, by its proof. Once a technique has been earned, you may use it again as a single move, and it saves you the lines it took to earn it.

Why keep to two words? Because the books use many: theorem, law, rule, principle, postulate, lemma, formula. On any page you read, each of them means a line that was laid down or a line that was earned, and the difference matters more than the word. An axiom can always be narrowed to what breaks without it, and a technique can always be asked for its proof. A principle is the big idea a rung exists to deliver this isn't part of the guesswork, it's just one of the things the guesswork can show, a big thing about the guessing rather than about the thing were guessing in.

Ways to reach a conclusion

Guesses are called here by their fancy name: induction. Induction can be done over a thing, literally guessing how it works. The ladder covers two methods, two input types, and two output types.

Induction over an arrangement is also called deduction, and it's the subject of formal logic, and the beginning of the ladder.

Induction over a series is also called mathematics, and it is the delivery method for the ladder.

Induction under the small case, also called science, is covered in the annexes, but nothing beats an experiment in a real lab.

Induction under the large case is called statistics, and it's the most intense kind of guessing there is.

Induction into the plan: how a design or a procedure might change the following steps. Higher education calls this abduction.
Induction into action: which meal to order, what speed to drive. This is transduction: the other side of the guess, where the result is part of the series, part of the world, and not a method for understanding it.

Natural induction is what you do before you've trained the areas of induction.

There are two ways to be careful, and they are not the same. The first is care about the shape of an argument. Its rules work whether or not anything real fits the shape: “every dragon breathes fire; this is a dragon; so it breathes fire” is a correct move with no dragons anywhere. One counterexample kills a rule, and a proof ends the question. The second is care about things that are there but can't be caught. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre.

How the ladder is built

Floors. The ladder has eight floors, each a run of rungs with one kind of guesswork at its heart: Logic, Analysis, Derivation, Geometry, Statistics, Mathematics, Advanced, and the Endless floor, which has no top.

Rungs. Each rung is a claim about how things are.

Steps. Each rung has steps, numbered with the rung.

Footings. A step is built in footings, a single move.

Questions. Every footing has a question, every step has a question pool for the laundry, a randomized practice, and a pool of flash cards.

Challenges and stars. Past the footings are challenge questions. Star a rung’s required steps and the rung collapses into a star.

The basement. Below each lesson’s bottom bar is its basement.

Certification. Once a step’s required questions are ticked, a gold certification button appears. Certification is a short sitting of fresh questions. .
