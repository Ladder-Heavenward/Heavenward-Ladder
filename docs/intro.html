introduction

How to read the ladder

Two directions at once

The ladder always goes in two directions. It tries to make the writing system of mathematics more legible in English. And it tries to make the techniques more rigorous in the writing system of mathematics. Every page pulls both ways: plain words toward the marks, and the marks back toward plain words.

It works in pairs the same way. It works the small case and the large case. It works the series and the arrangement. It works the plan and the behaviour. None of these is ever finished, and none is ever worked alone.

So the ladder is always caught between words, and the only way out is more words. Some sections go on long after you have the idea. That is on purpose. The ladder is for everyone, and a page that is too long for you is the right length for someone else. Skim what you already have, and slow down where you don’t.

Why it is worth it

Everything is possible to guess. That's not a contradiction, that's math. It sounds confusing. Don't be put off by a little confusion, confusion is a sign you've found something interesting. Mathematicians seek confusing things and learn what it takes to intuit the truth. Math can tell you x is 2 and 2 so x is 4 because it's obvious, but it's only obvious because of a long history of unraveling the confusion at the edge of observable reality and knitting something true and intuitive from it. From here we train your intuition, and you wouldn't believe how far it goes if you were just told. You have to see it to believe it.

Sometimes the confusion can be daunting, but look at it this way: math is already the most challenging course you'll have year on year, but if you climb the ladder, you'll quickly find that greater effort means greater height.

If you take the ladder to the limit, you'll climb alongside the people who actually wrote the books on the subject, and find they are beginners too, a little further up a ladder with no top. There are few enough influential figures, writing each in their own ways, that their innovations stay with us, but each of them have their quirks and you will too. Unnoticed quirks or deliberate style are normal to develop over the course of your climb, there's no one way to write anything. Don't let it slip, inconsistency and quirks are not the same thing. Your peers can't tell inconsistency from being sloppy, or being wrong, and their style is to assume the latter.

The ladder teaches the guesswork and the technique, not what has been observed. The annexes show the techniques at work in the real world, in physics, chemistry, biology and more, but they do not hand you the observations themselves. For those you still need a science lab.
Don't be fooled: the ladder says everything plainly, everything examined, but if you think you know what is being said, be bold, push through.

The ladder is for every learner and every student, and those words never stop applying to you.

Two words for where a line comes from

Every line you will ever write on the ladder stands on something. Either it is a line you start from, because there is nothing beneath it to stand on, or it is a line you earn, by moves from lines you already have. The ladder keeps one word for each, and only one.

A line you start from is an axiom. It says how things are, it is laid down rather than earned, and everything above it rests on it. A line you earn is a technique. Once a technique has been earned, you may use it again as a single move, and it saves you the lines it took to earn it. Even the famous ones are techniques. By the time you meet the one about the sides of a right triangle, it will be one tech in a kit that would have made its first owners dizzy.

Why keep to two words? Because the books use many: theorem, law, rule, principle, postulate, lemma, formula. On any page you read, each of them means a line that was laid down or a line that was earned, and the difference matters more than the word. An axiom can only be accepted or refused, and a technique can always be asked for its proof. The ladder keeps two more words, each for one job. A principle is the big idea a rung exists to deliver, whether it is laid down or earned. A law, from 5d on, is a regularity measured in the world, with its range and its regular exceptions. In an argument it sits where an axiom sits: you lay it down and build techniques on it. But it is earned, as a technique is, and earned by measuring, not by proof. So it stays a guess, kept to a known rate of failure. The function written for a law describes the regularity; the regularity itself is in the world. The other words are kept below the lessons, in the basement, with a note on which of these each one is.

Ways to reach a conclusion

Guesses are called here by their fancy name: induction. Induction can be done over a thing, literally guessing how it works. The ladder covers two methods, two input types, and two output types.

Induction over an arrangement is also called deduction, and it's the subject of formal logic, and the beginning of the ladder.

Induction over a series is also called mathematics, and it is the delivery method for the ladder.

Induction under the small case, also called science, is covered in the annexes, but nothing beats an experiment in a real lab.

Induction under the large case is called statistics, and it's the most intense kind of guessing there is.

Induction into the plan: how a design or a procedure might change the following steps. Higher education calls this abduction.
Induction into action: which meal to order, what speed to drive. This is transduction: the other side of the guess, where the result is part of the series, part of the world, and not a method for understanding it.

Each has its limitations, summarized as follows.

Induction over the arrangement guesses that lines set in a certain order hold together. Its promise is a conditional: if the lines at the top are true, the line at the foot is too. It says nothing about the top. And an arrangement can't be turned around. Turn it and it means something else: “if it rains, the street is wet” is not “if the street is wet, it rained”. So what is really being guessed at is the arrangement itself, and each arrangement says its own thing. It is the only way a line is earned on the ladder. Natural induction is what you do before you've trained the areas of induction.

Induction over the series is built from the parts of induction over the arrangement. Show a claim for the first case, show that it passes from each case to the next, and you have it for every case in the series, as far as the series runs. The counting numbers never end, and neither does the claim. The books call it mathematical induction. It reaches as far as the series does and no further: where the cases don't come one after another, each made from the one before, there is nothing for it to pass along. Time is a series too, each moment following the last, so induction over the series has a special affinity for things that change with time.

Induction under small cases is the guess of science. You look at a small case, a little cluster of results that points to one rule with its exceptions, and guess the rule. An experiment lets you repeat a small case on purpose. Every bus you caught this week was late, so the buses on your route run late. A rule like that can be very good. The rule for how a gas presses on its jar holds for the air in your tyres, and the old rule for how things move held for centuries. But a rule from small cases has to account for every condition in which the case varies. Squeeze the gas hard enough and cold enough, and its rule slips. Go fast enough, near the speed of light, and the old rule for motion needs a new one. A rule from small cases is as good as the conditions it has met.

Induction under large cases is the guess about a crowd, because most things vary. No two buses run quite the same, so the guess isn't about each bus. It's about all of them: most run late, by about this much. Instead of one rule for the case, it finds which situations vary a lot, and so which outcomes are probable, with words like fit, spread and distribution. This is statistics. It can tell you a great deal about the crowd, and it has no idea what any one member is doing. Said back in plain language, what it finds is natural induction: induction about the general state of things. The bus you are waiting for is not the average bus.

Which tool answers which question. A proof answers questions about the arrangement: does this line follow from those? Its answer is true, or false, or no answer at all, when the lines above can’t reach it. An equation answers questions within the series: is this the same as that, or more, or less? A function gives you single values, one input and its output at a time. The rates between functions give you techniques, each a claim about every input at once. So when a question wants true, false or nothing, it is probably about an arrangement. When it wants an equality or an inequality, it is about the series.

There are two ways to be careful, and they are not the same. The first is care about the shape of an argument. Its rules work whether or not anything real fits the shape: “every dragon breathes fire; this is a dragon; so it breathes fire” is a correct move with no dragons anywhere. One counterexample kills a rule, and a proof ends the question. The second is care about things that are there but can't be caught. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre. Here measurement takes the place of proof. An exception marks the edge of a range instead of killing the rule. The question never closes; it narrows. Both are rigorous: the first means no gaps in the chain, and the second means stating, in numbers, how wrong you can be.

Induction into the plan is guessing how a design or a procedure will change the steps that follow. A mechanic does it choosing which part to replace, and you will do it every time you look at the foot of a proof and guess which line could lead there. A plan holds up only as well as the world holds still. As it rolls out, it frays with every irregularity it meets, and it is never a line until an arrangement checks it.

Induction into action is the other side of the guesswork: not the guess, but the doing. You order the meal; you drive at a speed. Here you carry the guesses yourself. Embodying them means taking their reflection of reality back into the world, and you succeed or fail with them. Over days, and then over a lifetime, the guesses you live by shape what you notice first and which line you reach for. No page can hold it. The moment you describe a performance, you are back to the plan. Induction into the plan and into action have logic attached too; taken together, it is called analysis. As your guesswork becomes more developed, you will see how much you can really do.

So every kind of duction is a guess: induction over the arrangement, over the series, under small cases and under large cases, into the plan and into action. They are one kind of thing, and the ladder speaks of them in one language on purpose. But they are guesswork we have worked just as hard as any other material: rough timber sawed and planed into smooth lumber, rocks mined and smelted into iron, sand filtered and processed into computers, guesses proofed and argued into techniques. A craft is only as good as its tools, but a poor crafter blames their tools.

How the ladder is built

Floors. The ladder has eight floors, each a run of rungs with one kind of guesswork at its heart: Logic, Analysis, Derivation, Geometry, Statistics, Mathematics, Advanced, and the Endless floor, which has no top. A floor’s first page tells you what the floor is for. Its last page, the floor conclusion, restates the floor’s principles one to a rung, in the words and marks the floor built.

Rungs. Each rung is a claim about how things are, or a skill built on one, written in plain words at the top of its page. The rung’s own page is a survey: what the claim means, where it comes from, and what you can do because you know it. It has no exercises. Its conclusion page restates the claim in the marks once the steps have earned it.

Steps. Beneath each rung hang its steps, numbered with the rung and a small letter in turn: 0a, 0b, 0c. The steps carry the work. A step proves the rung’s claim, or builds a technique, or supports one. It teaches the techs the proof is made of, and it shows the tempting moves that are not allowed.

Footings. A step is built in footings, numbered in order: 5a1, 5a2, and on. Each footing does one thing. It says what it stands on, says the new thing in plain words, shows it in the marks, and gives you slides to push through at your own speed. Most footings end with a question.

Questions. Every step has three required questions, one of each kind. A do question asks you to use what the step built. An undo question asks you to run it backwards. A bend question asks you to push it somewhere new. Ticking all three finishes the step. The questions at the foot of a step do not review the page. They are plain, hard, and kept sharp, like good steel, and they push at the edge of what the ladder has built so far. You answer them in chat, with a tutor who points you back to the page and never does the work for you.

Challenges and stars. Past the required questions are the star questions and the challenges. A star question asks you to make something: a proof, a build, an argument. The last challenge on a step, its closer, often sets you inside one of the great arguments of the field’s history, and lets you take a side. Star a rung’s required steps and the rung wears a crown.

That is what the Illegal moves section near the foot of every lesson is for. It shows you how the guesses we make break apart in certain conditions: the move that looks right, the place it fails, and why. Each one marks an edge, so that you know where a guess is true and where it is not.

Green boxes. Beside the proofs, some steps carry a green box headed “Anexact, yet rigorous”. It shows the second kind of care: the kind that measures how wrong a guess might be instead of proving it. Red is kept for illegal moves, and green for these boxes, and for nothing else.

Flash cards. Each step has a deck, opened from the flash-card button in its bottom bar. A card shows an example and asks what is happening in it, or what it is called, with four answers to choose from and a one-line reason after you pick. The cards for a whole rung link together as you open its steps.

The basement. Below each lesson’s bottom bar is its basement. Push past the foot of the page and it opens. The basement keeps the academy’s names for things, the history and the people, and the worked wrong proofs. Nothing in it is needed to climb, and everything in it is true.

Certification. Once a step’s required questions are ticked, a gold certification button appears. Certification is a short sitting of fresh questions. Sit it under a proctor and it becomes a green check. Sit it on your own and it becomes a gold star. The last step of a rung carries the rung’s certification, and a floor’s certification sits beside its conclusion: one fresh question a rung, about an hour in all, covering the floor’s last rung too. Passing a floor’s certification gives you a title and opens the floor above. Like every title on the ladder, it is a word for what you have begun, not for what you have finished.

Annexes. Beside the steps of a rung hang its annexes, each titled as the question it investigates: physics, chemistry, biology, computing, economics, engineering, logistics, navigation, cryptography and more. The annexes show the techniques at work in the world, and they are built from questions rather than statements. An annex has footings, questions, challenges and flash cards like a step. Most annexes are gifts, not gates: hidden until you go looking, and never needed to open anything. Two kinds are required. The statistics annexes carry induction under the large case from the start. The newsflash annexes show something from an earlier rung breaking through because of this one. At the far end of a rung, the research front shows where the rung’s ideas are being worked on today.

The laundry. The laundry is where you practise. It draws fresh questions from the steps you have opened: which technique licenses this line, and what fills this box. A question never comes twice. Choose its pools by step. Now and then a stray turns up from a pool you switched off, because questions off the ladder never stay penned in either.

The tray. Every page has a keys button in its bottom corner. Tap it and a tray of keys slides up, with the marks a keyboard can’t type. The mark goes into whatever box you last touched.

That is everything about how to read the ladder. The pages themselves tell you only what they hold. Go to rung 0, and start.
