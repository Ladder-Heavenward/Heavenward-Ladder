How Does the Ear Tell One Pitch From Another?

How does the ear tell one pitch from another? Inside the ear is a coiled tube about 35 mm long, and a sound shakes it hardest at one place, high notes near the door and low notes at the far end. This page lays that map down and finds that it is a logarithm, so the ear’s own ruler is 37b’s pitch line. Then it counts how long an ear can stand a loud room before the damage is for good, and follows a bat in a barn near Merced that hears its way to a moth by its echoes.

Along the inner ear each octave takes about the same 5 mm, so a pitch step is a fixed move. The ear tells pitches apart more finely than its cells are spaced, so place is not the whole story. Each 3 dB louder halves the safe time. A bat reads a distance from an echo’s delay and a speed from its shift.

37bio1A Map of Pitch Along a Tube

The inner ear is a tube of fluid coiled like a snail shell, split along its length by a strip, the shaking strip, stiff and narrow near the door from the middle ear and loose and wide at the far end. A sound shakes the whole strip, but each frequency shakes it hardest at one place. Laid down, measured on human ears, with its rate: at the share x of the way from the far end, the strip answers best to about f = 165.4(10^(2.1x) − 0.88) Hz.

Place-map technique: above a few hundred hertz, each octave takes about the same length of the strip, 5 mm of its 35. Proof: for large f the 0.88 is small, so f ≈ 165.4 · 10^(2.1x) and log₁₀ f grows evenly with x; a doubling of f is a step of log₁₀ 2 / 2.1 ≈ 0.143 in x, and 0.143 × 35 ≈ 5 mm.

x = 0:\ 165.4(1 - 0.88) \approx 20 \text{ Hz} @@ the far end
x = 0.5:\ 165.4(11.22 - 0.88) \approx 1710 \text{ Hz} @@ halfway
x = 1:\ 165.4(125.9 - 0.88) \approx 20\,700 \text{ Hz} @@ the door: 37b2’s window, end to end
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an octave every } 5 \text{ mm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the place-map technique

So the ear is built on 37b3’s pitch line: equal ratios are equal lengths. That is why a tune keeps its shape when it moves up a key: it moves along the strip, all of a piece.

37bio2Finer Than the Cells

Along the strip stand about 3500 inner hair cells in a row, about 100 to a millimetre, each sending to the brain how hard its place is shaking. Laid down, measured, varying across listeners: near 1000 Hz most people tell two tones apart when they differ by about 0.3 %, some 5 cents on 37b3’s line.

Place-shift technique: a pitch step of k cents moves the strip’s best place by about 5k/1200 mm. Proof: k cents is k/1200 of an octave (37b3), and by the place-map technique an octave is about 5 mm.

1200\log_2(1003/1000) \approx 5.2 \text{ cents} @@ the pitch line
5 \times 5.2 / 1200 \approx 0.022 \text{ mm} @@ the place-shift technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about two cells apart}} \quad \blacksquare @@ yet the two are told apart

The peak of the shaking is broad, spread over many cells, so a move of two cells is a slender clue. The ear has another: below about 4000 Hz its nerves fire in step with the wave, and the brain counts the rate of the repeat (37b1) as well as the place. That second clue is how the missing fundamental of 37j7 is heard: no place on the strip shakes at 100 Hz, but the nerves still fire 100 times a second.

37bio3How Long an Ear Can Take It

37c4 laid down that long hours of loud sound wear the ear down for good: the hair cells of the last footing break and do not grow back. Laid down, with its rate, from studies of workers: 85 dB for 8 hours a day is about as much as most ears take over a working life without loss.

Dose technique: if 85 dB is safe for 8 hours, then L dB is safe for 8 / 2^((L − 85)/3) hours. Proof: 3 dB is twice the intensity (37c2), and the line laid down counts the energy taken in, intensity times time; so each 3 dB louder halves the time.

L = 100:\ 8 / 2^5 = 0.25 \text{ h} @@ fifteen minutes
L = 110:\ 8 / 2^{25/3} \approx 0.025 \text{ h} @@ about a minute and a half
\htmlClass{new}{\text{earplugs taking } 20 \text{ dB}:\ 90 \text{ dB, about } 2.5 \text{ h}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 8 / 2^{5/3}

The loss starts at the door end of the strip, with the high notes, around 4000 Hz, where the ear is keenest (37c4). The first thing lost is often the hiss band of 37f, so speech grows muddy before it grows quiet.

37bio4Hearing in the Dark

A bat calls at about 40 000 Hz, too high for us, a wavelength of 343/40 000 ≈ 8.6 mm, small enough to bounce well from a moth. By the echo technique (37e4) the delay gives the distance: d = ct/2.

Returned-shift technique: a bat calling at f while flying at v towards a still wall hears the echo at f (c + v)/(c − v). Proof: the wall hears a moving source, f c/(c − v) by 24·phys; it sends that back as a still source to a listener moving towards it, who hears (c + v)/c times as much, by 37·phys2.

t = 6 \text{ ms}:\ d = 343 \times 0.006 / 2 \approx 1.03 \text{ m} @@ the echo technique
v = 5 \text{ m/s}:\ 40\,000 \times 348/338 \approx 41\,180 \text{ Hz} @@ the returned-shift technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{distance from the delay, speed from the shift}} \quad \blacksquare @@ both from one echo

Some bats lower their call as they speed up, so that the echo always comes back at the one pitch their ears are sharpest for: by the place-map technique, a stretch of their strip is spread wide over that pitch alone.

The accepted names

The coiled tube is the cochlea and the shaking strip the basilar membrane; the place map is tonotopy, and 165.4(10^(2.1x) − 0.88) is the Greenwood function. Pitch from place is the place theory; pitch from the firing rate is the temporal theory, and the smallest step heard is the just-noticeable difference. The 3 dB rule is the exchange rate of a noise exposure limit. Hearing by echoes is echolocation.

Who said it first

Hermann von Helmholtz proposed a place map in 1863. Georg von Békésy watched the travelling wave in the cochleas of cadavers and received the Nobel Prize in 1961, the year Donald Greenwood published his function. Lazzaro Spallanzani found in the 1790s that blinded bats still fly well and deafened ones do not; Donald Griffin recorded their calls with George Pierce around 1938 and named echolocation in 1944.

A proof that is not allowed

“The ear has about 3500 inner hair cells over ten octaves, so it tells about 350 pitches an octave and no more.” Not allowed: it takes each cell as a pitch, but a tone shakes many cells at once, and the brain reads the shape of the shaking and the rate of firing too; the count of cells is not a count of pitches.
