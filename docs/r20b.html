20b · proof

Distance-keepers form a group

Rung 19 asked three things of a family of moves: that two in a row make one, that each can be undone, and that doing nothing is one of them. This step checks them for the moves that keep distances. They pass, and so do the similarities. From now on everything rung 19 proved about groups is true of the moves of the plane.

If two moves keep distances, so does one done after the other, so does the do-nothing move, and so does the move that undoes either. So the isometries form a group; the similarities form a group too, with factors that multiply, and the isometries are a part-group of it.

20b1Two in a row

Compose-keeps technique: an isometry done after an isometry is an isometry. Each keeps every distance, so the two together do.

g \circ f \text{ is a move} @@ shuffles-compose (19a1), for shuffles of ℝ²
d(g(f(P)), g(f(Q))) = d(f(P), f(Q)) @@ g keeps distances, at the points f(P) and f(Q)
= d(P, Q) @@ f keeps distances
\htmlClass{new}{g \circ f \text{ is an isometry}} \quad \blacksquare
  
f(x, y) = (y, x),\ \ g(x, y) = (x + 2, y) @@ two isometries (20a1; a slide)
(g \circ f)(x, y) = (y + 2, x),\ \ (f \circ g)(x, y) = (y, x + 2) @@ different moves: the order matters

20b2Undoing

Undo-keeps technique: the undo of an isometry is an isometry. Put f⁻¹(P) and f⁻¹(Q) into f, which keeps their distance, and you get P and Q back.

f^{-1} \text{ is a move} @@ f is one-to-one and onto, so it runs backwards (9d)
d(f^{-1}P, f^{-1}Q) = d(f(f^{-1}P), f(f^{-1}Q)) @@ f keeps distances
= d(P, Q) @@ f ∘ f⁻¹ does nothing
\htmlClass{new}{f^{-1} \text{ is an isometry}} \quad \blacksquare

20b3The group

Isometries-form-a-group technique: the isometries of the plane form a group under composing. Composing regroups (9b), the do-nothing move keeps every distance, and 20b1 and 20b2 do the rest.

Similarities-form-a-group technique: the similarities form a group, and their factors multiply. If f has factor k and g has factor k′, then g ∘ f has factor k′k and f⁻¹ has factor 1/k. The isometries are its part-group of factor 1.

d(gfP, gfQ) = k'\,d(fP, fQ) = k'k\,d(P, Q) @@ factor k′k
d(f^{-1}P, f^{-1}Q) = \tfrac{1}{k}\,d(f f^{-1}P, f f^{-1}Q) = \tfrac{1}{k}\,d(P, Q) @@ factor 1/k
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the similarities form a group}} \quad \blacksquare @@ regroup for composing; e has factor 1

20b4Moves that keep a figure

From here on, a symmetry of a set S of points is an isometry f with f(S) = S. This is the version of 19f’s symmetries built on the plane itself.

Symmetries-of-a-figure technique: the symmetries of any figure form a part-group of the isometries. If f(S) = S and g(S) = S then g(f(S)) = S, and f⁻¹(S) = S. A figure is more regular the bigger its part-group.

(x, y) \mapsto (x, y),\ \ (-x, -y) @@ do nothing; the half turn about O
(x, y) \mapsto (x, -y),\ \ (-x, y) @@ two moves that turn the plane over, which 20c names
\text{four, and } (-x, -y) = \text{the other two, one after the other} @@ a part-group of 4

The accepted names

The group of isometries of the plane is the Euclidean group, written E(2) or Isom(ℝ²). The symmetries of a figure are its symmetry group.

Who said it first

In 20b.4.5, the old writer is Plutarch, Life of Theseus (about 100 CE); the philosopher is Thomas Hobbes, De Corpore (1655).
