How Does a Screen Draw a World That Turns?

How does a screen draw a world that turns? A game or a film made by computer holds its world as lists of points, and sixty times a second it turns them, shrinks the far ones, hides the ones behind, and lights the rest. Every one of those jobs is a few lines of rungs 18 to 21. This page turns a model with cos and sin, watches rounding make a spinning model shrink, draws the far smaller by similar triangles, keeps the nearest point for each dot of the screen, and lights a surface by the cosine of its angle to the lamp.

Turn each point by the pair (cos θ, sin θ); turns compose by adding angles, so compute each frame from the angle, not from the last frame. Draw a point at depth z at d·x/z across the screen. For each dot, keep the point with the least depth. Light a surface by the dot product of its unit facing with the unit direction to the lamp, and by nothing when that is negative.

21comp1Turning a Model

Zoe is making a game. Her windmill is a list of points, each a pair (x, y) about its hub. To turn it by an angle θ, every point is turned by the pair (cos θ, sin θ), as in 20j and 21d.

(x, y) \mapsto (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\ x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta) @@ the pair-product technique (20j)
\theta = 30°:\ \cos\theta = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \sin\theta = \tfrac12 @@ the thirds-of-the-half technique (21b)
(2, 0) \mapsto (\sqrt3,\ 1) @@ a blade tip
\htmlClass{new}{|(\sqrt3, 1)| = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2} @@ a turn keeps every distance (18f)

A model of ten thousand points takes ten thousand turns a frame, four multiplications each, all with the same two numbers.

21comp2Why a Spinning Model Shrinks

To spin the windmill a degree each frame, it is tempting to turn last frame’s points by 1° again. But a computer keeps cos 1° and sin 1° rounded. Say to four places: 0.9998 and 0.0175.

0.9998^2 + 0.0175^2 = 0.99990629 @@ not 1, against the circle technique (21c)
\text{each turn stretches by } \sqrt{0.99990629} \approx 0.99995 @@ a turn and a stretch (20j)
0.99995^{360} \approx 0.983 @@ one full spin
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after one spin the windmill is } 1.7\% \text{ smaller}} @@ and it keeps shrinking

Fresh-turn technique: keep the angle and turn the original points by it each frame; turning the last frame’s points piles up the rounding. Turns compose by adding angles (21d), and adding angles costs nothing.

21comp3Far Things Drawn Smaller

Seen from the side, the eye sits at the origin looking along the z axis, and the screen is a line at distance d in front of it. A point at height x and depth z is drawn where the line from the eye to it crosses the screen.

\triangle(\text{eye, screen point}) \sim \triangle(\text{eye, point}) @@ the similar-triangles technique (18f)
\dfrac{x\prime}{d} = \dfrac{x}{z} @@ matching sides in proportion
\htmlClass{new}{x\prime = d\,x / z} @@ divide by the depth

Depth technique: a point at depth z is drawn at d·x/z, so a thing twice as far is drawn half as large. Two rails at heights 1 and −1 run off to great z, and both d/z and −d/z head to 0: parallel lines meet on the screen at one point.

21comp4The Nearest Point Wins

Many points can land on the same dot of the screen: the near side of a house and the far side behind it. Only the nearest should show. Painting from the farthest thing to the nearest fails when three things each overlap the next in a ring.

\text{for each dot: keep a depth, at first } \infty @@ nothing drawn yet
\text{a point lands with depth } z:\ z < \text{kept?} @@ compare
\text{yes: draw it and keep } z;\ \text{no: skip it} @@ one test per point
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each dot ends with its nearest point}} @@ in any order of drawing

Keeping the least depth at each dot shows the nearest point there, whatever order the points come in. It costs one number per dot of the screen, and no sorting at all.

21comp5Light by the Cosine

A surface facing a lamp catches its light squarely; tilted, the same light spreads over more surface and looks dimmer. Let n be the unit arrow the surface faces, and ℓ the unit arrow toward the lamp.

n \cdot \ell = \cos(\text{angle between}) @@ the opening is the cosine (21b), for unit arrows (18a)
n = (0, 1),\ \ell = (\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}):\ n \cdot \ell = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} @@ lamp 30° from overhead
n \cdot \ell < 0:\ \text{the lamp is behind} @@ no light at all
\htmlClass{new}{\text{brightness} = \max(0,\ n \cdot \ell)} @@ one dot product per point

Cosine technique: a dull surface is lit in proportion to the cosine of the angle between its facing and the lamp, which is a dot product of unit arrows, and not at all when the lamp is behind it. Shine and colour are added on top of this, by more lines of the same kind.

21comp7Turning Speed, Frame Rate and Field of View

A game turns a view at a set speed, but draws in frames. The turn each frame is the speed divided by the frames a second. And the d in d·x/z (21comp3) is set by how wide a field the screen should show: half the screen’s width over d is the tangent of half the field.

90^\circ/\text{s} \div 60 = 1.5^\circ \text{ a frame} = \tfrac{\pi}{120} @@ radians (21j)
\tan(\theta/2) = \dfrac{w/2}{d} @@ half the field
\theta = 90^\circ:\ \tan 45^\circ = 1,\ d = 960 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{frame } k:\ \text{turn by } 1.5^\circ \cdot k \text{ from the start, not } 1.5^\circ \text{ from the last}} @@ no shrink (21comp2)

Frame-turn technique: turn per frame = turning speed ÷ frame rate; set d = (w/2) ÷ tan(θ/2) for a field of view θ; and compute each frame’s turn from the total angle so the model keeps its size. Shown in a run: a model turned at 1.5° a frame for 60 frames ends exactly a quarter turn round, every distance unchanged to the last pixel.

Accepted names

Turning, moving and scaling a model are transformations, usually written as matrices. Drawing by d·x/z is perspective projection, and the meeting point of parallel lines a vanishing point. Keeping the least depth per dot is the z-buffer or depth buffer; painting far to near is the painter’s algorithm. Light by the cosine is Lambert’s cosine law, and the facing arrow is the surface normal. The jagged edge is aliasing.

Who said it first

Johann Heinrich Lambert stated the cosine law in Photometria, 1760. Edwin Catmull described the depth buffer in his 1974 thesis at the University of Utah.
