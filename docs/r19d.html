19d · proof

A part’s size divides the whole’s

Inside the 12 turns of a wheel by twelfths sit smaller groups: the quarter turns, the thirds, the half turn. Their sizes are 4, 3 and 2, and each divides 12. This step proves that this always happens in a finite group. Shift the smaller group by any member and you get a copy the same size; two copies never overlap; together they cover the whole. So the whole is a stack of equal copies.

If one group sits inside a finite group, its shifted copies all have as many members as it does, any two are equal or share nothing, and every member of the larger group lies in one. So the smaller group’s size divides the larger’s, and so does the number of steps it takes any member to come back round.

19d1Groups inside groups

From here on, a part-group of a group G is a part H of G that is a group with G’s own operation. Its accepted name is a subgroup.

Part-group test: a part H of a group is a part-group exactly when it is not empty, holds ab whenever it holds a and b, and holds a⁻¹ whenever it holds a. Regroup comes free from G, and e comes from any member a as aa⁻¹.

a \in H @@ H is not empty
a^{-1} \in H,\ \ aa^{-1} = e \in H @@ H holds undoes; then holds products
(xy)z = x(yz) \text{ for } x, y, z \in H @@ true in G, so in H
\htmlClass{new}{H \text{ is a group}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the e of G does nothing in H, and each inverse is in H

In the turns of a wheel by twelfths, write k for the turn by k twelfths, so 0 does nothing and combining is adding, taking away 12 when the sum reaches 12. Then {0, 4, 8}, the turns by thirds, is a part-group. So is {0, 3, 6, 9}. {0, 4} is not: 4 + 4 = 8 is missing.

19d2Shifted copies

From here on, for a part-group H of G and a member a of G, aH is the set {ah : h ∈ H}: every member of H with a done after it. When the operation is written +, it is a + H.

Copies-same-size technique: every copy aH has exactly as many members as H. The function h ↦ ah matches H with aH: it is onto aH by its definition, and one-to-one by cancelling.

\phi(h) = ah,\ \ \phi : H \to aH @@ define
ah = ak \Rightarrow h = k @@ cancel (19b3): one-to-one
x \in aH \Rightarrow x = ah \text{ for some } h @@ ﹀ aH: onto
\htmlClass{new}{|aH| = |H|} \quad \blacksquare @@ a matching (4k)
  
1 + H = \{1, 5, 9\},\ \ 2 + H = \{2, 6, 10\},\ \ 3 + H = \{3, 7, 11\} @@ three members each
4 + H = \{4, 8, 0\} = H @@ 4 is in H, so the copy is H itself

19d3Copies never overlap

Copies-never-overlap technique: two copies aH and bH are either the same set or share no member. And every a lies in its own copy, since a = ae.

c \in aH \cap bH:\ \ c = ah = bk @@ suppose they share c
a = bkh^{-1} @@ combine on the right with h⁻¹
ah' = b(kh^{-1}h') \in bH \text{ for every } h' \in H @@ regroup; kh⁻¹h′ is in H (the part-group test)
aH \subseteq bH,\ \text{and likewise } bH \subseteq aH @@ the same lines with a and b swapped
\htmlClass{new}{aH = bH} \quad \blacksquare @@ double-inclusion (2c)

So the copies sort G, the way 2g’s classes sorted a set: each member in exactly one pile.

19d4The count

Part-divides-whole technique: the size of a part-group of a finite group divides the size of the group.

G = a_1H \cup a_2H \cup \cdots \cup a_kH @@ every member lies in its own copy; keep one of each different copy
a_iH \cap a_jH = \varnothing \text{ for } i \ne j @@ copies-never-overlap
|G| = |a_1H| + \cdots + |a_kH| @@ sizes of piles that share nothing add (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{|G| = k\,|H|} \quad \blacksquare @@ copies-same-size

k, the number of copies, is how many times H fits into G. In the twelfths, {0, 4, 8} fits 4 times. In S₃, {e, s₁₂} fits 3 times, and {e, r, r²} twice.

19d5How soon a move comes back, again

Take any member a of a finite group and all its powers: e, a, a², …, up to a^(n − 1), where n is the order of a. They are a part-group: a product of powers is a power, and the undo of aⁱ is a^(n − i). They are n different members: a repeat aⁱ = aʲ with 0 ≤ i < j < n would give a^(j − i) = e, sooner than n.

Order-divides technique: in a finite group, the order of every member divides the size of the group. So a^|G| = e for every a.

\langle a \rangle = \{e, a, \ldots, a^{n-1}\},\ \ |\langle a \rangle| = n @@ its powers, n of them, all different
\langle a \rangle \text{ is a part-group} @@ part-group test, with the powers technique
n \mid |G| @@ part-divides-whole
\htmlClass{new}{a^{|G|} = (a^n)^{|G|/n} = e} \quad \blacksquare @@ powers technique; aⁿ = e

A group whose size is a prime has no room for anything in between: every member but e has the whole group as its powers.

The accepted names

A part-group is a subgroup; aH is a left coset and Ha a right coset; k = |G|/|H| is the index of H in G. ⟨a⟩ is the cyclic subgroup generated by a. Part-divides-whole is Lagrange's theorem; a^|G| = e is a form of what number theory calls Fermat's little theorem, which rung 40 takes up.

Who said it first

In 19d.4.5, the man is Joseph-Louis Lagrange, Réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations (1771). Pietro Abbati gave the proof for groups of shuffles (1803); Camille Jordan and others gave the modern form in the later nineteenth century.
