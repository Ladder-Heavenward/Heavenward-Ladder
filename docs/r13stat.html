13·stat · statistics

Which line fits the points best?

Which line fits the points best? 8·stat measured whether two lists rise together and proved the measure never passes 1. It did not say by how much one rises when the other does. For that, draw the pairs as points and put a line through them. But the points never lie on one line, so which line? 8·stat chose the mean as the best middle because it makes the squared misses least. Choose the line the same way: the one whose squared misses, measured straight up and down, add to the least. This page finds that line, proves there is exactly one, checks it with slopes, and then shows four lists that have the same best line and nothing else in common.

Among all lines, take the one whose squared misses, measured up and down, add to the least. Its slope is how the two lists vary together over how the first list varies alone. It passes through the point of the two means, its misses add to 0, and its slope is the correlation scaled by the ratio of the two spreads. The share of the second list’s spread that the line accounts for is the correlation squared.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 8·stat (squared-misses, dot-bound for lists, r), 9e (linear functions), 8i (completing the square), 13d (power for slopes, slopes-add, constant-factor for slopes, polynomial-slope), 13a (slope-of-a-line). 14a proves why the slope came out 0 at the best line.

Misses straight up and down

From here on, take paired data (x₀, y₀), …, (x₍ₙ₋₁₎, y₍ₙ₋₁₎) and a line y = k + bx. The fitted value at xᵢ is ŷᵢ = k + bxᵢ, read “y hat sub i”: the small roof marks a value worked out from the line, not measured. The miss is eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ. Later the same roof marks the best k and b: k̂ and b̂. The miss has the accepted name the residual. Write S(k, b) = Σᵢ eᵢ² for the total squared miss, and Sₓₓ = Σᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)², Sₓᵧ = Σᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ), Sᵧᵧ = Σᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ)². Assume the xᵢ are not all equal, so Sₓₓ > 0.

The misses are measured straight up and down, along y, because the question is how well x tells y. That choice treats x as known exactly and puts all the error in y. Measuring the other way, along x, gives another line; 13·stat.3.1 asks how different.

The one best line

Best-line technique (least squares): S(k, b) is least for exactly one line, with b̂ = Sₓᵧ/Sₓₓ and k̂ = ȳ − b̂x̄. The line passes through (x̄, ȳ), and b̂ = r · σ_y/σ_x. Its accepted name is the least squares line, or the regression line of y on x.

\text{fix } b:\ \ e_i = (y_i - bx_i) - k @@ the misses of the list zᵢ = yᵢ − bxᵢ from the single number k
S(k, b) = \textstyle\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)^2 + n(\bar z - k)^2 @@ 8·stat's squared-misses, with c = k
\text{least in } k \iff k = \bar z = \bar y - b\bar x @@ so the line passes through (x̄, ȳ)
\textstyle\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)^2 = \sum_i \big((y_i - \bar y) - b(x_i - \bar x)\big)^2 = S_{yy} - 2bS_{xy} + b^2S_{xx} @@ 5h, added over i
= S_{xx}\big(b - \tfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\big)^2 + S_{yy} - \tfrac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} @@ completing the square in b (8i); Sₓₓ > 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{least exactly at } \hat b = \tfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}},\ \hat k = \bar y - \hat b\bar x} \quad \blacksquare @@ a square is least only at 0 (square-never-negative); one k, one b

Since r = Sₓᵧ/√(SₓₓSᵧᵧ) and σₓ = √(Sₓₓ/n), σ_y = √(Sᵧᵧ/n), the slope is b̂ = r · σ_y/σₓ. So a correlation of 0.6 says: one σₓ more in x goes with 0.6 of a σ_y more in y, on average. A scientist found in 1886 that children of very tall parents are tall, but on average less far from the mean than their parents. He called it regression towards mediocrity. It is this factor r, below 1 by 8·stat's correlation-bound, and the name regression stuck to the line.

The check by slopes

The page found the line without slopes. Rung 13's slopes give a second look. With k set to ȳ − bx̄, the total squared miss is a polynomial in b, Q(b) = Sₓₓb² − 2Sₓᵧb + Sᵧᵧ. By polynomial-slope its slope is Q′(b) = 2Sₓₓb − 2Sₓᵧ, which is 0 at exactly b̂, negative below it and positive above it. In the same way, the slope of S in k, with b kept fixed, is −2Σᵢeᵢ, which is 0 exactly at the best k. So at the best line both slopes are 0. That is not a coincidence, but its reason is rung 14's.

What the misses do

Residuals technique: Σᵢ eᵢ = 0 and Σᵢ xᵢeᵢ = 0. And Sᵧᵧ = Σᵢ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)² + Σᵢ eᵢ², with Σᵢ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)² = r²Sᵧᵧ.

e_i = (y_i - \bar y) - \hat b(x_i - \bar x) @@ best-line: â = ȳ − b̂x̄
\textstyle\sum_i e_i = 0 - \hat b \cdot 0 = 0 @@ deviations add to 0 (8·stat)
\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)e_i = S_{xy} - \hat bS_{xx} = 0 @@ ﹀ b̂; and Σxᵢeᵢ = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)eᵢ + x̄Σeᵢ
\hat y_i - \bar y = \hat b(x_i - \bar x),\ \ \textstyle\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)e_i = 0 @@ the last two lines
S_{yy} = \textstyle\sum_i \big((\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i\big)^2 = \sum (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum e_i^2 @@ 5h; the cross term is 0
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 = \hat b^2S_{xx} = \tfrac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}} = r^2S_{yy}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ﹀ r

So the spread of y splits into a part the line accounts for and a part it misses, and r² is the share accounted for. It has the accepted name the coefficient of determination, R². A plot of the residuals against x, the residual plot, is the first thing to look at after fitting: if the line is right, it should show no pattern.

x: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5;\ \ y: 3, 5, 4, 8, 10 @@ 8·stat's pairs; x̄ = 3, ȳ = 6
S_{xx} = 10,\ \ S_{xy} = 6 + 1 + 0 + 2 + 8 = 17,\ \ S_{yy} = 34 @@ deviations −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and −3, −1, −2, 2, 4
\hat b = 1.7,\ \ \hat k = 6 - 5.1 = 0.9 @@ best-line: y = 0.9 + 1.7x
e: 0.4,\ 0.7,\ -2,\ 0.3,\ 0.6 @@ residuals: they add to 0, and Σxᵢeᵢ = 0.4 + 1.4 − 6 + 1.2 + 3 = 0
r^2 = \tfrac{17^2}{10 \cdot 34} = \tfrac{289}{340} = 0.85 @@ r ≈ 0.922, as 8·stat.1.1d finds
Anexact, yet rigorous · counting: the spread of a prediction

4j found that the spread of a crowd is part of the answer, 4·stat wrote the pile down, 8·stat gave a list its σ, and 9·stat drew the pile as a function. A fitted line is a prediction about a crowd of points, and its residuals are its spread. The line y = 0.9 + 1.7x is the middle. The misses 0.4, 0.7, −2, 0.3, 0.6 are how far the crowd wanders from it, and r² = 0.85 is the share of the wandering the line accounts for.

A prediction about a crowd states its spread; a prediction with no spread is a claim about one point in disguise.

The four lists of 1973 below share one line and one r. Only their misses tell them apart. Reported as a line alone, all four say the same thing. Reported with their residuals, they are four different crowds.

A line fitted to children's heights from age 2 to 10 predicts 140 cm at 10, and the residuals at 10 run from −9 cm to +8 cm. A school orders its desks for ten-year-olds from the line alone. What happens in the classroom?

A model, and what is packed in it

The worked line y = 0.9 + 1.7x stands for a lot. It holds five pairs, what x and y measure, the stretch of x from 1 to 5, the best k and b, and the misses. Give that bundle one name, and it can be carried around whole. A model is a statement that packs a bundle of facts, and using it starts by unpacking them. Its accepted name is a model; this one is a linear model, or a fitted line.

The unpack is 1a's unpack ﹀: the name gives way to what it stands for. Write out the data it was fitted on. Write what x and y measure, and in what units. Write the stretch of x it was fitted on. Only then do the techniques have their material. A prediction from the model is ŷ at some x, and it is worth something only inside the stretch.

From here on, a block of questions may open with a model. Every question in the block that needs one uses that same model. Your first move is to unpack it.

What the line does not say

In 1973 a statistician published four lists of eleven pairs each. All four have x̄ = 9, ȳ = 7.5, the same r, about 0.816, and the same best line, y = 3 + 0.5x. Drawn, they could hardly differ more. One is a fair cloud about a line, one a clean curve, and one a line with a single far point. The last is a stack at x = 8 with one point at x = 19 that fixes the whole slope. The numbers of this page are the same for all four, so no number of this page can tell them apart. Only a picture can.

Nor does the line say what happens beyond the data: a line through the heights of children aged 2 to 10 predicts adults four metres tall. Nor does it say that x makes y happen, any more than r did in 8·stat.

Who said it first

The scientist of 1886, who saw tall parents’ children come back towards the mean and called it a law of heredity, was Francis Galton. The four lists of 1973 are Francis Anscombe’s; their accepted name is Anscombe’s quartet.

A computer should make both calculations and graphs. Both sorts of output should be studied; each will contribute to understanding.
