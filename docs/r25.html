Finding Every Root

Some polynomials have no root on the number line. The simplest is x squared plus one. Rung 9 built a number whose square is minus one, rung 18 found it as a quarter turn, and rungs 20 and 21 used it to turn and stretch the plane. This rung asks what those numbers are worth. They cost something: no choice of positives among them keeps the old rules of order. In return they give something no other number system does. Every polynomial, of every degree, has a root among them, and splits into one line factor for each power. The search for roots that began with the quadratic ends here.

Once, a number whose square is minus one sounded like a trick. You have used it for several rungs now. This rung asks what it is worth, and the answer takes everything you have.

The new numbers form a field with no order. Size takes the place of order, and sizes multiply. Every polynomial that is not constant has a root in them, so it splits into line factors.

What it means to say it

To find every root of a polynomial is to find enough numbers that it splits completely, into a constant times one factor z − r for each power of z. On the line, x² + 1 does not split. In the plane, it splits as (x − i)(x + i).

Every polynomial that is not constant has a root in ℂ, so it splits into line factors there. The rung proves this, and counts what it cost.

Where it comes from

An Italian physician met square roots of negatives in 1545, solving cubics, and called them as subtle as they were useless. An Italian engineer calculated with them in 1572. A French mathematician claimed in 1629 that a polynomial of degree n has n roots. A French mathematician of the Encyclopédie tried to prove it in 1746, and a young German mathematician attacked the attempts in 1799. A bookkeeper in Paris gave the proof by least size in 1814, and a French mathematician made it rigorous in 1821. An Irish mathematician defined the numbers as pairs in 1837.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves four things, each from the ones before.

First, the pairs of rung 9, the turns of rung 18 and the moves of rungs 20 and 21 are one number system (25a1). The line sits inside it (25a2), and the flip across the line keeps sums and products (25a3).

Second, no choice of positives among the new numbers obeys the old rules of order (25b2). Their squares would all be positive, and minus one is a square.

Third, the size of a product is the product of the sizes (25c1), and the size of a sum is at most the sum of the sizes (25c2). So distance works in the plane as it did on the line.

Fourth, the size of a polynomial’s value has a lowest point (25d2), and a lowest point that is not zero can always be pushed lower (25d3). So every polynomial has a root (25d4).

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, the annexes carry green boxes. 25·cb7 carries the middle of a change: a battery is two sides at once, and its voltage sags as the change runs. 25·chem carries two sizes of truth: a mixture is at rest as a crowd while every molecule keeps changing. 25·eng carries the triangle: each force is split into an across part and an up part, and each part is checked alone. 25·econ carries laws with a known exception rate: poorer economies catch up among their like, at about two in a hundred a year, and the law fails across all countries. 25·now carries the cloud: a pixel is not its middle, so a picture of the set proves only escapes.

What follows

With those four, the techniques compute. A polynomial with real numbers in front has its roots in mirrored pairs, so it splits into real lines and real quadratics, which finishes rung 17’s partial fractions. The maps z ↦ az + b turn, stretch and slide the plane, and z ↦ 1/z turns it inside out, sending circles and lines to circles and lines. The annexes take the new numbers into batteries, chemical balance, the shape of an arch, the growth of nations, and the strange set of numbers that never escape when squared again and again.

What you can do because you know it

You can find every root of a polynomial, or know how many there are before you look. You can factor any real polynomial into pieces no bigger than quadratics. You can compute with the new numbers by hand, measure distances between them, and read multiplying as a turn and a stretch.

Every rung from here on uses ℂ freely. Waves, circuits, quantum states and the shapes of drums all live there.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 9’s pairs, rung 18’s turns, rungs 20 and 21’s turning and stretching, rung 12’s lowest values, rung 22’s settling threads and rung 8’s sizes on the line. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say every root is found, when it is true, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Gerolamo Cardano met square roots of negatives in 1545, solving cubics, and called them as subtle as they were useless. Rafael Bombelli calculated with them in 1572. Albert Girard claimed in 1629 that a polynomial of degree n has n roots. Jean le Rond d’Alembert tried to prove it in 1746, and Carl Friedrich Gauss attacked the attempts in 1799. Jean-Robert Argand gave the proof by least size in 1814, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy made it rigorous in 1821. William Rowan Hamilton defined the numbers as pairs in 1837.
