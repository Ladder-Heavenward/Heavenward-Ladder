16·cb12 · newsflash for 12·phys

Floating and sinking

12·phys found that a bar on a support balances where the turning effects add to 0. Such a point exists because the total slides continuously from one sign to the other. A floating log is a balance too: its weight pulls it down, the water pushes it up, and it settles where the two agree. 12·phys had no way to say how hard water pushes. 16j gives volume and density, and with them the push. There is the pressure under a column of water. And there is the buoyancy technique (Archimedes' principle): the water pushes up with the weight of the water the body has pushed aside.

Pressure is force per area. Down in still water, the pressure is the pressure at the surface plus the weight of the column of water above. The push up on a submerged body is ρgV, the weight of the water it displaces. A body floats with as much of its volume under as its heaviness for its size is to the water’s, and a depth where it settles exists by 12a, as 12·phys’s balance point did.

Pressure under water

Pressure is force per unit area, p = F/A; its accepted unit is the pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². A still liquid pushes equally in every direction at a point, and pushes straight against any surface it touches, with force pA on a small patch of area A. Take as given that a mass of m kg weighs mg newtons, with g ≈ 9.8 m/s² near the ground.

Pressure-with-depth technique: in a still liquid of density ρ, with pressure p₀ at the surface, the pressure at depth h is p₀ + ρgh.

\begin{aligned}&\text{a column of the liquid, base area } A,\\ &\quad \text{ from the surface to depth } h\end{aligned} @@ a prism: volume Ah (prism, 16j)
\text{its weight: } \rho A h\,g @@ mass ρV (16j), weight mg (given)
p(h)A = p_0 A + \rho g A h @@ the column is still: the push up on its base balances the push down on its top and its weight; the sides push across and cancel
\htmlClass{new}{p(h) = p_0 + \rho g h} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by A
  
\rho = 1025 \text{ kg/m}^3,\ h = 10 \text{ m}:\ \ \rho g h \approx 1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} @@ seawater in Puget Sound; g = 9.8 m/s²
p \approx 101\,325 + 100\,450 \approx 2.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} @@ ten metres down doubles the air's pressure

The push up

Buoyancy technique (Archimedes' principle): a body wholly under a still liquid of density ρ is pushed up with force ρgV, where V is its volume: the weight of the liquid it displaces.

\text{a box, top face area } A \text{ at depth } d, \text{ height } H @@ the four side faces are pushed across, and the pushes cancel in pairs
\text{up on the bottom: } (p_0 + \rho g(d + H))A;\ \ \text{down on the top: } (p_0 + \rho g d)A @@ pressure-with-depth at each face
\text{net up} = \rho g H A = \rho g V @@ subtract; V = HA (prism)

For any shape, cut the body into thin columns standing in the depth direction. Each is nearly a box, so each is pushed up by about ρg times its volume. The sum squeezes, as in 16j's cross-sections technique, to ρgV. Or, in the way it was first argued: replace the body by the liquid it displaces. That liquid would sit still, so the push on it balances its weight, ρgV. And the push depends only on the surface, which is the same. The push up has the accepted name the buoyant force: buoyant means “tending to float”.

An old story tells of a man set to find whether a goldsmith had mixed silver into the king's crown. He saw the water rise as he lowered himself into a bath. He leapt out and ran home, crying that he had found it. Then he compared the water the crown spilled with the water spilled by equal weights of gold and of silver.

Floating

Floating technique: a body of volume V and mass m in a liquid of density ρ settles at a depth where the submerged volume V_s has ρgV_s = mg. If its density m/V is less than ρ, such a depth exists, and the fraction under is (m/V)/ρ.

U(s) = \rho g V_s(s) - mg,\ \ s \text{ how far it has sunk} @@ the net push up; V_s(s) = ∫ of the slice areas up to s (16j)
U(0) = -mg \lt 0,\ \ U(\text{all under}) = \rho g V - mg \gt 0 @@ m < ρV
U(s) = 0 \text{ for some } s @@ V_s is continuous (16a's area-never-jumps); in-between-value (12a), as balance-point in 12·phys
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{V_s}{V} = \tfrac{m/V}{\rho}} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve ρV_s = m
  
\text{ice in seawater: } \tfrac{917}{1025} \approx 0.89 @@ about 89% of a berg is under the surface
\begin{aligned}&\text{a Douglas fir log, } 530 \text{ kg/m}^3,\\ &\quad \text{ in the Columbia}: 53\% \text{ under}\end{aligned} @@ fresh water, 1000 kg/m³
\text{a ferry of } 5000 \text{ t displaces } \tfrac{5 \times 10^6}{1025} \approx 4900 \text{ m}^3 @@ floating: the water pushed aside has the ferry's mass

Illegal moves

\begin{aligned}&\text{the push up depends}\\ &\quad\text{on how deep the body is}\,?\end{aligned} @@ for a body wholly under, ρgV at any depth: pressure-with-depth's ρgd cancels
\text{heavy things sink}\,? @@ dense things sink: a steel ship floats because its volume includes the air inside
\text{the push up is the body's weight}\,? @@ it is the displaced liquid's weight; they agree only when it floats
p = \rho g h \text{ at depth } h\,? @@ add the surface's p₀, unless you mean the pressure above the air's

Who said it first

Vitruvius told that Archimedes, set to find whether a goldsmith had mixed silver into the king’s crown, saw the water rise as he lowered himself into a bath, leapt out and ran home crying that he had found it, and then compared the water the crown spilled with the water spilled by equal weights of gold and of silver.

Vitruvius, De architectura, book 9; paraphrase

The buoyancy technique and the swap-in-the-water argument are Archimedes’, from On Floating Bodies. In 16·cb12.4.4, the young scientist of 1586 is Galileo, in his essay La Bilancetta.

Accepted names: the push up, ρgV, is the buoyant force; the buoyancy technique is Archimedes’ principle.
