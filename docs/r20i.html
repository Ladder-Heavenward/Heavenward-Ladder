20i · technique

Moving graphs

A graph is a statement that names a set of points, so every move of the plane moves it. The moved graph has an equation too, and finding it is a matter of one rule. A point is on the moved graph exactly when the move undone puts it back on the old one. So the equation changes the other way: to slide a graph right by three, take three from the input.

A point is on the moved shape exactly when undoing the move puts it on the shape. To slide a graph across and up, take the across amount from the input and add the up amount to the output. To flip it over the floor, turn the output’s sign. Over the upright axis, turn the input’s sign. To pull it upright, multiply the output.

20i1The equation moves the other way

Undo-in-the-equation technique: a point P is on the moved set g(S) exactly when g⁻¹(P) is on S. So to find the equation of g(S), put the coordinates of g⁻¹(x, y) into the equation of S.

P \in g(S) \iff P = g(Q) \text{ for some } Q \in S @@ what g(S) means
\iff g^{-1}(P) \in S @@ Q = g⁻¹(P), the only choice (20b2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the equation of } g(S) \text{ is the equation of } S \text{ at } g^{-1}(x, y)} \quad \blacksquare

The move is put in undone. That is why the signs look backwards in everything below.

20i2Slides

Graph-slide technique: the graph of y = f(x), slid by (h, k), is the graph of y = f(x − h) + k. The slide undone is (x, y) ↦ (x − h, y − k).

(x - h,\ y - k) \text{ on } y = f(x) @@ 20i1, with the slide undone
y - k = f(x - h) @@ substitute
\htmlClass{new}{y = f(x - h) + k} \quad \blacksquare @@ add k to both sides

20i3Flips

Graph-flip technique: flipped over the floor, y = f(x) becomes y = −f(x); flipped over the upright axis, it becomes y = f(−x). Each flip undoes itself, so it goes into the equation as it is.

\text{floor: } (x, -y) \text{ on } y = f(x) \Rightarrow -y = f(x) @@ 20i1; the floor flip undoes itself
\htmlClass{new}{y = -f(x)} @@ multiply by −1
\text{upright: } (-x, y) \text{ on } y = f(x) \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{y = f(-x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ likewise

So 9e’s even functions, with f(−x) = f(x), are exactly those whose graphs the upright flip keeps. Its odd functions, with f(−x) = −f(x), are those whose graphs the half turn about (0, 0) keeps.

20i4Pulls

Graph-pull technique: pulled upright by c, y = f(x) becomes y = c f(x); pulled sideways by c, it becomes y = f(x / c). Pulls are not isometries, but the rule of 20i1 works for any move that can be undone.

(x, y) \mapsto (x, cy):\ \ (x, y/c) \text{ on } y = f(x) @@ undone: divide y by c
\htmlClass{new}{y = c\,f(x)} @@ multiply by c
(x, y) \mapsto (cx, y):\ \ \htmlClass{new}{y = f(x / c)} \quad \blacksquare @@ undone: divide x by c

Together, y = c f(x − h) + k is y = f(x) pulled upright by c and then slid by (h, k). Every parabola y = a x² + bx + d is one of these, made from y = x².

The accepted names

Moving graphs is usually taught as transformations of functions: horizontal and vertical shifts, reflections in the axes, and vertical and horizontal stretches. Even and odd functions are as named on 9e.

Who said it first

In 20i.4.5, the geometer is René Descartes, La Géométrie (1637), and the critic is Isaac Newton, in Arithmetica Universalis (1707): “Equations are expressions of arithmetical computation, and properly have no place in geometry.” His Principia (1687) is written mostly in figures.
