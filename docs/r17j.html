17j · technique

Growth and decay

17f proved that a quantity whose rate of change is a fixed multiple of its size is its start times an exponential, and nothing else. That one fact describes money at interest, a culture of cells with room to spare, a sample of radium, a cup of coffee cooling toward the room. This step reads the multiple and the start in each, and finds doubling times and half-lives. It tells a quantity that grows by equal steps from one that grows by equal factors. And it asks how long an exponential can last.

A rate fixed by size makes a start times an exponential. Equal steps in time multiply the quantity by the same factor. The doubling time, or the half-life, is the logarithm of two divided by the rate. A fixed percent each period is a rate equal to the logarithm of one plus that percent. A cooling body closes the gap to the room’s temperature by a fixed factor in equal times.

17j1Equal steps, equal factors

Stands on 17f (proportional-slope) and 17e (adding-becomes-multiplying).

Equal-steps-equal-factors technique: let y have a slope at every t, with y(0) ≠ 0. Then y = y₀ekt if and only if, for every step h, y(t + h)/y(t) is the same for all t. That factor is ekh. A linear y = y₀ + mt is the one for which y(t + h) − y(t) is the same for all t; that step is mh.

\tfrac{y(t + h)}{y(t)} = \tfrac{y_0 e^{kt}e^{kh}}{y_0 e^{kt}} = e^{kh} @@ adding-becomes-multiplying (17e)
y(t + h) - y(t) = mh \text{ for } y = y_0 + mt @@ the same for every t

The other way, from the factors to the form, takes a slope. Suppose y(t + h)/y(t) is one number c(h) for every t. Then (y(t + h) − y(t))/h = y(t)(c(h) − 1)/h. At t = 0 the left side tends to y′(0), so (c(h) − 1)/h tends to k = y′(0)/y(0). Then at every t, y′ = ky, and proportional-slope gives the form. So a table of readings at equal steps is exponential if its ratios agree, and linear if its differences agree.

t:\ 0, 1, 2, 3 \quad y:\ 50, 60, 72, 86.4 @@ ratios 1.2, 1.2, 1.2: exponential, 20% a step
t:\ 0, 1, 2, 3 \quad y:\ 50, 60, 70, 80 @@ differences 10, 10, 10: linear, 10 a step

17j2Reading k

Stands on 17j1 and 17h (equations with exponents).

Doubling-time technique: if y = y₀ekt with k > 0, then y doubles every ln 2 / k, whatever t it starts from. If k < 0, y halves every ln 2 / |k|.

e^{kT} = 2 \iff T = \tfrac{\ln 2}{k} @@ equal-steps-equal-factors with h = T; 17h
e^{kT} = \tfrac12 \iff T = \tfrac{-\ln 2}{k} = \tfrac{\ln 2}{|k|} \quad \blacksquare @@ ln ½ = −ln 2 (log-of-a-quotient)

The doubling time has the accepted name doubling time; the halving time, for a decaying quantity, has the accepted name half-life. A quantity that grows by a fixed percent r each period has y = y₀(1 + r)ᵗ = y₀ekt with k = ln(1 + r), a little less than r. For small r, ln 2 / ln(1 + r) ≈ 0.693/r: the doubling time in periods is about 70 divided by the percent.

\text{cells double every 20 min:}\ \ k = \tfrac{\ln 2}{1/3} = 3\ln 2 \approx 2.079 \text{ per hour} @@ doubling-time read backwards
3\% \text{ a year:}\ \ T = \tfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.03} \approx 23.4 \text{ years};\ \ \tfrac{70}{3} \approx 23.3 @@ the rule of 70
\$1000 \text{ at } 5\% \text{ for } 10 \text{ years:}\ \ 1000e^{0.5} \approx \$1648.72 \text{ continuously};\ \ 1000 \cdot 1.05^{10} \approx \$1628.89 \text{ yearly} @@ A′ = rA gives e^(rt); (1 + r/n)^(nt) → e^(rt) as in 17e

17j3Cooling

Stands on 17j2 and 17f (proportional-slope).

The law of cooling (Newton's): an object's temperature T changes at a rate proportional to its difference from the room's, T′ = −k(T − A), k > 0.

u = T - A:\ \ u' = T' = -ku @@ A is a constant
u = u_0 e^{-kt},\ \ T = A + (T_0 - A)e^{-kt} @@ proportional-slope (17f)
T_0 = 90,\ A = 20,\ T(5) = 70:\ \ e^{-5k} = \tfrac{50}{70} \Rightarrow k = \tfrac{\ln 1.4}{5} \approx 0.0673 @@ coffee in a Portland kitchen, °C and minutes
T = 40:\ \ e^{-kt} = \tfrac{20}{70} \Rightarrow t = \tfrac{\ln 3.5}{k} \approx 18.6 \text{ min} @@ 17h

Who said it first

Malthus argued that a population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio, while the food to feed it can at best increase in an arithmetical ratio, so that the power of population is indefinitely greater than the power of the earth to produce subsistence.

Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798); paraphrase

The law of cooling is Isaac Newton’s (1701).
