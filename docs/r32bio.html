Pharmacy Logic: Stocking for Chronic and Acute Conditions

A pharmacist asks two different questions about a condition.

What is it like? That is a description, and it is made of every and some.

  • A condition is chronic in a person when, from some day on, they need the medicine every day.
  • A condition is acute in a person when they need the medicine on some days, and after that never.
  • A condition is recurring in a person when both are true at once: they have the condition every day, and its attacks come on some days, which are the exceptions, and the exceptions never run out. It is a chronic condition whose symptoms are acute.

Now change what the every and the some range over, from one person's days to the town's people. Each acute case is one person's some. But in a town there are many people, each with a small chance on any day. So for the town, on every day, some person is having theirs. What is a one-off for the person is a steady stream for the shelf.

How much do I stock? That is a response, chosen before the week is known. No finite shelf covers every way a week can go, so the pharmacist does not ask for never running short. They pick a chance of running short that they will accept, here 2 weeks in 100, and stock the least amount that keeps the chance at or below it.

From here on, the shelf for a medicine is that least amount, and the short-chance is the chance that the week's need is more than the shelf.

The Town

A town of 20,000 people has one pharmacy. The supplier's van comes once a week, so each shelf has to last seven days.

32bio1Many People, Every Day

Each of the 3,000 people takes one tablet every day. So the town swallows 3,000 tablets a day, 21,000 a week, and that number does not wobble at all. What wobbles is when people come in. A pack lasts 30 days, so in any week a person comes for a new pack with chance 7/30.

Large-count technique: if n people each come in with chance p, independently, the number who come has average np and spread np(1 − p); for large n it is close to bell-shaped, so a shelf of the average plus two spread widths has a short-chance of about 2 in 100.

X = \text{the number of packs asked for in a week} @@ from here on
X = X_1 + \dots + X_{3000},\ \text{each }X_i\text{ is 1 with chance }7/30 @@ one for each person
\operatorname{E}X = 3000 \cdot 7/30 = 700 @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
\operatorname{Var}X = 3000 \cdot (7/30)(23/30) = 536.7 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3)
\text{spread width} = \sqrt{536.7} = 23.2 @@ take the root
X\text{ is close to bell-shaped} @@ the central limit technique (30j3)
\text{shelf} = 700 + 2 \cdot 23.2 = 746\text{ packs} @@ the bell's area past two widths is about 0.023 (30m3)

The shelf is 746 packs for an average need of 700. The extra, 46 packs, is under 7 parts in 100 of the average. A common chronic condition is the easy shelf: the every in its description makes the need nearly certain, and the large count makes the wobble small beside it.

32bio2Two People, Every Week

The rare disorder is found in about 1 person in 10,000. For a town of 20,000 picked at random, that is an average of 2 people, and the rare-events technique (22j2) gives the chances:

\text{chance of exactly }k\text{ people} = e^{-2}\,2^k/k! @@ the rare-events technique (22j2), with average 2
k = 0{:}\ 0.135 \quad k = 1{:}\ 0.271 \quad k = 2{:}\ 0.271 @@ put in
k = 3{:}\ 0.180 \quad k \ge 4{:}\ 0.143 @@ put in; the five add to 1

So about one such town in seven has nobody with the disorder, and about one in seven has four or more. None of that is any use to this pharmacy. This town was not picked at random; it is the town the pharmacy is in, and the pharmacy can count.

Count-first technique: when the people who need a medicine can be listed, the need is read off the list, and no chance enters.

\text{the list has 2 people} @@ collected: the pharmacy's own record
\text{each needs 1 vial every week} @@ chronic: an every over weeks
\text{need} = 2\text{ vials, every week} @@ multiply; nothing here is a chance
\text{shelf} = 2\text{ vials, and 1 spare} @@ the spare is for a broken vial, not for a third person

The every that makes the condition chronic makes the need exact. Rarity changes one thing: there are too few people for an average to mean anything, so the pharmacist counts instead of estimating. The cost sits elsewhere. A vial is dear and does not keep, so each spare that passes its date is money lost; and a third person moving to town is a new line on the list, which the pharmacy learns by being told, not by arithmetic.

32bio3Anyone, Some Day

A broken bone is acute: for the person it is one day, a short course of pain medicine, and then it is over. Nobody can be listed in advance. But each of 20,000 people has a chance of 1 in 10,000 on any day, and one person's break does not make another's more likely.

\text{average breaks in a day} = 20000 \cdot 1/10000 = 2 @@ many tries, each with a tiny chance
\text{average breaks in a week} = 14 @@ seven days
\text{chance of exactly }k\text{ in a week} = e^{-14}\,14^k/k! @@ the rare-events technique (22j2)

Shelf technique for rare arrivals: for a need that is a rare count with average m, the shelf is the least s for which the chances of s + 1, s + 2, … add to no more than the short-chance you accept.

Stocking the average, 14, runs short in 43 weeks out of 100. The shelf is 22, which is 8 above the average: 57 parts in 100 more. Compare the tablets, where the extra was 7 parts in 100. The spread width of a rare count is the root of its average, so a small average has a wide wobble beside it. A small stream needs a deep shelf for its size.

32bio4Cases That Bring Cases

Influenza is acute too: a week or so for the person, and then over. But it is caught from someone. One case makes the next more likely, so the cases are not independent, and the rare-events technique does not apply to the week as a whole.

Treat the weeks as two kinds. In a quiet week, 9 weeks in 10, cases arrive singly from outside: a rare count with average 7. In a wave week, 1 week in 10, each case passes it on, and the count is a rare count with average 70.

\text{average need} = 0.9 \cdot 7 + 0.1 \cdot 70 = 13.3 @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3), on averages

That is almost the same average as the broken bones. Give it the same shelf, 22, and see what happens:

\text{quiet week: chance of more than 22 is below 0.0001} @@ a rare count with average 7 (22j2)
\text{wave week: chance of more than 22 is above 0.9999} @@ a rare count with average 70 (22j2)
\text{short-chance} = 0.9 \cdot 0.0000 + 0.1 \cdot 1.0000 = 0.100 @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3)

The shelf that ran short in 2 weeks out of 100 for broken bones runs short in 10 out of 100 for influenza: in every wave week.

Two-kinds technique: when the weeks are of two kinds, the short-chance is the share of each kind times the short-chance within it, added; and the spread is the average of the spreads within the kinds plus the spread of the kinds' averages.

\text{spread} = 13.3 + \big(0.9\,(7 - 13.3)^2 + 0.1\,(70 - 13.3)^2\big) @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2), split by kind
\phantom{\text{spread}} = 13.3 + 35.7 + 321.5 = 370.5 @@ put in
\text{spread width} = 19.2 @@ against 3.7 for the broken bones
\text{shelf for a short-chance of 0.02: 77 courses} @@ 0.1 · (chance a wave week needs more than 77) = 0.018

The same average, and a shelf of 77 against 22. To be short in only 2 weeks out of 100, the pharmacy has to cover most wave weeks, and so it holds 77 courses through the 9 weeks in 10 when it sells 7.

That is a costly way to answer a wave. The description offers another. A wave does not arrive all at once: it grows, each case bringing more than one new case (30i4's family line that does not die out). So a second response is a trigger: hold a shelf fit for quiet weeks, watch the daily count, and order at once when it climbs. The first response is held at every time; the second is set off by a some. Which is better depends on how fast the van can come, which is a fact about the supplier, not about the illness.

32bio5Every Day, and Some Days

Asthma is recurring. A person with asthma has it every day, and has an attack on some days. So one condition makes two needs, and each takes after a footing above.

The inhaler that prevents. Of the 1,600 people, 1,000 use a daily inhaler, one a month. This is the first footing again, with smaller numbers.

X = \text{the number of preventing inhalers asked for in a week} @@ from here on
\operatorname{E}X = 1000 \cdot 7/30 = 233.3 @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
\text{spread width} = \sqrt{1000 \cdot (7/30)(23/30)} = 13.4 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3)
\text{shelf} = 261 @@ the least s with short-chance at most 0.02, counted exactly

The inhaler that relieves. All 1,600 carry one and replace it when attacks have used it up. In an ordinary week a person needs a new one with chance 1/12.

\text{ordinary week: average }1600 \cdot 1/12 = 133.3,\ \text{spread width 11.1} @@ 30g1, 30g3
\text{shelf for ordinary weeks alone} = 156 @@ short-chance 0.020, counted exactly

The attacks are not caught from anyone, so one person's attack does not cause another's. But they are not independent either. Smoke, pollen or a cold snap reaches the whole town at once, and in such a week everyone's chance rises together. Call it a bad-air week, 1 week in 10, with the chance 1/4 in place of 1/12.

Shared-cause technique: tries that share a cause are independent within a week of one kind and not across kinds; so find the short-chance within each kind and add by shares, as for the two kinds of 4.

\text{bad-air week: average }1600 \cdot 1/4 = 400,\ \text{spread width 17.3} @@ 30g1, 30g3
\text{shelf 156 in a bad-air week: short, with chance above 0.9999} @@ 156 is fourteen widths below 400
\text{short-chance with shelf 156} = 0.9 \cdot 0.020 + 0.1 \cdot 1 = 0.118 @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3)
\text{shelf for a short-chance of 0.02: 415} @@ 0.1 · (chance a bad-air week needs more than 415) = 0.019
\text{average need} = 0.9 \cdot 133.3 + 0.1 \cdot 400 = 160 @@ the whole-chance technique, on averages

So the relieving inhaler behaves like the influenza tablets: an average of 160 and a shelf of 415. Two things make asthma the easier of the two.

The people can be listed. The pharmacy knows the 1,600, so it knows the most it could ever be asked for in a week, which no one can say for influenza.

The cause can be seen coming. A wave of influenza is noticed only by its cases. Bad air is forecast: a pollen count, a smoke warning, a cold front. So the trigger can be pulled before the first attack, and the standing shelf can stay near 156 with an order placed on the warning.

This is the two layers of the description answered one each. The every (the condition) gets a standing shelf, exact for the preventing inhaler. The some (the attacks) gets a shelf sized by chance and a trigger set by the cause.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The shelf is what stock-keepers call the order-up-to level, and one minus the short-chance is the cycle service level; the stock held above the average is safety stock. The rare count is the Poisson distribution. The two-kinds technique is the law of total variance, and a count whose spread is larger than its average, as for influenza, is called overdispersed. The trigger is a reorder point.

What is invented

The town, every rate, the weekly van, the one-in-ten wave weeks and bad-air weeks, and the averages in each. The chance of a broken bone, 1 in 10,000 a day, is on the high side of real figures and was chosen to make the day's average 2. The two-kinds model of influenza is the simplest one that shows cases are not independent; a real wave runs over several weeks, and the model treats each week alone.

What was computed

The chances in the tables (0.430 down to 0.005; 0.135 to 0.143; the shelf of 77) were computed by adding the rare-events chances exactly. The shelf of 746 uses the bell, not the exact count, so its short-chance is about 0.023, slightly over the 0.02 the others meet.
