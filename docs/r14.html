Reading a Shape From Its Slopes

Rung 13 gave every function with a derivative a number at each input: how fast it is changing there. That is a fact about one input at a time. A derivative at 3 says nothing about the value at 5, and 13b showed that a function can have a derivative at one input and be continuous at no other. So it is not obvious that derivatives tell you anything about the heights of a function across a stretch of inputs. This rung proves that they do, provided the function has a derivative at every input of an interval. A derivative that is 0 everywhere means the function never changes. A derivative that is positive everywhere means it climbs. Two functions with the same derivative everywhere differ by a constant. The bridge from one input to a whole interval is a single technique, and it is built from rung 12's highest-and-lowest technique.

You got this. The slope at a point is yours. Now you make it speak about a whole interval. The proofs get longer, and the payoff is the climb itself: you can see further from here than from any rung before.

At a top or a bottom inside the interval the derivative is 0. So a function that starts and ends at the same height has a level tangent somewhere between. Tilt that, and every chord has a tangent line beside it with the same slope. Then whatever the derivative says at every input, the heights must obey.

What it means to say it

A derivative is a statement that names a limit taken at one input. To know f′(w) you need f's values near w, as near as you like, and nothing else: change f far from w and f′(w) stays as it was. So a fact about derivatives at every input of an interval is a heap of local facts, one per input, each blind to the others. The claim of this rung is that the heap adds up. Know the derivative at every input of an interval, and you know how the heights across the interval are allowed to compare.

Here is what “control” means, in four statements. If f has derivative 0 at every input of an interval, then f has one value on the whole interval. If f has a positive derivative at every input of an interval, then f is increasing there, 13l's word: a later input always has a larger value. If the derivative is never more than some bound, then over a stretch of length L the value changes by at most the bound times L. And if two functions have the same derivative at every input of an interval, then one is the other plus a constant.

Each of these sounds obvious. A car whose speedometer reads 0 all afternoon has not moved. But none of them can be got from rung 13 by any technique applied input by input, because each technique there is about one input. Something has to reach across the interval. That something is rung 12.

Where it comes from

In the 1630s one mathematician found greatest and least values by a method he called adequality. Compare the value at an input with the value at a nearby input. Treat the two as nearly the same, divide, and then drop what is left over. His technique amounts to setting the derivative to 0, decades before anyone had the word. A rival attacked the method in 1638, and the two men argued about it through friends for a year.

In 1691 a fierce critic of the new calculus proved a fact about polynomials. Between two zeros of a polynomial there is a zero of another polynomial, built from it by a technique that is in fact the derivative. He did not think of it that way. He spent years in the Paris Academy arguing that the calculus's methods were unsound. The technique that carries his name is now where every course in the calculus begins its proofs.

The mean value technique was stated in 1797, in a book that tried to build the calculus without limits at all. A version of it was proved in lectures of 1823. The short proof this rung uses, which tilts the level-ends technique, was printed in a course of 1868. A technique for limits of a certain shape appeared in 1696, in the first textbook of the calculus, under the name of the book's author. The technique was another man's, sent to him in letters under a contract that paid for its discoveries.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the one before.

First, a top or a bottom has derivative 0. At a top, the chords to the right have slopes at most 0 and the chords to the left at least 0, and both families head to the one derivative (14a2).

Second, the level-ends technique. Take f continuous on a closed interval, with a derivative at every input strictly inside, and the same value at both ends. Rung 12's highest-and-lowest technique gives a greatest and a least value; either both sit at the ends and f is constant, or one sits inside, where the first principle gives derivative 0 (14b1). Either way some input strictly inside has derivative 0.

Third, the mean value technique. Drop the condition that the ends have the same value: subtract the chord through the two ends from f, and the level-ends technique does the rest (14c1). Between any two inputs, some tangent line has the same slope as the chord.

Fourth, zero slope means flat. Between any two inputs, the mean value technique gives an input where f′ equals the chord's slope, so a slope that is 0 everywhere makes every chord level (14d1), and a slope that is positive everywhere makes f increasing (14d2).

Fifth, same slopes, same shape lifted. If f′ and g′ agree at every input of an interval, then f − g has derivative 0 there, so by the fourth principle it is a constant (14e1). Every function with a given derivative on an interval is one of them plus a constant, which is why the answer to “what has this derivative?” comes with a “+ C”.

What follows

The inputs worth testing for a greatest or least value are few: the ends, where the derivative is 0, and where there is no derivative. The sign of the derivative between them says which way the function goes. The sign of the second derivative says which way it bends. Together these give the shape of a graph from a few numbers. Real problems that ask for the best of something, the cheapest, the fastest, the strongest, become a function to make greatest or least. The slope-ratio technique for 0/0 follows from a two-function form of the mean value technique, and settles which of two powers wins a race. And three science pages use it. The total momentum of two colliding carts has derivative 0, so it never changes. The length of a bond between two atoms is where an energy is least. And the time for a molecule to wander across a cell grows with the square of the distance, which keeps cells small.

What you can do because you know it

You can prove that a function never changes, or always climbs, from its derivative alone. You can find the greatest and least values of a function on a closed interval by testing a short list of inputs. You can bound how far a value can move from how fast it can move. You can say exactly which functions have a given derivative. You can draw a graph's shape from its first and second derivatives, and read them back off a drawing. You can set up and solve a problem of making something best. And you can take limits of 0/0 in one line, and say why the line is licensed.

This is a beginning. What you will go on to make best, the ladder cannot see from here.

And 14·stat asks which guess at a chance makes the record seen most likely, and finds by slopes that it is the share seen. It carries a box on counting.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down, and no choice is made. Everything rests on rung 12's highest-and-lowest technique. It spent the line’s no-holes once, at the least upper bound, and it proved that a greatest value is reached without saying where. That cost is inherited here. The mean value technique names no input: it proves that one exists strictly between the ends and stops. Rung 7's either-way principle is spent at the case split in the level-ends proof, where either both extreme values are at the ends or one is not. It is spent too wherever rung 12 spent it. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that slopes control heights, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat, in the 1630s, found greatest and least values by a method he called adequality: compare the value at an input with the value at a nearby input, treat the two as nearly the same, divide, and then drop what is left over. René Descartes attacked the method in 1638, and the two men argued about it through friends for a year.

Michel Rolle, in 1691, proved the fact about polynomials. He was one of the fiercest critics of the new calculus in the Paris Academy. The theorem that carries his name, Rolle’s theorem, is the ladder’s level-ends technique. The name itself was given long after him, in 1834 by Moritz Drobisch in Germany and in 1846 by Giusto Bellavitis in Italy.

Joseph-Louis Lagrange stated the mean value technique in 1797, in a book that tried to build the calculus without limits at all. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved a version of it in his lectures of 1823. The short proof this rung uses, which tilts Rolle’s technique, is usually credited to Ossian Bonnet; it was printed in Joseph Serret’s course of 1868. Guillaume de l’Hôpital put his name on a rule for limits of a certain shape in 1696, in the first textbook of the calculus. The rule was Johann Bernoulli’s, sent to him in letters under a contract that paid for Bernoulli’s discoveries.

Accepted names: the level-ends technique is Rolle’s theorem; the mean value technique is the mean value theorem; the slope-ratio technique is L’Hôpital’s rule; rung 7’s either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
