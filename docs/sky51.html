Can Cause Be Read From a Drawn Graph?

Can cause be read from a drawn graph? A study of kidney stones found that one operation cured more patients than another overall, and fewer in every group of patients, small stones and large. Which number says what the operation does? The numbers alone cannot say. Rung 30 answered that only an experiment, with the treatment given by a fair draw, reads a cause for certain, and for smoking, for schooling, for most of medicine, nobody can run that experiment. Since the 1990s a second answer has grown from computer science. Draw the variables as dots and every direct cause as an arrow. The drawing is a claim about the world, and it can be wrong; but once it is drawn, it says exactly which averages of the seen data give the effect of doing, and when no average can. This page draws the stones, proves the rule for which groups to average over on a graph of four dots, builds two worlds that look the same in every count and differ in what doing does, states the three rules that settle every case on a drawn graph and the 2006 proof that they are enough, and proves the old trick by which economists find a cause in a lottery. Who draws the graph, and whether the arrows can be read from data themselves, is argued, sometimes fiercely.

A graph without cycles claims that each variable depends only on its parents, so the chances of the whole multiply: P(z)P(x | z)P(m | x)P(y | m, z). Doing x cuts the arrows into X. When Z blocks every path from X to Y that starts with an arrow into X, P(y | do x) = Σ_z P(z)P(y | x, z): the stones give 83% and 78%, reversing the 78% and 83% seen. Two graphs can share every seen chance and give 0.8 and 0.5 for doing. If Z moves X and touches Y only through X, the effect is Cov(Z, Y)/Cov(Z, X).

sky51.1A Reversal, Drawn

A study of 700 patients with kidney stones, published in 1986, compared open surgery with a newer keyhole operation. The figures below are as the study is usually quoted. 30v1’s mixed-rate technique explains how the comparison can turn round. Here are the numbers that turned.

\text{open: small } 81/87 = 93\%,\ \text{large } 192/263 = 73\%,\ \text{all } 273/350 = 78\% @@ cured
\text{keyhole: small } 234/270 = 87\%,\ \text{large } 55/80 = 69\%,\ \text{all } 289/350 = 83\% @@ cured
\text{small: } 357/700 = 51\%,\ \text{large: } 49\% @@ the share of all patients
\htmlClass{new}{\text{open: } 0.931 \times 0.51 + 0.730 \times 0.49 = 83\%;\ \ \text{keyhole: } 78\%} \quad \blacksquare @@ each rate weighted by the whole group’s shares

Doctors gave open surgery mostly to large stones, which are harder to cure. Draw it, as dots and arrows in the manner of 42a1 with a direction on each link: stone size Z points to the choice X and to the cure Y, and X points to Y. A drawing like this, with no way round a loop of arrows, is a claim: each variable’s chances depend only on the variables with arrows into it, its parents. So the chances of the whole multiply, P(z, x, y) = P(z)P(x | z)P(y | x, z).

Doing is not seeing. To give every patient open surgery is to cut the arrow from Z into X and set X by hand, as 30v3’s fair draw does by coin. Write P(y | do x) for the chance of a cure in that changed world: the same product with the factor for X removed and x put in. Summing over z, P(y | do x) = Σ_z P(z)P(y | x, z), the weighted rates of the last line above. Open surgery does better, 83% against 78%, the reverse of the 78% and 83% seen.

The drawing did the work. Had size been caused by the operation, say a stone broken small by it, the same numbers would call for the overall rates instead. Numbers do not say which way the arrow points; the drawing does, and it can be argued with.

sky51.2The Side-Path Rule on Four Dots

Not every third variable should be averaged over. Add a fourth dot. Z points to X and to Y; X works on Y only through a middle step M, so X → M → Y. The product is P(z)P(x | z)P(m | x)P(y | m, z). The paths from X to Y are X → M → Y, the effect, and X ← Z → Y, a side path that starts with an arrow into X.

Side-path technique: on this graph, P(y | do x) = Σ_z P(z)P(y | x, z): averaging the seen rates over Z gives the effect of doing x.

P(y \mid \mathrm{do}\,x) = \textstyle\sum_{z, m} P(z)P(m \mid x)P(y \mid m, z) @@ cut the factor P(x | z)
P(m \mid x, z) = \tfrac{P(z)P(x \mid z)P(m \mid x)}{P(z)P(x \mid z)} = P(m \mid x) @@ the given-chance rule (30f2), the y-factors summing to 1
P(y \mid m, x, z) = \tfrac{P(z)P(x \mid z)P(m \mid x)P(y \mid m, z)}{P(z)P(x \mid z)P(m \mid x)} = P(y \mid m, z) @@ the same
P(y \mid x, z) = \textstyle\sum_m P(m \mid x, z)P(y \mid m, x, z) = \textstyle\sum_m P(m \mid x)P(y \mid m, z) @@ the whole-chance technique, given x and z
\htmlClass{new}{P(y \mid \mathrm{do}\,x) = \textstyle\sum_z P(z)P(y \mid x, z)} \quad \blacksquare @@ put the last line into the first
  
P(z) = \tfrac12;\ P(x \mid z) = 0.8,\,0.2;\ P(m \mid x) = 0.9,\,0.1;\ P(y \mid m, z):\ 0.9,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.1 @@ the chances of each dot, given its parents
\text{seen: } 0.78 - 0.22 = 0.56 @@ the side path adds its own link
\text{averaged over } M:\ 0.62 - 0.38 = 0.24 @@ wrong: it blocks part of the effect
\htmlClass{new}{\text{averaged over } Z:\ 0.66 - 0.34 = 0.32 = \text{doing}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the side-path technique

The general rule says the same on any graph: average over a set of dots Z when no dot of Z is downstream of X and Z blocks every side path, every path from X to Y that starts with an arrow into X. A path is blocked at a dot of Z where the arrows pass through or fan out, and at a dot where two arrows collide only if neither it nor anything below it is in Z. That last clause is a surprise: averaging over a common result of two causes creates a link between them where there was none. Its proof on every graph is the same as above with more bookkeeping, held in trust.

sky51.3What No Data Can Tell

Sometimes nothing seen will do. Build two worlds. In the first, X causes Y: P(x = 1) = ½, and Y = 1 with chance 0.8 when x = 1 and 0.2 when x = 0. In the second, X does nothing to Y: a hidden U, with P(u = 1) = ½, sets X = U and makes Y = 1 with chance 0.8 when u = 1 and 0.2 when u = 0.

Same-sight technique: these two worlds give the same chance to every pair of seen values of X and Y, and give P(y = 1 | do x = 1) as 0.8 in the first and 0.5 in the second. So no amount of data on X and Y alone can say which world it came from, or what doing X would do.

\text{first: } P(x, y) = \tfrac12 P(y \mid x) @@ the product of the graph X → Y
\text{second: } P(x, y) = P(u = x)P(y \mid u = x) = \tfrac12 P(y \mid u = x) @@ X copies U
P(y = 1 \mid x = 1) = 0.8,\ \ P(y = 1 \mid x = 0) = 0.2 \text{ in both} @@ every seen chance agrees
\text{do } x = 1:\ \ 0.8 \text{ in the first};\ \ P(u = 1)(0.8) + P(u = 0)(0.2) = 0.5 \text{ in the second} @@ cut the arrow into X
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same sight, effects } 0.6 \text{ and } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0.8 − 0.2, and 0.5 − 0.5

This is 30v1’s hidden third made exact. Measuring U would settle it; a fair draw of X would settle it. Without either, the two worlds are equally good accounts of everything seen. A graph in which a hidden cause can reach both X and Y and nothing else is drawn is the smallest case of a question with no answer from seeing, and the next footing says how to tell, on any graph, whether a question is of that kind.

sky51.4Three Rules for Doing

Between the side-path rule and the worlds that cannot be told apart lie graphs with hidden dots where some effects can be read and some cannot. In 1995 a set of three rules was published for rewriting chances that contain “do”. Stated, with the graph cut in the ways each names: a seen variable may be dropped from a chance when the cut graph shows it carries no information about Y; seeing may be swapped for doing when the cut graph shows no side path left; doing may be dropped when the cut graph shows that doing it changes nothing downstream. An effect can be read from seeing exactly when some chain of these rules removes every “do”.

The middle-step rule, stated: if a hidden U points to X and to Y, and X works on Y only through M, which U does not touch, then P(y | do x) = Σ_m P(m | x) Σ_x′ P(y | m, x′)P(x′). The rules derive it; the side-path technique cannot, since U is hidden. Checked on numbers:

P(u) = \tfrac12;\ P(x \mid u) = 0.8,\,0.2;\ P(m \mid x) = 0.75,\,0.25;\ P(y \mid m, u):\ 0.8,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.1 @@ a world with U hidden
\text{seen: } 0.660 - 0.275 = 0.385 @@ the side path through U
\text{true, by cutting: } 0.5625 - 0.3875 = 0.175 @@ needs U
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the rule, from seen chances only: } 0.5625 - 0.3875} \quad \blacksquare @@ agrees

Whether the rules were enough was open for a decade. In 2006 it was proved that they are: if the three rules cannot remove every “do”, no formula can, because a second world can then be built, as in the same-sight technique, that agrees with the first on everything seen and differs on the effect.

sky51.5A Lever From Outside

Economists found their own way past the hidden third, long before the graphs. Find a Z that moves X, is set as if by a coin, and touches Y only through X: a lottery for school places, a birth date that decides when a child starts school, a draft by drawn numbers. Suppose Y = βX + U, where U holds every hidden cause of Y and may be linked to X, and Z is independent of U.

Lever technique: if Y = βX + U, Cov(Z, U) = 0 and Cov(Z, X) ≠ 0, then β = Cov(Z, Y)/Cov(Z, X).

\mathrm{Cov}(Z, Y) = \mathrm{Cov}(Z, \beta X + U) @@ put in Y
= \beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(Z, X) + \mathrm{Cov}(Z, U) @@ the co-spread is straight in each place (30g2)
= \beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(Z, X) @@ Z independent of U
\htmlClass{new}{\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(Z, Y)/\mathrm{Cov}(Z, X)} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide
  
Z = 1 \text{ (won)}: 70\% \text{ attend},\ \text{average score } 52.0 @@ 
Z = 0 \text{ (lost)}: 20\% \text{ attend},\ \text{average score } 48.5 @@ 
\text{for } Z \text{ of } 0 \text{ or } 1:\ \beta = \tfrac{52.0 - 48.5}{0.7 - 0.2} @@ the co-spreads, each divided by the spread of Z
\htmlClass{new}{\beta = 7 \text{ points for attending}} \quad \blacksquare @@ not 3.5, the lottery’s own effect

On a graph, Z → X → Y with a hidden U pointing to X and Y, and no arrow from Z to Y or U. The three rules cannot read the effect on this graph from seeing alone; the lever reads it only because of the extra claim that Y is straight in X. Without it the lever gives the effect on those whom the lottery moved, and only if winning never makes anyone less likely to attend. The 2021 prize in economics went in part for methods of this kind.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞51.oq.1Can the arrows be learned from data? Independences in the data rule some graphs out: if X and Y are linked but become unlinked once Z is known, Z sits between them or above them. But graphs with the same links and the same colliding pairs give the same independences, so seeing alone can at best find a family of graphs. Methods that search for that family exist, and they need many records, assumptions about hidden dots, and luck with errors in the tests. How far learning arrows from real data can be trusted, and what extra claim (a time order, a shape of noise, data from several settings) best pins them down, is open.
∞51.oq.2What is a cause when units touch each other? The graphs above treat each patient alone: my treatment affects my cure. A vaccine protects the people around me; a training scheme changes the jobs others get; a post changes what friends see. Then each unit has as many futures as there are ways to treat everyone, and both the graphs and the fair draw of 30v3 need rebuilding. Which effects can be read, from which designs, when units interfere, is open.
∞51.oq.3Should causes be drawn as graphs or written as pairs of futures? 30v3 wrote each unit’s two futures, treated and not; the graphs draw arrows. Most questions can be put both ways, and the answers agree when both apply. Yet the two schools have argued about which is clearer, which hides its claims better or worse, and which is more use to someone with real data. Whether one is better for practice, or each for different work, is argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Statistics spent much of the twentieth century wary of talking about cause without an experiment. The fair draw made experiments convincing, and the lesson drawn was that seeing alone could show only links. The test came with smoking. When studies found that smokers got lung cancer far more often, one of the founders of modern statistics answered that a hidden inborn trait might cause both the wish to smoke and the cancer: the second world of sky51.3. The answer was not wrong in logic. It was answered by many studies pointing the same way, by the rise of risk with the dose, and by work in the laboratory, not by a formula.

Arrows for causes are older than the modern rules, in genetics and in the equations of economists. What was missing was a clear account of doing. In the 1990s it arrived from the study of machines that reason with uncertainty: graphs of chances were read as graphs of causes, the side-path rule was stated for every graph, and in 1995 a paper in a leading statistics journal set out the three rules. In 2006 the rules were proved complete. Their author received the highest award in computing for 2011, for a calculus of chance and cause.

Many statisticians were unconvinced. Another school wrote each unit’s futures, treated and not, as 30v3 does, and found the graphs unnecessary or worse: a drawing invites claims that nobody has checked. The graph school replied that every analysis of seen data makes such claims, and that a drawing at least puts them where they can be argued with. Each side has written that the other hides its assumptions, and the argument continues in print.

Economists took their own road. They sought natural experiments, lotteries, borders and changes in law that set a treatment as if by a coin, and they made exact what the lever estimates when effects differ from person to person. In 2021 the prize in economics went to three of them, two of them for methods of analysing causes.

Today the graphs are taught in epidemiology and used in medicine, in social science and in machine learning, where a model that learns links can fail when the world is changed by acting on it. Learning the arrows from data has grown into its own field with mixed results. And every reading of a cause from seeing still rests on a drawing, which is only as good as what its author knew.

The accepted names

A drawn graph of causes is a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG), or causal Bayesian network; the product over parents is the Markov factorization, and doing x is an intervention, written with the do-operator. The side-path technique is the back-door adjustment, and the general rule is the back-door criterion, with blocking defined by d-separation; a dot where two arrows collide is a collider, and averaging over it is collider bias. The same-sight technique is non-identifiability, and the smallest case is the bow graph. The three rules are the do-calculus, its completeness is the 2006 result, and the method that decides is the ID algorithm. The middle-step rule is the front-door criterion. The lever technique is the instrumental variable estimator, the Wald estimator for a Z of 0 or 1, and the effect on those whom Z moves is the local average treatment effect (LATE), under monotonicity. Genes as levers are Mendelian randomization. The reversal is Simpson’s paradox, and the second school is the potential outcomes framework, or Rubin causal model. Units that affect each other show interference, and learning arrows is causal discovery, which finds a Markov equivalence class.

Who said it first

The kidney stone study is by C. R. Charig and coworkers, BMJ, 1986, and is a standard example of Simpson’s paradox. Judea Pearl published the do-calculus in “Causal diagrams for empirical research” (Biometrika, 1995) and received the A. M. Turing Award for 2011. Yimin Huang and Marco Valtorta proved the calculus complete in 2006 (“Pearl’s calculus of intervention is complete”), as did Ilya Shpitser and Pearl. David Card, Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens shared the 2021 prize, Angrist and Imbens for their methodological contributions to the analysis of causal relationships. Ronald Fisher made the inborn-trait argument about smoking; the second school is the potential outcomes school of Donald Rubin, after Jerzy Neyman; Peter Spirtes, Clark Glymour and Richard Scheines developed causal discovery; Sewall Wright drew the first path diagrams.

A proof that is not allowed

“Open surgery cures 78% and keyhole surgery 83%, so keyhole surgery is better.” Not allowed: it reads seeing as doing. The patients chose, or were chosen, by stone size, and size also decides the cure. Only with a drawing that says so can the weighted rates be read as the effect.
