How Can a Body Be Ready for Germs It Has Never Met?

How can a body be ready for germs it has never met? Each B cell in the blood carries one kind of antibody, a shape that grips some shapes and not others. There are far too many germs for the body to keep a gene for each. Instead, each new B cell builds its antibody by picking one piece from each of a few boxes of gene pieces. This page counts the antibodies the picks can make, from one textbook’s counts of the pieces, and looks at the picking itself: one choosing function in each cell, made by chance, over finitely many boxes.

Count the ways to build one chain by stages-multiply, one stage a box. Where a chain may come from one of two families that share nothing, add the two counts by separate-piles. Then a heavy chain and a light chain together multiply again. Each cell’s picks are a choosing function over finitely many boxes, so 6c says no axiom is needed to make them.

6bio1A Grip Built From Pieces

An antibody is made of two kinds of chain, a heavy chain and a light chain, and the tip where they meet is the part that grips. A new B cell builds the tip of its heavy chain by joining three gene pieces: one V piece, one D piece and one J piece, each picked from its own box. It builds the tip of its light chain from one V piece and one J piece, from one of two families, called κ and λ.

6bio3Counting Light Chains

A light chain is built from κ pieces or from λ pieces, never from a mixture. So the κ chains and the λ chains share nothing.

\kappa:\ 40 \cdot 5 = 200 @@ stages-multiply
\lambda:\ 30 \cdot 4 = 120 @@ stages-multiply
\htmlClass{new}{200 + 120 = 320} @@ the separate-piles technique (4k): the two families share nothing

The counts add, not multiply, because a light chain takes one family or the other. Stages multiply; piles that share nothing add.

6bio4A Heavy Chain and a Light Chain

Each cell pairs one heavy chain with one light chain, and any heavy chain can go with any light chain. So the pair is two more stages.

10\,530 \cdot 320 @@ stages-multiply: a heavy chain, then a light chain
\htmlClass{new}{3\,369\,600} @@ about three and a half million grips from the pieces alone

Janeway’s book rounds the heavy count to 11 000 and gives about 3.5 million. Either way, a few hundred pieces in seven boxes make millions of grips, and the counting is nothing but 4k.

6bio5One Pick in Each Cell

Each new B cell makes its picks once, by chance, and keeps them for life. Its picks give one piece from each box it uses: that is a choosing function, in 6b’s sense, for the collection of boxes. There are finitely many boxes, so 6c’s finite-choice says the picks can be made in turn, and no axiom is needed. The cell does not need a rule that names its piece, as least-chooses does; chance picks, and the picking is finite.

The joins themselves add more. Where two pieces are joined, the cell trims a few letters and adds a few at random, so one choice of pieces can make many grips. Janeway’s book estimates as many as 1011 grips in all with the joins counted. That number is an estimate, not a count: the trimming has no fixed box of outcomes to multiply.

Last, the body uses the picks. A cell whose grip fits a germ it meets is told to divide, and its copies all carry the same grip. The body does not design a grip for each germ; it makes millions at random, and copies the ones that fit.

Accepted names

The pieces are gene segments, and joining them is V(D)J recombination. The heavy chain’s boxes are VH, DH and JH; κ and λ are the two light-chain loci. The count from the pieces alone is combinatorial diversity; the extra from trimming and adding at the joins is junctional diversity. Copying the cells whose grip fits is clonal selection, and all the grips together are the repertoire.

Where the numbers come from

The box counts and the estimate of 1011 are from Janeway’s Immunobiology, 5th edition, section 4-6 and 4-7. Its later editions and other sources give somewhat different counts, because people differ and because some pieces are counted as working by one source and not by another.
