34k · technique

Surfaces From a Square

In an old arcade game, a ship that flies off the right edge of the screen comes back on the left, and one that flies off the top comes back at the bottom. The ship is flying on a torus. A square with its sides sewn in pairs makes three of the surfaces of the list, depending only on which way round each pair is sewn. Sew both pairs straight and the result is the torus. Sew one pair with a twist and it is the one-sided bottle. Sew both with a twist and it is the cross-cap. This step builds each by hand, reads its count and its sides, finds it in the list, and follows straight lines across it. It then gives the move that brings a pair sewn the same way round side by side, and uses it to carry out the one move the list left to this step: a handle beside a twisted cap is three twisted caps.

A straight line of fraction slope through a corner of the torus closes up after as many crossings each way as the slope’s bottom and top, and a line of slope that is not a fraction never closes. The bottle sewn from a square is two cross-caps summed, and the square sewn with both pairs twisted is one cross-cap, with count one. A same-way pair x, with strings A and B between, can be cut and resewn so that a new pair sits side by side, and three such moves turn a handle beside a twisted cap into three twisted caps.

34k1The Torus, and Straight Lines on It

Sew the left side of the unit square to the right and the bottom to the top, straight across: the word is x y x⁻¹ y⁻¹, all four corners sew to one, and the count is 1 − 2 + 1 = 0, with two sides. That is the torus of the list, g = 1. A straight line on it is a straight line in the square, carried on from the opposite side each time it leaves. In the plane over the torus of 34g5, it is a straight line through the plane.

Slope-loop technique: on the square torus, the straight line from a corner with slope n/m, in lowest terms with m > 0, closes for the first time after crossing the left and right sides m times and the top and bottom |n| times; a line whose slope is not a fraction never closes.

(t,\ \tfrac nm t),\ \ t \ge 0 @@ the line in the plane over the torus, from the corner (0, 0)
\text{back at the corner} \iff t \text{ and } \tfrac{nt}{m} \text{ both whole} @@ the plane over the torus sends exactly the whole pairs to the corner (34g5)
m \mid nt \text{ and } n, m \text{ share no factor} \Rightarrow m \mid t @@ lowest terms (5b): every factor of m divides t
\text{first at } t = m:\ \ m \text{ crossings across},\ |n| \text{ up or down} @@ x passes 1, 2, …, m; y passes n whole values
\htmlClass{new}{\text{its loop is } (m, n)} @@ the pair it ends at, in 34g5’s count
s \text{ not a fraction},\ \ t,\ st \text{ whole},\ t > 0 \Rightarrow s = \tfrac{st}{t} \text{ a fraction} \quad \blacksquare @@ a false line: the line never closes

A line whose slope is not a fraction never closes, and it comes as near as you like to every point of the square, which is proved much higher up the ladder. A loop of slope 2/3 on the torus is the loop (3, 2): it winds three times round one way and twice round the other.

34k2The Bottle

Sew the bottom to the top straight, and the left to the right with a twist, so the left side’s bottom meets the right side’s top. The word is x y x y⁻¹. The x pair is sewn the same way round, so the surface holds a twisted band and has one side. All four corners sew to one: the count is 1 − 2 + 1 = 0.

Same-way move: a word x A x B, read round, with strings A and B, can be cut and resewn into z z A B⁻¹, the same surface. Cut along the diagonal z from the end of the first x to the end of the second. Two polygons result, one with A, x and z on its rim, one with B, x and z. Flip the second over and sew the two along x. The rim now reads z z A B⁻¹, up to where it starts, with z renamed. B⁻¹ is B read backwards, with every letter undone.

Bottle-sum technique: the bottle sewn from a square is two cross-caps summed.

x\,y\,x\,y^{-1} = x\,A\,x\,B,\ \ A = y,\ B = y^{-1} @@ read the word in the move’s form
\to z\,z\,y\,y @@ the same-way move: B⁻¹ = y
\htmlClass{new}{z\,z \text{ and } y\,y:\ \text{two cross-caps summed}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two same-way pairs, side by side (34f4); count 2 − 2 = 0, as before

The bottle cannot be built in space without passing through itself. Glass-blowers make it with its neck pushed through its own side, and the crossing is a picture, not part of the surface.

34k3The Cross-Cap From a Square

Sew both pairs with a twist. The word is x y x y. Corners: the bottom-left sews to the top-right, and the bottom-right to the top-left, so two classes. The count is 2 − 2 + 1 = 1, and there is one side.

Square-cap technique: the square sewn by x y x y is the cross-cap, the one-sided surface with count 1.

x\,y\,x\,y:\ \ V = 2,\ n = 2,\ \chi = 1 @@ the word-count technique (34f3)
\text{one side} @@ the x pair is sewn the same way round
\htmlClass{new}{\chi = 2 - k \Rightarrow k = 1:\ \text{the cross-cap}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the surface-list technique (34f4)

Another picture of the same surface: the lines through the centre of space. Each line meets the sphere in two points straight across, and the sphere over the cross-cap of 34g5 sends both to that line. The upper half of the sphere already meets every line once, except the flat ones, which meet its rim twice. So the cross-cap is a disc with each rim point sewn to the point straight across: x x. Painters met these lines as the points at infinity of 29m’s perspective.

34k4A Handle Beside a Twisted Cap

34f4’s list needed one move carried out here: a torus summed with a cross-cap is three cross-caps. The word of the sum is x y x⁻¹ y⁻¹ z z.

Handle-cap technique: a torus summed with a cross-cap is three cross-caps summed.

z\,z\,(x\,y)(x^{-1}\,y^{-1}) \to u\,(x\,y)\,u\,(y\,x) @@ the same-way move run backwards, with A = x y and B⁻¹ = x⁻¹ y⁻¹, so B = y x
x\,u\,x\,(y\,u\,y) \to w\,w\,u\,y^{-1}\,u^{-1}\,y^{-1} @@ read from the last x: A = u, B = y u y, and B⁻¹ = y⁻¹ u⁻¹ y⁻¹
v = y^{-1}:\ \ v\,u^{-1}\,v\,(w\,w\,u) \to t\,t\,u^{-1}\,u^{-1}\,w^{-1}\,w^{-1} @@ read from the first v: A = u⁻¹, B = w w u
\htmlClass{new}{t\,t,\ \ u^{-1}u^{-1},\ \ w^{-1}w^{-1}:\ \text{three cross-caps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ count 1 − 3 + 1 = −1 = 2 − 3 at every stage, one corner class, one side

So once a surface has one twisted cap, every handle on it can be traded for two more caps. That is why the list has no mixed kind. A doughnut-shaped surface with a twist sewn in somewhere is a surface with three twisted caps, and nothing else.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The bottle is the Klein bottle, and the cross-cap is the real projective plane, ℝP². The lines through the centre of space are the points of ℝP²; the sphere over it is its universal double cover. The same-way move is the standard lemma of the classification by cutting and pasting, and the handle-cap technique is the relation T # ℝP² ≅ ℝP² # ℝP² # ℝP². Straight lines on the torus of slope that is not a fraction are irrational windings, and the claim that they come near every point is Kronecker’s theorem.

Who said it first

Felix Klein described the bottle in 1882. Walther von Dyck proved the handle-cap relation in 1888. The arcade game is Asteroids, released by Atari in 1979.

A proof that is not allowed

“x y x y has two letters, each twice, like x y x⁻¹ y⁻¹. Both have four edges and one face, so they are the same surface.” Not allowed: the count needs the corner classes, 1 for the torus and 2 here, and the sides need the directions of the pairs. The two words make different surfaces.
