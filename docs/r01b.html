1b · proof

Not, and the totally false

The earliest joining word is the one that joins nothing: “not”. It takes a statement and denies it. This step gives “not” its mark and its techs. Alongside it, it builds a statement that is never true at all, the totally false, which is the target every denial aims at. With these comes the way to end a supposition that reaches something totally false: suppose a thing, reach something totally false, and conclude that the thing is not so. The step also gives “is not the same thing as” its mark, as “not” said of 0b's sameness. And it forecloses one step on purpose, from not-not to so, until rung 7.

To deny a statement, suppose it and reach something totally false. A statement and its denial together are totally false. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, and that is harmless, because the totally false is never reached from true lines.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0a (statements, the bracket), 0b (the mark =, the axioms self-sameness and swap, the techniques turn-about and chain), 1a (a reason for each sentence, unpack, the techniques turn-about and chain earned) and 1a (supposing for a while, nesting, the blame). Every later step uses ¬ and ⊥; 1c, 1d and 1e use the supposition opened here for their own techs; 1g lists which rewriting techniques about ¬ this rung licenses; rung 7 closes the gap left in this step.

1b1The word, then the mark

Stands on 0a3 (what a statement is).

“Ann is at home” is a statement: so or not so. “Ann is not at home” is also a statement, and it says exactly the opposite: it is so when the former is not, and not so when the former is. English scatters its denials through a sentence (“not”, “no”, “never”, “isn't”, “un-”), and a denial can be hard to find in a long sentence. The ladder puts every denial in the same place, in front, with a mark. Here is the line that brings it in:

¬P is called the negation of P.

So if P is “Ann is at home”, ¬P is “Ann is not at home”. If Q is “Tibbles is grey”, ¬Q is “Tibbles is not grey”. The mark denies whatever statement stands right after it, and only that.

1b2Brackets that group

Stands on 1b1 (the word, then the mark) and 0a4 (a claim, written with a bracket).

When the statement after the mark is itself built from pieces, it goes in brackets, so that the reach of the mark is plain. For example, ¬(x = b) denies the whole sameness: “it is not so that x is b”. Brackets, as in 0a, are writing and not things; they show what belongs together.

From here on, round brackets also do this job: they group, so that ¬(a = b) denies the whole of a = b. Their job from 0a, holding a name in the gap of a statement-with-a-gap as in P(a), still stands: a letter written just before the bracket says it is 0a's.

Notice that ¬P is a statement in 0a's sense. So it can be stated, be given a reason, and be denied in turn: ¬¬P, “it is not so that it is not so that P”. English finds that hard to hear; the mark makes it easy to write. Whether it says the same as P is the question this step ends on.

1b3The totally false

Stands on 1b2 (brackets that group) and 1a16 (when a supposition reaches something totally false).

A denial needs a target. What does it mean to deny P? In life: that the world is not that way. The ladder cannot look at the world, so it says instead: supposing P leads to something that can never be true. For that we want a statement that is never true, to serve as the thing reached. Here it is:

Read ⊥ as “totally false”. It is not any particular statement about cats or towns. It is the statement you arrive at when you have said something and its denial in the same breath. For example, “Ann is at home” and “Ann is not at home”, said together, are it. Reaching it is not a slip on your part. Reaching it marks the latest guess as one its own denial can counter in full, and finding how to reach it is a shortcut to saying no to that guess. Nothing on the ladder ever earns ⊥ from given lines that are so. Its use is as the place a refuted guess ends up. A supposition that leads to ⊥ was a supposition of something that is not so, and the argument has just found that out.

Typing an answer, with no key for these marks? Write the words they are read as: not P in place of ¬P, and totally false in place of ⊥. They mean the same thing, and the ladder reads them the same way.

1b4Clash

Stands on 1b3 (the totally false).

1a built supposing. You say “suppose P”, and keep it until we drop it. While it stands, what you say may lean on P. When you drop it, you keep one conclusion. Everything that leaned on P goes with the supposition. Suppositions nest, and the latest one is dropped first.

Every connective on this rung has a tech that ends a supposition in its own way. This step's closing tech is refute, and it needs a helper, clash. 1a used refute in words, held in trust, and left it for this step to set down. With the two techs set down here and in the next step, that foreclosure completes.

Clash: from P and ¬P, you may write ⊥. A statement and its denial together are totally false. Clash is how a “not” you have gets used. For example: Ann is at home, and ¬(Ann is at home); so, by clash, ⊥. The two sentences clash only because the second denies that very statement, word for word. You already do this. Told “the shop is open” and “the shop is not open”, you know at once that something has gone wrong.

1b5Refute

Stands on 1b4 (clash) and 1a16 (the blame).

Refute: if, supposing P, you reach ⊥, you may drop the supposition and conclude ¬P. Refute is how a “not” is made. Refute denies a supposition, never a sentence said under it. As 1a set out, when ⊥ appears the blame falls on the latest supposition still standing.

Here is refute at work. Mia is in class; could Mia be out sick?

Mia is in class. That is given. Suppose, until we drop it, that Mia is out sick. Unpack “out sick”: away from school because ill. So Mia is absent. Unpack “absent”: it means not in class. So ¬(Mia is in class). But Mia is in class. That is a clash, and a clash is totally false. So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬(Mia is out sick).

Go through it again. What was given stands throughout. Under the supposition, the words “out sick” are unpacked until a denial appears. That denial clashes with what was given. That earns ⊥, and ⊥ under a supposition is exactly what refute wants. The supposition is dropped, and what we conclude is the denial of what was supposed. Nothing said under the supposition may be used again. In particular, “Mia is absent” is not something you have. You have only two things: that Mia is in class, and that Mia is not out sick.

1b6Refute, with a swap

Stands on 1b5 (refute) and 0b5 (swap equals).

Here is the same tech, with 0b's swap axiom doing the work under the supposition. Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat. The cat on the mat is not grey. So Tibbles is not grey.

Tibbles is the cat on the mat: t = m. The cat on the mat is not grey: ¬(m is grey). Suppose, until we drop it, that t is grey. Since t = m, the swap axiom lets us write m where t stood. So m is grey. That clashes with ¬(m is grey). So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬(t is grey).

The shape is the one from the last step. A supposition, moves under it until a clash, and the supposition dropped. Only the move under the supposition changed. There it was an unpack; here it is a swap.

1b7Not the same thing as

Stands on 1b6 (refute, with a swap) and 0b2 (the mark for sameness).

0b gave sameness its mark. Its denial is common enough to want a mark of its own, and here it is, defined as “not” said of “=”:

A small milestone. ≠ is the first operator the ladder has built out of other operators. It is “not”, from this step, laid over “=”, from 0b, and “=” rests on the self-sameness axiom. Unpack ≠ and you reach ¬(a = b). Trace “=” back and you reach an axiom. The ladder will build operators out of operators, all the way down to an axiom, over and over. This is the first time.

≠ is defined by its “from here on” line. So every argument about ≠ begins by unpacking ≠ into ¬(x = b), and ends by packing it back. (1a's rule: unpacking a mark is the unpack tech applied to its “from here on” line.)

1b8Not-sameness turns about

Stands on 1b7 (not the same thing as) and 0b6 (sameness turns about).

Said of people first: Ann is not the baker, so the baker is not Ann. Of course. The argument below replaces “of course” with the names of techs.

Here is the earliest technique about ≠. Turn-about technique for not-same: if x is not the same thing as b, then b is not the same thing as x. It needs one line, x ≠ b. It writes b ≠ x.

Start from x ≠ b. Unpack ≠: ¬(x = b). Now suppose, until we drop it, that b = x. By 0b's turn-about technique, x = b. That clashes with ¬(x = b). So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬(b = x). Pack ≠: b ≠ x.

Compare it with the turn-about technique on 1a, which it follows sentence for sentence. There, a sentence from the self-sameness axiom was the thing to swap under the supposition. Here, the turn-about technique itself says the sentence that clashes.

1b9Same and not the same, mixed

Stands on 1b8 (not-sameness turns about) and 0b7 (chain).

Said of people: Ann is the baker, and Ann is not the postman; so the baker is not the postman. Of course. The argument below replaces “of course” with the names of techs. It also shows refute doing what it always does: a supposition, a clash, and the supposition dropped.

Mixed-sameness technique: if x is the same thing as b, and x is not the same thing as c, then b is not the same thing as c. It needs two lines, x = b and x ≠ c. It writes b ≠ c. Watch 0b's chain do the work under the supposition.

Start from x = b and x ≠ c. Unpack ≠: ¬(x = c). Now suppose, until we drop it, that b = c. By 0b's chain technique, x = b and b = c give x = c. That clashes with ¬(x = c). So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬(b = c). Pack ≠: b ≠ c.

1b10When nothing is true, everything is permitted

Stands on 1b5 (refute) and 1a12 (what a sentence under a supposition may cite).

When nothing is true, everything is permitted: from ⊥, you may write any statement at all. The medieval name is ex falso quodlibet: from the false, what you please. It looks reckless and is perfectly safe. Its everyday work is closing off a case that cannot happen; 1c will show that at once.

This tech troubles people. From ⊥ you may write anything: “Tibbles is grey”, “the moon is made of cheese”, whatever you like. How can that be reasoning? Think about when ⊥ is ever reached. Only under a supposition that is being refuted, or from given lines that clash with one another. In the first case, whatever you write from ⊥ still leans on the supposition, and is dropped with it. The tech is used to reach the line the case must reach, and 1c's reasoning by cases is where you will see why that is wanted. In the second case, the given lines were not all so to begin with, and nothing true has been put at risk. An argument whose given lines clash proves nothing worth having, whatever techs it used. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, and no true line is ever turned into a false one. Turning a true line into a false one is the only thing a tech must never do.

Rex is not indoors. Suppose, until we drop it, that Rex is indoors. That clashes with what was given: totally false. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, so we may say “Rex is asleep”. But that still leans on the supposition. Drop the supposition, by refute, and all we conclude is ¬(Rex is indoors). The “asleep” goes with the supposition. Nothing has been learned about sleep.

So a line written after ⊥ is a kind of private note. It can agree with the argument, as the library case will in 1c, or it can have no bearing on the argument at all, like Rex's sleep. Whatever is written after ⊥ stays behind when the guess is refused; only the refutation leaves.

1b11Not-not in

Stands on 1b5 (refute) and 1b2 (brackets that group).

Not-not-in technique: from any statement P, you may write ¬¬P. It needs P in hand. You already do this too. If Ann is at home, then it is not so that Ann is not at home, and nobody would argue.

Start from P. Suppose, until we drop it, ¬P. That clashes with P: totally false. So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬¬P.

Run it once on Ann. Ann is at home. Suppose, until we drop it, ¬(Ann is at home). That clashes with the given: totally false. So, by refute, ¬¬(Ann is at home): it is not so that Ann is not at home. Short, and always available.

1b12The step you cannot take

Stands on 1b11 (not-not in).

Now try the other way, from ¬¬P to P. Refute ends only at a statement that begins with ¬. Clash needs a statement and its denial, and you have only ¬¬P. Everything permitted needs ⊥. So no tech on this rung takes ¬¬P to P. The step is foreclosed, not because it cannot be closed here, but because the space between the one little rule from ¬¬P to P and so many techniques is an insight worth being able to feel. Rung 7 closes it. Until then, an argument that reaches ¬¬P stops there and says so.

Here is something to feel it on. ¬¬(Ann is at home) says only that “Ann is not at home” cannot stand. Nobody has seen Ann.

It helps to hear ¬P as “P leads to something totally false”. So “Rex is not indoors” is heard as “Rex being indoors leads to something totally false”. Then refute is proving that something leads to something totally false, by supposing it and getting there. Clash is following the lead: P, and P leads to something totally false, so something totally false. 1d will give “leads to” a mark of its own and make this exact. Under that hearing, ¬¬P says that “P leads to something totally false” itself leads to something totally false. That is plainly weaker than P.

1b13Illegal moves

Stands on 1b4 to 1b12.

Five moves look like uses of this step and are not. Naming the failure is the skill. One uses something said under a supposition after it has been dropped. One takes the lack of a proof of P as a proof of ¬P. One goes from ¬¬P to P, which is the gap. One treats a statement that only sounds contrary as a clash. And one writes ¬ in front of a bare name.

\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose Rex is indoors; } \dots\; \bot;\\ &\text{so } \neg(\text{Rex is indoors}); \text{ and Rex is }\\ &\quad \text{asleep (from under it).}\end{aligned} @@ a sentence said under a dropped supposition is cited after the drop. Those sentences leaned on the supposition; dropping it took them away
\begin{aligned}&\text{nobody has proved that Tibbles is }\\ &\quad \text{grey;}\\ &\text{so } \neg(\text{Tibbles is grey})?\end{aligned} @@ the absence of a proof of P is not a proof of ¬P. Refute needs a supposition that reaches ⊥, not a search that came back empty
\begin{aligned}&\neg\neg(\text{Ann is at home}),\\ &\quad \;\text{ so Ann is at home?}\end{aligned} @@ the gap: no tech on this rung does this. Rung 7
\begin{aligned}&\neg(\text{Rex is a good dog}) \\\ &\quad ;\text{ from } \neg(\text{Rex is a dog})?\end{aligned} @@ no tech: “not a dog” is a denial of “Rex is a dog”, which says nothing about “Rex is a good dog”. Clash needs a statement and that very statement denied
\neg\,\text{Rex} @@ a name is never bare: the self-sameness axiom stands behind every argument, so Rex comes with Rex = Rex already given. To deny Rex is to write ¬(Rex = Rex), the denial of the self-sameness axiom, and it clashes with the self-sameness axiom in every argument

The last two illegal moves, the sound-alike clash and the bare name, are the ones that fool careful people. Clash is exact. It wants P and ¬P, the same statement denied, and not merely a statement that sounds contrary. “Rex is not a dog” and “Rex is a good dog” feel like a clash and are not one in this step. To make them clash, unpack “Rex is a good dog” to “Rex is a dog”, which the given line denies; and the proof must show the unpacking. As for the gap: the temptation is strongest exactly where it is least needed, in everyday cases where you could simply go and look. The ladder cannot look, and does not pretend to.

1b14Running it backwards

Stands on 1b5 (refute) and 1a19 (reading the foot).

Wanting a statement that begins with ¬, you know how the argument must end: refute. So just before the end you need ⊥, reached under a supposition of the thing denied. Wanting ⊥, you need a clash: some statement and its denial, both under the supposition or carried in from the given lines. So open the supposition and ask what it can be unpacked or swapped into that is denied by something you have. That is the whole search.

For “Mia is not out sick”, the clash you want is “¬(Mia is in class)” against “Mia is in class”. You have the second. The supposition must produce the first, and unpacking “out sick”, then “absent”, does. Found backward, said forward.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • \neg P is also written ~P, and in code !P or NOT P. Some books write a bar over P, or a stroke after it.
  • \bot is also read “absurdity”, “contradiction” or “falsum”. The ladder says “totally false”: a statement that fails in every case, not one that is merely silly.
  • The tech “when nothing is true, everything is permitted” is called ex falso quodlibet in most books, or the principle of explosion.
  • A line such as ¬Rex, built against the way lines are built, is called ill-formed in most books, and a line built rightly is a well-formed formula. The ladder's reason above says why such a line fails.

Who said it first

Aristotle called this the firmest of all principles: the same thing cannot both belong and not belong to the same subject, at the same time and in the same respect. Anyone who denies it, he said, must still speak, and in speaking uses it.
