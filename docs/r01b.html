1b · proof

Not, and the totally false

The earliest joining word is the one that joins nothing: “not”. It takes a statement and denies it. This step gives “not” its mark, and the brackets that show how far it reaches. Alongside it, it builds a statement that is never true at all, the totally false. A line comes to the totally false when it holds a statement and its denial together, and then it cannot meet its operator’s condition, whatever that operator is. That is clash. The step works lines down to the totally false, carries denials across a sameness, and gives “is not the same thing as” its mark, as “not” said of 0b's sameness.

A statement and its denial in one line make the line totally false. A line that equals the totally false shows that its statements cannot all stand together, and nothing more. Which of them to deny is a proof’s output, and waits for proofs.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0a (statements, the bracket), 0b (the mark =, the axioms self-sameness and swap, the techniques turn-about and chain) and 1a (a line, its operator’s condition, givens, unpack and pack). Every later step uses ¬ and ⊥; 1c and 1d work lines with them; 1f lists which rewriting techniques about ¬ this rung licenses. Supposing comes with rung 3, and the denial a refuted line points to comes with proofs.

1b1The word, then the mark

Stands on 0a3 (what a statement is).

“Ann is at home” is a statement: so or not so. “Ann is not at home” is also a statement, and it says exactly the opposite: it is so when the former is not, and not so when the former is. English scatters its denials through a sentence (“not”, “no”, “never”, “isn't”, “un-”), and a denial can be hard to find in a long sentence. The ladder puts every denial in the same place, in front, with a mark. Here is the line that brings it in:

¬P is called the negation of P.

So ¬P is “Ann is not at home”. The mark denies whatever statement stands right after it, and only that.

1b2Brackets that group

Stands on 1b1 (the word, then the mark) and 0a4 (a claim, written with a bracket).

When the statement after the mark is itself built from pieces, it goes in brackets, so that the reach of the mark is plain. Brackets, as in 0a, are writing and not things; they show what belongs together.

From here on, round brackets also do this job: they group, so that ¬(x = b) denies the whole of x = b. Their job from 0a, holding a name in the gap of a statement-with-a-gap as in P(x), still stands: a letter written just before the bracket says it is 0a's.

Notice that ¬P is a statement in 0a's sense. So it can be stated, be given a reason, and be denied in turn: ¬¬P, “it is not so that it is not so that P”. English finds that hard to hear; the mark makes it easy to write. Whether it says the same as P is a question for later rungs: a line cannot settle it.

1b3The totally false

Stands on 1b2 (brackets that group) and 1a4 (what the operator tells you).

Sometimes a line holds a statement and its denial together. A statement and its denial together are totally false. For example: “Ben is married, Ben is not married”, both in one line. Writing such a line is not a mistake. It is the line showing that its statements cannot stand together.

A denial needs a target. What does it mean to deny P? In life: that the world is not that way. The ladder cannot look at the world, so it says instead: a line with P in it can be worked to something that can never be true. For that we want a statement that is never true, for the line to come to. Here it is:

Read ⊥ as “totally false”. It is not any particular statement about cats or towns. It is what a line comes to when it holds something and its denial in the same breath, as Ben's line did just above. A line that equals ⊥ cannot meet its operator’s condition, whatever its operator is. Reaching it shows that the line's statements cannot all be so together. No line of true givens ever equals ⊥. A line that does was holding something that is not so, and the working has just found that out, without saying which statement it was.

No key for these marks? Write not P for ¬P and totally false for ⊥, as 1a7 wrote unpack.

1b4Clash

Stands on 1b3 (the totally false).

1b3 built the totally false as the value a line comes to when it holds a statement and its denial. The tech that writes that value is clash.

Every joining word on this rung has techs for working a line that holds it. This step gives “not” its first: clash. The next steps work lines down to it.

Clash: a line that holds P and ¬P equals ⊥. A statement and its denial together are totally false. Clash is how a “not” in the line gets used. For example, the line “Ann is at home, ¬(Ann is at home)” ends, by clash: Ann is at home, ¬(Ann is at home) = ⊥. The line clashes only because the second statement denies the first, word for word.

1b5A line that cannot stand

Stands on 1b4 (clash) and 1a7 (unpack and pack).

Most lines that cannot stand do not show it at once. The clash is hidden inside a word, and unpacking brings it out. A line worked by techniques until it equals ⊥ is refuted: its statements cannot all be so together. That is everything a line can tell you. It does not say which statement is to blame.

Picking one statement out and writing its denial on its own is more than a line can output. A line can only say what its own statement comes to. Writing the denial of one statement from the line it sat in is a proof’s output, and it waits for proofs.

Here is a line refuted. Mia is in class; Mia is out sick. Can both stand?

Write both in one line: Mia is in class, Mia is out sick. Unpack “out sick”: away from school because ill. The line is now: Mia is in class, Mia is absent. Unpack “absent”: not in class. The line is now: Mia is in class, ¬(Mia is in class). That is a clash, so the line ends: Mia is in class, ¬(Mia is in class) = ⊥.

The line is refuted. Mia cannot be in class and out sick together. If you trust that Mia is in class, you will want to write ¬(Mia is out sick) on its own. That is the output a line cannot offer, and proofs will. And “Mia is absent” is not something you have either: it was a form of a line that came to ⊥.

1b6A denial carried by a swap

Stands on 1b5 (a line that cannot stand) and 0b5 (swap equals).

0b's swap axiom works inside a denial as well as anywhere else. Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat. The cat on the mat is not grey. So Tibbles is not grey.

Tibbles is the cat on the mat: t = m. The cat on the mat is not grey: ¬(m is grey). Write the line: t = m, ¬(m is grey). Since t = m, the swap axiom lets us write t where m stood, inside the denial. The line is now: t = m, ¬(t is grey). One line, one swap, and the denial has been carried from m to t.

Notice what this did and did not need. It never tried “t is grey”, and it never reached ⊥. It changed the line by a swap, as 0b changed lines, and the ¬ rode along inside the bracket. So a line can output a denial, as long as the denial was already in the line and only its names were swapped.

1b7Not the same thing as

Stands on 1b6 (a denial carried by a swap) and 0b2 (the mark for sameness).

0b gave sameness its mark. Its denial is common enough to want a mark of its own, and here it is, defined as “not” said of “=”:

A small milestone. ≠ is the first operator the ladder has built out of other operators. It is “not”, from this step, laid over “=”, from 0b, and “=” rests on the self-sameness axiom. Unpack ≠ and you reach ¬(x = b). Trace “=” back and you reach an axiom. The ladder will build operators out of operators, all the way down to an axiom, over and over. This is the first time.

≠ is defined by its “from here on” line. So every argument about ≠ begins by unpacking ≠ into ¬(x = b), and ends by packing it back. (1a7's rule: unpacking a mark is the unpack tech applied to its “from here on” line.)

1b8Not-sameness turns about

Stands on 1b7 (not the same thing as) and 0b6 (sameness turns about).

Said of people first: Ann is not the baker, so the baker is not Ann. Of course. The argument below replaces “of course” with the names of techs.

Here is the earliest technique about ≠. Turn-about technique for not-same: if x is not the same thing as b, then b is not the same thing as x. It needs one line, x ≠ b. It writes b ≠ x.

Start from the line x ≠ b. Unpack ≠: ¬(x = b). By 0b's turn-about technique, turn the sameness inside the bracket about: ¬(b = x). Pack ≠: b ≠ x. One line, written again three times.

Compare it with the turn-about argument on 0b, which it follows statement for statement. There, the sameness stood alone. Here, it stands inside a denial, and the technique works on it just the same.

1b9Same and not the same, mixed

Stands on 1b8 (not-sameness turns about) and 0b5 (swap equals).

Said of people: Ann is the baker, and Ann is not the postman; so the baker is not the postman.

Mixed-sameness technique: if x is the same thing as b, and x is not the same thing as c, then b is not the same thing as c. It needs two lines, x = b and x ≠ c. It writes b ≠ c. Watch 0b's swap do the work inside the denial.

Start from the line x = b, x ≠ c. Unpack ≠: x = b, ¬(x = c). Since x = b, the swap axiom lets us write b where x stood, inside the denial: x = b, ¬(b = c). Pack ≠: x = b, b ≠ c.

1b10When nothing is true, everything is permitted

Stands on 1b5 (a line that cannot stand).

When nothing is true, everything is permitted: a line that equals ⊥ may be written again as any statement at all. It looks reckless and is perfectly safe. Its everyday work is closing off a case that cannot happen; 1c will show that at once.

This tech troubles people. From ⊥ the line may become anything: “Tibbles is grey”, “the moon is made of cheese”, whatever you like. How can that be reasoning? Think about when a line ever equals ⊥. Only when its statements cannot all be so together. So whatever the line becomes still came from statements that cannot all be so, and it is worth exactly what they were worth together: nothing. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, and no true line is ever turned into a false one. Turning a true line into a false one is the only thing a tech must never do.

Rex is not indoors; Rex is indoors. Write the line: ¬(Rex is indoors), Rex is indoors. That is a clash, so the line equals ⊥. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, so the line may become “Rex is asleep”. But that form came from a line that cannot stand. Nothing has been learned about sleep.

So what a line becomes after ⊥ is a kind of private note. It can agree with the argument, as the library case will in 1c, or it can have no bearing on the argument at all, like Rex's sleep.

1b11Running it backwards

Stands on 1b5 (a line that cannot stand) and 1b4 (clash).

Read the foot. Wanting a line to equal ⊥, you know how the working must end: clash. So just before the end the line must hold some statement and its denial. Ask what each statement in the line can be unpacked or swapped into that something else in the line denies. That is the whole search.

For the Mia line, the clash you want is “¬(Mia is in class)” against “Mia is in class”. The line has the second. “Mia is out sick” must become the first, and unpacking “out sick”, then “absent”, makes it so. Found backward, said forward.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • \neg P is also written ~P, and in code !P or NOT P. Some books write a bar over P, or a stroke after it.
  • \bot is also read “absurdity”, “contradiction” or “falsum”. The ladder says “totally false”: a statement that fails in every case, not one that is merely silly.
  • The tech “when nothing is true, everything is permitted” is called ex falso quodlibet in most books, or the principle of explosion.
  • A line such as ¬Rex, built against the way lines are built, is called ill-formed in most books, and a line built rightly is a well-formed formula. The ladder's reason above says why such a line fails.

Who said it first

Aristotle called this the firmest of all principles: the same thing cannot both belong and not belong to the same subject, at the same time and in the same respect. Anyone who denies it, he said, must still speak, and in speaking uses it.
