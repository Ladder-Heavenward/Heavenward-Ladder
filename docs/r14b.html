14b · proof

Level ends, a level point between

14a's flat-top says a top inside the domain, where there is a slope, has slope 0. It does not say there is a top. 12c's highest-and-lowest says a function continuous on a closed interval reaches a greatest and a least value, and does not say they are inside. Put the two together on a function whose two ends have the same value. Either the greatest or the least value is reached inside, and there the slope is 0. Or both are reached only at the ends, so they are equal, and the function never moves. Either way some input strictly inside has slope 0. One technique that names no place, and one that names a place but promises nothing is there, make a technique that promises a place.

Equal ends, continuous on the closed interval, a slope inside. Highest-and-lowest gives a top and a bottom. If one is inside, flat-top makes its slope 0. If both are at the ends, the function is constant and every slope is 0.

14b1The technique, every line cited

Stands on 14a (flat-top) and 12c (highest-and-lowest).

Level-ends technique: if f is continuous on [α, β], has a slope at every input of (α, β), and f(α) = f(β), then f′(c) = 0 for some c with α < c < β.

\text{call } c_1:\ \forall x \in [\alpha, \beta]\ (f(x) \le f(c_1)) @@ highest-and-lowest (12c), the greatest value
\text{call } c_2:\ \forall x \in [\alpha, \beta]\ (f(c_2) \le f(x)) @@ highest-and-lowest, the least value
c_1 \in (\alpha, \beta) \;\lor\; c_2 \in (\alpha, \beta) \;\lor\; c_1, c_2 \in \{\alpha, \beta\} @@ either-way (7a), rung 7's principle, with three-way-order on ℝ placing each in [α, β]
  
c_1 \in (\alpha, \beta):\ f \text{ has a top at } c_1 @@ δ = min(c₁ − α, β − c₁), as in 14a's candidates
f'(c_1) = 0 @@ flat-top (14a); the slope at c₁ exists by hypothesis
c_2 \in (\alpha, \beta):\ f'(c_2) = 0 @@ the same, with a bottom
  
f(c_1) = f(\alpha) \text{ and } f(c_2) = f(\alpha) @@ each is f(α) or f(β), and f(α) = f(β)
f(\alpha) \le f(x) \le f(\alpha) \text{ for every } x \in [\alpha, \beta] @@ the two lines of highest-and-lowest
f(x) = f(\alpha) \text{ on } [\alpha, \beta] @@ at-most-is-α-partial-order on ℝ's antisymmetry: f is constant
c = \tfrac{\alpha + \beta}{2}:\ \ f'(c) = 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ slopes-decided-near (13a): f agrees with the constant f(α) on (α, β), which has slope 0 (constant-has-slope-0)

Where each hypothesis was spent. Continuity on the closed interval: in highest-and-lowest, both times. The slope inside: at flat-top, and only there. Equal ends: in case two. That is where a function with unequal ends escapes, since its greatest value can sit at one end and its least at the other. Rung 7's either-way principle: at the three-way split, and inside highest-and-lowest. The proof never says which case is true, so it never names c. In case one it points at an input that highest-and-lowest promised and did not find.

The proof of 1691 that the survey told of was for polynomials only, as part of a method for finding where a polynomial is 0. It used a rule for building a new polynomial from an old one, which is the slope that the polynomial-slope technique gives, without limits and without any word for slope. The method went in steps. The zeros of the slope cut the line into stretches, and each stretch holds at most one zero of the polynomial itself.

14b2Each hypothesis is needed

Stands on 14b1, 13f (no slope at a joint) and 11e (joints).

Take away any one hypothesis, and a function shows the conclusion fails.

Take away continuity at an end: f(x) = x for 0 ≤ x < 1, and f(1) = 0. Then f(0) = f(1) = 0, and f has slope 1 at every input of (0, 1). No slope inside is 0. Highest-and-lowest cannot be cited, and indeed f reaches no greatest value on [0, 1].

Take away the slope at one input: |x| on [−1, 1]. Then |−1| = |1|, and |x| is continuous, with slope −1 left of 0 and 1 right of 0, and none at 0. The bottom is at 0, inside, and flat-top cannot be cited there.

Take away equal ends: x on [0, 1]. Continuous, slope 1 everywhere, and 0 ≠ 1.

14b3Where the zeros of a slope are

Stands on 14b2, 12a (in-between-value) and 13d (polynomial-slope).

The technique's first use. Suppose a polynomial p is 0 at r and at s, with r < s. A polynomial is continuous and has a slope everywhere (polynomials-are-continuous, polynomial-slope). So level-ends on [r, s] gives a zero of p′ strictly between.

Zeros-of-slopes technique: if a polynomial p has zeros r₁ < r₂ < … < rₙ, then p′ has a zero strictly between each neighbouring pair, so at least n − 1 zeros. Turned about: if p′ has at most k zeros, then p has at most k + 1. The first part is level-ends on each [rᵢ, rᵢ₊₁]; the stretches do not overlap, so the zeros it finds are different. The second is the first refuted: k + 2 zeros of p would give k + 1 zeros of p′.

q(x) = x^3 + x + 1 @@ a polynomial
q(-1) = -1 \lt 0 \lt 1 = q(0) @@ in-between-value (12a): a zero between −1 and 0
q'(x) = 3x^2 + 1 \ge 1 @@ polynomial-slope; square-never-negative: never 0
q \text{ has } \htmlClass{new}{\text{exactly one}} \text{ zero} @@ zeros-of-slopes: q′ has 0 zeros, so q has at most 1

In-between-value says there is at least one; level-ends says there is at most one. Neither says where it is: 12e's bisection does.

f(x) = x(x - 1)(x - 3) = x^3 - 4x^2 + 3x \text{ on } [0, 1] @@ f(0) = f(1) = 0
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 8x + 3 @@ polynomial-slope
c = \tfrac{4 - \sqrt7}{3} \approx 0.451 @@ completing the square (8i); the other zero, (4 + √7)/3, is outside
  
x^3 - x \text{ on } [-1, 1]:\ \ 3x^2 - 1 = 0 \text{ at } \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} @@ both inside: level-ends promises at least one, not one

14b5Running it backwards

Stands on 14b1 to 14b3.

Handed a claim that some slope inside is 0, look for two inputs with equal values. Then check continuity between them and a slope at every input strictly between. Handed a claim that an equation has at most so many solutions, move everything to one side, take its slope, and count the slope's zeros. Zeros-of-slopes turns a count of the slope's zeros into a bound on the solutions. Pair it with in-between-value for at least one. Handed a function where the conclusion fails, find which of the three hypotheses is missing. Found upward; written downward.

14b.4.5seamFrom 1700, in the Paris Academy, the man who first proved level-ends argued that the new calculus gave false results. He said it rested on quantities no one could say were something or nothing. Two defenders of the calculus answered him, and the quarrel ran for years before he let it drop. Who said it first

Michel Rolle proved the polynomial version in 1691, in his Démonstration d’une méthode pour résoudre les égalités de tous les degrés. From 1700 he attacked the calculus in the Paris Academy, and Pierre Varignon and Joseph Saurin answered him.

Accepted names: the level-ends technique is Rolle’s theorem. The highest-and-lowest technique is the extreme value theorem, and the flat-top technique is Fermat’s theorem.
