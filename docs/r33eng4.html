How Does a Weld Make Two Pieces One?

How does a weld make two pieces one? Terry sets two steel plates edge to edge, strikes an arc along the seam, and when it cools the plates can be hung from a crane by either one. Glue holds two things together. A weld leaves one thing. This page asks what stops two pieces of metal being one already, and what the heat does about it.

Two pieces of clean metal brought within an atom’s width are one piece. A weld is whatever gets the dirt and the gaps out of the way: squeezing, rubbing, or melting. The heat that does it also changes the metal beside the seam.

33eng41Why Two Blocks Do Not Join

43·phys has a metal’s atoms sharing their outer electrons with every neighbour. An atom at the surface has neighbours on one side only. Put a second clean surface against it and the atoms cannot tell a join from the inside of a block. Two clean flat pieces of the same metal should fuse on contact.

They do not, for two reasons.

Dirt. A bare metal surface in air is coated within moments by a skin of oxide, and on that by water and gases. The surfaces that meet are not metal.

Roughness. The best machined face is a range of hills. Two faces touch at a few hilltops and are thousands of atoms apart everywhere else.

A toolroom’s steel gauge blocks, flat to fifty millionths of an inch, do cling when wrung together. They show how close the surfaces must come.

Joining technique: two metal surfaces become one piece when clean metal meets clean metal within an atom’s width. Every joining process is a way of removing the skin and closing the gaps.

33eng42Joining Without Melting

The oldest weld is the smith’s. Two bars are heated soft and hammered together, and the blow squirts the skin out sideways and flattens the hills.

Rubbing. Spin one bar against another under load. The rubbing heats the faces and squeezes the dirt out as a collar.

A blast. Fire one plate at another with an explosive (43chem11). The collision drives the skin out ahead of it.

Shaking. Rub the faces at 10,000 to 75,000 times a second. This only breaks the skin up and buries it.

Waiting. Press clean faces at two thirds of their melting temperature under 500 to 5,000 pounds on the square inch, and atoms wander across (33·eng3’s walk). The joint can be nearly perfect and takes hours.

Which metals allow it depends on the skin. Iron, copper, nickel and titanium dissolve their own oxide when hot and bond easily. Aluminium and magnesium cannot, and are hard to bond.

Solder and its hotter cousin brazing are a different thing: a third metal, melted, that wets both faces and freezes between them (12·chem). The dividing line is 450 °C. A flux is used to dissolve the skin so that the liquid can wet.

33eng43The Arc

Melting is the direct way: liquid metal has no hills, and the skin floats off. It needs heat delivered faster than the plate can carry it away (33eng45), and a flame is not always enough. An oxygen and fuel-gas flame reaches about 3,200 °C.

An arc is a flame helped by electricity. Current is driven through a centimetre of gas between a rod and the plate. The gas is at about 10,000 K, hot enough that some of its atoms have lost an electron, and so it conducts. Nearly all the current, 99 parts in 100, is carried by the electrons, which move a hundred times faster than the ions.

\text{10 to 15 volts} @@ push across the arc
\text{50 to 300 amperes} @@ current
\htmlClass{new}{15 \times 200 = 3{,}000\text{ watts}} @@ power at 15 V and 200 A

Delivered to a spot at about 10,000 watts on each square centimetre.

The pool must be kept from the air while it is liquid, or it takes up oxygen and nitrogen. The rod’s coating melts to a glassy slag that floats over the pool, or a stream of gas that will not react is blown over it.

Where no supply reaches, chemistry does the heating. Rail gangs weld with 43chem12’s powder, which reaches about 2,000 °C.

33eng44Heat Made at the Joint Itself

A car body is held by several thousand small welds, each made in a blink with no arc. Two sheets are pinched between copper tips and a very large current is passed for a very short time.

7·phys: the heat made in a resistance is current squared, times resistance, times time. The same current runs through the tips, the sheets and the place where the sheets touch. The touch is a few hilltops, so its resistance is the highest in the line, and the heat appears there first.

\text{about 1,400 joules a gram} @@ to melt steel from cold
\htmlClass{new}{\text{140 joules}} @@ a spot of a tenth of a gram

Current 10,000 A through a touch of one ten-thousandth of an ohm: 10,000² × 0.0001 = 10,000 watts.

\htmlClass{new}{140 / 10{,}000 = 0.014\text{ second}} @@ time

The roughness that prevented joining in 33eng41 is what places the heat.

Spot technique: in a series line every part carries the same current, so the heat appears where the resistance is greatest. Between two sheets that is the contact.

33eng45How Far the Heat Goes

Rung 33: heat put in at a place spreads, and the distance it has reached after a time t goes as the square root of αt, where α is the metal’s readiness to spread heat.

s is how deep the melt has gone. J compares the heat needed to warm the metal to melting with the heat needed to melt it.

\htmlClass{new}{\text{steel, }\alpha \text{ about 0.000012 m}^{2}\text{/s}} @@  

In 1 second heat reaches about √(0.000012) = 3.5 mm.

\htmlClass{new}{\text{in 10 seconds, about 11 mm}} @@  

So a slow weld heats a wide band and a fast one a narrow band. That is the reason for wanting intensity. A beam of light or of electrons delivers its power to a far smaller spot than an arc, is done sooner, and leaves a band a fraction as wide. The welder also has a least speed: move slower than the melt deepens and the pool falls through.

Copper and aluminium spread heat several times faster than steel. The heat runs off into the plate as fast as it arrives, and they are harder to weld for it.

Heated-band technique: the width of metal heated beside a weld goes as the square root of the metal’s readiness to spread heat times the time the heat is applied.

33eng46A Quench Beside the Seam

The band of 33eng45 did not melt. It was taken above the line on 33·eng3’s map where iron changes its packing, and then cooled, fast, by the cold plate all round it. 33·eng3 has a name for heating steel past that line and cooling it fast. The plate quenches its own weld.

In a steel with enough carbon the band comes out hard and brittle, with soft plate on one side and soft weld on the other. Welds in such steel crack beside the seam, not in it.

The cures follow from the two pages.

Warm the whole plate first, so that the band cools slowly (33eng25’s time chart).

Or reheat afterwards to temper it.

Or use a steel with little carbon.

One more thing is left behind. The seam set while hot and shrank as it cooled, held at both sides by plate that was never hot. It ends stretched, pulling on the plate, and a long welded plate bows toward its weld.

Accepted names

Joining without melting is solid-state welding: forge, friction, explosive, ultrasonic welding and diffusion bonding. Melting the joint is fusion welding. The hills are asperities. The arc’s conducting gas is a plasma. A coated rod is stick welding; a gas stream over a fed wire is MIG, over a tungsten point TIG. The floating glass is slag. Pinched sheets are resistance spot welding; the heat is Joule heating. The readiness to spread heat is thermal diffusivity; J is the Jakob number. The band is the heat-affected zone. The hard product of the quench is martensite; warming first is preheat. Locked-in pull is residual stress.
