How Are Seats Shared Out?

How are seats shared out? A council of three towns has 8 seats, and each town should get seats in proportion to its people. The towns have 5,200, 3,000 and 1,800 people, and their fair shares come out as fractions of a seat. Somebody has to round, and every way of rounding favours someone. This page finds each town’s share with 5j’s ratios, rounds it two ways, breaks the claim “if the council grows, no town loses a seat” with one example, and meets a pair of fair-sounding claims that no way of rounding can make true together.

A town’s quota is its share of the people times the number of seats. Round every quota down, then hand the leftover seats to the biggest remainders; or divide every town by one number, chosen so the rounded-down results add to the seats. Test each with a claim written as an if, and look for the case where the if-part is true and the then-part false.

5econ21Each Town’s Quota

The towns are A, B and C, with 5,200, 3,000 and 1,800 people: 10,000 in all. Each town’s share of the council should be its share of the people, as a ratio (5j).

From here on, on this page, a town’s quota is its people over all the people, times the seats.

q = \dfrac{\text{town’s people}}{\text{all the people}} \times \text{seats} @@ a ratio, scaled (5j)
q_A = \tfrac{5200}{10000} \times 8 = 4.16 @@ 
q_B = \tfrac{3000}{10000} \times 8 = 2.4 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{q_C = \tfrac{1800}{10000} \times 8 = 1.44} @@ the three add to 8

Seats come whole. Each town will get its quota rounded down or rounded up, and the question is which towns get which.

5econ22Largest Remainders

One way: give each town its quota rounded down, then hand the seats left over to the towns with the biggest fractions left behind.

\text{rounded down: } 4,\ 2,\ 1 \quad (7 \text{ seats}) @@ 1 seat left
\text{left behind: } 0.16,\ 0.4,\ 0.44 @@ C’s is biggest
\htmlClass{new}{A\ 4,\ B\ 2,\ C\ 2} @@ C gets the last seat

Each town gets its quota rounded down or up, never further off. Call that staying within quota: every town’s seats are its quota rounded one way or the other.

5econ23Dividing by One Number

Another way: pick a number d, divide each town’s people by d, and round down. Raise or lower d until the seats add up to 8.

d = 1250:\ 4.16,\ 2.4,\ 1.44 \to 4,\ 2,\ 1 @@ 7 seats: d too big
d = 1040:\ 5,\ 2.88,\ 1.73 \to 5,\ 2,\ 1 @@ 8 seats
\htmlClass{new}{A\ 5,\ B\ 2,\ C\ 1} @@ a different answer

The two ways disagree: largest remainders gives C its second seat, the divisor gives it to A. Rounding down by one divisor leans toward big towns, because each town loses less than a whole seat to rounding, and that loss is a smaller share of a big town’s seats.

5econ24If the Council Grows, Can a Town Lose a Seat?

It sounds safe to claim: if the council grows by one seat, no town loses a seat. Write it as an if: G is “the council grows from 8 to 9”, and L is “no town has fewer seats than before”. The claim is G ⇒ L.

q:\ \tfrac{5200}{10000} \times 9 = 4.68,\ \ 2.7,\ \ 1.62 @@ the new quotas
\text{rounded down: } 4,\ 2,\ 1 \quad (7 \text{ seats}) @@ 2 seats left
\text{left behind: } 0.68,\ 0.7,\ 0.62 @@ B and A are biggest
\htmlClass{new}{A\ 5,\ B\ 3,\ C\ 1} @@ C had 2 seats at 8

G is true and L is false, so G ⇒ L is false for largest remainders: one example breaks the claim. The divisor way never does this. With one more seat, d must fall to let the seats add up, and a smaller d makes every town’s people over d bigger, so no town’s rounded-down number can drop.

5econ25Two Claims That Cannot Both Hold

The divisor way passed footing 4’s test but can fail to stay within quota. Take towns of 8,600, 700 and 700 people and 10 seats: the quotas are 8.6, 0.7 and 0.7. Divide by d = 860: the results are 10, 0.81 and 0.81, rounded down to 10, 0, 0. A gets 10 seats, more than its quota rounded up.

Write Q for “the method always stays within quota” and P for “a town whose people grow, while another’s shrink, never loses a seat to it”. Largest remainders makes Q true and P false; the divisor way makes P true and Q false.

Quota technique: no way of sharing seats among three or more towns makes both Q and P true: every way gives one up, so ¬Q ∨ ¬P holds for every method. Two researchers proved it in 1982, by showing that a method keeping both would be forced into a contradiction for some set of towns. The proof is long; the page uses its result.

\lnot (Q \land P) @@ the result of 1982
\lnot Q \lor \lnot P @@ push the not in (1f)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{choose which to give up}} @@ the council’s choice, not the arithmetic’s

A clerk sharing out a council by hand works out both ways in a table like footing 3’s and shows the council where they disagree. The arithmetic cannot settle which to use; it can only show what each choice costs.

Accepted names

Sharing seats in proportion is apportionment. Largest remainders is Hamilton’s method, and dividing by one number and rounding down is Jefferson’s method; rounding to the nearest is Webster’s. A town losing a seat when the council grows is the Alabama paradox, the failure of P is the population paradox, and the result of footing 5 is the Balinski and Young impossibility theorem.

Who said it first

Michel Balinski and H. Peyton Young proved the impossibility in 1982, in Fair Representation. The council-grows paradox was first noticed in 1880, when a clerk worked out the shares for every council size from 275 to 350.
