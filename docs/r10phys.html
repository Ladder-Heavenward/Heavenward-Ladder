Can a Runner Ever Catch a Tortoise?

Can a runner ever catch a tortoise? Nearly two and a half thousand years ago, a Greek thinker argued that a fast runner can never catch a slow one with a head start. First the runner must reach the place where the tortoise began. By then the tortoise has moved on a little. Then the runner must reach that new place, and the tortoise has moved on again. However many times the runner closes the gap, a gap is left. Every runner who has ever passed anyone knows the conclusion is false. People asked the question because the argument looks airtight, and the broken step is hard to find. What makes it hard is that the argument is right about the stages. There are endlessly many of them, and no stage ends with the gap closed. What it gets wrong is the time they take. This page cuts the race into its endless stages and adds up their times with rung 9's sum of steps that multiply. It proves with rung 10's limits that the totals head to a finite number. Then it finds the moment of catching directly, with rung 9's functions of time, and shows the two answers agree. And it asks which endless runs of shrinking times have a finite total, and which do not.

Each stage's gap is the last one times the tortoise's speed over the runner's. So each stage's time is the last one times the same fraction, less than one. The time of the first so many stages adds up to the first stage's time, times one less a power of the fraction, over one less the fraction. The power heads to nothing, so the totals head to a finite time, and every stage ends before it. Write each position as a function of time, and the two meet at that very moment. Endless stages take a finite time when they shrink fast enough. Shrinking alone is not enough.

The race, cut into stages

From here on, on this page, the runner moves at a steady speed v and the tortoise at a steady speed u, in metres per second, with 0 < u < v. At time 0 the tortoise is D metres ahead. The fraction q = u/v is the tortoise's speed as a share of the runner's, so 0 < q < 1.

Stage 0 runs from time 0 until the runner reaches the tortoise's starting place. Each next stage runs until the runner reaches the place where the tortoise was when that stage began. Let d_k be the gap at the start of stage k, and t_k the stage's time. So d₀ = D. The runner covers d_k at speed v, in t_k = d_k/v (5j). Meanwhile the tortoise moves u · t_k = q · d_k. That is the next gap.

d_{k+1} = q\,d_k,\ \ d_0 = D @@ the gap at each stage is the last one times q
d_k = D\,q^k @@ steps-that-multiply (9h)
t_k = \tfrac{d_k}{v} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{D}{v}\,q^k} @@ time is distance over speed (5j); a run of times with factor q

Here is a race to work by hand, a model. The runner jogs at 5 m/s. A generous tortoise walks at 0.5 m/s, with 100 m start. Then q = 1/10, and the stages take 20 s, 2 s, 0.2 s, 0.02 s, and on. No stage ends with the gap closed, and there is no last stage. The old argument is right about that much.

The stage times, added

Let T_n be the time when stage n − 1 ends: the first n stage times added. 9h's ratio-sum adds a run of steps that multiply, for any factor other than 1.

T_n = \textstyle\sum_{k \lt n} \tfrac{D}{v}\,q^k = \tfrac{D}{v}\,(1 + q + \cdots + q^{n-1}) @@ factor-out (4k)
= \tfrac{D}{v} \cdot \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1 - q^n}{1 - q}} @@ ratio-sum, with b = q ≠ 1, top and bottom times −1
20 + 2 + 0.2 + 0.02 = 22.22 @@ the model: four stages, n = 4, q = 1/10

The totals 20, 22, 22.2, 22.22, 22.222, … grow at every stage, and they seem to settle near 22.2 repeating, which 8·cb5 reads as 200/9. To prove it, the power qⁿ must be shown to head to 0.

Powers of a fraction head to 0

One small tool first. One-plus-a-power technique: a power of one plus a positive amount is at least one plus that many of the amount. Let h > 0 and let n be a whole number at least 1. Then (1 + h)ⁿ ≥ 1 + nh. For n = 1 it is an equality. If it holds for n, then (1 + h)ⁿ⁺¹ ≥ (1 + nh)(1 + h) = 1 + (n + 1)h + nh² ≥ 1 + (n + 1)h, by keep-order for times (8g), since 1 + h > 0, and nh² ≥ 0. Induction (4c) does the rest. ∎

Powers-to-0 technique: the powers of a fraction between 0 and 1 head to 0. Let 0 ≤ q < 1. Then qⁿ → 0 as n → ∞.

q = 0:\ \ q^n = 0 \text{ for } n \ge 1 @@ every term from n = 1 on is the limit itself
0 \lt q \lt 1:\ \ \tfrac1q = 1 + h,\ \ h \gt 0 @@ positive-reciprocal (8g): 1/q > 1
\big(\tfrac1q\big)^n \ge 1 + nh \gt nh @@ one-plus-a-power
0 \lt q^n \lt \tfrac{1}{h} \cdot \tfrac{1}{n} @@ positive-reciprocal again
\tfrac1h \cdot \tfrac1n \to 0 @@ one-over-n and constant-factor (10f, 10c)
\htmlClass{keep}{q^n \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the squeeze, far out (10d, 10f), between 0 and (1/h)(1/n)

The bound is slow: for q = 2/3 it promises qⁿ < 0.01 from n = 201 on, where in fact n = 12 is enough. A slow bound still proves the limit. The game asks only for some threshold, not the best one.

Endless stages, a finite total

Stage-sum technique: a run of times that shrink by a fixed factor less than one adds up to a finite total. Let 0 ≤ q < 1 and let c be a real. Then c(1 + q + ⋯ + q^(n−1)) → c/(1 − q) as n → ∞.

c\,(1 + q + \cdots + q^{n-1}) = \tfrac{c}{1 - q} - \tfrac{c}{1 - q}\,q^n @@ ratio-sum, spread
\tfrac{c}{1 - q}\,q^n \to \tfrac{c}{1 - q} \cdot 0 = 0 @@ powers-to-0, constant-factor
\to \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{c}{1 - q} - 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ limits-subtract (10c)

Read with c = D/v, the stage times add up to a total that heads to

T_n \to \tfrac{D/v}{1 - u/v} @@ stage-sum
= \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{D}{v - u}} @@ times v/v, which is 1
T_n = \tfrac{D}{v - u}\,(1 - q^n) \lt \tfrac{D}{v - u} @@ qⁿ > 0: every stage ends before that moment
\text{model: } \tfrac{100}{5 - 0.5} = 22.\overline{2} \text{ s} @@ 200/9 seconds

Every one of the endless stages is over before 22.2 repeating seconds. And for any moment before that, some stage ends after it: that is what heading to the limit says, with ε the time still to go. The stages fill exactly the time before the catch, and no more.

The moment, found directly

9·phys gave a body in uniform motion a position that is a linear function of time. Measure positions from the runner's start.

x_R(t) = v\,t,\ \ \ x_T(t) = D + u\,t @@ uniform motion: linear functions of time
v\,t = D + u\,t \;\Leftrightarrow\; (v - u)\,t = D @@ the same place: subtract ut from both sides
t^* = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{D}{v - u}} @@ divide by v − u, positive; the same number as the stage total
x_R(t) \gt x_T(t) \text{ for } t \gt t^* @@ (v − u)(t − t*) > 0: after it, the runner is ahead

Two roads, one answer. The direct road never mentions the stages, and it needs no limit at all. The stages road shows what the old argument missed. Endless stages need not take endless time. The argument counted the stages, and never added up their times. It also smuggled in a stop: it watched the race only until the catch, and then asked why the catch never came.

Shrinking Is Not Enough

Does every endless run of shrinking times add up to a finite total? No. Take stage times of 1, ½, ⅓, ¼, … seconds, each shorter than the last, and heading to 0 by one-over-n. Group them: ⅓ + ¼ is more than ¼ + ¼ = ½. The next four, ⅕ to ⅛, are each at least ⅛, so they add to at least ½. And so on.

Grouped-halves technique: adding one over each number from 1 to a power of two gives at least one plus half the count of doublings. In marks, 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/2^k ≥ 1 + k/2 for every whole k.

k = 0:\ \ 1 \ge 1 @@ the base
\tfrac{1}{2^k + 1} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}} \ge 2^k \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}} = \tfrac12 @@ 2^k terms, each at least the last (positive-reciprocal); same-direction-add
\textstyle\sum_{j \le 2^{k+1}} \tfrac1j \ge 1 + \tfrac{k}{2} + \tfrac12 = \htmlClass{keep}{1 + \tfrac{k + 1}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hypothesis, plus the new group; induction (4c)

So for any bound M, past 2^k terms with k > 2M the total is past M: the totals pass every bound (10f), and have no limit (passing-every-bound). A race with these stage times would never end. The difference is speed of shrinking: q^k shrinks by the same factor every time, and 1/k shrinks by less and less.

A chase at Hayward Field

On the track at Hayward Field in Eugene, a miler is 20 m behind with one lap to go. She runs at 6.5 m/s, and the leader at 6.3 m/s, a model. Then q = 6.3/6.5 ≈ 0.969, close to 1, and the stages shrink slowly. The first takes about 3.08 s.

t^* = \tfrac{20}{6.5 - 6.3} = \htmlClass{new}{100 \text{ s}} @@ the moment, directly
\text{gap under } 1 \text{ m after } 96 \text{ stages, at about } 95.0 \text{ s} @@ 20 q⁹⁶ < 1; T₉₆ by ratio-sum
\text{gap under } 1 \text{ cm after } 244 \text{ stages, at about } 99.95 \text{ s} @@ worked by machine
\text{every stage ends before } 100 \text{ s} @@ T_n = 100(1 − qⁿ)

A lap at 6.5 m/s takes about 62 s. So she runs out of track before the catch, however many stages she completes. The limit gives the moment exactly; the race decides whether it comes in time.

Illegal Moves

\text{endless stages, so endless time}\,? @@ stage-sum: shrinking by a fixed factor q < 1, they add to D/(v − u)
\text{the times shrink to } 0 \text{, so their total is finite}\,? @@ grouped-halves: 1, ½, ⅓, … shrink to 0 and the totals pass every bound
\text{the catch happens at the last stage}\,? @@ there is no last stage; the catch is the limit of the stage ends, and no stage ends there
T_n = \tfrac{D}{v - u} \text{ for } n \text{ large enough}\,? @@ T_n = (D/(v − u))(1 − qⁿ), always short of it; the limit is never a term
\infty \cdot 0 \text{, so the total is } 0\,? @@ ∞ is not a number (10f); the total is a limit of finite sums

Running It Backwards

Handed a first stage time t₀ and a total, find the factor: total = t₀/(1 − q), so q = 1 − t₀/total. Handed a catch time and the speeds, find the head start: D = (v − u) t*. Handed a run of shrinking times, ask whether each is at most a fixed q < 1 times the last. If so, the totals stay below t₀/(1 − q). If they shrink by less and less, as 1/k does, nothing is promised.

Who said it first

The race is Zeno of Elea's, from the fifth century BC; the runner is Achilles. Zeno's own book is lost, and the argument comes down through Aristotle's Physics, which answers it by saying that a stretch of time is divisible in the same way as a stretch of road, so endlessly many parts of the road are crossed in endlessly many parts of the time. Archimedes, in the third century BC, added up a run of quarters, 1 + ¼ + 1/16 + ⋯, by showing that the total could be neither more nor less than 4/3. Nicole Oresme, in Paris around 1350, proved by grouping that 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ passes every bound. The modern reading, with the totals' limit as the meaning of an endless sum, is Augustin-Louis Cauchy's (1821).

Aristotle answers that Zeno's argument wrongly assumes one cannot pass through or touch endlessly many things in a finite time; but length and time are endlessly divisible in the same way, so the endless parts of the length are passed through in the endless parts of the time.

Aristotle, Physics, book VI, chapter 2; paraphrase

The second reader of 10·phys.4.4 was given a sharp form by James Thomson in 1954: a lamp switched on and off at shorter and shorter intervals, endlessly often within two minutes. Is it on or off at the end? Such endless lists of tasks done in a finite time are called supertasks.

Accepted names

The race is Achilles and the tortoise, one of Zeno's paradoxes. A run whose terms are each a fixed factor of the last is a geometric series when its terms are added, and the stage-sum technique is the sum of a geometric series, a/(1 − r) for |r| < 1. An endless sum whose totals head to a limit converges; one whose totals do not diverges. The totals are its partial sums. 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ is the harmonic series, and grouped-halves is Oresme's proof that it diverges. One-plus-a-power is Bernoulli's inequality.
