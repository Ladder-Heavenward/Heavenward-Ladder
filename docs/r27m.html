27m · technique

Turns of space

Rung twenty turned the plane about a point, and every such turn was one pair of numbers. Space can be turned in far more ways: tip a globe, spin a ball, roll a die across a table. A turn of space keeps every length and keeps which way round things stand. Turn a ball any way you like, as many times as you like, and one line through its centre ends up exactly where it started. That line is the axis, and the whole tangle of turns comes down to one turn about it. The proof is a few lines of determinants, and it needs space to have an odd number of directions. This step finds the axis of a turn by elimination, its angle from the sum down its middle, and builds the grid of any turn from its axis and angle. And it finds what rung twenty never met: two turns of space, done in the two orders, give two different turns.

A grid keeps lengths exactly when its columns have length one and stand at right angles. A turn of space keeps lengths and handedness, and it always leaves one line in place: an arrow it only stretches by one. About that axis it is a turn of the plane, and its angle is read off the sum down its middle. Turns of space do not commute.

27m1Grids that keep lengths

Stands on 27i1 (the dot), 27h2 (the two-slot technique), 27d3 (the undoable-group technique), 27g1 (the side-turn technique) and 27e3 (the det-product technique).

Length-keeper technique: for a square grid Q, these three are the same: Q keeps every length, |Qx| = |x|; Q keeps every dot, Qx · Qy = x · y; QᵀQ = I, so the columns of Q have length 1 and stand at right angles. Then Q⁻¹ = Qᵀ, and det Q is 1 or −1.

x\cdot y = \tfrac12\big(|x + y|^2 - |x|^2 - |y|^2\big) @@ the dot technique (27i1), as in the right-triangle technique (18a4)
|Qx| = |x| \text{ for all } x \Rightarrow Qx\cdot Qy = x\cdot y @@ Q keeps sums (27c1), so every length on the right is kept
Qx\cdot Qy = \textstyle\sum_{j,k} x_j\,(Qe_j\cdot Qe_k)\,y_k @@ the two-slot technique (27h2), with Gⱼₖ = Qeⱼ · Qeₖ
\text{keeps dots} \iff Qe_j\cdot Qe_k = e_j\cdot e_k \iff Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I @@ the grid of a two-slot reading is unique; column j of Q is Qeⱼ, so Qeⱼ · Qeₖ is place j, k of QᵀQ (27d2)
Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I \Rightarrow Q \text{ loses only } 0 \Rightarrow Q^{-1} = Q^{\mathsf T} @@ Qx = 0 gives x = QᵀQx = 0; the undoable-group technique (27d3)
\htmlClass{new}{(\det Q)^2 = \det Q^{\mathsf T}\det Q = \det I = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the side-turn (27g1) and det-product (27e3) techniques

A turn of space is a 3 × 3 grid that keeps lengths and has det 1. The det says which way round: Qe₁, Qe₂, Qe₃ stand the way e₁, e₂, e₃ do, as 20g asked of the plane. A grid that keeps lengths with det −1, like a mirror, swaps the hands. Turns of space form a group, a part-group of the undoable grids (27d3, 19a): a turn after a turn keeps lengths, with det 1 · 1 = 1, and the undo of a turn keeps lengths, since |Q⁻¹x| = |QQ⁻¹x| = |x|, with det 1. Accepted name: the turns of space form SO(3).

R = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} @@ e₁ ↦ e₂, e₂ ↦ −e₁, e₃ ↦ e₃: a quarter turn of the floor, the up arrow held
\text{columns of length } 1,\ \text{dots } 0,\ \det R = 1 @@ the length-keeper technique; along the last column, det = 0·0 − (−1)·1 (27k2)
M = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix} @@ the mirror of the floor: up becomes down
\htmlClass{new}{R \text{ is a turn};\ \ M \text{ keeps lengths, but } \det M = -1} \quad \blacksquare @@ M swaps the hands: a mirror, not a turn

27m2Every turn has an axis

Stands on 27m1, 27e3, 27g1, 27e4 (the nonzero-det technique), 27i3 (the cross technique), 27i2 (the read-off technique), 27l1 (the middle-sum technique), 20j (turns of the plane as pairs) and 21b (cos and sin as coordinates).

Axis technique: every turn Q of space keeps some arrow u ≠ 0 in place: Qu = u. Take u of length 1. Then Q keeps the plane of arrows at right angles to u and turns it: for one θ, Qw = cos θ w + sin θ (u × w) for every w in that plane. And the middle sum of Q is 1 + 2 cos θ. The line along u is the axis of the turn, and θ its angle.

\det(Q - I) = \det(Q - QQ^{\mathsf T}) = \det Q\,\det(I - Q^{\mathsf T}) @@ QQᵀ = I (27m1); Q − QQᵀ = Q(I − Qᵀ); the det-product technique (27e3)
= \det\big((I - Q)^{\mathsf T}\big) = \det(I - Q) = (-1)^3\det(Q - I) @@ det Q = 1; the side-turn technique (27g1); each of three columns stretched by −1 (27e2)
\det(Q - I) = 0 \Rightarrow Qu = u \text{ for some } u \ne 0 @@ a number equal to its opposite is 0; the nonzero-det technique (27e4)
w\cdot u = 0 \Rightarrow Qw\cdot u = Qw\cdot Qu = w\cdot u = 0 @@ Q keeps dots (27m1): the plane at right angles to u is kept
w_1 \perp u,\ |w_1| = 1,\ w_2 = u\times w_1:\ \ Qw_1 = c\,w_1 + s\,w_2,\ \ c^2 + s^2 = 1 @@ u, w₁, w₂ are of length 1 and at right angles (27i3); read Qw₁ off (27i2)
Qw_2 = \pm(-s\,w_1 + c\,w_2) @@ of length 1, in the plane, at right angles to Qw₁
\text{in the basis } u, w_1, w_2 \text{ the grid of } Q \text{ has det } \pm(c^2 + s^2) = \pm1 = \det Q = 1 @@ P⁻¹QP has the same polynomial, so the same det (27l1): the sign is +
c = \cos\theta,\ s = \sin\theta,\ \ Q(\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2) = c(\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2) + s\,u\times(\alpha w_1 + \beta w_2) @@ a turn of the plane is a pair (20j) at one angle (21b); u × w₂ = −w₁, as (u × w₂) · w₁ = det[u w₂ w₁] = −1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{middle sum of } Q = 1 + c + c = 1 + 2\cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the middle-sum technique (27l1): P⁻¹QP has rows (1, 0, 0), (0, c, −s), (0, s, c)
  
C = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix} @@ e₁ ↦ e₂ ↦ e₃ ↦ e₁; columns of length 1 at right angles, det 1: a turn
C - I = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0&1\\1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&-1\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} @@ lowest terms (27j2)
u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1) @@ x = z, y = z, z free; stretched to length 1
\htmlClass{new}{1 + 2\cos\theta = 0 + 0 + 0,\ \ \cos\theta = -\tfrac12,\ \ \theta = \pm\tfrac{2\pi}{3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the middle sum: a third of a turn about the long diagonal of the cube

The proof used three in one place: (−1)³ = −1. In the plane the same line gives det(Q − I) = (−1)² det(Q − I), which says nothing, and indeed a quarter turn of the plane moves every arrow but 0. A turn of space with an even number of directions need not have an axis; with an odd number it always does.

27m3A turn from its axis and angle

Stands on 27m2, 27i2, 27i3, 27e1 (the column-slide technique) and 27e2.

Axis-turn technique: the turn by θ about an arrow u of length 1 sends x to cos θ x + sin θ (u × x) + (1 − cos θ)(u · x)u. And for a turn Q with axis u, and any w ≠ 0 at right angles to u, sin θ has the sign of det[w Qw u]. The second sentence settles which way round a turn goes, which the middle sum cannot see.

x = (u\cdot x)\,u + w,\ \ w = x - (u\cdot x)\,u,\ \ w\cdot u = 0 @@ the part along u, and the rest (27i2)
u\times x = u\times w @@ u × u = 0: u and u are not free (27i3)
Rx = (u\cdot x)\,u + \cos\theta\,w + \sin\theta\,(u\times w) @@ R keeps u and turns the plane at right angles to u (27m2)
\htmlClass{new}{Rx = \cos\theta\,x + \sin\theta\,(u\times x) + (1 - \cos\theta)(u\cdot x)\,u} @@ put back w = x − (u · x)u
\det[w\ \ Qw\ \ u] = \sin\theta\,\det[w\ \ u\times w\ \ u] @@ Qw = cos θ w + sin θ (u × w); straight and flat in column 2 (27e2)
\det[w\ \ u\times w\ \ u] = \det[u\ \ w\ \ u\times w] = (u\times w)\cdot(u\times w) > 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ two swaps keep the sign (27e1); the cross technique (27i3)
  
\theta = \pi,\ \ u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0):\ \ Rx = -x + 2(u\cdot x)\,u @@ cos π = −1, sin π = 0
Re_1 = -e_1 + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0) = (0, 1, 0) @@ u · e₁ = 1/√2
Re_2 = (1, 0, 0),\ \ Re_3 = -e_3 + 0 = (0, 0, -1) @@ the same, with u · e₂ = 1/√2 and u · e₃ = 0
\htmlClass{new}{R = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the columns are Re₁, Re₂, Re₃ (27d1); check: middle sum −1 = 1 + 2 cos π

Two turns about different axes, done one after the other, are one turn about a third axis, since turns form a group and every turn has an axis. But the order matters. A quarter turn about the up arrow, done after a quarter turn about e₁, is a third of a turn about (1, 1, 1); done before it, a third of a turn about (1, −1, 1). Rung 20’s turns of the plane commuted. Turns of space do not.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A grid that keeps lengths is an orthogonal matrix, with QᵀQ = I; the length-keepers form the orthogonal group O(n), and those with det 1 the special orthogonal group SO(n). The turns of space are rotations, and SO(3) is the rotation group; a length-keeper with det −1 is an improper rotation, a mirror reflection followed by a turn. The axis technique is Euler’s rotation theorem. The axis-turn technique is Rodrigues’ rotation formula. That turns of space do not commute is the non-commutativity of SO(3).

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler proved in 1775 that any motion of a sphere about its centre leaves a diameter fixed. Olinde Rodrigues gave the axis-turn formula, and the rule for composing two turns, in 1840. George Peacock set out the principle of permanence of equivalent forms in his Treatise on Algebra of 1830. William Rowan Hamilton found the quaternions on 16 October 1843, walking by the Royal Canal in Dublin, and gave up turn-about multiplication to have them; they describe turns of space to this day, in spacecraft and in video games.
