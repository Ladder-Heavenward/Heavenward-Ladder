How Do X-Rays See Inside a Crystal?

How do X-rays see inside a crystal? A grain of table salt is a cube, and it breaks into smaller cubes, and the smaller cubes into smaller ones still. Chemists guessed for a century that inside it the atoms sit in a pattern that repeats, cell after cell. But atoms are far too small to see with light: the gap between two of them is a few ten-millionths of a millimetre, thousands of times smaller than the waves of light. X-rays have waves about that size. Shine them through a crystal, and on a screen beyond it appears not a shadow but a scatter of sharp bright spots in a fixed pattern. What makes the question hard is that the spots look nothing like the crystal. This page reads them with turnings. A repeating pattern is a sum of turnings in space, the waves scattered by it add as turnings, and each bright spot is one term of the sum. From where the spots fall, the spacing of the atoms can be read, and from how bright they are, which atoms sit where.

A crystal’s density of electrons repeats in space, so it is a sum of turnings in space, one for each triple of whole numbers. An X-ray scattered by the crystal adds up the turnings of every cell, and the cells agree only in the directions where the change of the wave is one of those turnings: that is a spot, and for planes of atoms a spacing d apart it falls where twice d times the sine of the angle is a whole number of wavelengths. Each spot’s brightness is the energy of one coefficient, a sum over the atoms of one cell. For salt, some spots are bright, some faint and some missing, and the first bright one gives the spacing of the atoms.

32chem1A Repeating Pattern in Space

Take a crystal whose cells are cubes of side L, and let ρ(r) be its density of electrons at the point r = (x, y, z). It repeats L along each axis. The turnings that repeat with it are e^(2πi(hx + ky + lz)/L), one for each triple (h, k, l) of whole numbers, and the turning-shadow technique runs in three directions at once, with the weight 1/L³ over one cell.

Each turning is constant on a family of flat sheets, the planes hx + ky + lz = constant. Those sheets are L/√(h² + k² + l²) apart: the spacing d of the planes of (h, k, l).

32chem2Where the Spots Fall

An X-ray of wavelength λ comes in along a direction and leaves along another, each of length 1/λ as a vector s₀, s. Each electron at r sends out a wave that arrives with the turn e^(−2πi(s − s₀)·r). The crystal’s wave is the total of these over all its electrons: the transform of ρ at q = s − s₀.

Spot-condition technique: a crystal scatters strongly only when the change of wave q is one of its turnings, (h, k, l)/L; for its planes of spacing d, that is when 2e sin θ = λ, with 2θ the angle the ray is turned through.

\text{wave} = \textstyle\sum_{\text{cells}} e^{-2\pi i\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R}\int_{\text{cell}}\rho(\mathbf r)\,e^{-2\pi i\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d\mathbf r @@ each cell is the first, slid by R
\textstyle\sum_{m=-N}^{N} e^{-2\pi i\,q_xmL} = D_N(2\pi q_xL) @@ along one axis, the turning kernel of 32c1: sharp peaks at q_x L whole
\text{strong only when } \mathbf q = (h, k, l)/L @@ elsewhere the cells cancel
|\mathbf q| = 2\cdot\tfrac1\lambda\sin\theta,\ \ |(h, k, l)/L| = \tfrac1d @@ two vectors of length 1/λ at an angle 2θ (21d); the planes’ spacing (32·chem.1)
\htmlClass{new}{2d\sin\theta = \lambda} \quad \blacksquare @@ the condition, for each family of planes

A spot is a coefficient made visible. A crystal of a million cells along a side makes peaks a millionth as wide as the gaps between them, 32c’s kernel with N a million: the sharpness of the spots is the size of the crystal.

32chem3How Bright, and Which Are Missing

The brightness of the (h, k, l) spot is in proportion to |F_hkl|². Split ρ into its atoms: each atom j, at the place (xⱼ, yⱼ, zⱼ)L in the cell, scatters by its own amount fⱼ, roughly its count of electrons.

Cell-sum technique: a spot’s coefficient is the sum, over the atoms of one cell, of each atom’s amount times the turning at its place: F_hkl = Σ fⱼ e^(−2πi(hxⱼ + kyⱼ + lzⱼ)).

\text{Na at } (0,0,0),\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0),\ (\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12),\ (0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12);\ \ \text{Cl at } (\tfrac12,0,0),\ (0,\tfrac12,0),\ (0,0,\tfrac12),\ (\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12) @@ the cell of salt: four of each
(2,0,0):\ \ F = 4f_{\text{Na}} + 4f_{\text{Cl}} @@ every turning is e^(−2πi × whole) = 1
(1,1,1):\ \ F = 4f_{\text{Na}} - 4f_{\text{Cl}} @@ at each Na place hx + ky + lz is whole, at each Cl place a half: turns e^(−iπ) = −1
(1,0,0):\ \ F = f_{\text{Na}}(1 - 1 - 1 + 1) + f_{\text{Cl}}(-1 + 1 + 1 - 1) = 0 @@ mixed odd and even: the cell cancels itself
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all even: bright; all odd: faint; mixed: missing}} \quad \blacksquare @@ f_Na ≈ 11 and f_Cl ≈ 17 at small angles, so (111) is about (6/28)² of (200)

The missing spots tell the shape of the cell, the faint ones which atoms are where. What a spot cannot tell is the turn of F_hkl, only its size. For salt the turns are ±1 and easy to guess. For a protein with thousands of atoms, finding them is the hard part of the work, and a bright heavy atom put in on purpose, or a guess checked against the spots, is how it is done.

32chem4Reading the Spacing of Salt

\lambda = 1.5406\ \text{Å},\ \ \text{first bright spot at } 2\theta = 31.7^\circ @@ copper’s X-rays; the (2, 0, 0) spot, measured
d = \tfrac{\lambda}{2\sin 15.85^\circ} = 2.82\ \text{Å} @@ the spot-condition technique
L = 2d = 5.64\ \text{Å} @@ d = L/√(2² + 0 + 0)
\tfrac{4 \times 58.44\ \text{g}}{6.022 \times 10^{23} \times (5.64 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{cm})^3} = 2.16\ \text{g/cm}^3 @@ four units of salt in a cell, each of 58.44 g per mole
\htmlClass{new}{\text{measured density of salt: } 2.165\ \text{g/cm}^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spacing and the weighing agree

The faint (1, 1, 1) spot should sit at 2θ = 27.4°, and (2, 2, 0) at 45.4°: both are found there. One spot gave the cell, and the cell gave the density to three places. That agreement, between an angle on a screen and a weight on a balance, is how the guess that atoms sit in a repeating pattern became a law.

The accepted names

The spots are Bragg reflections, the spot condition is Bragg’s law, and (h, k, l) are Miller indices; the turnings of the crystal form its reciprocal lattice, and q = (h, k, l)/L the Laue condition. F_hkl is the structure factor, fⱼ the atomic form (or scattering) factor, and the missing spots are systematic absences. The lost turns are the phase problem; heavy atoms put in on purpose are isomorphous replacement. Salt’s cell is face-centred cubic (the rock-salt structure).

Who said it first

Max von Laue, with Walter Friedrich and Paul Knipping, found X-ray spots from a crystal of copper sulphate in 1912. William Lawrence Bragg gave the spot condition that year, and with his father William Henry Bragg solved salt in 1913, showing that it holds no molecules of salt at all, only alternating atoms. Dorothy Hodgkin solved penicillin (1945), vitamin B12 (1956) and insulin (1969); Max Perutz and John Kendrew solved the first proteins, haemoglobin and myoglobin, with heavy atoms, around 1960. Rosalind Franklin’s photographs of DNA (1952) were read by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953.
