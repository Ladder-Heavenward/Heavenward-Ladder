27·then · newsflash

What If Everything Depends on Everything Else?

What if everything depends on everything else? Most of what the sciences study is not one thing changing but many things changing together. Two carts joined by a spring pass their swing back and forth. Every bar of a bridge pushes on every joint it touches. Lynx in the forests of the North Cascades eat snowshoe hares, and so the number of hares sets the number of lynx, which sets the number of hares. A sick person passes an illness on, and every new case changes who is left to catch it. Carbon moves between the air and the sea, and young deer become old deer who have young. What makes all of this hard is that nothing can be worked out first, since each part waits on all the others. For a long time each science had its own tricks for its own tangle. This page shows that most of these tangles are one kind of thing: a grid of numbers, read with the techniques of rung twenty-seven. The stretches of the grid say how each tangle moves, and the vectors the grid only stretches say what shape it settles into.

Two carts joined by a spring have two ways to swing in which each keeps its own pace: together, and against each other. Every other motion is a mix of these two. A frame of bars is a list of samenesses, one pair for each joint, and the loads fix every force exactly when its grid can be undone. Predators and prey rest at a point where the prey count is set by the predators’ rates, and the predator count by the prey’s. An illness spreads only while the share of people still open to it is above one over its spreading number, and it always leaves some untouched. A reaction with charged pieces needs its charge balanced as well as its atoms. Carbon split between the air and the sea settles into fixed shares. And a population of many ages settles into one shape that grows by the same factor every year.

27then1Two Carts and a Spring Between

Two carts of mass m ride on an air track, each held toward its own rest place by a spring of stiffness k, with a weaker spring κ joining them. Mass times acceleration is the sum of the forces on each cart.

mx_1'' = -kx_1 - \kappa(x_1 - x_2),\ \ mx_2'' = -kx_2 - \kappa(x_2 - x_1) @@ each cart’s own spring, and the spring between pulls by the gap
x'' = -Kx,\ \ K = \tfrac1m\begin{pmatrix}k + \kappa & -\kappa\\ -\kappa & k + \kappa\end{pmatrix} @@ one grid for both rules
K(1, 1) = \tfrac km(1, 1),\ \ K(1, -1) = \tfrac{k + 2\kappa}{m}(1, -1) @@ two stretched vectors, with different stretches, so free (27f)
x = q_1(1, 1) + q_2(1, -1) \Rightarrow q_1'' = -\omega_1^2q_1,\ \ q_2'' = -\omega_2^2q_2 @@ the parts along free vectors match one by one; ω₁² = k/m, ω₂² = (k + 2κ)/m
\htmlClass{new}{x = (A_1\cos\omega_1t + B_1\sin\omega_1t)(1, 1) + (A_2\cos\omega_2t + B_2\sin\omega_2t)(1, -1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the steady-swing technique (24h), once along each vector

From here on, a way of swinging in which every part keeps one pace is a normal mode. Normal-mode technique: if x″ = −Kx and K has n free vectors that it only stretches, by numbers ω₁², …, ωₙ² above 0, then every motion is a sum of steady swings, one along each vector, each at its own pace. Swinging together, the carts never stretch the spring between, so ω₁ does not see κ. Swinging against each other, they stretch it twice over.

m = 0.5 \text{ kg},\ \ k = 20 \text{ N/m},\ \ \kappa = 2 \text{ N/m}:\ \ \omega_1 \approx 6.325,\ \ \omega_2 \approx 6.928 @@ radians a second
x(0) = (5, 0) \text{ cm, at rest}:\ \ x = 2.5\cos\omega_1t\,(1, 1) + 2.5\cos\omega_2t\,(1, -1) @@ (5, 0) = 2.5(1, 1) + 2.5(1, −1)
x_1 = 5\cos\tfrac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t}{2}\cos\tfrac{(\omega_2 - \omega_1)t}{2} @@ the sum technique (21d), on cos(P + Q) + cos(P − Q)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cart 1 stands still after } \tfrac{\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1} \approx 5.2 \text{ s},\ \text{and cart 2 swings 5 cm}} @@ the swing passes over to cart 2, and back again every 10.4 s

27then2Every Bar at Once

18·eng solved frames of bars one joint at a time, starting where only two forces were unknown. The joints can also be taken all at once. Each joint gives two samenesses, across and up. The unknowns are the bars’ pulls and the pushes of the supports. So a frame is one system of samenesses, and one grid.

A = (0, 0),\ B = (8, 0),\ C = (4, 3):\ \text{rafters } AC, BC,\ \text{tie } AB,\ \text{a load of } 10 \text{ kN at } C @@ each rafter 5 m: run 4, rise 3; a pin at A pushes two ways, a roller at B only up
A:\ \tfrac45T_{AC} + T_{AB} + H_A = 0,\ \ \tfrac35T_{AC} + V_A = 0 @@ the bar-force technique (18·eng): each bar pulls along itself
B:\ -\tfrac45T_{BC} - T_{AB} = 0,\ \ \tfrac35T_{BC} + V_B = 0 @@ the joint technique: the forces at a pin add to nothing
C:\ -\tfrac45T_{AC} + \tfrac45T_{BC} = 0,\ \ -\tfrac35T_{AC} - \tfrac35T_{BC} = 10 @@ six samenesses in six unknowns: a 6 × 6 grid
\htmlClass{new}{T_{AC} = T_{BC} = -8.33,\ \ T_{AB} = 6.67,\ \ H_A = 0,\ \ V_A = V_B = 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ row moves (27j, the row-move technique); the grid’s determinant is −0.96, not 0, so these are the only forces

Frame-grid technique: a pinned frame with j joints, b bars and r support pushes gives 2j samenesses in b + r unknowns; when 2j = b + r, the loads fix every force exactly when the grid’s determinant is not 0, and a determinant of 0 means some loads cannot be carried at all. The second half is 27c’s lost-plus-reached technique: a square grid that loses some vector reaches less than every list of loads.

A farm gate of four bars, hung on two hinges, has j = 4, b = 4 and r = 4: eight samenesses in eight unknowns. Its grid has determinant 0. The lost vector is the gate leaning over, every bar keeping its length, and the weight at the free end is a load the bars cannot carry. That is why a gate sags unless it has a brace across, and why the gates of farms from the Willamette Valley to the Sierra foothills carry one.

27then3Lynx and Hares

Snowshoe hares in the forests of the North Cascades grow on their own, and are eaten by lynx at a rate in proportion to how often the two meet. Lynx die off on their own, and grow by what they eat. With x hares and y lynx, a simple pair of rules is x′ = ax − bxy and y′ = −cy + dxy.

x' = x(a - by) = 0,\ \ y' = y(-c + dx) = 0,\ \ x, y \gt 0 @@ a rest with both alive
\htmlClass{new}{x^* = \tfrac cd,\ \ y^* = \tfrac ab} @@ each bracket must be 0
J = \begin{pmatrix}a - by^* & -bx^*\\ dy^* & -c + dx^*\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -\tfrac{bc}{d}\\ \tfrac{ad}{b} & 0\end{pmatrix} @@ the tangent grid at the rest
T = 0,\ \ \Delta = ac \gt 0 @@ a centre of the grid, turning at √(ac) (27·cb24.3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on the border line: the grid cannot tell}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 27·cb24.4; 28·bio finds a quantity kept along each path, and the paths close

Crossed-rest technique: at the rest, the number of prey is set by the predators’ rates, c/d, and the number of predators by the prey’s, a/b. More hare births per year raise the lynx at the rest, not the hares. Trap both at an extra rate h a year, and a becomes a − h while c becomes c + h. The rest moves to more hares, (c + h)/d, and fewer lynx, (a − h)/b.

With a = 0.8, b = 0.2, c = 0.6 and d = 0.03 a year, hares in thousands and lynx in hundreds, the rest is 20 thousand hares and 4 hundred lynx. Steps forward (24i) from 30 thousand hares send both counts round a loop, hares peaking every 9.2 years, near the 2π/√(ac) ≈ 9.1 of the grid. The fur records of northern Canada show peaks about every ten years.

27then4How Far an Illness Spreads

A town of N people splits into S still open to an illness, I ill, and R recovered. Meetings between the open and the ill pass it on at a rate β, and each ill person recovers at a rate γ a day. Every count steers the others.

S' = -\beta\tfrac{SI}{N},\ \ I' = \beta\tfrac{SI}{N} - \gamma I,\ \ R' = \gamma I @@ three boxes, and people only move along
I' = \gamma I\big(R_0\tfrac SN - 1\big),\ \ R_0 = \tfrac\beta\gamma @@ factor: the illness grows exactly while R₀S/N > 1
\tfrac{S'}{S} = -\tfrac{R_0}{N}R' \Rightarrow \big(\ln S + \tfrac{R_0}{N}R\big)' = 0 @@ divide S′ by S, and use I = R′/γ; the slope of ln S is S′/S (17)
\ln\tfrac{S_\infty}{S_0} = -\tfrac{R_0}{N}R_\infty @@ a flat function keeps its value (14); R starts at 0
\htmlClass{new}{s_\infty = e^{-R_0(1 - s_\infty)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ S₀ ≈ N, and at the end no one is ill: R∞ = N − S∞, with s∞ = S∞/N

Threshold technique: an illness grows exactly while the share of people still open to it is above 1/R₀. To stop it before it starts, the share open must be below 1/R₀ from the outset.

Final-size technique: the share never touched solves s = e^(−R₀(1 − s)), and it is above 0. With R₀ = 2 that share is about 0.203: four in five fall ill, and one in five never do. Measles has R₀ between 12 and 18, so 92% to 94% must be immune. In 2019 an outbreak in Clark County, Washington, where fewer children had been vaccinated, made 71 people ill.

27then5Balancing Charge as Well as Atoms

In well water across the Puget Sound lowlands, dissolved iron stains sinks. Permanganate, added at a treatment plant, takes it out of solution: MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ + H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ + H₂O. Put a, b, c in front of the three on the left and d, e, f in front of the three on the right. Each element’s atoms are kept: one sameness each. This e is not 17e's number e.

\text{Mn}: a = d,\ \ \text{O}: 4a = f,\ \ \text{Fe}: b = e,\ \ \text{H}: c = 2f @@ four samenesses in six unknowns
\text{the grid reaches at most 4 dimensions, so it loses at least 2} @@ the lost-plus-reached technique (27c): 6 = lost + reached
a = 1:\ d = 1,\ f = 4,\ c = 8,\ \text{and } b = e \text{ is still free} @@ a whole line of answers, even after fixing the scale
\text{charge}: -a + 2b + c = 2d + 3e \Rightarrow -1 + 2b + 8 = 2 + 3b @@ charge is kept too: a fifth sameness
\htmlClass{new}{\text{MnO}_4^- + 5\,\text{Fe}^{2+} + 8\,\text{H}^+ \to \text{Mn}^{2+} + 5\,\text{Fe}^{3+} + 4\,\text{H}_2\text{O}} \quad \blacksquare @@ b = e = 5; one answer up to scale

Charge-row technique: charge is kept as atoms are, so it adds one more sameness; for a reaction that moves charge, it is the row that fixes the answer. Each manganese takes 5 charges from 5 irons: its share of charge drops from 7 to 2, while each iron’s rises from 2 to 3. 27·chem asks when a balance is one, many, or none.

27then6Carbon in the Air and the Sea

Carbon passes between the air and the upper layer of the sea. Before factories, the air held about 600 billion tonnes of it, written GtC, and the upper sea about 900, with about 90 GtC a year crossing each way. Two boxes, two rates: A′ = −k₁A + k₂O and O′ = k₁A − k₂O, with k₁ = 0.15 and k₂ = 0.1 a year.

(1, 1)\begin{pmatrix}-k_1 & k_2\\ k_1 & -k_2\end{pmatrix} = (0, 0) @@ each column adds to 0: what leaves one box enters the other, so A + O is kept
\det M = \det M^{\mathsf T} = 0 @@ the side-turn technique (27g): the turned grid loses (1, 1)
\text{stretch } 0 \text{ on } (k_2, k_1),\ \ \text{stretch } -(k_1 + k_2) \text{ on } (1, -1) @@ the stretch-test technique (27f); T = −(k₁ + k₂)
(100, 0) = 20\,(2, 3) + 60\,(1, -1) @@ a pulse of 100 GtC into the air; (k₂, k₁) = (0.1, 0.15) points along (2, 3)
\htmlClass{new}{A - 600 = 40 + 60\,e^{-0.25t}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the split-system technique: 40% of the pulse stays in the air

Kept-total technique: in a box model where every column of the grid adds to 0, the total is kept, 0 is a stretch, and when every other stretch has real part below 0 the boxes settle to the shares of the vector with stretch 0. In two boxes, the air keeps the share k₂/(k₁ + k₂) of any pulse, and settles in about 1/(k₁ + k₂) = 4 years.

Anexact, yet rigorous · lumping the world into boxes

A box model is a guess at the grid. The sea is not one box: its upper hundred metres mix in years, its depths in centuries, and the land and its forests take carbon at rates of their own. Every box is many places lumped together, and the grid’s numbers are measured with error.

A box model is a guess at the grid: say which boxes were lumped together, and how far the answer moves when one is split.

The guess can still be honest. Split one box in two and see how far the answer moves. Add the deep sea, about 37 000 GtC exchanging slowly, as a third box, and the grid gains a small third stretch. Then the 40% left in the air is not the end. It drains away, but over centuries.

A model of Puget Sound’s water as one box, and one with three basins, disagree on how long a spill stays. Which would you trust more, and how would you find out?

27then7A Population of Many Ages

Count the does of a herd of mule deer in the Sierra in three ages: fawns, yearlings and adults. Each year a share of each age lives into the next, and each yearling and adult has female fawns. A grid takes this year’s counts to next year’s.

x_{k+1} = Lx_k,\ \ L = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0.6 & 0.9\\ 0.5 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0.8 & 0.8\end{pmatrix} @@ row 1: fawns born per yearling and per adult; half the fawns live; 80% of yearlings and adults live
\det(L - \lambda I) = -\lambda^3 + 0.8\lambda^2 + 0.3\lambda + 0.12 @@ the three-rules formula (27e), along the first row
\lambda_1 \approx 1.151,\ \ \lambda_{2,3} \approx -0.176 \pm 0.271i,\ \ |\lambda_{2,3}| \approx 0.323 @@ one real root, and a pair of complex ones (25e)
x_k = L^kx_0 = c_1\lambda_1^kv_1 + c_2\lambda_2^kv_2 + c_3\lambda_3^kv_3 @@ three different stretches give three free vectors (27f)
\tfrac{x_k}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1v_1 + c_2\big(\tfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_2 + c_3\big(\tfrac{\lambda_3}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_3 \to c_1v_1 @@ the leading-stretch technique (27l): |λ₂| and |λ₃| are below |λ₁|
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shares} \to (0.413,\ 0.179,\ 0.408),\ \ \text{growing about } 15\% \text{ a year}} \quad \blacksquare @@ v₁ scaled to add to 1, whenever c₁ ≠ 0

So 27l’s leading-stretch technique settles the herd: from any start with a part along v₁, the herd’s shares settle to the shares of v₁, and the herd grows by λ₁ each year. Start with 100 fawns and nothing else. After one year the herd is all yearlings, after two it has no yearlings at all, and after ten its shares are 0.413, 0.179 and 0.408 to three places. The start is forgotten.

The accepted names

Two carts swapping their swing show beats, and the modes are the normal modes of coupled oscillators. Solving every joint at once is the matrix form of the method of joints; a frame with 2j = b + r and a nonzero determinant is statically determinate, and the sagging gate is a mechanism. The rules for hares and lynx are the Lotka–Volterra equations, and the crossed rest with trapping is Volterra’s principle. The three boxes of an illness are the SIR model, R₀ is the basic reproduction number, 1 − 1/R₀ is the herd immunity threshold, and s∞ = e^(−R₀(1 − s∞)) is the final size equation. A reaction that moves charge is a redox reaction, and each atom’s share of charge is its oxidation state. The share of a pulse that stays in the air is the airborne fraction. The grid of ages is a Leslie matrix, its largest stretch the population’s growth rate, and its vector the stable age distribution.

Who said it first

Alfred Lotka (1925) and Vito Volterra (1926) wrote the predator and prey rules; Volterra was asked by Umberto D’Ancona why the fish markets of the Adriatic sold more sharks during the First World War, when fishing stopped. William Kermack and Anderson McKendrick gave the epidemic rules and the threshold in 1927. Patrick Leslie gave the grid of ages in 1945. In 27·then.9.5, Charles Elton studied the fur company’s lynx records in 1924 and looked to sunspots; the island hares and the fenced plots in the Yukon were studied in the 1980s and 1990s by Charles Krebs and his colleagues.
