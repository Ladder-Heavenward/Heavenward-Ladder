What Holds a Molecule Together?

What holds a molecule together? Why should a ladder of logic have anything to say about chemistry? Not because chemistry is physics with harder sums. The logic itself reaches into chemistry. A molecule is a set of atoms. An atom is a statement with gaps, as many gaps as it has bonds. A molecule holds together when every gap is filled. The bonds between atoms are a relation, and that relation has some of 2i’s properties and lacks another. This page builds each of those readings and puts them to work on water, on air and on the gas you breathe out.

A molecule is a set of atoms, and an atom is a statement with as many gaps as its bonds. So the checks a chemist runs on a drawing of a molecule are reading checks the ladder already has.

2chem1A Molecule Is a Set

Take one molecule of water and read it as the set of its atoms. It has one oxygen and two hydrogens. Written with names that repeat, that is {H, O, H}. 2e8 says a repeat names the same set: {H, O, H} = {H, O}. That set has two members, and the molecule has three atoms.

The two hydrogens are two things, so each gets its own name: H₁ and H₂. Then the molecule is a set with three members.

A cup of water is a set of molecules, c. The molecule w is in c, and H₁ is in w. H₁ is not in c: the cup’s members are molecules, not atoms. That is 2e12’s warning that in and part of must be kept apart, now with a cup in your hand.

Chemists write water as H₂O. The small 2 counts the hydrogens, and counting waits for rung 4. The set with named members needs no count: it says which atoms, and nothing else.

2chem2An Atom Is a Statement With Gaps

0a took the names out of “the cat is on the mat” and left “() is on the ()”, a statement with two gaps. Read each atom the same way, with one gap for each bond it makes. A hydrogen has one gap. An oxygen has two. A nitrogen has three. A carbon has four.

O(x, y) reads “an oxygen bonded to x and to y”. Fill both gaps with the hydrogens and you have water. Each hydrogen’s one gap is filled by the oxygen.

No gap is left anywhere. A statement with every gap filled says something whole, and a molecule with every gap filled holds together.

The same reading gives the gas in a stove, methane, as C(H₁, H₂, H₃, H₄), and ammonia as N(H₁, H₂, H₃).

Gap technique: an atom is a statement with as many gaps as its bonds, and a molecule is what you get when every gap is filled.

2chem3Bonds Are a Relation

On the atoms of a molecule, let b be “is bonded to”. 2i11 made a relation a set of pairs. In water:

Now test 2i12’s three properties. b is symmetric: a bond joins two atoms, and it joins them both ways. b is not reflexive: no atom is bonded to itself. b is not transitive: H₁ is bonded to O, and O to H₂, but H₁ is not bonded to H₂.

A bond and a further bond is not a bond. That is what the chemist’s drawing H-O-H says: two lines, each to the oxygen, and none between the hydrogens. Joined in one or more steps is 2i18’s r⁺. In a molecule every atom is joined to every other that way, and that is what makes it one molecule and not two.

2chem4One Partner Fills Two Gaps

0a5 said one name may fill two gaps: M(z, z) is “Zoe lives next door to Zoe”. In chemistry this happens all the time. The oxygen in air comes as two oxygens, O₁ and O₂. Each has two gaps, and each fills both of the other’s.

Chemists call that a double bond, and draw it with two lines. The gas you breathe out, carbon dioxide, has two of them. The carbon’s four gaps take each oxygen twice.

Now look at b for carbon dioxide. It holds (C, O₁) once, since a set of pairs has no repeats. The relation says who is bonded to whom. The gap statement also says how many times. Each reading carries something the other drops.

2chem5A Gap Left Open, a Name Too Many

A statement with a gap open is not yet a statement. 0a said so of “() is on the mat”. Take water and pull off one hydrogen. What is left is O(H₁, y), an oxygen with a gap open. A given, since the ladder cannot run a lab: chemists find that such a piece does not stay alone for long. It fills its gap with the first partner it can. They call it a radical.

The other way round, a drawing of C(H₁, H₂, H₃, H₄, H₅) gives five names to four gaps. It cannot be read at all, so no molecule is drawn. That is the first check a chemist runs on a proposed drawing: count each atom’s lines against its gaps.

2chem7One Line for Each Bond

The ladder already has the tool for the repeats. “Is bonded to” is symmetric (2chem3):

So a bond said from one end is said from the other. Write each bond once, as a line between its two atoms. Where one partner fills two gaps, write two lines.

Stay on that symmetry a moment, because it is exactly where a large part of chemistry happens. The line says that two atoms share a bond, and it says the same from either end. It does not say the two ends are alike. A given from the lab: in most bonds one atom holds the shared part closer than the other does. In water the oxygen holds it closer than either hydrogen. Let h be “holds the shared part closer than”. It is a relation on the same atoms, it lies inside b, and it is not symmetric:

So each line of a drawing carries a second fact that the line does not show: which end holds closer. In methane the carbon holds closer than each of its four hydrogens. In carbon dioxide each oxygen holds closer than the carbon. Burn methane and the carbon goes from the first to the second: on every one of its bonds it was the holder, and now it is the one held from. Through that change b stays symmetric, and h is what moves.

The small numbers can go as well. They were names, put on in 2chem1 because a set cannot hold the same name twice. A drawing is not a set of letters. Each letter sits in its own place on the page, and the place tells the atoms apart.

The letters lean on transitive. “Is the same kind of atom as” is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, so it is a sameness relation (2i14). Its classes in phenol are the carbons, the hydrogens and the one oxygen (2i15). A class needs one name, so one letter, C, does for all six carbons.

The list of members can go too. “Is bonded to” is not transitive (2chem3), but “is joined in one or more steps” is. That relation is b⁺, and 2i18 made it transitive. It is symmetric, because b is and a path of bonds can be walked back. In a molecule of two or more atoms it is reflexive, by a bond out and the same bond back. So b⁺ is a sameness relation, and its classes are the molecules. Let u be every atom in a jar of phenol. One molecule of it is the class of its C₁:

Draw the lines and the members are told: every atom the lines reach, and no other. The thirteen names of 2chem6’s set need not be listed. A cup of water is chopped into its molecules the same way, and 2i17 called such a chopping a partition.

What is left is a letter for each atom and a line for each bond. Chemists call it a structural formula. Reading it back is an unpack: for each letter, list what its lines reach, and you have its gap statement again.

2chem8What the Gaps Force

The structural formula still writes more than it has to. Every hydrogen has one gap and every carbon four. Draw the lines between the carbons and the oxygen, and look at what each carbon has left. C₂ has three of its gaps filled by lines to C₁ and C₃. One gap is left, and only a hydrogen is there to fill it. The same holds at every carbon:

That is an every, and an every can be used wherever it applies (2c6). So the hydrogens on carbons need not be drawn. The reader puts them back.

One more rule finishes it. Carbon is the atom these drawings are mostly made of, so a corner with no letter is read as a carbon. Phenol is now a hexagon, three second lines for the doubles, and the letters OH.

Chemists call this a skeletal formula. It is the thirteen statements, packed. Two rules unpack it: a bare corner is a carbon, and a carbon’s leftover gaps hold hydrogens.

2chem9Parts With Names

Some parts of a molecule turn up again and again. In phenol the oxygen and its hydrogen are a part of p:

An oxygen with one hydrogen and one gap still open is found in a great many molecules, so chemists give the part a name, the hydroxyl group, and write it OH. The ring with its five hydrogens is another named part, the phenyl group, written C₆H₅. Name the parts in order and phenol fits on one line: C₆H₅OH. That is a condensed formula.

Shorter still, sift the atoms by kind:

That sift is the class of carbons from 2chem7. Write each kind with how many passed the sift, and phenol is C₆H₆O. That is a molecular formula. It keeps the kinds and how many of each, and drops b altogether.

The sugar in your blood, glucose, is written this way: C₆H₁₂O₆. Six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens, and not a word about which is bonded to which. Fruit sugar, fructose, is C₆H₁₂O₆ as well: the same atoms, bonded another way. So a molecular formula is a condition on molecules, and more than one molecule can pass it.

Each way of writing keeps less than the one before it.

2chem10Numbers Round the Ring

Chemistry is written in sentences like this one, about the painkiller paracetamol: “The classical methods for the production of paracetamol involve the acetylation of 4-aminophenol with acetic anhydride as the last step.” The sentence expects you to draw 4-aminophenol from its name alone. Why is it written that way?

The phenol’s oxygen group takes the 1 position on its chain, so we can identify the 4th positions on a chain or ring, and see the amine there.

Take the name apart from its end. “Phenol” is the ring with OH. The part that names the molecule takes the first place, so the carbon that holds the OH is corner 1. From there the corners are numbered round the ring, each one bonded to the one before. “Amino” names a part, as hydroxyl did in 2chem9: a nitrogen with two hydrogens, written NH₂. A nitrogen has three gaps, so one is left for the ring. “4” says which corner fills it.

In 2chem7 the numbers were dropped, because on a drawing each letter has its place. A name is a line of words, and a line of words has no places. So the numbers come back, and this time they are not names picked to keep two carbons apart. They are places, counted from the part that names the molecule.

Counted the other way round the ring, corner 4 is corner 4 again. A part on the corner next to the OH could be called 2 or 6, and chemists count whichever way gives the smaller number.

The same directions work on a chain. Take four carbons in a row, with an OH on the carbon at one end. That carbon is place 1, and the places are counted along the chain from it: 1, 2, 3, 4. Put the amino part on the carbon at the far end and the molecule is 4-aminobutan-1-ol. “Butan” says the chain has four carbons, “1-ol” says the OH is at place 1, and “4-amino” puts the NH₂ at place 4.

On a chain the count has an end to start from. On a ring every corner is like every other until one part is picked to be place 1. Either way, once place 1 is fixed, place 4 is found by counting.

Naming technique: a chemical name is a set of directions: the parent, where to start counting, and which part sits at which place.

2chem11A Letter for Any Part

Phenol is a ring with OH on it. The alcohol in drinks is a short chain of two carbons with OH on it. Chemists often want to speak of the OH and whatever it is on, without saying what that is. They write a letter for the rest:

R stands for any part with one gap left to fill. Read that way, R-OH is 0a’s statement with a gap once more: “()-OH”. Fill the gap with the phenyl group and it is phenol. Fill it with the two-carbon chain and it is the alcohol in drinks.

R is not the name of any one part. It is 2c5’s fresh name, about which nothing is said but that it has one gap. What a chemist writes about R-OH is meant of every molecule made by filling the gap. Whether it holds of each one is a given from the lab.

2chem12Why the Ladder May Do Chemistry

Put the pieces side by side. A molecule is a set, with 2e’s members and same members. An atom is a statement with gaps, read by 0a’s rules. Bonds are a relation, with 2i’s properties. The chemist’s first checks are reading checks: every gap filled, no name too many. None of that needed a number from physics. The logic applies to the chemistry itself, and that is the ladder’s licence for every chemistry annex above this one.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a rule with known exceptions

The gap counts are a model, and chemists know where it bends. Sulfur is drawn with two bonds, or four, or six. For carbon monoxide, carbon is drawn with 3 lines to its oxygen, which is one more than oxygen takes, and one less than carbon takes, but it’s still stable that way.

In phenol’s ring the drawing puts the doubles at C₁ and C₂, C₃ and C₄, C₅ and C₆. It could as well put them at C₂ and C₃, C₄ and C₅, C₆ and C₁, and every gap would still be filled. Measured, the six bonds of the ring are all alike, so chemists draw either one, or a circle inside the hexagon.

For hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen, the atoms most of life is made of, the counts hold in most molecules. Inside the model, every check is a proof. Whether the model fits a given molecule is a given from the lab.

Accepted names

An atom’s gap count is its valence. A set in which repeats count is a multiset, or bag. A drawing of a molecule with every bond shown is a structural formula. H₂O is a molecular formula, and its small number is a subscript. A relation that holds of no pair (x, x) is irreflexive, so “is bonded to” is irreflexive and symmetric. Atoms as points and bonds as lines make a molecular graph. “Holds the shared part closer” is electronegativity, and a bond whose ends differ in it is a polar bond. The two drawings of phenol’s ring are resonance structures. The rules for names such as 4-aminophenol are the IUPAC nomenclature, and the numbers in them are locants.

Who said it first

Not yet revisited: these dates have not been checked against two sources. Edward Frankland wrote of an atom’s combining power in 1852. August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper each set out in 1858 that carbon makes four bonds, and Couper drew the bonds as lines. Gottlob Frege called the part of a statement left when a name is taken out “unsaturated”, in 1891. Chemistry used “saturated” of a compound whose bonds are all taken.
