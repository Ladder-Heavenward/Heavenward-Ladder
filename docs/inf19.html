Is a Logic the Same Thing as a Space?

Is a logic the same thing as a space? Rung 44 showed that every finite algebra of and, or and not is the parts of a set, the set of its atoms. Here the set is read another way: as the algebra’s ways of deciding every question at once. For a finite algebra each atom is one way. For an endless algebra the ways of deciding are more than the atoms, and they form a space, finitely covered and keeping points apart, from which the algebra is read back as the pieces that are both open and closed. Every arrow is turned round on the way: maps of algebras become functions back between their spaces. Rung 38’s finitely-many-suffice for statements is rung 34’s finitely-many-suffice for this space. Since 2008 the same turning round has been used to sort the patterns that rung 41’s reading machines accept.

A way of deciding B is a map h : B → {0, 1} keeping ∧, ∨, ¬. Finite B: the ways match the atoms, and B is the parts of the set of ways. Maps turn round: f : B → C gives h ↦ h ∘ f from the ways of C to the ways of B. The finite-or-cofinite parts of ℕ have the ways n ∈ ℕ and one more, ∞, “is the part cofinite?”; with open pieces [x] = {h : h(x) = 1} the space ℕ ∪ {∞} is finitely covered and its open-and-closed pieces are exactly the [x]. In a listed algebra every x ≠ 0 has a way with h(x) = 1, by 38d2’s growth.

inf19.1A Way of Deciding Everything

Take an algebra B of and, or and not. A way of deciding it answers every member yes or no, consistently: a map h from B to {0, 1} with h(x ∧ y) = h(x) ∧ h(y), h(x ∨ y) = h(x) ∨ h(y) and h(¬x) = ¬h(x). On the parts of {1, 2, 3}, one way of deciding is “is 2 in it?”.

Atom-way technique: in a finite algebra, the ways of deciding are matched with the atoms: the way h goes to the one atom a with h(a) = 1, and the atom a goes to the way “is a below it?”.

1 = \textstyle\bigvee \{a \text{ atom}\} \Rightarrow h(a) = 1 \text{ for some atom} @@ h keeps ∨ and h(1) = 1; <a href="r44l.html#ea5030">44l5</a>
a \ne b \text{ atoms} \Rightarrow a \land b = 0 \Rightarrow \text{not both } h(a), h(b) = 1 @@ h keeps ∧ and h(0) = 0
h_a(x) = 1 \iff a \le x \text{ keeps } \lor, \neg @@ 44l5: a ≤ x ∨ y gives a ≤ x or a ≤ y; a ≤ ¬x exactly when a ≰ x
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ways of deciding} \leftrightarrow \text{atoms}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so B is the parts of its set of ways

inf19.2Every Arrow Turned Round

Let B be the algebra with members ∅, {1}, {2, 3} and {1, 2, 3}, sitting inside C, the parts of {1, 2, 3}. The map that puts B inside C keeps ∧, ∨ and ¬. A way of deciding C, read through that map, is a way of deciding B.

Turned-round technique for algebras: a map f from B to C keeping ∧, ∨, ¬ gives a function h ↦ h ∘ f from the ways of C to the ways of B; and running two maps in a row turns into running their functions in the other order. This is 47b3’s turned-round technique, for the reading “ways of deciding”.

\text{ways of } C\text{: “is 1 in?”, “is 2 in?”, “is 3 in?”} @@ the atom-way technique: atoms {1}, {2}, {3}
\text{ways of } B\text{: “is } \{1\} \text{ below?”, “is } \{2,3\} \text{ below?”} @@ B’s atoms are {1} and {2, 3}
h_2 \circ f\,(\{2,3\}) = 1,\ \ h_3 \circ f\,(\{2,3\}) = 1 @@ read through the map, 2 and 3 decide B alike
\htmlClass{new}{1 \mapsto \{1\},\ 2 \mapsto \{2,3\},\ 3 \mapsto \{2,3\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the map B → C turned into a function {1, 2, 3} → {atoms of B}

Going the other way, a function between finite sets gives a map between their algebras of parts by pulling back, and the two constructions undo each other. So finite algebras with their maps are matched with finite sets with their functions, every arrow turned round.

inf19.3One More Point

44l5 ended with an endless algebra that is not the parts of anything: the parts of ℕ that are finite or have a finite rest. It has an atom {n} for each n, and a way of deciding “is n in?” for each. It has one more way.

One-more-point technique: the ways of deciding the finite-or-cofinite parts of ℕ are the ways “is n in?”, one for each n, and one more, “is the part cofinite?”.

h(\{n\}) = 1 \text{ for some } n \Rightarrow h = h_n @@ the atom-way argument, for that atom
h(\{n\}) = 0 \text{ for all } n \Rightarrow h(x) = 0 \text{ for every finite } x @@ a finite x is a finite ∨ of atoms
\Rightarrow h(x) = 1 \text{ for every cofinite } x @@ h(¬x) = ¬h(x), and ¬x is finite
\htmlClass{new}{h = h_\infty:\ h(x) = 1 \iff x \text{ cofinite}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and h∞ keeps ∧, ∨, ¬: two cofinite parts meet in a cofinite one

Make the ways into a space, as 34b1 asks: the open pieces are the unions of the sets [x] = {h : h(x) = 1}. For finite x, [x] is a finite set of numbers; for cofinite x, [x] holds ∞ and all but finitely many numbers. So each number is alone in an open piece, and every open piece round ∞ holds all numbers from some point on: the numbers, with ∞ at the end.

inf19.4Finitely Many Suffice

34d called a space finitely covered when every cover by open pieces has a finite part that still covers. The space ℕ ∪ {∞} is one, and its open-and-closed pieces give the algebra back.

Clopen-back technique: ℕ ∪ {∞} is finitely covered, and its pieces that are both open and closed are exactly the [x], one for each member of the algebra.

\infty \in U_0 \Rightarrow U_0 \supseteq \{n, n+1, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\} @@ an open piece round ∞, from the last footing
0, \dots, n - 1 \text{ each lie in some } U_1, \dots, U_n @@ finitely many numbers left over
U \text{ open and closed, } \infty \notin U \Rightarrow \text{the rest, round } \infty\text{, is cofinite} \Rightarrow U \text{ finite} @@ the rest is open
\htmlClass{new}{\text{open and closed} = [x] \text{ for finite or cofinite } x} \quad \blacksquare @@ with ∞, U is the rest of a finite part

For any algebra the space of ways is finitely covered, and this is 38d4’s finite-parts technique in another form: a set of statements is a set of pieces [φ], a world is a way of deciding them all, and “every finite part has a world” is “no finite part of the complements covers”. The two names, compactness, are one name for one fact.

inf19.5Where Choosing Enters

To read an algebra back from its space, there must be enough ways of deciding: for every x ≠ 0, some way says yes to x. For a finite algebra the atoms supply them. For an endless one they must be built, as 38d2 grew a clash-free collection until it held each statement or its denial.

Growth technique: in an algebra whose members can be listed, every x ≠ 0 has a way of deciding with h(x) = 1.

F_0 = x,\quad b_0, b_1, b_2, \dots \text{ a list of } B @@ start from x ≠ 0
F_{i+1} = F_i \land b_i \text{ if this is } \ne 0\text{, else } F_i \land \neg b_i @@ F ∧ b = 0 and F ∧ ¬b = 0 would give F = 0
h(b) = 1 \iff F_i \le b \text{ for some } i @@ each b or ¬b is reached by step i + 1
\htmlClass{new}{h \text{ keeps } \land, \lor, \neg \text{ and } h(x) = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ no stage reaches 0, so never both b and ¬b

Without a list the steps cannot be taken one at a time, and a choosing function of 6b takes their place: for algebras that cannot be listed, enough ways of deciding exist only by a form of choosing. That is where 6b’s axiom spends itself in this theory.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In the 1930s a mathematician showed that every algebra of and, or and not is the open-and-closed pieces of a finitely covered space that keeps points apart, and that maps of algebras are continuous maps of spaces turned round.

In 2008 three mathematicians used this turning round to show that a family of patterns accepted by reading machines, closed under and and or, is exactly the family cut out by a set of equations on the space of endless inputs, which unified several earlier sorting results.

The accepted names

A way of deciding is a homomorphism to the two-element Boolean algebra, the same as an ultrafilter; the space of them is the Stone space, and the matching is Stone duality, an equivalence between Boolean algebras and Stone spaces (compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected) with arrows reversed. Open-and-closed is clopen. ℕ ∪ {∞} is the one-point compactification of ℕ. The growth technique is the ultrafilter lemma for countable algebras; in general it is the Boolean prime ideal theorem, weaker than the axiom of choice. Equations on the space of inputs are profinite equations.

Who said it first

Marshall Stone, “The theory of representations for Boolean algebras” (1936). Mai Gehrke, Serge Grigorieff and Jean-Éric Pin, “Duality and equational theory of regular languages” (ICALP 2008).
