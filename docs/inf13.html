Can Chance Be Drawn?

Can chance be drawn? A chance rule is a box that takes an input and returns an output with chances. Boxes run in a row by rung 30’s chance grids and side by side by independence. Add a dot that copies a wire and a dot that ends one, and the statements of probability become pictures. Every chance rule passes through the ending dot, and only plain functions pass through the copying dot; that one fact separates chance from certainty. Independence is a picture that falls into two pieces, turning a rule around by Bayes is bending a wire, and the difference between seeing and doing is cutting a wire into a box. From 2019 the pictures were made a theory of their own, and in 2021 a classical result on rows of draws was proved inside it. How much of rung 30 holds in every such world, and which results need the numbers, is open.

Chance rules f : A → B are boxes; in a row by grid products, side by side by multiplying chances. copy : a ↦ (a, a), discard : a ↦ ∗. discard ∘ f = discard always; copy ∘ f = (f ⊗ f) ∘ copy exactly when f is a plain function. A joint chance is independent exactly when it equals its two marginals side by side. Bayes’s turned rule g is the one box with (g ⊗ id) ∘ copy ∘ (f ∘ π) = swap ∘ (id ⊗ f) ∘ copy ∘ π. Doing t replaces the wire into T by the fixed input t.

inf13.1Chance Rules as Boxes

A chance rule f from A to B gives, for each a, a chance list f(b | a) on B: ∞1’s chance wrapper, read as a box with wire A in and wire B out. In a row they compose by 30p1’s chance grids: (g ∘ f)(c | a) = Σb g(c | b) f(b | a). Side by side, (f ⊗ f′)(b, b′ | a, a′) = f(b | a) f′(b′ | a′): the two run independently.

Chance-boxes technique: chance rules in a row and side by side obey 47·eng.2’s box-drawing rule, (g ⊗ g′) ∘ (f ⊗ f′) = (g ∘ f) ⊗ (g′ ∘ f′).

\big((g \otimes g^{\prime}) \circ (f \otimes f^{\prime})\big)(c, c^{\prime} \mid a, a^{\prime}) = \textstyle\sum_{b, b^{\prime}} g(c \mid b)\, g^{\prime}(c^{\prime} \mid b^{\prime})\, f(b \mid a)\, f^{\prime}(b^{\prime} \mid a^{\prime}) @@ grids in a row, side by side
\phantom{\big(\cdots\big)} = \textstyle\sum_b g(c \mid b) f(b \mid a) \cdot \sum_{b^{\prime}} g^{\prime}(c^{\prime} \mid b^{\prime}) f^{\prime}(b^{\prime} \mid a^{\prime}) @@ the double sum splits
\htmlClass{new}{= (g \circ f)(c \mid a)\,\cdot\,(g^{\prime} \circ f^{\prime})(c^{\prime} \mid a^{\prime})} \quad \blacksquare @@ read a drawing row by row or column by column

inf13.2Copy and Discard

Two dots on a wire. Copy sends a to (a, a) with chance 1: a wire that splits in two. Discard sends every a to the single point ∗: a wire that ends. Every chance rule passes through discard, discard ∘ f = discard, because f(b | a) adds to 1 over b. Copy is another matter.

Copy-sorts technique: copy ∘ f = (f ⊗ f) ∘ copy exactly when f is a plain function: for each a, one b with chance 1.

(\mathrm{copy} \circ f)(b, b^{\prime} \mid a) = f(b \mid a) \text{ if } b = b^{\prime}\text{, else } 0 @@ draw once, then copy
((f \otimes f) \circ \mathrm{copy})(b, b^{\prime} \mid a) = f(b \mid a)\, f(b^{\prime} \mid a) @@ copy, then draw twice
b \ne b^{\prime} \text{ both with chance } > 0 \Rightarrow 0 \ne f(b \mid a) f(b^{\prime} \mid a) @@ two outcomes break it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{equal exactly when each } f(- \mid a) \text{ is one outcome with chance 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and then f(b|a)² = f(b|a)

inf13.3A Picture That Falls Into Two

A joint chance ψ on X × Y is a box with no input and two output wires. Its marginal on X is ψ followed by discarding Y. 30f2 called A and B independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B).

Falls-apart technique: ψ makes every event about X independent of every event about Y exactly when ψ is its two marginals side by side: the picture falls into two pieces.

\psi = \psi_X \otimes \psi_Y \Rightarrow \psi(A \times B) = \psi_X(A)\,\psi_Y(B) @@ side by side multiplies chances
\psi(A \times B) = \psi(A \times Y)\,\psi(X \times B) @@ that is P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)
\text{independence for single points: } \psi(x, y) = \psi_X(x)\,\psi_Y(y) @@ take A = {x}, B = {y}
\htmlClass{new}{\psi = \psi_X \otimes \psi_Y \iff \text{independent}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a finite chance is fixed by its points

Given-and-independent is a picture too: X and Z are independent given Y when the joint is a chance on Y, copied three ways, with a box to X on one copy and a box to Z on another. Arrangement and sequence go; what is left is which wires touch.

inf13.4Turning a Rule Around

Start with a belief π on Θ and a rule f from Θ to X. Their joint is π copied, with f on one copy: (id ⊗ f) ∘ copy ∘ π, a chance on Θ × X. The turned rule g from X to Θ is the box that gives the same joint from the other side: the chance f ∘ π on X, copied, with g on one copy.

Turn-around technique: (g ⊗ id) ∘ copy ∘ (f ∘ π) equals the joint, with the wires swapped, exactly when g(θ | x) = f(x | θ) π(θ) / Σt f(x | t) π(t) wherever x has chance above 0.

\text{joint}(\theta, x) = \pi(\theta)\, f(x \mid \theta) @@ copy π, apply f to one copy
\text{other side}(\theta, x) = g(\theta \mid x)\,(f \circ \pi)(x) @@ copy f ∘ π, apply g to one copy
(f \circ \pi)(x) = \textstyle\sum_t f(x \mid t)\pi(t) @@ grids in a row
\htmlClass{new}{g(\theta \mid x) = \tfrac{f(x \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\sum_t f(x \mid t)\pi(t)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide, where the bottom is above 0: 30u1’s belief update

30u1’s belief-update technique is the one way to bend the wire from Θ to X back to a wire from X to Θ while keeping the picture of the joint.

inf13.5Seeing Against Doing

A cause Z (say, how ill a patient is) feeds both a treatment T and an outcome Y, and T feeds Y. Seeing T = t keeps the picture and asks about the patients who happened to get t. Doing t cuts the wire from Z into T and puts a box that always gives t. 30v3’s fair split does the cutting in practice: a coin, not Z, decides T.

Cut-the-wire technique: seeing gives Σz P(y | t, z) P(z | t); doing gives Σz P(y | t, z) P(z). They agree when the wire from Z to T is absent.

P(z = \text{ill}) = \tfrac12;\ P(t \mid \text{ill}) = 0.8,\ P(t \mid \text{well}) = 0.2 @@ the ill are treated more
P(\text{recover} \mid t, \text{ill}) = 0.5,\ P(\text{recover} \mid t, \text{well}) = 0.9 @@ the treatment helps less than being well
\text{seeing: } P(\text{ill} \mid t) = 0.8 \Rightarrow 0.8 \cdot 0.5 + 0.2 \cdot 0.9 = 0.58 @@ 30f2’s given-chance, and the mix
\htmlClass{new}{\text{doing: } \tfrac12 \cdot 0.5 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.9 = 0.70} \quad \blacksquare @@ cut the wire: Z keeps its own chances

The treated look worse because they were the ill, as in 30v1’s mixed rates. The cut picture asks what the treatment does, whoever gets it.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Drawing chance as networks of causes goes back to the 1920s, in the study of inheritance. In the 1980s and 1990s a computer scientist made such networks a calculus of seeing and doing, and the cut wire became the standard picture of an experiment.

From 2019 chance rules with copy and discard were made a theory of their own: a category with a copying and an ending dot for every wire, obeying a few rules, in which only some arrows pass through copy. Bayes’s turned rule and given-and-independent were defined from the pictures alone.

In 2021 a result of 1931 on rows of draws whose order does not matter, that they are mixtures of independent rows, was proved inside such categories. Others have followed, and the question of how far the pictures go remains active.

The accepted names

Chance rules are Markov kernels; a category of them with copy and discard is a Markov category, and copy and discard are a comonoid on each wire. Arrows that pass through copy are deterministic. The turned rule is the Bayesian inverse; the picture of conditional independence is a string diagram. Doing t is Pearl’s do-operator, and the cut picture is an intervention on a causal graph; the cause Z is a confounder. The 1931 theorem is de Finetti’s theorem on exchangeable sequences.

Who said it first

Sewall Wright, path analysis (1920s). Judea Pearl, Causality (2000) and earlier papers. Kenta Cho and Bart Jacobs, “Disintegration and Bayesian inversion via string diagrams” (2019). Tobias Fritz, “A synthetic approach to Markov kernels, conditional independence and theorems on sufficient statistics” (2019 preprint; 2020). Bruno de Finetti (1931). Tobias Fritz, Tomáš Gonda and Paolo Perrone, de Finetti’s theorem in categorical probability (2021).
