34a · proof

Spaces with a distance

Rung 10 caught nearness with a gap: a distance on the line smaller than any challenge. Rung 18 measured distance in the plane and in space, rung 24 measured the gap between two functions by the largest gap between their values, and rung 22 asked when a list of points settles. Each time the proof used the same few facts about distance, and never what the points were. This step collects those facts into four rules, and calls any set with a distance that obeys them a space with a distance. In every such space there are balls, open sets, closed sets, settling lists and crowding lists, with the old proofs carried over almost word for word. Then the step finds something it was not looking for. Continuity can be said with open sets alone, and the distance drops out of the statement. That is the door to the next step. The step ends on a property that does need the distance, and that the next steps will lose.

A distance is a function of two points that is never negative, is zero only from a point to itself, reads the same both ways, and never beats a detour by a third point. Balls are open, open sets survive any unions and finitely many overlaps, and a set is closed exactly when it keeps every limit of its own members. A map is continuous everywhere exactly when it pulls every open set back to an open set. A space is complete when every crowding list settles, and a closed part of a complete space is complete.

34a1A distance

On the line, 8h measured how far apart x and y are by |x − y|. 18a did it in space, and 24c measured two continuous functions on [u, v] by their largest gap, ‖f − g‖. The proofs that used these never looked inside the points. They used four facts.

From here on, a distance on a set X is a function d that gives each pair of members x, y a real d(x, y), with four rules. It is never negative. It is 0 exactly when x = y. It reads the same both ways: d(x, y) = d(y, x). And a detour never wins: d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z). X with its distance is a space with a distance. Its accepted name is a metric space.

Five spaces with a distance, each used again on this rung. The line, with |x − y|. The plane, with the straight distance of 18a. The plane again, with the street distance |x₁ − y₁| + |x₂ − y₂|, the way a taxi in downtown Portland counts blocks. The continuous functions on [u, v], with the largest gap. And any set at all, with the all-or-nothing distance: 0 from a point to itself, and 1 between any two different points.

Back-triangle technique: in a space with a distance, the distances from two points to a third differ by at most the distance between the two.

d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z) @@ a detour never wins
d(x, z) - d(y, z) \le d(x, y) @@ move d(y, z) across (8g)
d(y, z) - d(x, z) \le d(y, x) = d(x, y) @@ the same line with x and y swapped; it reads the same both ways
\htmlClass{new}{|d(x, z) - d(y, z)| \le d(x, y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ unpack-the-bars (8h), with both lines above

34a2Balls and open sets

The ball of reach r about x is B(x, r), the set of points y with d(x, y) < r. On the line it is the stretch (x − r, x + r), which is 10a’s neighbourhood with its point kept in. In the plane with the street distance it is a square standing on one corner. With the all-or-nothing distance, B(x, 1) is just {x}.

Pages since rung 26 have spoken of open sets loosely. Here is the definition. A part U of X is open when every point of U has some ball about it that lies inside U. So an open set has no point on its own edge: from every point there is room to move a little in every direction and stay inside.

Ball-open technique: every ball is an open set.

y \in B(x, r),\ \ s = r - d(x, y) > 0 @@ the room y has left inside the ball
z \in B(y, s) \Rightarrow d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z) < d(x, y) + s = r @@ a detour never wins
\htmlClass{new}{B(y, s) \subseteq B(x, r)} \quad \blacksquare @@ every point of the ball has a ball about it inside

Open-family technique: in a space with a distance, ∅ and X are open, every union of open sets is open, and the overlap of two open sets is open.

\emptyset \text{ is open};\ \ X \text{ is open} @@ ∅ has no point to check (1h); every ball lies inside X
x \in \textstyle\bigcup_i U_i \Rightarrow x \in U_j \text{ for some } j \Rightarrow B(x, r) \subseteq U_j \subseteq \bigcup_i U_i @@ U_j is open
x \in U \cap V:\ \ B(x, r) \subseteq U,\ \ B(x, s) \subseteq V @@ both are open
\htmlClass{new}{B(x, \min(r, s)) \subseteq U \cap V} \quad \blacksquare @@ lesser-below-both (10a); so, by induction (4b), the overlap of finitely many open sets is open

The overlap of endlessly many open sets need not be open. The stretches (−1/n, 1/n), for n = 1, 2, 3, …, overlap in {0} alone, and no ball about 0 fits inside {0}. The shrinking minimum has no positive floor.

34a3Closed sets keep their limits

A list x₁, x₂, x₃, … of points of X settles at x when d(xₙ, x) heads to 0: for every ε > 0 some N makes d(xₙ, x) < ε for every n ≥ N. That is rung 22’s settling, with the distance in place of |xₙ − x|.

A part A of X is closed when its leftover X ∖ A is open. Closed is not the opposite of open. On the line, [0, 1] is closed, (0, 1) is open, [0, 1) is neither, and the whole line is both.

Keeps-limits technique: a part of a space with a distance is closed exactly when every list of its members that settles in the space settles at one of its members.

A \text{ closed},\ \ x_n \in A,\ \ x_n \to x,\ \ \text{suppose } x \notin A @@ the first half
B(x, r) \subseteq X \setminus A \text{ for some } r > 0 @@ the leftover is open
d(x_n, x) < r \text{ for } n \ge N,\ \text{so } x_N \in X \setminus A @@ the list settles at x; a false line, so x ∈ A
A \text{ not closed}:\ \ \text{some } x \notin A \text{ has every } B(x, \tfrac1n) \text{ meeting } A @@ the second half: the leftover is not open at x
\text{pick } x_n \in A \cap B(x, \tfrac1n) @@ countably many picks: choice, countably often (6b)
\htmlClass{new}{x_n \in A,\ \ x_n \to x \notin A} \quad \blacksquare @@ d(xₙ, x) < 1/n; so a closed set is what keeps its limits

So [0, 1) is not closed: 1 − 1/n settles at 1, which it leaves out. The fractions inside the line are not closed either, as 22c3 found with a list that settles at √2.

34a4Continuity sees only the open sets

Let X have the distance d and Y the distance e. A map f from X to Y is continuous at x when for every ε > 0 some δ > 0 makes e(f(x), f(y)) < ε whenever d(x, y) < δ. That is 11a’s continuity, with the two distances for the two gaps. In balls: f carries B(x, δ) inside B(f(x), ε).

The mark f⁻¹ gets one more job. 9d used it for the function that runs f backwards, which exists only when f is a bijection. From here on, for a part V of Y, f⁻¹(V) also names the pull-back of V: the set of x in X with f(x) in V. The pull-back exists for every f. When f is a bijection, the two meanings agree: the pull-back of V is what the backwards function makes of V.

Pull-back technique: a map between spaces with distances is continuous at every point exactly when the pull-back of every open set is open.

f \text{ continuous},\ \ V \subseteq Y \text{ open},\ \ x \in f^{-1}(V) @@ the first half
B(f(x), \varepsilon) \subseteq V \text{ for some } \varepsilon > 0 @@ f(x) ∈ V, and V is open
f(B(x, \delta)) \subseteq B(f(x), \varepsilon) \subseteq V,\ \ \text{so } B(x, \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(V) @@ continuity at x; so the pull-back is open
\text{every pull-back of an open set open};\ \ x \in X,\ \varepsilon > 0 @@ the second half
x \in f^{-1}(B(f(x), \varepsilon)),\ \text{an open set} @@ the ball-open technique (34a2)
\htmlClass{new}{B(x, \delta) \subseteq f^{-1}(B(f(x), \varepsilon)) \text{ for some } \delta > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ that open set holds a ball about x: which is continuity at x

Read the right side of the technique again. It speaks of open sets and pull-backs, and of nothing else. The distance was needed to say which sets are open, and after that it is never used. Two different distances that give the same open sets give the same continuous maps. Is a distance needed at all?

34a5Crowding, and complete spaces

A list in X crowds when for every ε > 0 some N makes d(xₘ, xₙ) < ε for all m, n ≥ N. 22c proved that a list that settles crowds, with only a detour through the limit, so the proof carries to every space with a distance. The other way is the question. A space is complete when every list that crowds in it settles in it.

The line is complete: that is 22c’s crowd technique. The fractions, with the same distance, are not. The continuous functions on [u, v] with the largest gap are complete: that is 24c’s function-crowd technique. The all-or-nothing distance is complete for a lazy reason: a list that crowds within 1/2 is constant from some point on.

Closed-part technique: with the same distance, a closed part of a complete space is complete, and a complete part of any space with a distance is closed.

A \subseteq X \text{ closed},\ X \text{ complete},\ \ x_n \in A \text{ crowds} @@ the first half
x_n \to x \text{ for some } x \in X @@ the list crowds in X too: the distance is the same
\htmlClass{new}{x \in A} @@ the keeps-limits technique (34a3); so A is complete
A \text{ complete},\ \ x_n \in A,\ \ x_n \to x \in X @@ the second half
x_n \text{ crowds, so } x_n \to y \text{ for some } y \in A @@ settle-crowd (22c); A is complete
d(x, y) \le d(x, x_n) + d(x_n, y) \to 0,\ \text{so } x = y @@ a detour never wins; zero only at home
\htmlClass{new}{x \in A,\ \text{so } A \text{ is closed}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the keeps-limits technique

So [0, 1] is complete and (0, 1) is not: the list 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … crowds and leaves for 0. Completeness needs the distance in a way continuity did not. The next step keeps only the open sets, and on 34e (0, 1) turns out to have the same open sets as the whole line, matched point for point. One of them is complete and the other is not.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A distance is a metric, and a space with a distance is a metric space. The ball B(x, r) is the open ball. The back-triangle technique is the reverse triangle inequality. The street distance is the taxicab or Manhattan metric, ℓ¹; the largest gap is the sup metric; the all-or-nothing distance is the discrete metric. A pull-back is a preimage or inverse image. A list that crowds is a Cauchy sequence, and a space in which every one settles is a complete metric space.

Who said it first

Georg Cantor studied closed sets of the line, and the limits they keep, in the early 1880s. Maurice Fréchet, in his thesis of 1906, gathered the rules of a distance and studied spaces of functions with them. Felix Hausdorff named the metric space in his book of 1914. Stefan Banach made complete spaces of functions the ground of a whole subject in 1922.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every stretch (−1/n, 1/n) is open. An overlap of open sets is open. So {0} is open.” Not allowed: the open-family technique promises overlaps of two sets, and by induction of finitely many. Each overlap of finitely many of these stretches is open; the overlap of all of them is not, because the reaches 1/n have no positive floor.
