16i · technique

Improper integrals

Rung 15's integral needs a closed interval and a bounded function. The region under one over the square, from one onwards, has no right end: it runs off to the right forever. The region under one over the square root, from zero to one, has a bounded base but climbs forever at the left. Neither has an area in rung 15's sense. Each does have areas up to any finite cut, and 16c computes them in a line. This step asks what those areas do as the cut runs out to forever, or in to the bad point. Sometimes they settle on a number, and that number is taken as the area. Sometimes they grow past every bound. A short list of powers decides most cases, and a comparison decides the rest.

Out to forever, the area is what the areas out to farther and farther ends close in on. Where the function climbs at an end, the area is what the areas stopping nearer and nearer that end close in on. For one over a power: out to forever, the area is finite exactly when the power is more than one; in to zero, exactly when it is less than one; the power one fails both ways. A smaller positive function under one with a finite area has one too.

16i1Out to forever, and in to a bad point

Stands on 16c (area-is-a-change) and 10f (far out).

From here on, if f is integrable on [a, b] for every b > a, then ∫ₐ^∞ f(x) dx means lim_{b→∞} ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx, when that limit exists. If f is integrable on [c, b] for every c in (a, b], then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx means lim_{c→a⁺} ∫_cᵇ f(x) dx, when it exists; the same at the right end. When the limit exists the integral converges; otherwise it diverges. Their accepted name is improper integrals. Each mark is a name, for that limit, when it exists.

\textstyle\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx = -\tfrac1x\Big|_1^b = 1 - \tfrac1b @@ negative-powers backwards; area-is-a-change
\textstyle\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty}\big(1 - \tfrac1b\big) = 1 @@ 10f: 1/b → 0 as b → ∞
\textstyle\int_c^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx = 2x^{1/2}\Big|_c^1 = 2 - 2\sqrt{c} @@ fraction-power for slopes backwards; area-is-a-change
\textstyle\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx = \lim_{c \to 0^+}(2 - 2\sqrt c) = 2 @@ √ is continuous at 0 from the right (10e, 11a)

So a region that never ends can have area 1, and a region that climbs past every height can have area 2. 16c.4.2's 1 was this second kind.

16i2The powers

Stands on 16i1, 16f (the table, the missing entry) and 15i (geometric sums).

Out-to-forever technique: for rational p, ∫₁^∞ x⁻ᵖ dx converges when p > 1, to 1/(p − 1), and diverges when p ≤ 1.

p \ne 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = \tfrac{b^{1-p} - 1}{1 - p} @@ the table (16f); area-is-a-change
p \gt 1:\ \ b^{1-p} = \tfrac{1}{b^{p-1}} \to 0,\ \ \text{limit } \tfrac{1}{p - 1} @@ 10f: a positive power of b passes every bound, so its reciprocal → 0
p \lt 1:\ \ b^{1-p} \text{ passes every bound} @@ the same fact; the integral passes every bound: it diverges
p = 1:\ \ \text{on } [k, k + 1],\ \tfrac1x \ge \tfrac{1}{k+1} @@ x ≤ k + 1 and order-and-arithmetic-agree
\textstyle\int_1^{n+1} \tfrac{dx}{x} \ge \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{n+1} @@ smaller-function-smaller-area on each piece; interval-cut-in-two to add them
\tfrac11 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{2^m} \ge 1 + \tfrac{m}{2} @@ grouping by doubling: induction on m (4b); the 2ᵐ terms from 1/(2ᵐ + 1) to 1/2ᵐ⁺¹ are each at least 1/2ᵐ⁺¹
\text{so } \textstyle\int_1^{b} \tfrac{dx}{x} \text{ passes every bound} \quad \blacksquare @@ the area function rises, since 1/t > 0 (split-in-any-order, smaller-function-smaller-area); take n + 1 = 2ᵐ, m large

In-to-0 technique: for rational p, ∫₀¹ x⁻ᵖ dx converges when p < 1, to 1/(1 − p), and diverges when p ≥ 1.

p \ne 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_c^1 x^{-p}\,dx = \tfrac{1 - c^{1-p}}{1 - p} @@ the table; area-is-a-change
p \lt 1:\ c^{1-p} \to 0;\ \ p \gt 1:\ c^{1-p} = \tfrac{1}{c^{p-1}} \text{ passes every bound} @@ powers near 0 (10e)
p = 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_c^1 \tfrac{dx}{x} = \int_1^{1/c} \tfrac{dt}{t} @@ substitution for areas with t = 1/x: dt = −dx/x², so dx/x = −dt/t, and the ends 1/c and 1 swap back
\text{which passes every bound as } c \to 0^+ \quad \blacksquare @@ out-to-forever's case p = 1, with b = 1/c

So x⁻¹ sits exactly on the line: too much area out to forever, too much in to 0. 16f's missing entry is the same function.

16i3Comparison

Stands on 16i2, 16a (the area function) and 8c (completeness).

Comparison technique for areas: if 0 ≤ f(x) ≤ g(x) for x ≥ u, both integrable on every [u, b], and ∫ᵤ^∞ g converges, then ∫ᵤ^∞ f converges, and is at most ∫ᵤ^∞ g.

F(b) = \textstyle\int_a^b f,\ \ G(b) = \int_a^b g @@ area functions from a (16a)
F \text{ and } G \text{ are increasing};\ \ F(b) \le G(b) \le \textstyle\int_a^\infty g @@ f, g ≥ 0, so each area function rises (split-in-any-order); smaller-function-smaller-area; G increases to its limit
L = \sup\{F(b) : b \ge a\} \text{ exists} @@ no-holes (8c): the values of F are bounded above
\text{fix } \varepsilon;\ \text{some } b_0:\ F(b_0) \gt L - \varepsilon @@ L − ε is not an upper bound
b \gt b_0 \Rightarrow L - \varepsilon \lt F(b_0) \le F(b) \le L @@ F increasing; L an upper bound
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_a^\infty f = L \le \int_a^\infty g} \quad \blacksquare @@ 10f's line with threshold b₀; L ≤ ∫ₐ^∞ g since that is an upper bound too
  
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^3 + 1}} \le x^{-3/2} \text{ for } x \ge 1 @@ x³ + 1 ≥ x³; √ and 1/ reverse the order (8d)
\textstyle\int_1^\infty \tfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x^3 + 1}} \text{ converges, to at most } 2 @@ comparison for areas with out-to-forever, p = 3/2
\tfrac{1}{x^2 + 1} \le \tfrac{1}{x^2} \text{ for } x \ge 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_1^\infty \tfrac{dx}{x^2 + 1} \le 1 @@ comparison for areas with out-to-forever, p = 2; its value is not in the table

16i.4.4seam In 1641 a solid was found that stretches out forever along the graph of 1/x and yet has a finite size. Philosophers had argued, since ancient Athens, that an endless thing could not be finished. One of them called the result madness. The region under the given graph is the flat version: endless, with area 1.
Is that region a finished thing with an area, or only a limit of areas that are each finished?
Does the difference matter, if the number is the same?

Who said it first

Hobbes wrote that to take Torricelli’s result in its plain sense, a solid endlessly long and yet of finite size, a man need not be a geometer or a logician; he would need to be mad.

Hobbes, in his quarrel with Wallis; paraphrase

The solid of 1641 is Evangelista Torricelli’s, and its accepted name is Gabriel’s horn, or Torricelli’s trumpet. The philosophers’ doctrine goes back to Aristotle. The grouping of the sums 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … by doubling is Nicole Oresme’s.
