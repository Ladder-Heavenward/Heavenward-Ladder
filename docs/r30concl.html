30·∎ · conclusion

Size and Chance Add Over Countably Many Pieces

The sized sets keep exact books over countably many pieces, and a chance is such a size with total one. From that come totals by slicing heights, limits through totals, given and turned-around chances, averages that settle, counts stored in one function, the bell, and fair games that no system beats.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 30k no system beats a fair game is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 30 asked what a chance is, and what can be proved once it is said. A chance is a statement that names a size with total one, so averages are totals, and the techniques of size give the laws of averages, sums and fair games. The price is that some sets have no size at all, and the ladder leaves them out rather than let its books fail.

30a built outer size and found a set without a size. 30b picked out the sets that have sizes, and 30c totalled functions by slicing their heights. 30d passed limits through totals, and 30e took totals on the plane in either order. 30f made chance a size, 30g made averages totals, and 30h showed averages settle. 30i stored a count in one function, 30j found the bell, and 30k showed no system beats a fair game. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

The sets of reals that pass the split test are closed under leftovers and countable unions, contain every interval, and on them size adds over countably many separate pieces. If choice is true, not every set passes. A chance space is such a size with total one.

Every sized f ≥ 0 has a total, which agrees with rung fifteen’s area for continuous f. Rising lists, and lists fenced by one function with a finite total, pass their limits through the total, and a total on the plane can be taken in either order when f ≥ 0 or ∫|f| is finite.

Given chances, independence, the whole chance and the turned-around chance follow from the rules of size. Averages add, and spreads of independent parts add. The average of n independent copies strays ε from μ with chance at most σ²/(nε²), and with chance 1 it settles at μ. A whole-number count is fixed by its chance function, and independent counts multiply theirs. The rescaled sum of independent copies has an average-function that heads to the bell’s. In a fair game, a bounded stopping rule keeps the average fortune, and a fair walk from k reaches N before 0 with chance k/N.

So the rung’s claim holds: size and chance add over countably many pieces, and from that one demand come the laws of averages, sums and fair games. One step is held in trust, on 30j: that matching average-functions give matching limits.
