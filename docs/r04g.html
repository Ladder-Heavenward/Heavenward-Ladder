4g · proof

Multiplying and powers

Adding was the next-one gesture, repeated. Multiplying is adding, repeated, and raising to a power is multiplying, repeated. Each is defined the way 4f defined adding, by a start and a step, with 4d's technique doing the work. This step says exactly how that technique applies when the step has a number built into it. Then the techniques, each by induction. Multiplying spreads over adding. The order and the brackets of a product do not matter. One changes nothing. And the two rules for powers turn adding and multiplying of the counts into multiplying and powering of the results. Then the whole ladder of operations at once, with two times two and two to the power two worked one move per line. At the end you can do arithmetic.

Multiplying a number by a count: start at 0, and add the number once for each step up to the count. Raising a number to a power: start at 1, and multiply by the number once for each step up to the power. Each operation starts at the number that does nothing to the one below it.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 4d (the recursion technique, the rule-makes-a-function technique, the low mark as a letter) and 4f (+ and its techniques plus-zero, plus-a-next, sums-are-numbers, plus-one, zero-plus, next-on-the-left for plus, turn-about for plus, regroup for plus, cancel for plus). 4h proves what order does to sums and products, and cancels products; 4i settles which brackets may be left out; 4j divides; 4k counts with products and powers.

4g1Multiplying, in words

Stands on 4f (adding) and 4d1 (defining by repeating).

Two times three is two, added again and again: start from nothing, add two, add two, add two.

As a start and a step: k times 0 is 0; k times the next one after b is (k times b) with k added. The start is 0. The step is “add k”, the rule y ↦ y + k.

Example: 2 times 3. Start at 0. Add 2 for the first step: 2. Add 2 again: 4. Add 2 a third time: 6.

4g2A step with a number in it

Stands on 4g1, 4d8 (the recursion technique), 4d5 (rule-makes-a-function) and 4f3 (sums-are-numbers).

Here is the only new thing about multiplying. Recursion takes a set, a start and a step, and the step must be a function from the set to itself. “Add k” has a number, k, built into it, so it is a different step for each k.

That is no obstacle. Plan: fix k, show “add k” is a function, then use recursion once for each k.

Fix a number k. The rule y ↦ y + k names a number for every number y (the sums-are-numbers technique). So 4d's rule-makes-a-function technique makes it a function:

Example: g₂ is {(0, 2), (1, 3), (2, 4), …}, and g₂(5) = 7.

Now recursion applies, with A = ℕ, start 0 and step gk, and gives exactly one function. Do it again for another k and recursion applies again, with another step. The low mark keeps track of which number each function goes with. The recursion technique's “exactly one”, used once for each k, makes the name honest for every k at once.

4g4Powers

Stands on 4g3 (times, products-are-numbers) and 4d8 (the recursion technique).

Two to the power three is two, multiplied in again and again: start from one, multiply by two, by two, by two.

As a start and a step: k to the power 0 is 1; k to the power of the next one after b is (k to the power b) times k.

For a fixed k the step is y ↦ y · k, a function from ℕ to ℕ by products-are-numbers and rule-makes-a-function. Recursion, with start 1, gives exactly one function.

From here on, for numbers a and b, (with b written small and high after a) names , where is the one function with and for every number n. Read it “a to the power b”, or “a to the b”.

The high mark is not 3d's low mark: is a number made from two numbers, and the high place holds exactly what is written in it.

In a power kb, k is called the base and b the exponent.

Example: in 2³, 2 is the base and 3 the exponent. Start at 1, multiply by 2 three times: 2, 4, 8.

So kb · c is (kb) · c, and kb + c is k with b + c in the high place.

2f said the word “power” in “power set” was only a name, and that a later rung would give it work. This is the work, and 4k shows why the two share the word. The line's techniques:

  • Power-zero technique: any number to the power 0 is 1. In marks: k0 = 1.
  • Power-of-a-next technique: k to the power of the next one after b is k to the power b, times k. In marks: kS(b) = kb · k.
  • Powers-are-numbers technique: a number to the power of a number is a number.

4g5The ladder of operations

Stands on 4f (adding), 4g3 (times) and 4g4 (powers).

Put the four side by side. Each is the one below it, repeated, starting from somewhere.

operationrepeatsstartstep
the next one(the foot of the ladder)none
addingthe next one
multiplyingadding k
powersmultiplying by k

Look down the start column. Adding starts at k itself, since no step has been taken. Multiplying starts at 0, and 0 is the number that does nothing when added (plus-zero, zero-plus). Powers start at 1, and 1 is the number that does nothing when multiplied (the one-changes-nothing technique, in 4g7).

Each operation starts at the do-nothing of the operation it repeats.

That is the whole reason k0 is 1 rather than 0. Zero multiplications leave you where multiplying starts, as zero additions leave you where adding starts.

The definition settles 00 too: it is 1, by power-zero, like every k0. Some books leave 00 without a value; on this ladder the line for powers gives it one.

4g7Two small techniques

Stands on 4g3 (times-zero, times-a-next), 4f (plus-zero, zero-plus, plus-one) and 4c (induction).

Each technique of multiplying is an induction on the right-hand letter, as 4f's were, and each step leans on techniques already proved. First the two small ones.

Zero-times technique: 0 times any number is 0. In marks: 0 · k = 0.

Plan: induct on k. Base: 0 · 0 = 0 by times-zero. Step: 0 · S(k) = (0 · k) + 0 by times-a-next, = 0 · k by plus-zero, = 0 by the hypothesis.

One-changes-nothing technique: multiplying by 1, on either side, changes nothing. In marks: k · 1 = k and 1 · k = k. You know this too: one bag of five apples is five apples.

The first half needs no induction: k · 1 = k · S(0) = (k · 0) + k = 0 + k = k, by times-a-next, times-zero and zero-plus.

The second half does. Base: 1 · 0 = 0 by times-zero. Step: 1 · S(k) = (1 · k) + 1 = k + 1 = S(k), by times-a-next, the hypothesis and plus-one.

Example: 0 · 3 = 0 and 1 · 3 = 3.

4g8Times spreads over plus

Stands on 4g3, 4f (plus-a-next, regroup for plus) and 4c (induction).

Spread technique for times over plus: a number times a sum is the sum of the two products. In marks: k · (b + c) = (k · b) + (k · c). You already do this. Three friends each get a pencil and a rubber: that is three pencils and three rubbers.

Example: 2 · (1 + 2) = 2 · 3 = 6, and (2 · 1) + (2 · 2) = 2 + 4 = 6.

Multiplying spreads over adding, and it is the technique that ties the two rungs together.

Plan: keep k and b fixed and induct on c.

Stands on 4g3, 4f (regroup, plus-a-next, turn-about for plus) and 4c (induction).

The order of a product needs a partner of times-a-next first, as turn-about for plus needed next-on-the-left for plus.

Next-on-the-left technique for times: the next one after k, times b, is k times b, plus b. In marks: S(k) · b = (k · b) + b.

4g11The order of a product

Stands on 4g10 (next-on-the-left for times), 4g7 (zero-times) and 4c (induction).

And then the order. Turn-about technique for times: a product is the same in either order. In marks: k · b = b · k.

Example: 2 · 3 = 6 and 3 · 2 = 6.

Plan: induct on b. Base: k · 0 = 0 = 0 · k, by times-zero and zero-times. Step: k · S(b) = (k · b) + k by times-a-next, = (b · k) + k by the hypothesis, = S(b) · k by next-on-the-left for times turned about.

With turn-about for times, spread for times over plus works from the other side too: (k + b) · c = (k · c) + (b · c). The accepted names follow those for adding, and are in the basement.

4g12Powers add their counts

Stands on 4g4 (powers), 4g7 (one-changes-nothing), 4g9 (regroup for times) and 4c (induction).

Power-one technique: a number to the power 1 is itself. In marks: k1 = k.

Its proof is one chain: k1 = kS(0) = k0 · k = 1 · k = k, by power-of-a-next, power-zero and one-changes-nothing.

Then the two rules that make powers worth having. Powers-add-their-counts technique: multiplying by k, b times and then c more times, is multiplying by k, (b + c) times. In marks: kb + c = kb · kc.

Example: 21 + 2 = 2³ = 8, and 2¹ · 2² = 2 · 4 = 8.

Plan: keep k and b fixed and induct on c.

4g13A power of a power

Stands on 4g12 (powers-add-their-counts), 4g3 (times-a-next) and 4c (induction).

Power-of-a-power technique: doing “b times” c times over is doing it b · c times. In marks: (kb)c = kb · c.

Example: (2¹)² = 2² = 4, and 21 · 2 = 2² = 4.

Plan: induct on c. The step uses powers-add-their-counts, turned about.

(a^b)^0 = 1 = a^0 = a^{b \cdot 0} @@ power-zero; power-zero turned about; times-zero in the high place, turned about: the base
(a^b)^{S(c)} = (a^b)^c \cdot a^b @@ the power-of-a-next technique, with a^b as the number being repeated
\phantom{(a^b)^{S(c)}} = \htmlClass{keep}{a^{b \cdot c}} \cdot a^b @@ the hypothesis, inside
\phantom{(a^b)^{S(c)}} = a^{(b \cdot c) + b} @@ the powers-add-their-counts technique, turned about
\phantom{(a^b)^{S(c)}} = a^{\htmlClass{new}{b \cdot S(c)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the times-a-next technique in the high place, turned about; the induction technique

Read the rules back for their pattern. In powers-add-their-counts the counts are added while the results are multiplied. In power-of-a-power the counts are multiplied while the results are powered. The count always does the arithmetic of the rung below the operation acting on the results.

And the spreading techniques follow the same ladder. Multiplying spreads over adding (spread for times over plus). Powers spread over multiplying: (k · b)c = kc · bc, this step's question. And powers do not spread over adding, which is two rungs down.

4g15Running it backwards

Stands on 4g1 to 4g13.

Given a product or a power, unfold it. Unpack the right-hand name, use the step, repeat until the start applies, and cite the rung below for the arithmetic it leaves.

Given a technique to prove, find the earlier techniques its step needs and prove those first. Turn-about for times needed next-on-the-left for times. Next-on-the-left for times needed regroup for plus, plus-a-next and turn-about for plus.

Given a number written as a power, read the rules backwards: 4 = 2² = 21 + 1 = 2¹ · 2¹ by powers-add-their-counts, and (2¹)² = 21 · 2 = 2² by power-of-a-power.

A sameness between two different-looking arrangements of the same factors is always regroup for times and turn-about for times, one move per line. Found upward; written downward.

4g16Where you stand

Stands on 4b to 4g.

You can add, multiply and raise to powers, and every move you make with them has a reason you could write in the column beside it. The reason is a defining line, or a technique you proved by induction from defining lines. Those stand on 4d's technique, which stands on the counting numbers of 4b, which stand on an axiom and the tools of rung 2, down to the ground floor. You know which moves are techniques (turning about, regrouping and cancelling for adding; turning about, regrouping and spreading for multiplying; the two rules for powers) and which tempting moves are not. What is left for the rung is order, which is 4h, and the hands-on craft of the rung's second part.

Now you're a person who can do arithmetic.

Who said it first

The writer is Giuseppe Peano; the schoolteacher is Hermann Grassmann.

Peano's arithmetic gave multiplying the same pair of lines as this step, k times nothing and k times the next one, right after adding. Grassmann had already done the same, and proved the techniques of multiplying from them by induction, as here.

Peano, Arithmetices principia (1889); Grassmann, Lehrbuch der Arithmetik für höhere Lehranstalten (1861)

Accepted names

Turn-about for times is the commutative law of multiplication; regroup for times, the associative law; spread for times over plus, the distributive law, since multiplying distributes over adding. Powers-add-their-counts and power-of-a-power are the laws of exponents.
