25a · proof

The Pairs Looked Back On

You have met these numbers three times already. First as pairs with a strange rule for multiplying, chosen so that one pair squares to minus one. Then as turns of the plane, where a quarter turn done twice is a half turn. Then as moves that turn and stretch at once. This step puts the three pictures side by side and finds they are one system. The old number line sits inside it, unchanged. And the flip across the line keeps every sum and every product, which is the fact the rest of this rung leans on.

The pairs, the turns and the moves are one number system, and the line sits inside it. Flipping across the line keeps sums and products.

25a1One System, Three Pictures

9·cb8 built ℂ as pairs (a, b), with (a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c, b + d) and (a, b) · (c, d) = (ac − bd, ad + bc), and showed they obey the nine techniques of adding and multiplying. 18f2 built the quarter turn i = (0, 1). 20·cb8 read each pair as the move that multiplies by it. 21·cb20 wrote each pair other than 0 as r(cos θ + i sin θ), and found that multiplying multiplies lengths and adds turns.

From here on, for z = a + bi in ℂ, a is the floor part of z and b its upright part. The size of z is |z| = √(a² + b²), its distance from 0, and its flip is z̄ = a − bi. Accepted names: the real part, the imaginary part, the absolute value, the conjugate.

(0,1)\cdot(0,1) = (-1,0) @@ the pair product (9·cb8.2)
i \cdot i = \text{a quarter turn, twice} = \text{the half turn} @@ turns multiply as pairs (18f2)
z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta),\ w = s(\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi) @@ out and round (21·cb20.1)
\htmlClass{new}{zw = rs\,(\cos(\theta+\varphi) + i\sin(\theta+\varphi))} \quad \blacksquare @@ the multiply-and-add technique (21·cb20.2)

So one rule, the pair product, is at once the algebra of 9·cb8, the turning of 18f and the stretching of 21·cb20. Each picture proves things the others find hard. This rung uses all three.

25a2The Line Inside

Floor-inside technique: sending each real a to the pair (x, 0) keeps sums, products, 0 and 1, and sends different reals to different pairs. So ℝ sits inside ℂ, and every fact about the reals is a fact about the floor.

(x,0) + (y,0) = (x+y,\ 0) @@ the pair sum
(x,0)\cdot(y,0) = (xy - 0\cdot 0,\ x\cdot 0 + 0\cdot y) = (xy,\ 0) @@ the pair product; anything times zero (5e)
(0,0) \text{ and } (1,0) \text{ are the } 0 \text{ and } 1 \text{ of } \mathbb{C} @@ (1,0)(c,d) = (c,d) by the pair product
(x,0) = (y,0) \Rightarrow x = y @@ equal pairs have equal entries (2h)
\htmlClass{new}{x \mapsto (x,0) \text{ keeps } +,\ \cdot,\ 0,\ 1, \text{ and is one-to-one}} \quad \blacksquare

From here on a real x and the pair (x, 0) are the same number, and a + bi means (a, 0) + (b, 0)(0, 1) = (a, b). On the floor, the size |x| is 8h’s size, since √(x²) = |x| by the size-squared technique.

25a3The Flip Keeps Arithmetic

Flip technique: the flip of a sum is the sum of the flips, and the flip of a product is the product of the flips; z is on the floor exactly when z̄ = z; and z z̄ = |z|².

\overline{(a+bi)+(c+di)} = (a+c) - (b+d)i = (a-bi)+(c-di) @@ the pair sum, then regroup
\overline{(a+bi)(c+di)} = (ac-bd) - (ad+bc)i @@ the pair product, flipped
(a-bi)(c-di) = (ac - bd) + (-ad - bc)i = (ac-bd) - (ad+bc)i @@ the pair product; two negatives (5e)
\overline{z} = z \iff -b = b \iff b = 0 @@ equal pairs have equal parts
z\,\overline{z} = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 @@ the bi terms cancel (20·cb8.4)
\htmlClass{new}{\overline{z+w} = \overline{z}+\overline{w},\ \ \overline{zw} = \overline{z}\,\overline{w},\ \ z\overline{z} = |z|^2} \quad \blacksquare

Two more follow at once. Flipping twice gives z back. And z + z̄ = 2a, twice the floor part, which is a real number. In the picture, the flip is the mirror in the floor. A mirror that keeps sums and products is rare. It is why, on 25e, the roots of a polynomial with real numbers in front come in mirrored pairs.

25a.4.5seamFor three hundred years these numbers were called false, impossible, imaginary. An algebraist of the 1500s called work with them as subtle as it is useless. A philosopher of the 1600s named them imaginary. A great calculator of the 1700s multiplied their square roots wrongly in print. Then, in 1837, they were rebuilt as pairs of reals, and nothing imaginary was left in them.
25a.4.3 adds a twist. Every true line about sums and products of i stays true with −i in its place. No fact of arithmetic tells them apart.
If nothing can tell which square root of −1 is i, what is i?

The accepted names

ℂ is the field of complex numbers. The floor part and upright part are the real and imaginary parts, Re z and Im z; the size is the absolute value |z|; the flip is the complex conjugate. The floor-inside technique says the map x ↦ (x, 0) is an embedding of fields. The picture of ℂ as a plane is the complex plane, or Argand diagram.

Who said it first

Gerolamo Cardano met square roots of negatives in 1545, and Rafael Bombelli calculated with them in 1572. Caspar Wessel (1799), Jean-Robert Argand (1806) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1831) drew them as points of a plane. William Rowan Hamilton defined them as pairs of reals in 1837, which is the definition 9·cb8 used.

In 25a.4.5, the algebraist is Cardano (Ars Magna, 1545, chapter 37), the philosopher Descartes (La Géométrie, 1637), the calculator Leonhard Euler, who wrote √−1 · √−4 = √4 = 2 in his Algebra (1770), and the pairs are Hamilton’s. That nothing in the arithmetic of ℂ tells i from −i is the starting point of Galois theory, much higher up the ladder.
