What Is the Cheapest Way to Move a Pile of Sand?

What is the cheapest way to move a pile of sand? A heap of earth dug from one place must fill holes in another, and every barrow load costs its weight times the distance it goes. In 1781 a French geometer asked for the cheapest way to send each grain to a place, and could not answer: grains cannot be split, and his cost, the plain distance, behaves badly. In 1942 a Soviet mathematician changed the question. Let each piece of the pile be shared among the holes, and ask for the cheapest sharing; then the question is the shipping problem of 42·logi with endlessly many ports, it always has an answer, and it has a twin, a price at every place, that gives the same total. On a line the answer is to fill the holes in order, left to left. For the cost that squares the distance, the cheapest way in any dimension is to move each grain along the uphill vector of a bowl-shaped function, proved in 1991. The least cost is a distance between two piles of sand, and it measures them as nothing else does. Since 2006 it has been used to say what curvature means for spaces too rough to have slopes, and since 2017 to train machines that draw pictures. How smooth the cheapest way is, and how to find it fast for piles of a million grains in many dimensions, are open.

Piles μ and holes ν: a plan is a sharing π with margins μ and ν, and its cost is Σ c(x, y)π(x, y). Prices φ, ψ with φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y) give Σφμ + Σψν ≤ cost, with equality at the best. On a line, with c a bending-up function of x − y, the best plan matches in order. For |x − y|² the best plan keeps Σ x · T(x) largest, and with smooth piles T is the uphill vector of a bowl. On a line the least cost with c = |x − y| is ∫|F − G|. Between two piles one place apart, it is 1 at any distance θ ≠ 0 for the average-surprise gap, and θ for the sand. This π (little pi) is not the round number.

inf52.1Grains or Shares

42·logi.3 shipped 700 containers from two ports to three cities at least cost. Sand is the same question with a port at every point. Write μ for the pile, the amount of sand on each set of places, and ν for the holes, the amount each set of places needs, with equal totals.

The 1781 question asks for a map T sending each grain at x to one place T(x), so that the sand landing in each set is what the holes need. The 1942 question asks for a plan: an amount π(x, y) of sand from x to y for every pair, a size on the plane of pairs as in 30e1, whose total over y is μ at x and whose total over x is ν at y. Every map gives a plan; a plan may split a grain’s place among several holes.

Shared-plan technique: a plan always exists, π(x, y) = μ(x)ν(y)/m with m the total; a map may not. The totals of μ(x)ν(y)/m over y are μ(x)m/m = μ(x), and over x they are ν(y).

\mu:\ 2 \text{ at } 0;\ \ \nu:\ 1 \text{ at } -1,\ 1 \text{ at } 1 @@ one pile, two holes
\text{a map sends all of } 0 \text{ to one place} @@ it fills one hole twice and the other not at all
\pi(0, -1) = \pi(0, 1) = 1,\ \ \text{cost } 1 + 1 = 2 @@ split, and it works
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no map; the best plan costs } 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shared plan is the best here

So plans are the right objects: they always exist, and for whole pile sizes at finitely many points the best is found by 42·logi.3’s program. The old question survives in a sharper form: when is the best plan a map? When the pile is spread smoothly, with no lump at a point, the answer is often yes, and inf52.4 finds when.

A shared plan is one of ∞13.1’s chance boxes: from each place of the first pile, a chance rule saying where its sand goes, and the box run after the first pile gives the second. Plans run in a row by running the boxes in a row, so the least cost between piles obeys ∞9.1’s detour rule and is a distance in its sense. The plan, here in the plain sense, is the box; which grain goes where, and the order of moving, are let go.

inf52.2A Price at Every Place

42·logi.4 priced each port and city so that the price gap on every route was at most its cost. The same holds for sand with any cost c(x, y) and amounts that need not be whole: a price φ(x) on sand at x and ψ(y) on a hole at y, with φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y) on every pair.

Sand-price technique: for every plan π and every such prices, Σ φμ + Σ ψν ≤ Σ cπ. A plan and prices that give equal totals are both best, and that happens exactly when the plan uses only pairs where φ(x) + ψ(y) = c(x, y). For finitely many places the best plan and best prices always give equal totals, by 42g4’s equal-prices technique.

\textstyle\sum_x \varphi(x)\mu(x) + \sum_y \psi(y)\nu(y) = \sum_{x,y} (\varphi(x) + \psi(y))\,\pi(x, y) @@ the plan’s totals are μ and ν
\le \textstyle\sum_{x,y} c(x, y)\,\pi(x, y) @@ φ + ψ ≤ c, and π ≥ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{equal} \iff \pi > 0 \text{ only where } \varphi + \psi = c} \quad \blacksquare @@ each term of the gap is (c − φ − ψ)π ≥ 0
  
\mu:\ 2 \text{ at } 0,\ 1 \text{ at } 1,\ 1 \text{ at } 3;\ \ \nu:\ 2 \text{ at } 2,\ 2 \text{ at } 4 @@ c = (x − y)²
\pi:\ 0 \to 2:\ 2;\ \ 1 \to 4:\ 1;\ \ 3 \to 4:\ 1;\ \ \text{cost } 8 + 9 + 1 = 18 @@ a plan
\varphi = 4,\ 1,\ -7;\ \ \psi = 0,\ 8:\ \ 4 + 8 \le 16,\ 1 + 0 \le 1,\ -7 + 0 \le 9 @@ every pair checked; equal on the three in use
\htmlClass{new}{8 + 1 - 7 + 0 + 16 = 18} \quad \blacksquare @@ prices meet cost: both are best

With endlessly many places the equal totals still hold for any continuous cost on a closed and bounded region, but the proof needs tools past this page: held in trust. The prices are the second way to read the problem. A haulier who charges φ(x) to take sand away from x and ψ(y) to deliver it to y, never more than the cost of doing it oneself, can charge at most the least cost, and the best haulier charges exactly that.

inf52.3On a Line, Fill in Order

Put the piles and holes on a line, n grains of equal size each, at x₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ xₙ and y₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ yₙ, and let the cost be h(x − y) for a function h that bends up (14g1), such as (x − y)² or |x − y|. A matching crosses when x < x′ but x goes to a y larger than the one x′ goes to.

Uncrossing technique: if x₁ < x₂, y₁ < y₂ and h bends up, then h(x₁ − y₁) + h(x₂ − y₂) ≤ h(x₁ − y₂) + h(x₂ − y₁). So matching in order, x₁ to y₁ up to xₙ to yₙ, is a cheapest matching.

d_1 = x_1 - y_2,\ d_2 = x_2 - y_1,\ e_1 = x_1 - y_1,\ e_2 = x_2 - y_2 @@ crossed and uncrossed gaps
d_1 < e_1,\ e_2 < d_2,\ \ e_1 + e_2 = d_1 + d_2 @@ the uncrossed gaps lie between, with the same sum
e_1 = s\,d_1 + (1 - s)\,d_2,\ \ e_2 = (1 - s)\,d_1 + s\,d_2,\ \ 0 \le s \le 1 @@ the sum fixes e₂ once e₁ is placed
h(e_1) + h(e_2) \le s h(d_1) + (1 - s)h(d_2) + (1 - s)h(d_1) + s h(d_2) @@ each below its chord (14g1)
\htmlClass{new}{h(e_1) + h(e_2) \le h(d_1) + h(d_2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ uncrossing never costs more
  
x = 0, 1, 3;\ \ y = 2, 4, 5;\ \ c = (x - y)^2 @@ 
\text{in order: } 4 + 9 + 4 = 17 @@ the best of all six matchings
0 \to 5,\ 1 \to 2,\ 3 \to 4:\ 25 + 1 + 1 = 27 @@ crossed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{uncross } 0 \to 5,\ 1 \to 2:\ \ 0 \to 2,\ 1 \to 5:\ 4 + 16 + 1 = 21} \quad \blacksquare @@ lower, and one more uncrossing gives 17

Each uncrossing removes at least one crossed pair, so after finitely many the matching is in order, and the cost never rose. For sand with no lumps the same holds with the grains made fine: the cheapest plan sends the place below which a share s of the pile lies to the place below which a share s of the holes lies. A pile sorted is a pile solved. In two dimensions there is no order to follow, and the next footing finds what replaces it.

inf52.4The Bowl Behind the Map

For the squared distance, |x − y|² = |x|² − 2x · y + |y|², and the sums of |x|² over the pile and |y|² over the holes do not depend on the plan. So the cheapest plan is the one that makes the total of x · y largest.

Cycle-swap technique: for squared distance, a matching T of n grains to n holes is cheapest exactly when Σ x · T(x) is largest; so no cycle of reassignments, x₁ to T(x₂), x₂ to T(x₃), …, x_k to T(x₁), raises Σ x · T(x).

x_1 = (0, 0),\ x_2 = (1, 0);\ \ y_1 = (0, 1),\ y_2 = (2, 1) @@ 
x_1 \to y_1,\ x_2 \to y_2:\ \ \text{cost } 1 + 2 = 3,\ \ \textstyle\sum x \cdot y = 0 + 2 = 2 @@ 
x_1 \to y_2,\ x_2 \to y_1:\ \ \text{cost } 5 + 2 = 7,\ \ \textstyle\sum x \cdot y = 0 + 0 = 0 @@ cost = 7 − 2 Σ x · y each time
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the larger total of } x \cdot y \text{ is the cheaper plan}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cycle-swap technique

On a line, matching in order makes T rising. A rising T is the slope of a function that bends up: its area function F(x) = ∫ T, by 16b, has slope T, and a rising slope is the bends-up technique. That function is a bowl, and T is its uphill vector, in the sense of 28g1. In 1991 this became the general rule.

The bowl rule, stated: if the pile has a density in the plane or in space and the cost is the squared distance, there is exactly one cheapest plan; it is a map, and the map is the uphill vector ∇u of a function u that bends up along every line. Held in trust: the proof joins the sand-price technique with the cycle-swap technique for endlessly many grains. Where u is smooth, sending μ’s density f to ν’s density g says f(x) = g(∇u(x)) times the factor by which ∇u stretches area, an equation for u whose solutions may have corners.

inf52.5A Distance Between Piles

The least cost between two piles of equal total is a number, and with cost |x − y| it behaves as a distance: zero only for the same pile, the same either way, and never more than the cost through a third pile (held in trust). On a line it can be read off the two running totals. Write F(t) for the share of the pile at or left of t and G(t) for the holes.

Gap-area technique: for n equal grains at x₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ xₙ and n equal holes at y₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ yₙ, the least cost with c = |x − y|, divided by n, equals the area ∫|F(t) − G(t)| dt between the running totals.

|x - y| = \textstyle\int \big|\mathbf 1[x \le t] - \mathbf 1[y \le t]\big|\,dt @@ the integrand is 1 exactly for t between x and y
\textstyle\sum_i |x_i - y_i| = \int \sum_i \big|\mathbf 1[x_i \le t] - \mathbf 1[y_i \le t]\big|\,dt @@ the sum of integrals
\text{at each } t:\ \{i : x_i \le t\} = \{1, \ldots, nF(t)\},\ \ \{i : y_i \le t\} = \{1, \ldots, nG(t)\} @@ both sorted, so both are first stretches
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i |x_i - y_i| = \int |F(t) - G(t)|\,dt} \quad \blacksquare @@ two first stretches differ in |nF − nG| places; in order is cheapest (inf52.3)
  
\tfrac13(|0 - 2| + |1 - 4| + |3 - 5|) = \tfrac73 @@ in order
|F - G| = \tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac13 \text{ on } [0,1), [1,2), [2,3), [3,4), [4,5) @@ the running totals
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac13 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac73} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same

The distance sees where the sand is, not only how much is at each point. Two piles of one grain, at 0 and at θ, are θ apart in sand, however small θ is; measured place by place, comparing amounts at each point, they are as different as two piles can be until θ is exactly 0. That is why this distance is the one to use when piles are close but not on top of each other, as two photographs of the same face are.

inf52.6Curvature From Moving Sand

Move sand the cheapest way and stop halfway: each grain travels part of the way along its straight path. On a line, moving an even pile on [0, 1] to an even pile on [2, 4] in order, the grain at x goes to 2 + 2x, and at time t it sits at (1 − t)x + t(2 + 2x). So at time t the sand is spread evenly over a stretch of length (1 − t) · 1 + t · 2.

Log-length technique: if sand moving in order spreads evenly over a length (1 − t)L₀ + tL₁ at time t, then log of that length is at least (1 − t) log L₀ + t log L₁. The log bends down (slope 1/x falling, 14g1), so it lies above its chord.

L_0 = 1,\ L_1 = 2,\ t = \tfrac12:\ \ \log 1.5 = 0.405 @@ how spread the sand is halfway
\tfrac12 \log 1 + \tfrac12 \log 2 = 0.347 @@ the chord
\text{on a ball: paths starting } 0.1 \text{ apart on the equator are } 0.1\cos 60° = 0.05 \text{ apart at } 60° @@ straightest paths meet as they go
\htmlClass{new}{0.405 \ge 0.347} \quad \blacksquare @@ flat space: spread is above the chord

On a curved surface the straightest paths (29g) bend toward each other where the surface bends like a ball, K > 0 in 29e3, and apart on a saddle. So sand moved across a ball is more spread out in the middle than on a flat sheet, and across a saddle less. Between 2006 and 2009 this was turned round into a definition: a space has curvature at least 0, in the averaged sense that counts every direction, when the spread of sand, in an exact sense, lies above its chord along every cheapest move. The definition needs no slopes, only distances and amounts, so it applies to spaces with corners and to limits of shapes where the old definition fails. Held in trust: that it agrees with the old definition for smooth spaces.

inf52.7Training Machines That Draw

A machine that draws faces turns random numbers into pictures. Training compares the pile of its pictures with the pile of real photographs and moves the settings to bring the piles closer. Everything depends on how close is measured. One common measure uses 30comp1’s average surprise H: for piles P and Q, with M the half-and-half mix, the surprise gap is H(M) − ½H(P) − ½H(Q).

Flat-surprise technique: for a pile P of one grain at 0 and Q of one grain at θ, the surprise gap is 1 bit for every θ ≠ 0 and 0 for θ = 0; the sand distance is |θ|. P and Q are sure things, so H(P) = H(Q) = 0. For θ ≠ 0, M puts ½ at each of two places, so H(M) = log₂ 2 = 1.

\theta = 0.001:\ \ \text{gap } 1 \text{ bit},\ \ \text{sand } 0.001 @@ 
\theta = 1000:\ \ \text{gap } 1 \text{ bit},\ \ \text{sand } 1000 @@ the gap cannot tell near from far
\text{the slope of the gap in } \theta \text{ is } 0 @@ no hint which way to move
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the slope of the sand distance is } \pm 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ always pointing toward the photographs

A paper of 2017 made this argument, with a pile spread along a line in place of a grain, and trained picture machines by the sand distance instead. For that it used the twin of inf52.2 for the cost |x − y|, stated: the distance equals the largest value of the average of f over one pile minus its average over the other, over all functions f whose slope is never steeper than 1. A second machine learns such an f. Keeping its slope below 1 was crude in the first paper, by the paper’s own account, and later work improved it.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞52.oq.1How smooth is the cheapest map? For the squared distance the map is ∇u, and u solves an equation in which its bends multiply. When the holes fill a region that bulges outward everywhere and the densities are smooth, u is smooth; when the region of holes is not of that shape, the map can tear along curves. Exactly which piles and holes give a smooth map, and for which costs other than the squared distance, is understood in part, through a condition found in 2005 on the cost, and not in general, especially on curved spaces.
∞52.oq.2What happens with the 1781 cost, the plain distance? Then the cheapest plan is far from unique: on a line, moving sand one step right can be done in order or by moving the end grain to the far end, at the same cost. Whether a cheapest map exists in every dimension took two centuries to settle, around the year 2000. How regular the best of them can be chosen, and how they behave on curved spaces and for other costs that do not bend up, is still being worked out.
∞52.oq.3How fast can the cheapest plan be found? For n places, 42g’s program finds it, but its time grows quickly with n, and a picture has a million places. Since 2013 a trick that blurs the plan slightly, trading exactness for speed, has made plans of that size practical. And if the piles are known only through samples, the distance between the samples approaches the true one slowly in many dimensions, roughly as n to the power −1/d in d dimensions (held in trust, and not revisited here). Whether the blurred answers can be trusted, and how to beat the slow approach when the piles have hidden structure, is open.
∞52.oq.4What does curvature from moving sand allow? The 2006 to 2009 definition makes sense for spaces with corners, and much has been proved about them since: they split, they compare to balls, their limits keep the property. Which results about smooth spaces with curvature bounded below carry over, and whether every such rough space is close to a smooth one in a useful sense, are asked in many forms and answered in few.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question is older than most of the tools that answer it. In 1781 a French geometer posed the cheapest way to move a heap of earth into a set of holes, cost being weight times distance. The problem was beyond the means of his time, and for a century and a half it stayed a curiosity of geometry.

The turn came from planning. A Soviet mathematician who worked on the best use of resources wrote in 1942 a short paper on moving masses that replaced maps by plans and found the twin problem of prices. He shared the prize in economics in 1975, for the theory of the best use of resources, with an economist in the United States.

For decades the problem lived in economics and operations research, as the transport problem of 42·logi, and in probability, where its distance was found more than once and carries several names. In 1991 a French mathematician found the bowl rule, and the subject turned back to geometry and to equations, where in the 1990s it was tied to the spreading of heat and to inequalities about shapes.

The years from 2006 to 2009 brought curvature. Two groups, one American and French, one German, independently defined curvature bounded below through moving sand, for spaces with no smooth structure at all. One of those authors received the best-known medal in mathematics in 2010, and another specialist in the subject received it in 2018. Whether such rough spaces are a new world or a closure of the old one is a live question (∞52.oq.4); what is not in doubt is that a question about earthworks now sits at the centre of geometry.

Then came the machines. A paper of 2013 made the plans cheap to approximate, and in 2017 a paper used the sand distance to train machines that draw pictures, which made the subject known to engineers. What is argued now is how much of the success is the mathematics and how much the engineering around it, and whether the slow approach of sample distances in many dimensions (∞52.oq.3) makes the method less reliable than it looks.

The accepted names

The subject is optimal transport. The 1781 question is the Monge problem and the 1942 one the Kantorovich problem; a plan is a coupling or transport plan, and the shared plan is the product coupling. The sand-price technique is weak Kantorovich duality, with φ and ψ the Kantorovich potentials; strong duality for continuous costs is the Kantorovich duality theorem. The uncrossing technique gives the monotone rearrangement, the quantile coupling on the line. The cycle-swap technique is cyclical monotonicity for the quadratic cost, the bowl rule is Brenier’s theorem, its equation the Monge–Ampère equation, and the 2005 condition the Ma–Trudinger–Wang condition. The distances are the Wasserstein distances W₁ and W₂, also called earth mover’s, Kantorovich–Rubinstein or Monge–Kantorovich distances; the gap-area technique is the formula W₁ = ∫|F − G| on the line, and the twin used in 2017 is Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality with 1-Lipschitz functions. The halfway pile is displacement interpolation, and the curvature definition is the curvature-dimension condition CD(K, N), a synthetic lower bound on Ricci curvature. The surprise gap is the Jensen–Shannon divergence, and the 2017 machines are Wasserstein GANs. The 2013 trick is entropic regularization, solved by Sinkhorn’s iterations.

Who said it first

Gaspard Monge, “Mémoire sur la théorie des déblais et des remblais”, 1781. Leonid Kantorovich, “On the translocation of masses”, 1942; he shared the 1975 prize in economics with Tjalling Koopmans “for their contributions to the theory of optimum allocation of resources”. Yann Brenier proved the polar factorization in 1991. John Lott and Cédric Villani (Annals of Mathematics, 2009) and Karl-Theodor Sturm (Acta Mathematica, 2006) defined curvature through transport. Villani received the Fields Medal in 2010, and Alessio Figalli in 2018. Lawrence Evans and Wilfrid Gangbo (1999) and others settled the existence of maps for the distance cost. Xi-Nan Ma, Neil Trudinger and Xu-Jia Wang found their condition in 2005. Marco Cuturi made entropic transport fast in 2013. Martin Arjovsky, Soumith Chintala and Léon Bottou wrote “Wasserstein GAN” in 2017.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each grain should go to the nearest hole, so the cheapest plan sends every grain to its nearest hole.” Not allowed: the nearest holes fill up. In the three grains of inf52.3, 1 and 3 are both nearest to 2, which holds only one; the cheapest plan is a balance across all grains at once, which is what the prices of inf52.2 keep account of.
