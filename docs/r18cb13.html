18·cb13 · newsflash for 13l

Slopes of circles and curves given by equations

13l took slopes of both sides of an equation in two coordinates, and found the slope of a curve without writing it as a function. It worked on a sideways parabola, on a hyperbola and on a curve built from cubes. It left the circle for later, because the ladder could not yet say what a circle is. 18d said it: the points at one distance from a centre, and the flag 13l opened for the circle is closed. Now the old tech meets the new shape. The slope of a circle comes out in one line, and it says the tangent line stands at a right angle to the radius. From there: the two tangent lines from a point outside, the points where a curve's tangent lies flat or stands upright, and which way a curve bends.

On a circle, the slope at a point is the negative of the horizontal gap from the centre over the vertical gap. The tangent at a point is the line perpendicular to the radius there. From a point outside, the two touching points lie on one line, and each tangent segment is as long as the square root of the squared distance to the centre less the squared radius. After slopes of both sides, the curve’s slope is a negative quotient. Where the top is zero the curve is flat; where the bottom is zero it stands upright; where both are, look closer. On a circle about the origin, the slope of the slope shows the top half bends down and the bottom half bends up.

The slope of a circle

Take the circle with centre O = (p, q) and radius r: (x − p)² + (y − q)² = r². Above the centre's level, it is the graph of q + √(r² − (x − p)²) on the open interval (p − r, p + r). Below, it is the graph of q − √(r² − (x − p)²). Each is a branch in 13l's sense, and each has a slope there, by chain for slopes and slope-of-√. At the two side points (p ± r, q) there is no branch at all: every point of the circle has (x − p)² ≤ r², so no open interval around p + r fits under it.

Circle-slope technique: at a point of the circle not level with the centre, the slope is minus the run from the centre over the rise from the centre. In marks: y′ = −(x − p)/(y − q), for y ≠ q.

(x - p)^2 + (y - q)^2 = r^2 @@ with y = φ(x) on a branch: true at every x of an open interval
2(x - p) + 2(y - q)\,y' = 0 @@ slopes of both sides (13l): chain for slopes, with the inside y on the left; a constant has slope 0 on the right
\htmlClass{new}{y' = -\dfrac{x - p}{y - q}} \quad (y \ne q) \quad \blacksquare @@ adding-keeps-answers, then multiplying-keeps-answers by (2(y − q))⁻¹
  
x^2 + y^2 = 25 \text{ at } (3, 4):\ \ y' = -\tfrac34 @@ circle-slope
\big(\sqrt{25 - x^2}\big)' = \dfrac{-2x}{2\sqrt{25 - x^2}} = -\tfrac34 \text{ at } 3 @@ the long way, on the branch: chain for slopes with slope-of-√
\text{at } (3, -4):\ \ y' = \tfrac34 @@ the other branch, from the same formula, as in 13l

The tangent stands at a right angle to the radius

13k defined the tangent line by its slope. 18d called a line a tangent when it meets the circle exactly once, and proved that such a line is perpendicular to the radius. On a circle the two meanings agree. Tangent-meets-radius technique: the tangent line at a point of a circle is the line through that point at right angles to the radius. In marks, the tangent at P is (P − O) · (X − P) = 0, and it meets the circle only at P.

P = (x_0, y_0),\ x_0 \ne p,\ y_0 \ne q:\ \ \tfrac{y_0 - q}{x_0 - p} \cdot \Big(-\tfrac{x_0 - p}{y_0 - q}\Big) = -1 @@ the radius's slope times circle-slope's
\text{so the tangent line is perpendicular to } OP @@ perpendicular-lines (18c): slopes that multiply to −1
x_0 = p:\ \ y' = 0,\ \text{radius upright} @@ circle-slope at the top or the bottom: a flat tangent, and still perpendicular
y_0 = q:\ \ \tfrac{dx}{dy} = -\tfrac{y - q}{x - p} = 0,\ \text{tangent } x = x_0 @@ no branch in x, so swap the roles (13l's inverse dodge on the swapped pairs): x is a branch in y there, with slope 0
\htmlClass{new}{(P - O)\cdot(X - P) = 0} @@ every case: the line through P with normal P − O (two-descriptions-agree, 18c)
\begin{aligned}&\text{nearest point to } O \text{ on it is } P,\\ &\quad \ \text{at distance } r \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ nearest-point; line-meets-circle: it meets the circle only at P
  
(x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 25 \text{ at } (6, 2) @@ the radius runs (4, 3)
4(x - 6) + 3(y - 2) = 0,\ \ 4x + 3y = 30 @@ tangent-meets-radius; slope −4/3, as circle-slope gives

Two tangent lines from a point outside

13k found the tangent lines of x² through a point off the graph by leaving the touching point as an unknown. Do the same for a circle. Let Q be a point outside the circle, so |Q − O| > r. Touching-points technique: from a point outside a circle there are exactly two tangent lines, and the two tangent segments are equally long. Their touching points lie on the line (P − O) · (Q − O) = r². Each segment from Q to its touching point is √(|Q − O|² − r²) long.

Q \text{ on the tangent at } P \iff P \cdot (Q - P) = 0 @@ tangent-meets-radius, with O = (0, 0)
\iff P \cdot Q = P \cdot P = r^2 @@ the dot technique; P is on the circle
P \cdot Q = r^2 \text{ is a line at distance } \tfrac{r^2}{|Q|} \text{ from } O @@ a line with normal Q (18c); nearest-point
\tfrac{r^2}{|Q|} \lt r @@ |Q| > r: Q is outside
\text{it meets the circle in exactly two points} @@ line-meets-circle: two touching points, two tangent lines
\htmlClass{new}{|QP|^2 = |Q|^2 - r^2} \text{ for each} \quad \blacksquare @@ right-triangle (18a): OP ⊥ PQ
  
x^2 + y^2 = 25,\ Q = (13, 0):\ \ 13x = 25 @@ touching points on P · Q = r²
P = \big(\tfrac{25}{13}, \pm\tfrac{60}{13}\big),\ \ 5x \pm 12y = 65 @@ the circle gives y; tangent-meets-radius gives each line, and Q passes: 5 · 13 = 65
|QP| = \sqrt{169 - 25} = 12 @@ both tangent segments

The same right angle tells you how far you can see. Take the Earth as a ball of radius R ≈ 6371 km, and slice it through its centre and through your eye. The slice is a circle, and your line of sight to the sea's edge is a tangent line. From the top of Fremont Peak, above Monterey Bay, about 0.966 km above the sea, the tangent segment is √((R + 0.966)² − R²) = √(2R · 0.966 + 0.966²) ≈ 111 km. Air bends light a little, so on a clear day the edge is a little further still.

Where a tangent lies flat or stands upright

For the equations on this page, slopes of both sides end with T + B · y′ = 0. Here T and B are formulas in x and y. So y′ = −T/B: call T the top and B the bottom. Horizontal-and-vertical technique: where the top is 0 and the bottom is not, the tangent lies flat; where the bottom is 0 and the top is not, it stands upright. Where both are 0, the formula says nothing, and you must look closer. Each claim needs a branch through the point, in x or in y, with a slope: 13l's caution stands.

Why upright: swap the roles. Take slopes of both sides with x a branch in y. Each term gives the same pieces, with the chain's factor now on the x terms: T · x′ + B = 0. So x′ = −B/T, and it is 0 where the bottom is 0. The tangent line is then x = constant.

2x + (y + x\,y') + 2y\,y' = 0 @@ slopes of both sides: product for slopes on xy
y' = -\dfrac{2x + y}{x + 2y} @@ T = 2x + y, B = x + 2y; adding-keeps-answers, multiplying-keeps-answers
y = \dfrac{-x \pm \sqrt{12 - 3x^2}}{2} @@ the branches exist: completing the square in y (8i); a slope wherever 12 − 3x² > 0
\text{top } 0:\ y = -2x \Rightarrow 3x^2 = 3:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{(1, -2),\ (-1, 2)} @@ flat; the bottom there is −3 and 3
\text{bottom } 0:\ x = -2y \Rightarrow 3y^2 = 3:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{(-2, 1),\ (2, -1)} @@ upright; the top there is −3 and 3

The curve is a tilted oval. Its highest point is (−1, 2) and its lowest (1, −2). Its leftmost is (−2, 1) and its rightmost (2, −1). None of them sits on an axis, and the equation found them without drawing.

Which way it bends

14g's bends-up technique reads the bend from the second slope. On a curve given by an equation, y′ is a formula with y in it. So take slopes again, and let chain for slopes handle each y once more. Circle-bending technique: the top half of a circle bends down and the bottom half bends up, and a smaller circle bends harder. In marks, on x² + y² = r², y″ = −r²/y³, which is −1/r at the top.

y' = -\dfrac{x}{y} @@ circle-slope, centre (0, 0)
y'' = -\dfrac{1 \cdot y - x\,y'}{y^2} @@ quotient for slopes (13i), y a branch with a slope
= -\dfrac{y + x^2/y}{y^2} = -\dfrac{x^2 + y^2}{y^3} @@ put in y′ = −x/y
\htmlClass{new}{y'' = -\dfrac{r^2}{y^3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the circle's equation
y \gt 0:\ y'' \lt 0,\ \text{bends down};\ \ y \lt 0:\ \text{bends up} @@ bends-up (14g); at the top (0, r), y″ = −1/r

On the tilted oval, the second round gives (x + 2y)y″ = −2(1 + y′ + y′²). And 1 + y′ + y′² = (y′ + ½)² + ¾ is never below ¾. So the sign of y″ is the opposite of the sign of x + 2y. On the upper branch, x + 2y = √(12 − 3x²) > 0: it bends down. The lower branch bends up.

Who said it first

Descartes wrote that finding the line at right angles to a curve at any point was the most useful and most general problem in geometry that he knew, and that he had ever wished to know.

René Descartes, La Géométrie, Book II (1637); paraphrase

In 18·cb13.4.4, the mathematician of 1637 is René Descartes, and the rival is Pierre de Fermat, whose small change he set to nothing has the accepted name adequality. They quarrelled by letter in 1637 and 1638, through Marin Mersenne.

Accepted names: the line P · Q = r² is the polar of Q; a point like (0, 0) on y² = x³ is a cusp.
