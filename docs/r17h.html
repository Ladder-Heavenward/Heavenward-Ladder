17h · technique

Logs and exponents by hand

17a to 17g built ln, exp and pˣ and proved their techniques. This step is about using them. It rewrites an expression in the form a question wants, and solves an equation whose unknown sits in an exponent. It reads the graph of a power or a logarithm off its base and its shifts. And it builds an exponential from two measurements. It ends with the machine that put these techniques in every engineer's pocket for three hundred years: two rulers marked in logs, slid along each other.

To free an exponent, take a log of both sides. To free a log, raise its base to both sides. Change base by dividing by the logarithm of the new base. Check that every answer is in the domain: logs only of positive numbers. When you divide by a log less than 0, the inequality turns.

17h1Changing base, and rewriting

Change-of-base technique: for p, s > 0, both ≠ 1, and x > 0: logₚ x = logₛ x / logₛ p. In particular logₚ s · logₛ p = 1.

\log_p x = \tfrac{\ln x}{\ln p},\ \ \log_s x = \tfrac{\ln x}{\ln s} @@ log-base-p (17g), twice
\tfrac{\log_s x}{\log_s p} = \tfrac{\ln x / \ln s}{\ln p / \ln s} = \tfrac{\ln x}{\ln p} @@ ln s ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\log_p x = \tfrac{\log_s x}{\log_s p}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with x = s: logₚ s · logₛ p = 1
  
\log_{10} 6 = \log_{10} 2 + \log_{10} 3 \approx 0.3010 + 0.4771 = 0.7782 @@ log-of-a-product for log₁₀ (log-base-p)
\log_2 7 = \tfrac{\ln 7}{\ln 2} \approx \tfrac{1.9459}{0.6931} \approx 2.8074 @@ change-of-base with s = e
8^{2/3} = (2^3)^{2/3} = 2^2 = 4 @@ real-exponent; write both in one base
\tfrac{9^x}{3^{x+1}} = \tfrac{3^{2x}}{3^{x+1}} = 3^{x-1} @@ real-exponent
\tfrac12\ln 25 - \ln 10 = \ln 5 - \ln 10 = \ln\tfrac12 @@ log-of-a-power, log-of-a-quotient
e^{\,\ln 3 + 2\ln x} = 3x^2 @@ adding-becomes-multiplying; exp undoes ln; x > 0

17h3Graphs

The graph of pˣ for p > 1 rises through (0, 1) and (1, p). It approaches the line y = 0 as x → −∞ without reaching it. A line approached this way has the accepted name asymptote. For 0 < p < 1 the graph is that of (1/p)ˣ run the other way along x, since pˣ = (1/p)⁻ˣ. logₚ x has the swapped pairs (9d). It passes through (1, 0) and (p, 1), and approaches the line x = 0 as x → 0⁺. ex − h + k is eˣ slid h along and k up, with its asymptote at y = k. ln(x − h) is defined only for x > h.

17h4From two measurements

y = A b^t,\ \ y(2) = 800,\ \ y(5) = 1200 @@ a town near Turlock, people in year t
\tfrac{y(5)}{y(2)} = b^3 = 1.5 \Rightarrow b = 1.5^{1/3} \approx 1.1447 @@ A cancels in the ratio
A = \tfrac{800}{b^2} \approx 610.5 @@ y(2) = Ab²
y \approx 610.5 \cdot 1.1447^t = 610.5\,e^{0.1352t} @@ b = e^(ln b), ln b ≈ 0.1352 (17g)
\begin{aligned}&\text{each year it grows by a factor } 1.1447,\\ &\quad \ \text{about } 14.5\%\end{aligned} @@ the percent growth is b − 1

Two points fix a straight line by their difference and an exponential by their ratio. A third measurement tests which, if either, the data follow.

17h5Two rulers

Mark a ruler so that the number x sits at distance log₁₀ x from its left end: 1 at 0, 10 at 1, 2 at 0.301. Slide a second, identical ruler along it until its 1 sits over p on the first. Then q on the second sits over the point at distance log p + log q = log(pq) on the first, which is marked pq. A product is read off by adding two lengths. Its accepted name is the slide rule.

Who said it first

Oughtred complained in print that a former pupil had taken his circular rule of proportion, made it public as his own invention, and sold it, when he himself had shown it to him years before.

William Oughtred, An Apologeticall Epistle (1633); paraphrase
