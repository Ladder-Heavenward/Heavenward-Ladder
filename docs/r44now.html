Can Two Cubes Ever Add to a Cube?

Can two cubes ever add to a cube? 3² + 4² = 5², and there are endless such squares. But no two cubes of whole numbers other than 0 add to a cube, no two fourth powers to a fourth power, and so on for every power above 2. The claim was written in a book margin in the 1630s with no proof, and it stood unproved for over 350 years. In 1847 a proof for every prime power was announced to the academy in Paris, built on factoring in a ring of whole combinations of a turning ζ. It spent a technique that is false in that ring, as 44e found for a smaller ring, and the repair, ideals, is where much of this rung comes from. The proof that finally stood, in 1995, went through curves like those of 44·crypt and a kind of function this ladder does not reach. This page builds what can be built by hand and names the rest.

For every n ≥ 3, xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no answer in whole numbers other than 0. On the clock of 9, the cubes are 0, 1 and 8, which forces one of x, y, z to be a multiple of 3. Factoring xᵖ + yᵖ over ζ works where factoring is unique and fails where it is not; ideals repair it for the primes that do not divide the count of ideal classes, all but 37, 59 and 67 below 100. The 1995 proof showed that a solution would give a curve whose point counts on every clock cannot follow the pattern that every such curve must follow.

44now1A Clock Rules Out Most of It

A first test of any equation in whole numbers: read it on a clock. If it fails on some clock for every choice of hours, it fails in the whole numbers.

Nine-clock technique: if x³ + y³ = z³ in whole numbers, then one of x, y, z is a multiple of 3. On the clock of 9, every cube is 0, 1 or 8, and only multiples of 3 give 0. If none of the three is a multiple of 3, the left side is 1 + 1, 1 + 8 or 8 + 8, that is 2, 0 or 7, and the right side is 1 or 8: never the same.

0^3, 3^3, 6^3 = 0;\ \ 1^3, 4^3, 7^3 = 1;\ \ 2^3, 5^3, 8^3 = 8 @@ 64 = 7 · 9 + 1, 125 = 13 · 9 + 8
\{1, 8\} + \{1, 8\} = \{2, 0, 7\} @@ 9 = 0 and 16 = 7 on the clock
\{2, 0, 7\} \cap \{1, 8\} = \varnothing @@ no match
\htmlClass{new}{3 \mid xyz} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nine-clock technique

No clock can finish the job. The equation x³ + y³ = z³ has answers on every clock, for instance 1 + 0 = 1, which in the whole numbers is 1³ + 0³ = 1³. Clocks rule out answers of certain kinds, and something else must rule out the rest.

44now2Factoring the Sum

Let p be an odd prime and ζ the turning by 2π/p (25d). Since ζᵖ = 1, the polynomial tᵖ − 1 has the p zeros 1, ζ, …, ζᵖ⁻¹, and putting t = −x/y gives the factoring of the sum.

Factor-the-sum technique: xᵖ + yᵖ = (x + y)(x + ζy)(x + ζ²y) ⋯ (x + ζᵖ⁻¹y) for an odd prime p, in the ring ℤ[ζ] of whole combinations of the powers of ζ. If xᵖ + yᵖ = zᵖ and the p factors share no factor, the 1847 argument went on, each is a pth power up to an undoable member, by 44d’s one-factoring technique, and a contradiction follows by descent.

2^3 + 1^3 = 9 @@ not a cube, so no contradiction is expected
(2 + \zeta)(2 + \zeta^2) = 4 + 2(\zeta + \zeta^2) + \zeta^3 = 4 - 2 + 1 = 3 @@ 1 + ζ + ζ² = 0 and ζ³ = 1
(2 + 1)(2 + \zeta)(2 + \zeta^2) = 3 \cdot 3 @@ the factor-the-sum technique
\htmlClass{new}{9 = 9} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked

For p = 3 the ring ℤ[ζ] has division with remainder by the size a² − ab + b² of a + bζ, as 44d’s rings with division do, so factoring there is unique. The argument can be made to work, and the cube case is settled this way. The trouble is at larger p.

44now3The Flaw

In ℤ[ζ] for p = 23 factoring is not unique, and the step “factors sharing nothing whose product is a pth power are each a pth power” fails. The ring of 44e, ℤ[ω] with ω = √−5, shows the same failure in a size that can be checked by hand.

Ideal-power technique: in ℤ[ω], the ideal (2) is the square P² of an ideal, but 2 is not an undoable member times the square of a member. P² = (2) is 44e’s ideal-product technique. If 2 = ±w² with w = a + bω, then sizes multiply (44e’s whole-size technique): 4 = N(w)², so N(w) = a² + 5b² = 2, which has no answer in whole numbers.

(2) = P^2 @@ the ideal is a square
2 = \pm w^2 \Rightarrow N(w)^2 = N(2) = 4 @@ the whole-size technique
a^2 + 5b^2 = 2:\ \ b = 0 \Rightarrow a^2 = 2;\ \ b \ne 0 \Rightarrow a^2 + 5b^2 \ge 5 @@ no answer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a square ideal, from a member that is no square}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the 1847 step, in small

The repair is to argue with ideals, which do factor uniquely (44e’s ideal-factoring technique, held in trust). The step then shows that the ideal (x + ζy) is the pth power of an ideal I. To get back from ideals to members, I must be the ideal of one member, and that holds when p does not divide the number of classes of ideals of ℤ[ζ]: then Iᵖ being made by one member forces I to be.

44now4The Primes the Repair Reaches

Call an odd prime p regular when it does not divide the number of classes of ideals of ℤ[ζ]. The repaired argument proves the claim for every regular prime, and a test for regularity was found that needs no ideals at all.

Regular-test technique: an odd prime p is regular exactly when it divides none of the tops of the fractions B₂, B₄, …, B_{p−3}, in lowest terms, where the Bₖ are the numbers in t/(eᵗ − 1) = Σ Bₖ tᵏ/k!. Held in trust: the proof needs the analysis of ℤ[ζ] far past this rung. The numbers come from multiplying out the series of eᵗ − 1 and dividing.

B_{12} = -\tfrac{691}{2730} @@ 691 is a prime, and divides B₁₂’s top: 691 is not regular
B_{32} = -\tfrac{7709321041217}{510} @@ the top is 37 · 683 · 305065927
37 \text{ divides the top of } B_{32},\ \ 32 \le 37 - 3 @@ the regular-test technique
\htmlClass{new}{37 \text{ is the first prime that is not regular}} \quad \blacksquare @@ below 100: 37, 59 and 67

So by 1850 the claim was proved for every prime below 100 except 37, 59 and 67, which were done later by harder means. Whether there are endlessly many regular primes is still not known, though there are endlessly many that are not.

44now5Through Curves

The proof of 1995 took another road. If aᵖ + bᵖ = cᵖ, form the curve y² = x(x − aᵖ)(x + bᵖ), and count its points on the clock of each prime ℓ, as 44·crypt did. Write the count as ℓ + 1 − a_ℓ, so that a_ℓ measures how far it is from ℓ + 1.

Point-list technique: a curve y² = f(x) with whole coefficients gives a list of whole numbers a_ℓ = ℓ + 1 − (points on the clock of ℓ, with the far point), one for each prime ℓ not dividing 2 times the discriminant of f, and |a_ℓ| ≤ 2√ℓ. The count is 44·crypt’s square-count technique, done on each clock; the bound is held in trust there.

\ell = 3, 7, 11, 19, 23:\ \ a_\ell = 0 @@ 4, 8, 12, 20, 24 points: exactly ℓ + 1
\ell = 5, 13, 17, 29:\ \ a_\ell = -2,\ 6,\ 2,\ -10 @@ 8, 8, 16, 40 points
5 = 1^2 + 2^2,\ 13 = 3^2 + 2^2,\ 17 = 1^2 + 4^2,\ 29 = 5^2 + 2^2 @@ the odd square is ¼ of a_ℓ squared
\htmlClass{new}{|a_\ell| = 2 \times \text{the odd one of } u, v \text{ with } \ell = u^2 + v^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ on every prime checked

The list follows a pattern that no single clock explains. Every curve with whole coefficients has a list of this kind that matches the list of a modular form, a function on the upper half of ℂ with a great deal of symmetry, which this ladder names only. The 1995 proof showed this for enough curves, and a proof of 1986 had shown that the curve from aᵖ + bᵖ = cᵖ would have a list matching none. So there is no such curve, and no such a, b, c.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The claim is Fermat’s Last Theorem, stated: for n ≥ 3 there are no positive integers x, y, z with xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ. ℤ[ζ] is the ring of integers of the cyclotomic field ℚ(ζ_p), and the number of classes of ideals is its class number h_p. The regular-test technique is Kummer’s criterion, and the Bₖ are the Bernoulli numbers. The point-list technique gives the traces of Frobenius, and the bound is Hasse’s. The curve y² = x(x − aᵖ)(x + bᵖ) is the Frey curve. That every elliptic curve over ℚ matches a modular form is the modularity theorem, once the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture; the 1986 result is Ribet’s theorem, from a conjecture of Serre. The pattern of y² = x³ − x comes from its complex multiplication by i.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat wrote the claim in his copy of Diophantus, around 1637, and proved the case n = 4 by descent. Leonhard Euler gave the cube case in 1770. Gabriel Lamé announced the general proof to the Paris Academy on 1 March 1847; Joseph Liouville pointed out the gap at once, and Ernst Kummer wrote that he had shown in 1844 that unique factoring fails in these rings, and proved the claim for regular primes. Gerhard Frey proposed the curve in 1984, Ken Ribet proved in 1986 that it cannot be modular, and Andrew Wiles, with a final step joint with Richard Taylor, proved enough of modularity in 1995.

A proof that is not allowed

“It has been checked by computer for every exponent up to four million, so it holds.” Not allowed as a proof: a check of many cases is not a check of all. The checks, done through Kummer’s methods, were real evidence, and the 1995 proof is what settles every exponent at once.
