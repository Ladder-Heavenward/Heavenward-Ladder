How Many of Ten Thousand Findings Can You Trust?

How many of ten thousand findings can you trust? A lab tests ten thousand genes at once for a link with a disease. At the usual one-in-twenty bar, five hundred genes with no link at all will pass by chance. 30·now showed how passed findings mislead; this page gives the tools that keep a list of findings honest. It finds the chance of at least one false alarm, the old cure that divides the bar, a newer one that controls the share of false findings instead, why that share is kept, and how the costs of each mistake should set a bar in the first place.

With m tests at bar α, false alarms average about α times the number of untrue ideas. Dividing the bar by m keeps the chance of even one false alarm below α, at a heavy price. To keep the share of false claims near q instead, sort the chances p₍₁₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₘ₎, find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ kq/m, and claim the k smallest. When acting has a gain and a loss, act when the chance of being right passes loss/(gain + loss).

33stat1Twenty Tests, Twenty Chances

From here on, on this page, a test’s chance p is the chance of a result at least so strong if there is nothing there, as on rung 30, and a test passes when p is below the bar α = 0.05. A false alarm is a pass where there is nothing.

\text{one test of nothing: false alarm with chance } 0.05 @@ the bar
20 \text{ independent tests of nothing: none pass with chance } 0.95^{20} @@ chances multiply (30f)
1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64 @@ at least one passes
\htmlClass{new}{10\,000 \text{ genes of nothing: about } 500 \text{ pass}} @@ averages add (30g)

Run enough tests and false alarms are certain; the bar for one test says nothing about a list. A lab that reports its passes without saying how many it ran has reported almost nothing.

33stat2Divide the Bar

The oldest cure: with m tests, pass only those with p below α/m.

P(\text{some false alarm}) \le \textstyle\sum P(\text{false alarm}_i) @@ chance of a union is at most the sum (30f)
\le m \cdot \alpha/m = \alpha @@ whatever the links between tests
m = 10\,000:\ \text{bar } 0.000005 @@ five in a million
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a real but modest link is almost never found}} @@ safe, and nearly blind

Divided-bar technique: passing only p below α/m keeps the chance of even one false alarm in the whole list below α. For a handful of tests that matter one by one, it is right. For screening thousands to choose a few for a closer look, it throws away nearly everything.

33stat3Control the Share Instead

For screening, a few false findings on a long list are tolerable, if their share is kept small. Ten tests, with these chances, and a share q = 0.05 to be kept:

p₍ₖ₎.001.008.012.030.041.09.2.4.6.9
kq/m.005.010.015.020.025.030.035.040.045.050
\text{sort the chances: } p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \cdots \le p_{(m)} @@ smallest first
\text{largest } k \text{ with } p_{(k)} \le kq/m:\ k = 3 @@ .012 ≤ .015; .030 > .020, and none later pass
\text{claim the 3 smallest} @@ against 1 with the divided bar, 5 with no care
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on average, at most } q \text{ of claims are false}} @@ for independent tests

Step-up technique: sort the chances, find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ kq/m, and claim the k smallest; the share of false claims then averages at most q. The bar rises the further down the sorted list it reaches, so a crowd of small chances lifts each other.

33stat4Why the Share Is Kept

Say m₀ of the m ideas are untrue. A chance from an untrue idea is equally likely to fall anywhere from 0 to 1 (30i), so it falls below a bar t with chance t.

\text{claim the } k \text{ with } p \le kq/m @@ the rule’s bar t = kq/m
\text{false claims average } m_0 \cdot kq/m @@ each untrue idea passes with chance t
\text{share} \approx \dfrac{m_0 kq/m}{k} = \dfrac{m_0}{m}\, q @@ false claims over all claims
\htmlClass{new}{\le q} @@ since m₀ ≤ m

The step-up bar is chosen so that the false claims expected at that bar are about q of the claims made. The careful proof handles the fact that k itself depends on the data; the answer stays m₀q/m for independent tests.

33stat5Let the Costs Set the Bar

A bar of 0.05 is a habit, not a law. When a choice follows, such as treating a patient, the costs of the two mistakes should set it. Say acting when right gains G, and acting when wrong loses L.

\text{act: average } \pi G - (1 - \pi) L @@ π the chance of being right, given the data (30u)
\text{do nothing: } 0 @@ no gain, no loss
\pi G - (1 - \pi) L > 0 \iff \pi > \dfrac{L}{G + L} @@ take from both sides (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{G = 9,\ L = 1:\ \text{act when } \pi > 0.1} @@ a cheap test for a grave illness

Cost technique: act when the chance of being right, given the data, passes the loss over the gain plus the loss. A cheap, safe follow-up deserves a low bar; a costly, risky one a high bar. The step-up rule is the right tool when the list is for looking closer, not for acting at once.

Accepted names

The chance p is the p-value; many tests at once raise the multiple comparisons problem. The chance of any false alarm is the family-wise error rate, and dividing the bar is the Bonferroni correction. The share of false claims is the false discovery rate, and the step-up rule the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Setting the bar by gains and losses is decision theory, with L/(G + L) a Bayes decision threshold.

Who said it first

Carlo Emilio Bonferroni gave the inequality behind the divided bar in 1936. Yoav Benjamini and Yosef Hochberg introduced the false discovery rate and the step-up rule in 1995. Abraham Wald built statistics as a theory of decisions in 1950.
