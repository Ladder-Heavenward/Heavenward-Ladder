16·then · newsflash

How much has piled up, if you only know the rate?

How much has piled up, if you only know the rate? Most things that are measured are measured as rates: litres a second past a gauge, millimetres of rain an hour, cars a minute through a toll, people a year into a county. What is wanted is usually a total: how much water is in the lake, how much rain fell, how many cars came through. 16c says the total change is the area under the rate. This page uses that one fact to keep a lake's books, and to find when the lake is fullest. It estimates a total from readings taken once an hour. And it settles, with rain gauges and a river, an argument that ran for two thousand years: where rivers come from.

The water in the lake now is the water at the start plus the area under its rate of change since. With a rate in and a rate out, the rate of change is the one less the other. The lake is fullest where that difference changes from positive to negative. From readings, a sum of boxes stands in for the integral.

The books balance

Net-change technique: if Q has a continuous slope Q′ on [0, T], then Q(T) = Q(0) + ∫₀ᵀ Q′(t) dt. It is area-is-a-change with G = Q. Its accepted name is the net change theorem.

\text{in}(t) = 300 + 2t(24 - t),\ \ \text{out}(t) = 450 \quad (\text{m}^3/\text{s},\ t \text{ in hours}) @@ a model: a storm's inflow that peaks at noon; a dam letting out a steady flow
V'(t) = \text{in}(t) - \text{out}(t) = 2t(24 - t) - 150 @@ what comes in less what goes out, in m³/s
\textstyle\int_0^{24} V'(t)\,dt = 2\big(12t^2 - \tfrac{t^3}{3}\big)\Big|_0^{24} - 150 \cdot 24 = 4608 - 3600 = 1008 @@ the table; area-is-a-change. Units: m³/s times hours
1008 \cdot 3600 \approx 3.63 \times 10^6 \text{ m}^3 @@ an hour is 3600 s: the lake gains about 3.6 million cubic metres

When is the lake fullest?

V'(t) = 0 \iff t^2 - 24t + 75 = 0 \iff t = 12 \pm \sqrt{69} @@ 8i's quadratic formula; √69 ≈ 8.31
V' \lt 0 \text{ on } [0, 3.69),\ \ V' \gt 0 \text{ on } (3.69, 20.31),\ \ V' \lt 0 \text{ after} @@ the sign of the quadratic between and outside its zeros
\text{trough at } t \approx 3.69,\ \text{crest at } t \approx 20.31 @@ first-test (14f)
\text{fullest over } [0, 24]: \text{ at } 20.31 \text{ or at an end} @@ candidates (14a): compare V(20.31) with V(0) and V(24)

The lake is fullest not when the inflow peaks, at noon, but when the inflow, falling again, drops back to the outflow. Until then more is still coming in than going out.

From readings

\begin{aligned}&\text{rain at a gauge near Tracy, mm/h, read}\\ &\quad\text{on the hour:\ } 0,\ 2,\ 5,\ 9,\ 6,\ 3,\ 1\end{aligned} @@ seven readings, t = 0 to 6 h
\text{left sum: } (0 + 2 + 5 + 9 + 6 + 3) \cdot 1 = 25 \text{ mm} @@ 15h's strip sum, each hour's height at its start
\text{right sum: } (2 + 5 + 9 + 6 + 3 + 1) \cdot 1 = 26 \text{ mm} @@ each hour's height at its end
\text{between them, if the rate rose then fell once} @@ strip-sum-between (15h) needs the least and greatest on each hour; the readings only suggest them

With readings alone, no technique pins the total down: the rain could have spiked between readings. What net-change gives is the meaning of the number. The depth of rain that fell is the area under the rate, and every way of estimating it is a way of estimating that area.

In 1674 a careful measurer took the rain and snow that fell over three years on the upper basin of the Seine. He worked out how much water that made over the whole basin. He found it about six times what the river carried away in the same time. That was enough, he concluded, to feed the river and the springs, with plenty lost to the air and the plants.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, at every instant

1a's ledger, again, now with a slope. 5·chem closed the stuff column of a reaction, 7·phys closed charge at a junction, 11·chem closed heat in a box, 14·phys closed momentum in a crash, and 15·econ closed a loan. Each was a before and an after. A lake's books are kept every second. The change in water is what comes in less what goes out, and the net-change technique adds the seconds up.

What piled up is the area under in less out; when the books don't close, something was left out of the story.

The 1674 measurer above ran the same books on a whole river basin, and they did not close: six times the river's water fell as rain. He did not doubt the books. He went looking for where the rest went, and found the air and the plants.

A reservoir's inflow and outflow gauges read true all summer, yet the lake ends the summer 2 million cubic metres lower than the books say. Where could the water have gone?
