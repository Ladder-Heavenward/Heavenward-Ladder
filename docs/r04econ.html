Can a Threat Be Empty?

Can a threat be empty? Sarah wants to open a coffee cart outside the station, where Mike already sells coffee. Mike tells her: open, and I will cut my price until neither of us makes anything. If she believes him, she stays away. But the threat is about a move Mike makes after hers, and once she has opened, a price war costs him too. This page writes a game in turns as a tree of moves, solves it from the last move back to the first, proves by induction that every game in turns with finitely many moves has an outcome that best play reaches, and uses that outcome to tell a threat that will be carried out from one that is only words.

Draw the game as a tree: each fork is one player’s turn, and each end says what every player gets. Find a fork whose branches all end, give it the branch best for the player whose turn it is, and put that end in the fork’s place. Repeat until the first fork is gone. A threat about a later move counts only if the player would pick it when that fork comes.

4econ1A Game in Turns

Sarah moves first: she opens a cart, or she stays at her other job. If she opens, Mike moves: he shares the corner, or he starts a price war. Here is what each of them makes, in dollars an hour, at each way the game can end.

how it endsSarahMike
Sarah stays away310
Sarah opens, Mike shares55
Sarah opens, Mike fights12

From here on, on this page, a fork is a turn: a player and the branches that player may take. An end is a place where the game stops, with a pair (what Sarah gets, what Mike gets). A game in turns is a first fork, each of whose branches leads to an end or to another game in turns.

Each player knows the whole tree, and sees every move made before their own. The question is what each should do. Sarah cannot read her best branch off her own column, because what she gets after opening depends on a move that is not hers.

4econ2The Last Move First

Begin where nothing comes after. Mike’s fork has two branches, and both are ends. At that fork the game is Mike’s alone, and he takes what pays him more.

\text{Mike: share } 5,\ \text{fight } 2 @@ the table, Mike’s column
2 < 5 @@ the any-two-compare technique (4h)
\text{Mike’s fork} \to (5, 5) @@ he shares: put that end in the fork’s place
\text{Sarah: open } 5,\ \text{stay away } 3 @@ her fork now has two ends
3 < 5 @@ any-two-compare
\htmlClass{new}{(5, 5)} @@ she opens, and he shares

Last-move-first technique: at a fork whose branches all end, the player whose turn it is takes the branch that pays them most, so the fork may be replaced by that end. Each use makes the tree one fork smaller. When no fork is left, one end remains, and it is where best play goes.

The order matters. Sarah’s fork could not be settled first: until Mike’s fork was replaced, one of her branches was not an end, and she had nothing to compare.

4econ3Every Game in Turns Has a Best-Play End

Does the technique always finish? Call an end the best-play end of a game when it is the end reached if every player, at every fork, takes the branch that pays them most from there on. Best-play technique: every game in turns with finitely many forks, in which no player is ever offered the same amount on two branches of one fork, has exactly one best-play end. The proof is by strong induction (4h) on the number of forks.

\text{suppose: every game with fewer than } n \text{ forks has one} @@ strong induction: the hypothesis for every count below n
n = 0:\ \text{the game is an end, and that end is it} @@ no fork, no choice
n \ne 0:\ \text{first fork } F,\ \text{branches } B_1, \ldots, B_k @@ a game with forks has a first one
\text{each } B_i \text{ has fewer than } n \text{ forks} @@ F is not inside any branch
\text{each } B_i \text{ has one best-play end } e_i @@ the hypothesis
\{\text{mover’s amount at } e_1, \ldots, e_k\} \text{ has a greatest} @@ the greatest-member technique (4k): finite, with a member
\text{only one } e_i \text{ gives it} @@ no two branches of F pay the mover the same
\htmlClass{new}{\text{that } e_i \text{ is the game’s one best-play end}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the mover takes it, and best play below goes to e_i

The proof is the last-move-first technique turned around: it settles each branch first, by the hypothesis, and only then the fork on top. It says nothing about how big the tree is. A game with a million forks has a best-play end, as Sarah’s does.

4econ4An Empty Threat, and a Real One

Mike’s threat named the branch “fight”. At his fork, fight pays him 2 and share pays 5. So when his fork comes, he shares. Empty-threat technique: a threat about a later fork changes nothing when the player would not take that branch once the fork is reached. Sarah need only check his fork, not his words.

Now Mike makes his threat real before Sarah decides. He signs a deal with the station: if anyone else sells coffee there, he sells at cost or pays the station 4 dollars an hour. Sharing now leaves him 1 an hour. The tree is the same; one end has changed.

how it endsSarahMike
Sarah stays away310
Sarah opens, Mike shares51
Sarah opens, Mike fights12
\text{Mike: share } 1,\ \text{fight } 2 @@ the new column
\text{Mike’s fork} \to (1, 2) @@ last-move-first: he fights
\text{Sarah: open } 1,\ \text{stay away } 3 @@ her fork
\htmlClass{new}{(3, 10)} @@ she stays away, and Mike makes 10

By making one of his own ends worse, Mike went from 5 an hour to 10. A player can do better by taking a good branch away from themselves, when the others can see it is gone.

4econ5Taking Turns at a Growing Pot

Claire and Jean take turns at a pot of coins, Claire first, for 4 turns. At turn t the player whose turn it is may take: they get t + 1 coins, and the other gets one fewer than t. Or they may pass, and the pot grows. If all 4 turns pass, each gets 4.

turnwhose turntake: the mover getsthe other gets
1Claire20
2Jean31
3Claire42
4Jean53
all pass4 each

Passing all the way pays both of them more than any take at turn 1, 2 or 3. Best play does not go there. The claim, for every turn counted back from the end: the player whose turn it is takes.

\text{turn 4: take } 5,\ \text{pass } 4 @@ Jean takes: 4 < 5
\text{turn 3: take } 4,\ \text{pass} \to 3 @@ pass, and Jean takes at 4, leaving Claire 3
\text{turn 2: take } 3,\ \text{pass} \to 2 @@ Claire takes at 3, leaving Jean 2
\text{turn 1: take } 2,\ \text{pass} \to 1 @@ Jean takes at 2, leaving Claire 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Claire takes at turn 1: } (2, 0)} @@ each turn settled from the one after it

The four lines are one step done four times: if the next player takes, passing leaves you t, and taking gives you t + 1, so you take. That is an induction counted back from the last turn, and it works for a pot of any length.

The page says only what best play does, when each player takes what pays them most at every fork. Whether real players do that, with real money at stake, is a question about people, and only watching them can answer it.

Accepted names

A game in turns is a game in extensive form with perfect information, and its drawing is a game tree. Settling it from the last move back is backward induction. Best play at every fork is a subgame perfect equilibrium. An empty threat is a non-credible threat, and taking a branch away from yourself is commitment. The growing pot is the centipede game.

Who said it first

Ernst Zermelo used this kind of reasoning on chess in 1913. Reinhard Selten made best play at every fork the test of a sound plan, and shared the 1994 Nobel memorial prize in economics for it. Robert Rosenthal set out the growing pot in 1981, in a paper on price wars.
