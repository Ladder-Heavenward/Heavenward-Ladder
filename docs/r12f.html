12f · technique

Square Root by Hand

12d found the averaging step: from a fraction u above √2, the average of u and 2/u is a smaller fraction still above it. Here the step works for any n. Each guess comes with a second number on the far side of the root, so every step is a trap like 12e’s, and the trap closes far faster than halving: the error of the square is squared at every step. With whole numbers and fractions alone, three steps give √7 to eight places.

Average a guess and n over the guess. The root sits between the two, and the next guess overshoots the square by the square of the old overshoot, over four times the guess squared.

12f1Two Sides of the Root

For n > 0, x² − n is continuous on [0, n + 1], is below 0 at 0 and above 0 at n + 1, so by 12a it has a zero there: √n is a number. A guess u > 0 for it is too big when u² > n. Then n/u is too small, and the root is trapped between the two.

Root-trap technique: if u > 0 and u² > n > 0, then n/u < √n < u, and the trap [n/u, u] has width (u² − n)/u.

u^2 \gt n \Rightarrow u \gt \sqrt n @@ squares keep order on numbers above 0
\big(\tfrac nu\big)^2 = \tfrac{n^2}{u^2} \lt \tfrac{n^2}{n} = n @@ u² > n puts a bigger number under n²
\tfrac nu \lt \sqrt n @@ the same order, read the other way
u - \tfrac nu = \tfrac{u^2 - n}{u} @@ one fraction (5h)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac nu \lt \sqrt n \lt u,\ \text{width } \tfrac{u^2 - n}{u}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

Call u² − n the overshoot of u. A guess with overshoot 0 is the root. A small overshoot makes a narrow trap.

12f2The Averaging Step, for Any n

12d averaged u and 2/u. Average u and n/u instead: u′ = (u + n/u)/2, one fraction (u² + n)/(2u). Its overshoot is a square.

Square-overshoot technique: if u > 0 and u′ = (u + n/u)/2, then u′² − n = (u² − n)²/(4u²). So u′ is never below √n, whatever u was, and its overshoot is the old one squared, over 4u².

u' = \tfrac{u^2 + n}{2u} @@ the average, as one fraction
u'^2 - n = \tfrac{(u^2 + n)^2 - 4nu^2}{4u^2} @@ square it, and write n over 4u²
(u^2 + n)^2 - 4nu^2 = (u^2 - n)^2 @@ the square of a sum less 4ab is the square of a difference (4j)
\htmlClass{new}{u'^2 - n = \tfrac{(u^2 - n)^2}{4u^2} \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a square over a square

After the first step every guess is above the root or on it, so the root-trap technique applies at every step after that. A guess below the root is no trouble: the first step lifts it over.

12f3√7 With Fractions

Start at 3, since 3² = 9 is near 7. Each line is one averaging step, kept as a fraction, with its overshoot and its trap.

u_0 = 3,\ \ u_0^2 - 7 = 2 @@ start: the trap [7/3, 3] is a third wide
u_1 = \tfrac{3 + 7/3}{2} = \tfrac83,\ \ \tfrac{64}{9} - 7 = \tfrac19 @@ the square-overshoot technique: 2²/(4 · 9) = 1/9
u_2 = \tfrac{8/3 + 21/8}{2} = \tfrac{127}{48},\ \ \tfrac{16129}{2304} - 7 = \tfrac1{2304} @@ 127² = 16129 and 7 · 48² = 16128: one apart
\tfrac{336}{127} \lt \sqrt7 \lt \tfrac{127}{48} @@ the root-trap technique: width (1/2304)/(127/48) = 1/6096
u_3 = \tfrac{32257}{12192},\ \ u_3^2 - 7 = \tfrac{1}{148\,644\,864} @@ the overshoot squares again: 12192² = 148 644 864
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt7 = 2.6457513 \ldots} \quad \blacksquare @@ the trap is now narrower than 3 in a billion

From 8/3 on, the fractions come out one apart: 8² and 7 · 3² differ by 1, and so do 127² and 7 · 48². That is no accident of 7. Whenever an overshoot is 1 over a square, the next fraction’s top squared and n times its bottom squared differ by 1 again.

12f4How Fast the Trap Closes

12e’s trap halves at every step: ten halvings buy three places. Here the overshoot squares. Once u is above √n, 4u² is at least 4n, so the new overshoot is at most the old one squared, over 4n.

Doubling-places technique: once u ≥ √n, each averaging step takes an overshoot g to at most g²/(4n); so when g is below 1, the number of places the overshoot is good to at least doubles at each step.

u \ge \sqrt n \Rightarrow 4u^2 \ge 4n @@ squares keep order
g' = \tfrac{g^2}{4u^2} \le \tfrac{g^2}{4n} @@ the square-overshoot technique, with a bigger bottom
g \lt 10^{-k} \Rightarrow g^2 \lt 10^{-2k} @@ squaring doubles the count of zeros after the point
\htmlClass{new}{g \lt 10^{-k} \Rightarrow g' \lt 10^{-2k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 4n ≥ 1 for n ≥ 1/4

For √7 the overshoots ran 2, 1/9, 1/2304, 1/148 644 864: about 0.1, then 0.0004, then 0.000000007. Bisection on [2, 3] needs 29 halvings to reach the width the third averaging step gave.

12f5Choosing the Start

Any start above 0 works, since the first step lifts it over the root. A good start saves steps. Take the whole number whose square is nearest n. For a large n, split its digits in pairs from the point: each pair is one digit of the root, so 5300, read 53 | 00, has a root of two digits, and 7² = 49 is the nearest square below 53, so the root is about 70.

73^2 = 5329,\ \ g = 29 @@ 72² = 5184 is further off
u' = \tfrac{73 + 5300/73}{2} = \tfrac{10629}{146} @@ one averaging step
g' = \tfrac{29^2}{4 \cdot 5329} = \tfrac{841}{21316} \lt 0.04 @@ the square-overshoot technique
\text{width } \tfrac{g'}{u'} \lt \tfrac{0.04}{72} \lt 0.0006 @@ the root-trap technique
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt{5300} = 72.80 \ldots} \quad \blacksquare @@ 10629/146 = 72.8013…, and the trap is narrower than 0.0006

Long division does the rest by hand: n/u to as many places as you want, then the average. Carry two more places than the overshoot promises, and no more.

12f7Running It Backwards

Handed a guess, square it and subtract n: that is its overshoot, and the overshoot over the guess is the width of its trap. Handed a width you need, square the overshoot until it passes: each step about doubles the places. Handed a fraction p/q with p² − nq² = 1, say at once that p/q is above √n by less than 1/(pq), and that the next step keeps the pattern. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The averaging step is often called Heron’s method, after Heron of Alexandria, who in his Metrica (first century) finds √720 this way: 27 squared is 729, 720/27 is 26 2/3, and their average 26 5/6 is his answer, with a remark that the step can be run again. It is older than Heron. A Babylonian clay tablet, YBC 7289 at Yale, gives √2 as 1;24,51,10 in base sixty, which is 1.41421296, right to six places. How it was found is not written on it; the averaging step is one suggested way. The tablet of question 12f.8.4 is that one.

Accepted names: the averaging step is Heron’s method or the Babylonian method; it is Newton’s method run on x² − n (13m). The doubling-places technique says it converges quadratically. Fractions p/q with p² − nq² = 1 are solutions of Pell’s equation (43k).
