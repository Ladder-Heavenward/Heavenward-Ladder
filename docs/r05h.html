5h · technique

Fractions by hand, and decimals

Every working rule for fractions is one of the twelve techniques, or 5c's definitions, in working clothes. Putting a fraction in lowest terms is choosing a member of its class, and rung 4's greatest common divisor chooses it in one step. Multiplying is 5c's line. Dividing is multiplying by a reciprocal. Adding needs one denominator for both, and the least such denominator is read off the prime lists. Fractions inside fractions come apart by one multiplication, and fractions in an equation are cleared by 5g's multiplying-keeps-answers. Then the step runs 4i's place value on past the ones and builds the finite decimals as fractions over powers of ten. It proves exactly which fractions have one, rounds the rest to the nearest hundredth, and gives the hundredth its everyday name, percent.

A fraction has many names: pick the one that makes the job easy. To multiply, multiply numerators and denominators, cancelling first. To divide, multiply by the reciprocal. To add, rename both over one denominator. To write a decimal, find a name whose denominator is a power of ten; one exists exactly when the denominator's only primes are 2 and 5.

5h1One fraction, many names

5c built 3/4 as a class: the pairs (3, 4), (6, 8), (−3, −4), (30, 40) and every other pair that cross-multiplies with (3, 4) to the same thing. Any member names the fraction. So the first skill with fractions is not a calculation at all; it is choosing a member. 5c's scaling says (kp)/(kq) = p/q for any integer k other than 0, so going from 3/4 to 6/8 is free, and so is going back. One member is special.

From here on, for a fraction pair (p, q), the statement “(p, q) is in lowest terms” means its denominator q is positive and gcd(n, q) = 1. Here n is the number with p = n or p = −n (5b's member-with-a-0).

Lowest-terms technique: every fraction has a member in lowest terms. In words: take a member with a positive denominator, divide numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor, and nothing more can be divided out.

One-lowest-terms technique: if (p, q) and (r, s) are both in lowest terms and p/q = r/s, then p = r and q = s. Take the case where p is a positive number n. (The case p = 0 forces r = 0 and q = s = 1, since gcd(0, q) = q. The negative case is the same column run on −p and −r.) Then ns = qr by cross-multiplying, so r is positive and is a number m, and ns = qm. Now q ∣ qm = ns, and gcd(q, n) = 1, so q ∣ s by factor-that-shares-nothing. In the same way s ∣ q. So q = s by 4j's divides-both-ways, and n = m by cancel for times. ∎ So every fraction has exactly one name in lowest terms, and asking for it is asking a question with one answer.

5h2Multiplying, and cancelling first

5c's line multiplies numerators and denominators: (p/q)(r/s) = (pr)/(qs). The skill is to cancel before multiplying, while the numbers are small. All four numbers sit in one product on top and one below. So by regroup for times on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ, any factor of either numerator may be paired with any factor of either denominator, and scaling removes it.

\tfrac{14}{15}\cdot\tfrac{25}{28} = \tfrac{14 \cdot 25}{15 \cdot 28} @@ 5c's line for · on ℚ
\phantom{\tfrac{14}{15}\cdot\tfrac{25}{28}} = \tfrac{(\htmlClass{new}{14}\cdot 1)(\htmlClass{keep}{5}\cdot 5)}{(\htmlClass{keep}{5}\cdot 3)(\htmlClass{new}{14}\cdot 2)} @@ write each as a product: 25 = 5 · 5, 15 = 5 · 3, 28 = 14 · 2
\phantom{\tfrac{14}{15}\cdot\tfrac{25}{28}} = \tfrac{(14\cdot 5)(1\cdot 5)}{(14\cdot 5)(3\cdot 2)} = \tfrac{5}{6} @@ regroup for times on ℤ, turn-about for times on ℤ to bring the shared factors together; scaling with k = 70

To work with fractions whose numerators and denominators are themselves rationals (and soon letters), the same techs are needed one level up. Reciprocal-of-a-product technique: if b ≠ 0 and k ≠ 0, then (bk)⁻¹ = b⁻¹k⁻¹. By middle-swap (for ·), (bk)(b⁻¹k⁻¹) = (bb⁻¹)(kk⁻¹) = 1 · 1 = 1, and 5c's reciprocal says the reciprocal is the only rational that does this. ∎ From it, the fractions-of-rationals technique, for rationals u, c and b, d, k other than 0:

iReciprocal-of-a-product; then regroup for times on ℚ, turn-about for times on ℚ and reciprocal on ℚ bring kk⁻¹ = 1 together; times-one on ℚ
iiMiddle-swap (for ·), then reciprocal-of-a-product turned about
iiiSpread on ℚ turned about, with turn-about for times on ℚ
iv5c's line for dividing; (cd⁻¹)⁻¹ = dc⁻¹ by reciprocal-of-a-product and reciprocal; then ii

Part i is “multiply numerator and denominator by the same thing”, now for any rational k other than 0. Part iv is “invert and multiply”: not a trick, but 5c's definition of dividing with the reciprocal written out. Brackets matter: (1/2)/3 = 1/6, while 1/(2/3) = 3/2. Dividing has no regrouping rule, so a stacked fraction is drawn with its main bar longest, to show which division is done last.

5h3Adding: one denominator for both

Adding is only easy over one denominator (fractions-of-rationals iii): 9 twelfths and 8 twelfths are 17 twelfths. So first rename both fractions over a common denominator. Fractions-of-rationals i with k = d and with k = b gives the general line:

That always works (it is 5c's definition), but the numbers get large. The smallest common denominator keeps them small.

From here on, for numbers q and s other than 0, lcm(q, s) names the least member of the set of numbers l ≠ 0 with q ∣ l and s ∣ l. Read it “the least common multiple of q and s”.

The name is honest. The set is settled (4h's word), because ∣ is settled (4j's settled-divides) and so is l ≠ 0 (settled-order). And it has the member qs, which is not 0 by no-zero-product. So 4h's least-member gives it a least member. To find it, read the prime lists. If q ∣ l then l = qk. By one-prime-list, the prime list of l is q's list together with k's, rearranged. So every common multiple carries each prime at least as often as q does, and at least as often as s does. The list that takes each prime as often as whichever denominator has it most is therefore a common multiple that divides every other, and by 4j's divisor-not-past it is the least. With q and s the denominators of two fractions, lcm(q, s) has the accepted name least common denominator.

\tfrac56 + \tfrac{7}{10} @@ denominators: 6 has the list 3, 2; 10 has 5, 2
\operatorname{lcm}(6, 10) = 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 30 @@ each prime as often as the denominator that has it most
\tfrac{5\cdot\htmlClass{new}{5}}{6\cdot\htmlClass{new}{5}} + \tfrac{7\cdot\htmlClass{new}{3}}{10\cdot\htmlClass{new}{3}} @@ fractions-of-rationals i: each denominator times what it lacks of 30
\tfrac{25}{30} + \tfrac{21}{30} = \tfrac{46}{30} @@ fractions-of-rationals iii: one denominator, add the numerators
\phantom{\tfrac{25}{30} + \tfrac{21}{30}} = \tfrac{23}{15} @@ lowest terms: gcd(46, 30) = 2

The tempting illegal move is to add numerators and add denominators: ½ + ⅓ “=” 2/5. (⅓, ¼ and ⅕ are 1/3, 1/4 and 1/5 written small, as ½ is 1/2 from 5f: one mark each, naming the same rational.) 5c showed that this is not even a rule on fractions: with the member 2/4 for ½ it gives 3/7 instead. And 2/5 is less than ½, though something positive was added to ½. Yet the fake has a real property, whichever members you feed it. Fake-sum-between technique: if b and d are positive and u/b < c/d, then u/b < (u + c)/(b + d) < c/d. By 5f's compare-by-cross-multiplying, u/b < c/d says ud < bc. Adding ub to both (keep-order for plus on ℚ) gives ub + ud < ub + bc, which is u(b + d) < b(u + c). Compare-by-cross-multiplying reads that back as u/b < (u + c)/(b + d). The other half is the same with cd added. ∎ The fake is a fine way to find a fraction between two others, and a terrible way to add them.

Subtracting is adding the negative, and a minus in front of a fraction belongs to its whole numerator: −(u/b) = (−u)/b (5d's line, with negative-out on ℚ). So 1 − (x − 1)/2 = (2 − (x − 1))/2 = (3 − x)/2. Writing 2 − x − 1 there forgets that the minus reaches the 1 as well.

5h4Fractions with letters in them

Nothing changes when numerators and denominators have letters in them, except one thing. A denominator with a letter may be 0 for some value of the letter, and there the fraction names nothing. Say so on the first line, and keep saying it.

\tfrac1x + \tfrac{1}{x+1} @@ x ≠ 0 and x ≠ −1, or a denominator is 0
\tfrac{1\cdot\htmlClass{new}{(x+1)}}{x\,\htmlClass{new}{(x+1)}} + \tfrac{1\cdot\htmlClass{new}{x}}{(x+1)\,\htmlClass{new}{x}} @@ fractions-of-rationals i, twice: rename each over x(x + 1)

(Agreeing at one number does not prove the moves right; disagreeing proves them wrong.) Cancelling with letters has the same small print as with numbers. 5h5Fractions inside fractions

A fraction whose numerator or denominator holds fractions comes apart in one move. Use fractions-of-rationals i with k a common denominator of all the inner fractions. It is multiplying by k/k, and it clears every inner bar at once.

\dfrac{1 + \frac1x}{1 - \frac1x} @@ x ≠ 0 (an inner denominator) and x ≠ 1 (the outer denominator would be 0)
\dfrac{(1 + \frac1x)\cdot\htmlClass{new}{x}}{(1 - \frac1x)\cdot \htmlClass{new}{x}} @@ fractions-of-rationals i with k = x, allowed since x ≠ 0
\dfrac{x + 1}{x - 1} @@ spread on ℚ on each, and (1/x) · x = 1 by reciprocal on ℚ

The tidy answer names the same rational as the tangle at every allowed x, and you should say which x were excluded. Fractions stacked many deep come apart from the inside out. Start from 1 and keep replacing a fraction r by 1 + 1/r. You get 2, then 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, 13/8, each a tangle a level deeper than the one before, each untangled by one use of fractions-of-rationals.

5h6Clearing fractions from an equation

An equation full of fractions becomes one with none by a single change from 5g: multiply both sides by a common denominator (multiplying-keeps-answers). When the denominators are numbers, that is licensed: x/3 + x/4 = 7 times 12 is 4x + 3x = 84, so x = 12. When x is in a denominator, the common denominator has x in it, and 5g's table says what that costs: strangers can walk in exactly where it is 0. Those x were never allowed in the first line, so throw them out.

x = 2x - 3 @@ rewrite: spread on ℚ, negative-out on ℚ, regroup for plus on ℚ

5h7Splitting: adding run backwards

Adding makes one fraction from several. Running it backwards finds pieces that add to a given fraction, and that is often the more useful direction. Fractions-of-rationals iii backwards splits a sum in the numerator: (x + 1)/x = x/x + 1/x = 1 + 1/x. A little arithmetic splits a number: 5/6 = 1/2 + 1/3. And one split pays for itself many times:

Check it by adding the right side over n(n + 1): ((n + 1) − n)/(n(n + 1)) = 1/(n(n + 1)). Now 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/12 + 1/20 is (1 − ½) + (½ − ⅓) + (⅓ − ¼) + (¼ − ⅕). Everything in the middle meets its negative, leaving 1 − 1/5 = 4/5.

From here on, a unit fraction is a fraction 1/q with q a number other than 0.

An Egyptian scribe, copying an older text, set out how to write fractions as sums of different unit fractions; two fifths, for instance, is a third and a fifteenth. Many centuries later a merchant’s son from Pisa gave a method that always succeeds: take the largest unit fraction that fits, and repeat on what is left.

For 3/7 the largest unit fraction that fits is 1/3, leaving 3/7 − 1/3 = 2/21; the largest that fits 2/21 is 1/11, leaving 1/231. So 3/7 = 1/3 + 1/11 + 1/231.

5h8Decimals: fractions over a power of ten

The fractions that decimals name need everyday words first.

From here on, the words third, quarter (or fourth), fifth, sixth, and so on, tenth, hundredth, thousandth, name the reciprocals 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, 1/6, …, 1/10, 1/100, 1/1000. “Three quarters” names 3 · (1/4), which is 3/4.

These order words gain a job: the third of a list, or a third thing, keeps its everyday use, and “a third”, “two thirds” of something name the fraction.

4i's reading rule writes 406 as 4 · 10² + 0 · 10 + 6: each place is worth ten times the place to its right. Now run the places on past the ones, with a point to mark where the ones end. Each step to the right is worth a tenth of the place before: tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths.

From here on, a numeral, then a point, then a list of k digits e₁ e₂ ⋯ eₖ with k ≠ 0, as in 2.54, names the rational N + E/10^k. Here N is the value of the numeral before the point. E is the value of the list e₁ ⋯ eₖ read by 4i's reading rule (whose start and step read any list of digits, 0s at the front included). It is a finite decimal, and its digits after the point are its decimal places. A − in front names its negative.

So 2.54 = 2 + 54/100 = 254/100 = 127/50, and 0.375 = 375/1000 = 3/8 (gcd 125). Running it backwards, a fraction has a finite decimal when some member has a power of ten for its denominator: 7/20 = 35/100 = 0.35. Which fractions have one? First, a small technique 4g left for a question. Power-of-a-product technique: (wb)^k = w^k b^k for numbers. By induction on k: (wb)⁰ = 1 = 1 · 1; and (wb)^{S(k)} = (wb)^k · wb = (w^k b^k)(wb) = (w^k w)(b^k b) = w^{S(k)} b^{S(k)}, by power-of-a-next and middle-swap (for ·). ∎ So 10^k = 2^k · 5^k.

Finite-decimal technique: a fraction has a finite decimal exactly when its denominator in lowest terms has no prime in its prime list but 2 and 5. Take a positive fraction p/q in lowest terms (for a negative one, run it on the negative; 0 is 0.0).

q = 2^a \cdot 5^b @@ given: q's prime list has only 2s and 5s (one-prime-list's list, rearranged by turn-about for times and regroup for times)
k \text{ the greater of } a, b;\ \ a + j = k,\ \ b + i = k @@ any-two-compare; at-most-is-adding, twice
q \cdot (2^j 5^i) = 2^{a+j}\, 5^{b+i} = 2^k 5^k = \htmlClass{new}{10^k} @@ middle-swap (for ·), powers-add-their-counts; the power-of-a-product technique

So 3/8 = 0.375 (8 = 2³), 7/40 = 0.175, 1/25 = 0.04. But 1/3, 1/6, 5/12 and 1/7 have no finite decimal, because 3 and 7 are not 2 or 5. That is not a failure of patience. Finite-decimal proves that no power of ten, however large, is a multiple of 3.

Decimal arithmetic is fraction arithmetic in this uniform. Lining up the points to add is choosing a common denominator: 0.4 + 0.25 = 40/100 + 25/100 = 0.65. Multiplying multiplies the denominators, which is why the decimal places add: 0.3 · 0.02 = (3/10)(2/100) = 6/1000 = 0.006. The tempting slip is to line up the last digits instead of the points: 0.4 + 0.25 is not 0.29, because 4 tenths and 25 hundredths are counts of different-sized pieces.

What can be done with 1/7? Run 4j's division with remainder on it, one place at a time. 10 = 7 · 1 + 3 gives the digit 1 and the remainder 3. 30 = 7 · 4 + 2 gives 4 and 2. Then come 2, 8, 5, 7, with remainders 6, 4, 5, 1. The remainder 1 is where it started, so from here every step repeats: 0.142857, then 142857 again, without end. The right picture runs these steps for any fraction. What an endless run of digits could name is a question for rung 8; on this rung, what you can always do is round.

5h9Rounding

From here on, for a positive rational v, let k be the number with k/100 ≤ v < (k + 1)/100. (For v = p/q, k is the quotient of 100p by q, by 4j's division-with-remainder.) Then v rounded to two places, or to the nearest hundredth, is k/100 when v − k/100 < (k + 1)/100 − v, and (k + 1)/100 otherwise. For a negative rational, round its negative and write − in front. Rounding to m places is the same with 10^m in place of 100.

“Nearest” has a meaning made only of differences and order. v is nearer to k/100 when the difference up from k/100 to v is less than the difference up from v to (k + 1)/100. When the two differences are the same, the line sends v up; that choice is a convention, not a technique. In practice the remainder decides. If 100p = qk + r with r < q, then v − k/100 = r/(100q) and (k + 1)/100 − v = (q − r)/(100q). So v rounds down exactly when r < q − r, that is, when 2r < q (compare-by-cross-multiplying, then keep-order for plus on ℚ).

\tfrac13:\ \ 100 = 3\cdot 33 + \htmlClass{new}{1},\ \ 2\cdot 1 \lt 3 @@ division-with-remainder; the test: round down
\tfrac13 \text{ rounds to } 0.33 @@ k/100 with k = 33
\tfrac23:\ \ 200 = 3 \cdot 66 + \htmlClass{new}{2},\ \ 2\cdot 2 \ge 3 @@ round up
\tfrac23 \text{ rounds to } 0.67 @@ (k + 1)/100 with k = 66
\tfrac18 = 0.125:\ \ 100 = 8\cdot 12 + 4,\ \ 2\cdot 4 = 8 @@ exactly halfway: the line sends it up, to 0.13

From here on, v ≈ b (read “v is about equal to b”) is a statement: it says that b is v rounded to as many decimal places as b shows. It is a statement about rounding, not a sameness: 1/3 ≈ 0.33, and 1/3 ≠ 0.33.

5h10Percent

From here on, for a rational n, n% names n/100. Read it “n percent”: per cent is Latin for “by the hundred”. And n% of w is (n/100) · w.

So 20% = 20/100 = 1/5, and 20% of 60 is 12. A percent change is multiplying by one fraction. A rise of 20% multiplies by 1 + 20/100 = 6/5, a fall of 20% by 4/5. Seeing the multiplier makes running it backwards easy, and that is where the classic slip lives.

\tfrac65\, P = 60 @@ after a rise of 20% the price is 60: what was it before?
P = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac56}\cdot 60 = 50 @@ multiplying-keeps-answers with 5/6, the reciprocal of 6/5
\tfrac65 \cdot 50 = 60 \;\checkmark @@ check forward

The tempting wrong answer takes 20% off 60 and gets 48. But 20% of the new price is not 20% of the old: the rise was 20% of 50, which is 10, and 20% of 60 is 12. Undoing a multiplication by 6/5 is multiplying by 5/6, a fall of 1/6, which is 16.67% to two places. Percents of different wholes are fractions of different things. Two changes in a row multiply: 50% off and then 20% off leaves (1/2)(4/5) = 2/5 of the price, which is 60% off, not 70%.

Who said it first

The scribe is Ahmes, and the merchant’s son is Fibonacci. The factor-that-shares-nothing technique, in its prime case, is Euclid’s lemma.

The scribe Ahmes, copying an older text, set out how to write fractions as sums of different unit fractions; two fifths, for instance, is a third and a fifteenth. Many centuries later Fibonacci gave a method that always succeeds: take the largest unit fraction that fits, and repeat on what is left.

the Rhind papyrus (copied about 1550 BCE); Fibonacci, Liber Abaci (1202)
