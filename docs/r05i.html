5i · technique

Expanding and Factoring

One rule runs this step, the spread technique on the fractions: a number times a sum is the number times each part, added up. Read left to right, it multiplies out a product of sums, and that always works: it is a procedure. Read right to left, it puts a product back together, and that needs you to recognise what the sum came from. This step makes both directions fast. A product of two sums is laid out as a grid of cells, one cell for each pair of terms. Three products come up so often that they are worth knowing on sight both ways: the square of a sum, the square of a difference, and the difference of squares. Then comes factoring: by a common factor, by grouping, and by the two numbers whose sum and product you are given. A proof follows that whole numbers are enough to search. And, as always, the illegal moves and the whole thing run backwards.

Expanding spreads each term of one sum over each term of the other and collects. Factoring reads a sum and says which product made it. It may be a common factor, a difference of squares, a square of a sum, or two numbers that multiply to the last term and add to the middle one. To build one that factors your way, multiply out the factors you want.

5i1One Rule, Two Directions

One of the founders of algebra proposed writing the quantities being sought with vowels and the given ones with consonants. Then a whole kind of problem could be worked at once, instead of one set of numbers at a time. He called the new art a way to leave no problem unsolved.

Some words first, because this step talks about the pieces of sums and products all the time.

From here on, the pieces joined by + in a sum are its terms, and the pieces joined by · in a product are its factors. (w − b counts as the sum w + (−b), with terms w and −b.) In a term such as 5x or −3xy, the number in front is its coefficient. To expand a product of sums is to rewrite it as a sum, by spread on ℚ. To factor a sum is to rewrite it as a product, by spread on ℚ turned about. Terms that differ only in their coefficients, such as 3x and 5x, are like terms. Collecting them is spread on ℚ turned about: 3x + 5x = (3 + 5)x = 8x.

From here on, 4g's powers carry over to the rationals. For a rational r and a number n, rⁿ names the one function of n with r⁰ = 1 and r to the power S(n) equal to rⁿ · r. So r³ = r² · r and r⁴ = r³ · r.

5h's phrase “lowest terms” keeps its meaning: there the terms are a fraction pair's numerator and denominator.

Expanding 3x(2x − 5) uses spread on ℚ forwards: 6x² − 15x. Factoring 6x² − 15x uses it backwards: see that 3x is a factor of each term, and bring it out to the front. Both rewrite (5g). They change how a side is written, never what it names, so nothing is ever done to the other side.

Notice which way the rule goes. Times spreads over plus; plus does not spread over times. 1 + 2 · 3 = 7, but (1 + 2)(1 + 3) = 12. In rung 1's logic each of ∧ and ∨ spreads over the other (1f's techniques spread and over or, and spread or over and). The numbers keep only one of the two. So a tech that is legal for ∧ and ∨ can be illegal for · and +.

The two directions are not equally hard, for the same reason that multiplying 17 by 23 is easy and finding the factors of 391 is not. Forward, you follow a rule. Backward, you have to recognise what you are looking at. So the way to get good at factoring is to expand a great deal, watching what the answers look like. Every pattern you learn to see is a product you have expanded often enough to know on sight.

5i2A Product of Sums: The Grid of Cells

For two sums, use spread on ℚ twice: spread the first sum, taken as one thing, over the second's terms, then spread each piece. Grid-of-cells technique: (w + b)(c + d) = wc + wd + bc + bd.

(a + b)(c + d) = (a + b)c + (a + b)d @@ spread on ℚ, with a + b taken as one rational
\phantom{(a + b)(c + d)} = c(a + b) + d(a + b) @@ turn-about for times on ℚ, twice
\phantom{(a + b)(c + d)} = (ca + cb) + (da + db) @@ spread on ℚ, twice
\phantom{(a + b)(c + d)} = \htmlClass{new}{ac + bc + ad + bd} @@ turn-about for times on ℚ on each product; regroup for plus on ℚ lets the brackets go
\phantom{(a + b)(c + d)} = ac + ad + bc + bd \quad \blacksquare @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ, inside

Every term of one sum meets every term of the other exactly once. Lay that out as a grid, the way 2h laid out a product of two sets. Give it a row for each term of the first sum and a column for each term of the second. In each cell goes the product of its row's term and its column's term. The product of the sums is the sum of all the cells. A sum of m terms times a sum of n terms has m · n cells, by 4k's product rule. The same spreading, done again and again, proves the grid for any m and n: (x + 2)(x² − x + 3) has a grid of two rows and three columns, six cells. Here is the commonest case, with the cells read off row by row:

(2x - 3)(x + 4) @@ rows 2x and −3; columns x and 4
\htmlClass{new}{2x \cdot x} + 2x \cdot 4 + (-3)x + (-3)4 @@ the grid-of-cells technique: the four cells
2x^2 + 8x - 3x - 12 @@ regroup for times on ℚ, turn-about for times on ℚ; 5f's line for ²; negative-out on ℚ
2x^2 + \htmlClass{new}{5x} - 12 @@ collect the like terms: 8x − 3x = 5x

The one place it goes wrong is signs. A minus belongs to the term after it. So in (2x − 3)(x + 4) the second row is −3, not 3, and the last cell is −3 · 4 = −12. The left picture draws the grid for any two sums of the shape mx + p, one cell per product, coloured by the kind of term it holds.

5i3Three Products to Know Both Ways

Square-of-a-sum technique: (w + b)² = w² + 2wb + b². By 5f's line, (w + b)² is (w + b)(w + b), and grid-of-cells gives ww + wb + bw + bb. Turn-about for times on ℚ makes bw into wb, and wb + wb = 2wb by 5f's w + w = 2w. ∎ Square-of-a-difference technique: (w − b)² = w² − 2wb + b². It is square-of-a-sum with −b in the place of b. The middle cells are w(−b) twice, which is −2wb by negative-out on ℚ, and the last is (−b)² = b² by two-negatives. ∎ Difference-of-squares technique: (w + b)(w − b) = w² − b².

(a + b)(a + (-b)) = aa + a(-b) + ba + b(-b) @@ ﹀ −; the grid-of-cells technique
\phantom{(a + b)(a + (-b))} = a^2 + (-(ab)) + ab + (-(b^2)) @@ negative-out on ℚ, twice; turn-about for times on ℚ; 5f's line for ²
\phantom{(a + b)(a + (-b))} = a^2 + \htmlClass{new}{0} + (-(b^2)) @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ, regroup for plus on ℚ; negative on ℚ: the middle cells cancel
\phantom{(a + b)(a + (-b))} = a^2 - b^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ plus-zero on ℚ; ︿ −

Square-of-a-difference has a name on this ladder of its own. With a letter in the place of w and a fixed rational in the place of b, it reads (x − h)² = x² − 2hx + h².

From here on, (x − h)², with h a rational kept fixed, is called the displacement pattern: x less a fixed amount, then squared. Square-of-a-difference expands it as x² − 2hx + h².

Its signs, read backwards: the end terms are squares, x² and h², and the middle term is twice the product of what was squared, with the sign of −h. So x² − 10x + 25 is (x − 5)², since 25 = 5² and 10x = 2 · 5 · x. Rung 8 builds on this pattern. The accepted name for a three-term sum that is such a square, like x² − 10x + 25, is a perfect square trinomial.

Difference-of-squares is the one that hides best: in its grid the two middle cells are −wb and wb, and they cancel. It does arithmetic in your head: 47 · 53 = (50 − 3)(50 + 3) = 2500 − 9 = 2491, and 98 · 102 = 10000 − 4 = 9996. And it completes the foreclosure 5g left. Same-squares technique: if w² = b², then w = b or w = −b. From w² = b², adding-keeps-answers gives w² − b² = 0. Difference-of-squares makes that (w + b)(w − b) = 0. 5e's zero-product says w + b = 0 or w − b = 0, and adding-keeps-answers finishes each case. ∎ That is exactly why squaring both sides lets strangers in: knowing the squares agree leaves two options open.

The tempting illegal move is the one everybody makes once: (w + b)² = w² + b². Test it: (3 + 4)² = 49, but 3² + 4² = 25. The missing 24 is 2 · 3 · 4, the two middle cells of the grid. Squaring spreads over a product, (wb)² = w²b² (middle-swap for ·), but not over a sum. Its cousin: x² + 9 is not (x + 3)(x − 3), which is x² − 9.

5i4Factoring: Read the Sum, Name the Product

From here on, a sum with an unknown x in it is factored when it is written as a single product, of two or more factors. The product must name the same rational as the sum at every rational x.

Try these in order. Each is a product you have just seen expanded.

  • A common factor. Always first. If every term shares a factor, bring it out: 12x² − 18x = 6x(2x − 3). Check by expanding back.
  • Two terms, a square less a square. Difference-of-squares: 9x² − 25 = (3x)² − 5² = (3x + 5)(3x − 5).
  • Three terms with squares at both ends. Test for square-of-a-sum or square-of-a-difference: is the middle term twice the product of what was squared? 4x² + 12x + 9 is (2x + 3)², since 12x = 2 · 2x · 3.
  • x² + px + q. Two numbers whose product is q and whose sum is p. Below.
  • Four terms. Group them in pairs and look for a common bracket. Below.

Sum-and-product technique: (x + m)(x + n) = x² + (m + n)x + mn. Grid-of-cells gives xx + xn + mx + mn; turn-about for times on ℚ and spread on ℚ turned about collect xn + mx as (m + n)x. ∎ So in x² + px + q the last term is the product of the two numbers and the middle coefficient is their sum. To factor, list the pairs of integers whose product is q, and look for the one whose sum is p.

x^2 - x - 12 @@ need mn = −12 and m + n = −1
\begin{aligned}&(1,-12),\,(-1,12),\,(2,-6),\,(-2,6),\\ &\quad \,(\htmlClass{new}{3,-4}),\,(-3,4)\end{aligned} @@ the integer pairs with product −12; one positive and one negative, since −12 is negative
3 + (-4) = -1 \;\checkmark @@ the pair whose sum is the middle coefficient
(x + 3)(x - 4) @@ the sum-and-product technique, read backwards
x^2 - 4x + 3x - 12 = x^2 - x - 12 \;\checkmark @@ check by expanding: always

Why is it enough to search integers? Whole-answers technique: if p and q are integers and a rational r has r² + pr + q = 0, then r is an integer.

r = a/b,\ \ \text{in lowest terms, } b \text{ positive} @@ 5h's lowest-terms
a^2 + pab + qb^2 = 0 @@ multiplying-keeps-answers with b²; 5h's fractions-of-rationals and cross-multiplying clear the denominators
a^2 = b\,(-(pa + qb)) @@ adding-keeps-answers with −(pab + qb²); spread on ℚ, negative-out on ℚ, turn-about for times on ℚ
a = n \lor a = -n;\ \ a^2 = nn @@ member-with-a-0; two-negatives
b \mid nn @@ nn = b · t with t a number (t is not the negative of a positive: b is positive and nn is not), so ︿ ∣
\gcd(b, n) = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; b \mid n @@ lowest terms; 5h's factor-that-shares-nothing, with nn as n · n
b \mid n,\ b \mid b \;\Rightarrow\; b \mid \gcd(n, b) = 1 @@ the gcd's every-common-divisor part
b = 1,\ \ r = a/1 = a \quad \blacksquare @@ 4j's divisor-not-past: b ≤ 1 and b ≠ 0; 5c's naming line

So suppose x² + px + q, with integer p and q, is the product (x − r)(x − s) of two factors with rationals r and s. Then r and s are answers of x² + px + q = 0, since each makes its factor 0, by anything-times-zero. Hence they are integers, by whole-answers. The integer search finds every factoring there is. Take x² + x + 1. The integer pairs with product 1 are (1, 1) and (−1, −1), with sums 2 and −2. Neither is 1, so no factoring with fractions exists, and x² + x + 1 = 0 has no rational answers. (It has none at all. x² + x + 1 = (x + ½)² + 3/4 by square-of-a-sum, and a square is 0 or positive, 5f, so the sum is positive.) Learning that nothing works is information, not failure.

5i5Grouping, and Splitting the Middle Term

Four terms can sometimes be paired so that each pair has a common factor and the same bracket is left over:

xy + 3x + 2y + 6 @@ four terms, two unknowns
x(\htmlClass{keep}{y + 3}) + 2(\htmlClass{keep}{y + 3}) @@ spread on ℚ turned about on each pair: the same bracket appears
(x + 2)(\htmlClass{keep}{y + 3}) @@ spread on ℚ turned about once more, with y + 3 taken as one rational

If the two brackets don't match, that pairing doesn't work: try pairing the terms differently, or say that grouping fails. When x² has a coefficient other than 1, as in vx² + bx + c, grouping does the work after one preparation. Find two integers whose product is vc and whose sum is b, and split the middle term with them.

6x^2 + 7x - 3 @@ the coefficients: 6, 7 and −3
6\cdot(-3) = -18:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{9}\cdot(\htmlClass{new}{-2}) = -18,\ \ 9 + (-2) = 7 @@ two integers: product vc, sum b
6x^2 + \htmlClass{new}{9x - 2x} - 3 @@ a rewrite: 9x − 2x = 7x
3x(2x + 3) - 1(2x + 3) @@ spread on ℚ turned about on each pair; the same bracket appears
(3x - 1)(\htmlClass{keep}{2x + 3}) @@ spread on ℚ turned about, the bracket taken as one rational

Why does it work? Expand (3x − 1)(2x + 3) by the grid. The two middle cells are 9x and −2x, whose coefficients multiply to 9 · (−2) = −18. That is the same as the coefficient of x² times the number term, 6 · (−3). In any grid for (mx + p)(nx + q) the middle cells are mqx and pnx. And (mq)(pn) = (mn)(pq) by middle-swap (for ·), so the product of the middle coefficients is always v times c. Splitting the middle term finds those two cells.

One thing that is not a factoring: x² + 3x + 2 = x(x + 3) + 2. True, but it is still a sum. Factored means the whole thing is one product; this one is (x + 1)(x + 2).

5i6Factoring to Solve

5e's zero-product says a product is 0 only when a factor is. So an equation with 0 on one side and a factored product on the other splits into small equations you can solve on sight. To solve x² = x + 12, collect on one side by adding-keeps-answers: x² − x − 12 = 0. Factor: (x + 3)(x − 4) = 0. Zero-product gives x + 3 = 0 or x − 4 = 0, so the solution set is {−3, 4}. It works only with 0 on the other side. (x + 3)(x − 4) = 8 says nothing about either factor alone, since 8 is the product of many pairs of rationals.

5i8Running It Backwards: Build What Factors Your Way

Factoring is expanding run backwards. So run it backwards again: start from the factors you want, and expand. Want a sum with integer coefficients whose answers to “= 0” are 3 and −1/2? An answer r comes from a factor that is 0 at r. For 3 that is x − 3; for −1/2 it is 2x + 1, which is 0 at x = −1/2 and has no fraction in it. Expand: (x − 3)(2x + 1) = 2x² − 5x − 3. Any multiple of it by a rational other than 0 has the same answers (5g's multiplying-keeps-answers).

You can hide one pattern inside another. (x + 1)² − 9 is a difference of squares, with x + 1 in the place of w and 3 in the place of b. So it is ((x + 1) + 3)((x + 1) − 3) = (x + 4)(x − 2), and expanded it is x² + 2x − 8. Read the other way, from x² + 2x − 8 to (x + 1)² − 9, the displacement pattern has been found inside a sum that did not show it. That move is the one rung 8 builds on.

Who said it first

The founder is François Viète.

Viète proposed writing the quantities being sought with vowels and the given ones with consonants, so that a whole kind of problem could be worked at once instead of one set of numbers at a time. He called the new art a way to leave no problem unsolved.

Accepted names

A sum of three terms that is the square of a sum or of a difference, such as 4x² + 12x + 9, is called a perfect square trinomial.
