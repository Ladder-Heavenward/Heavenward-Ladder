11·chem · chemistry

Why does boiling water stop getting hotter?

Why does boiling water stop getting hotter? Put a pan of ice from the freezer on a steady flame and watch a thermometer in it. The temperature climbs to 0 °C, then stops for a long time while the ice melts. It climbs again to 100 °C, then stops for much longer while the water boils away. Draw the temperature against the heat put in, and you get a continuous curve made of pieces that climb at different rates and meet at joints: 11e exactly. Read the same measurements the other way, heat held against temperature, and the function jumps at 0 °C and at 100 °C: 11f's jumps, with sizes every chemist knows. This page reads the one experiment both ways, then uses the bookkeeping behind it, heat lost is heat gained, to measure heat in the laboratory and on paper.

Heat put into a substance either warms it, in proportion to its mass and to the rise, or changes its phase at a fixed temperature, in proportion to its mass alone. So temperature against heat put in is continuous, with joints; heat held against temperature jumps by the hidden heat at every change of phase.

Heat is not temperature

Temperature is 5j's “how hot”: what a thermometer reads. Heat is energy, in 7·phys's sense: what it takes to make something happen, never made or destroyed, only moved and changed, and measured in joules. Heat flows from a hotter thing to a colder one because of the difference in their temperatures. A substance comes in phases: solid, liquid, gas. For water they are ice, liquid water, and steam (water as a gas, also called water vapour). At open-air pressure at sea level, ice melts at its melting point, 0 °C, and water boils at its boiling point, 100 °C (5j's line for the degree Celsius).

From here on, the kelvin, written K, is a unit of temperature: a kelvin is the same size as a degree Celsius, and a temperature in kelvins is the temperature in degrees Celsius plus 273.15. Its zero, 0 K = −273.15 °C, is the coldest temperature there can be. Since 2019 it is fixed, like 5j's kilogram, by a constant of nature.

For most of the eighteenth century, heat and temperature were blurred together. A lecturer in Glasgow, in the early 1760s, pulled them apart with an experiment anyone can repeat. Bring a block of ice into a warm room. When it reaches its melting point it does not melt all at once, as it would if a little more heat were enough to turn it to water. It sits at the melting point for hours, drawing in heat the whole time while the thermometer stays still.

The Glasgow lecturer taught that when ice melts, a great quantity of heat enters it without making it any warmer. The heat is absorbed and concealed in the water it forms, where no thermometer can find it. He called such heat latent, and found that boiling conceals far more of it than melting.

The page's letters, and Δ

From here on, on this page, as is usual, q names a quantity of heat, m a mass, c a specific heat capacity (below), and T a temperature. Q names the heat put in since the start and H the heat held, both measured from a stated starting state. L_melt and L_boil name the latent heats (below). All are quantities in 5j's sense. Here L is not a limit, H is not 9a's H, and c is not a constant of 10c's lines. Nor is q 9a's function q, T 1e's table mark, or m 5j's metre, whose mark is upright.

From here on, Δ (the Greek letter delta, in its capital form) is written before the letter of a quantity to name its change, final less starting: ΔT = T − T₀, 8h's displacement of T from T₀. ΔT is one name, read “delta T”; the Δ is not a number multiplying T. 10a's small δ is a different mark.

Warming: heat in proportion to mass and to the rise

Warming a substance takes heat directly proportional (5j) to its mass, and directly proportional to the rise in temperature: twice the water, twice the heat; twice the rise, twice the heat. The constant of proportion depends on the substance and its phase. It is the substance's specific heat capacity c: the heat that warms 1 g of it by 1 °C, in J/(g · °C). So q = mcΔT. Because ΔT is a difference, it is the same in degrees Celsius and in kelvins, and c may be written in J/(g · K) as well. The three values this page needs, near the temperatures used here: ice 2.09, liquid water 4.18, steam 2.01, each in J/(g · °C). Water's is unusually large, which is why the sea warms slowly in spring and a kettle takes minutes.

q = 1000\ \text{g} \cdot 4.18\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{g} \cdot {}^\circ\text{C}} \cdot (100 - 20)\ {}^\circ\text{C} @@ a litre of water, 1000 g, from 20 °C to the boil: ΔT = 80 °C. Units cancel as 5j's letters do
q = \htmlClass{new}{334\,400}\ \text{J} @@ multiply: about a third of a million joules
t = \tfrac{334\,400\ \text{J}}{1500\ \text{J/s}} \approx 223\ \text{s} @@ a 1500 W kettle supplies 1500 J each second (7·phys's watt): under four minutes
\Delta T = \tfrac{q}{mc} @@ backwards: multiplying-keeps-answers by (mc)⁻¹ (5g), with mc ≠ 0
\Delta T = \tfrac{10\,450}{250 \cdot 4.18} = \htmlClass{new}{10}\ {}^\circ\text{C} @@ 10 450 J into 250 g of water warms it by 10 degrees

The tempting illegal move is q = mcT, with the temperature in place of its change. Warm 100 g of water from 10 °C to 30 °C. With T in degrees Celsius it gives 100 · 4.18 · 30 = 12 540 J, and with T in kelvins it gives 100 · 4.18 · 303.15 ≈ 126 717 J. The honest q = mcΔT gives 8360 J on either scale. A quantity of heat cannot depend on where someone put the zero of a scale. Only the displacement survives a change of zero.

Melting and boiling: heat with no change of temperature

At the melting point, heat goes into breaking up the ice's ordered structure, not into warming it. The heat per gram that melts a solid at its melting point is its latent heat of melting, L_melt. The heat per gram that boils a liquid at its boiling point is its latent heat of boiling, L_boil. So q = mL for a change of phase. For water, L_melt = 334 J/g and L_boil = 2260 J/g. The “concealed” heat, in modern numbers: melting a gram of ice takes as much heat as warming a gram of water by 334/4.18 ≈ 80 °C. Latent is from the Latin latere, “to lie hidden”.

Boiling is the expensive one. Boiling a gram away takes as much heat as warming about 5.4 g of water from 0 °C to 100 °C (2260/418 ≈ 5.4). Boiling the kettle's litre dry would take 2 260 000/1500 ≈ 1507 s, about 25 minutes, which is why kettles switch themselves off. It is also why steam scalds. A gram of steam condensing on skin and cooling to 37 °C gives up 2260 + 4.18 · 63 ≈ 2523 J. That is nearly ten times the 263 J a gram of water at 100 °C gives up cooling to 37 °C.

The heating curve: continuous, with joints

Now the whole experiment: 100 g of ice at −20 °C, heated steadily until it is steam at 120 °C. Five stages, each an mcΔT or an mL.

Call the temperature as a function of the heat put in T(Q), with Q in kJ on [0, 309.40]. It is a function in 9a's sense: each amount of heat put in gives one temperature. On each stage it agrees with a linear function (9e) in Q. On a warming stage it climbs 1/(mc) degrees per joule, which is 1000/(mc) degrees per kJ; on a change of phase it is level.

T = -20 + \tfrac{Q}{0.209} \quad (0 \le Q \le 4.18) @@ mc = 100 · 2.09 J per °C = 0.209 kJ per °C: rate 1/0.209 ≈ 4.78 °C per kJ
T = 0 \quad (4.18 \le Q \le 37.58) @@ melting: rate 0

T = 100 \quad (79.38 \le Q \le 305.38) @@ boiling: rate 0
T = 100 + \tfrac{Q - 305.38}{0.201} \quad (305.38 \le Q \le 309.40) @@ steam: rate 1/0.201 ≈ 4.98 °C per kJ

Each join is a place where the formula changes. Test it the way 11e taught: the heights arriving from each side, and the value.

\lim_{Q \to 4.18^-} T = -20 + \tfrac{4.18}{0.209} = -20 + 20 = 0 @@ the left piece is a polynomial in Q: continuous, so substitute (polynomials-are-continuous)
\lim_{Q \to 4.18^+} T = 0 @@ the right piece is the constant 0
T(4.18) = 0 @@ either formula gives the value: they agree at the join
\htmlClass{keep}{4.18 \text{ is a joint}} @@ glued-functions: continuous at Q = 4.18
\text{rates: } 4.78 \text{ on the left},\ \ 0 \text{ on the right} @@ different rates: continuous all the same (11e's principle, in a pan)

Every join goes the same way, because every stage starts at the temperature where the last one stopped. So T(Q) is continuous on [0, 309.40] in 11a's sense: two-sided inside, from the right at 0 and from the left at 309.40. It is a curve, 11a's word, with four joints where the rate changes all at once. The physics behind it is a premise, stated plainly. Heat put in a little at a time changes the temperature a little, or not at all; it never makes the temperature leap. And T(Q) is not one-to-one (9c): every Q from 4.18 to 37.58 has the output 0 °C.

Double the mass, and every stage takes twice the heat. Each Q at the end of a stage doubles, the levels stay at 0 °C and 100 °C, and every rate is halved. The ice's rate is about twice the water's at any mass, since c_ice is about half of c_water.

The same measurements read the other way: heat held jumps

A chemist usually asks the question the other way: at a given temperature, how much heat does the substance hold? Measure it from a fixed starting state, here ice at −20 °C, and call it H. Chemists call heat held at constant pressure enthalpy. Since H is measured from the same start as Q, the pairs (T, H) of the states of the pan are the pairs (Q, T) with their entries swapped: 9d's swapped-pairs. And 9d warned at once what that gives: T(Q) is not one-to-one, so the swapped pairs are not a function (9d's swapped-pairs-function). Read piece by piece:

H = 0.209\,(T + 20) \quad (-20 \le T \lt 0) @@ the ice piece solved for Q (5g); T + 20 is the displacement T − (−20)
H = 37.58 + 0.418\,T \quad (0 \lt T \lt 100) @@ the water piece solved for Q
H = 305.38 + 0.201\,(T - 100) \quad (100 \lt T \le 120) @@ the steam piece; T − 100 measures from the boiling point
\lim_{T \to 0^-} H = 4.18,\ \ \lim_{T \to 0^+} H = 37.58 @@ polynomials-are-continuous on each piece, side by side: both one-sided limits exist
37.58 - 4.18 = \htmlClass{new}{33.4}\ \text{kJ} = m\,L_\text{melt} @@ they differ: a jump (11f), of size 100 g · 334 J/g
\lim_{T \to 100^-} H = 79.38,\ \ \lim_{T \to 100^+} H = 305.38 @@ a jump of size \htmlClass{new}{226} kJ = m L_boil

And the value at 0 °C? There is no single honest one. A pan at exactly 0 °C may be all ice, all water, or any mixture, holding anywhere from 4.18 to 37.58 kJ. In the pairs of real states, the column at T = 0 holds a whole stretch of pairs, so 9e's column test fails there, and likewise at 100. So H is a function on [−20, 0) ∪ (0, 100) ∪ (100, 120]. It is continuous on each of the three intervals, so continuous in 11a's sense, with breaks at the holes 0 and 100. Both breaks are jumps, and 11f's mended-function says no value mends a jump: the hidden heat is exactly the size of each jump. The flats of T(Q) and the jumps of H(T) are one physical fact, a change of phase, seen from the two sides of a swap.

Chemists tabulate the jumps per mole (5·chem). Water's molar mass is 18.0 g/mol. So the jump at melting is 334 · 18.0 ≈ 6010 J ≈ 6.01 kJ per mole, and at boiling 2260 · 18.0 ≈ 40 700 J ≈ 40.7 kJ per mole: the values in every table.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change; loose, then locked

2e set beside gather and merge a principle of the second kind of care: things are made in two steps, a loose gathering and then a lock. Its question asked about slush, half-way between drift and ice. Here the slush is measured. A pan at exactly 0 °C may hold any mixture of ice and water, and the heat it holds is anywhere from 4.18 to 37.58 kJ. The middle is a real state, not a failure of the two named ones.

Name what's in the middle, and say what locked it and what would unlock it.

The lock has a price. Freezing gives up 334 J a gram, and melting takes it back. Nobody has watched one molecule lock into ice, and the price is still known to three figures. 6·chem's two-sided change and 10·bio's enzyme belong to the same principle: a change has a hill to climb through its middle, and the hill can be measured though the middle is never caught alone.

A glass of ice water sits in a warm room for ten minutes, and its thermometer reads 0 °C the whole time. Is anything happening in the glass?

Calorimetry: heat lost is heat gained

Heat is energy, and energy is never made or destroyed (7·phys). Put a hot thing and a cold thing together in a box that lets no heat in or out, and wait. The heat the hot one gives up is the heat the cold one takes in, and they end at one shared temperature. That is one equation in one unknown (5g). A calorimeter is an instrument built to make that bookkeeping exact. Two French scientists built one in the winter of 1782 to 1783. It was a vessel packed with ice at 0 °C, so that any heat given up inside shows itself as melted water you can weigh.

Their idea was to set a warm body inside a hollow of ice kept at its melting point. As the body cools to the temperature of the ice, the weight of ice it melts is a measure of the heat it has given up.

Set a 100 g iron block at 100 °C (c ≈ 0.449 J/(g · °C)) in the ice. It gives up 100 · 0.449 · 100 = 4490 J as it cools to 0 °C, and melts 4490/334 ≈ 13.4 g of ice. Run it backwards, and the melted water measures the iron's specific heat capacity. With a mixture of ice and warm water the bookkeeping has pieces, and you must know which piece you are on.

50\ \text{g ice at } {-10}\,{}^\circ\text{C} + 200\ \text{g water at } 40\,{}^\circ\text{C} @@ the question: the final temperature T?
50 \cdot 2.09 \cdot 10 + 50 \cdot 334 = 17\,745\ \text{J} @@ the heat to bring all the ice to water at 0 °C
200 \cdot 4.18 \cdot 40 = 33\,440\ \text{J} @@ the most the warm water can give by cooling to 0 °C: more, so all the ice melts
17\,745 + 50 \cdot 4.18\,T = 200 \cdot 4.18\,(40 - T) @@ heat taken in by the ice side = heat given up by the water side
17\,745 + 209\,T = 33\,440 - 836\,T @@ expand (spread on ℚ)
1045\,T = 15\,695 @@ adding-keeps-answers twice: add 836T, take away 17 745
T = \htmlClass{new}{15.0}\ {}^\circ\text{C} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers; 15 695/1045 ≈ 15.02. Check: about 20 884 J each way

The third line is not optional. Drop 100 g of ice at 0 °C into 100 g of water at 20 °C and skip it. The equation 100 · 334 + 100 · 4.18 T = 100 · 4.18 · (20 − T) gives T ≈ −30 °C, colder than anything you started with. The algebra was licensed; the equation was the wrong piece. The water can give at most 100 · 4.18 · 20 = 8360 J, which melts only about 25 g. The answer is 0 °C, with about 75 g of ice still floating: a state on a level piece of the heating curve.

That bookkeeping is itself a function with a joint. Keep the ice (100 g at 0 °C) and vary the warm water's starting temperature T₀ (100 g). Suppose 100 · 4.18 · T₀ is at most 33 400 J, that is, T₀ is at most 33 400/418 ≈ 79.9 °C. Then not all the ice melts, and the final temperature is 0 °C. Above that, all of it melts and the final temperature is T₀/2 − 33 400/836 ≈ T₀/2 − 39.95 °C. At T₀ ≈ 79.9 both pieces arrive at 0 °C: a joint, with rates 0 and ½ degree per degree. Continuous, by glued-functions; at T₀ = 100 °C the final temperature is about 10.05 °C.

The heat-sum law: adding chemical changes

Chemical changes give out or take in heat too. Heat-sum law: the heat of a change is the same whether it happens in one step or in several. A chemist in St Petersburg showed it in 1840, by careful calorimetry. Its use is that chemical changes, written as 5·chem writes them, can be added like 5g’s equations, adding their heats along with them. Write ΔH for the change in heat held, final less starting, per the moles the coefficients name: negative means heat is given out.

\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 \qquad \Delta H = -393.5\ \text{kJ} @@ burning carbon fully: measurable
\text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 \qquad \Delta H = -283.0\ \text{kJ} @@ burning carbon monoxide: measurable
\text{CO}_2 \to \text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \qquad \Delta H = \htmlClass{new}{+283.0}\ \text{kJ} @@ run the second backwards: the sign of ΔH turns about (5b's negative)
\text{C} + \text{O}_2 + \htmlClass{keep}{\text{CO}_2} \to \htmlClass{keep}{\text{CO}_2} + \text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 @@ add the first and the reversed second, side to side (5g's AP)
\text{C} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO} \qquad \Delta H = \htmlClass{new}{-110.5}\ \text{kJ} @@ take away what appears on both sides; add the heats: −393.5 + 283.0

The last change cannot be measured directly: burn carbon in too little oxygen and you get a mixture of CO and CO₂. The heat-sum law gets it by taking away. The two tempting illegal moves are the ones 5g warned of. One is reversing a change without turning the sign of its heat about. The other is doubling a change, to balance it, without doubling its heat. A ΔH belongs to the change as written, like a coefficient.

The heating curve is a heat-sum loop in disguise. Turning ice at 0 °C directly into vapour happens as frost does on a cold dry day. Per gram it takes the heat to melt plus the heat to evaporate the water at the same temperature: 334 + 2501 = 2835 J. (Water evaporating at 0 °C takes 2501 J per gram, more than at 100 °C.) The measured value is 2834 J. Go round any loop of states and back to the start, and the heats add to 0. The heat held depends only on the state, not on the path that reached it, as 7·phys's loop law said of voltage.

Who said it first

The lecturer in Glasgow is Joseph Black. The two French scientists who built the ice calorimeter are Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace. The chemist in St Petersburg is Germain Henri Hess. In 11·chem.4.4, the French chemist is Lavoisier and the engineer in Munich is Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford. The loop law of 7·phys is Kirchhoff’s.

Black taught that when ice melts, a great quantity of heat enters it without making it any warmer; the heat is absorbed and concealed in the water it forms, where no thermometer can find it. He called such heat latent, and found that boiling conceals far more of it than melting.

Black, Lectures on the Elements of Chemistry, ed. Robison (Edinburgh, 1803), from lectures of the 1760s

Lavoisier and Laplace proposed to measure heat by setting a warm body inside a hollow of ice kept at its melting point: as the body cools to the temperature of the ice, the weight of ice it melts is a measure of the heat it has given up.

Lavoisier and Laplace, “Mémoire sur la chaleur”, read to the Académie royale des sciences (1783)

Hess reported that the heat given out in forming a compound is constant, whether the compound is formed directly or by way of intermediate steps.

Hess, “Thermochemische Untersuchungen”, Annalen der Physik und Chemie 50 (1840)

Accepted names: the heat-sum law is Hess’s law, also called the law of constant heat summation; a heat-sum loop is a Hess cycle (or Born–Haber cycle, for one kind); heat held at constant pressure is enthalpy; the heat per gram that changes a phase is latent heat.
