21n · technique

Trig Integrals and Substitution; Circle Area

Every slope found on this rung, read backwards, is an area found for free. This step collects them, then goes further: it trades a hard area for an easy one by pretending the input is the height of a point going round a circle. That trade finally answers the oldest area question there is. A disc covers π times its radius times its radius.

Read the rung’s slopes backwards and you have its areas. Letting the input be the height of a point on a circle turns square roots into the circle’s own coordinates. That is how the area of the disc comes out as π times the radius squared.

21n1The Slopes, Read Backwards

Trig-area technique: ∫ cos = sin, ∫ sin = −cos, ∫ sec² = tan, ∫ sec tan = sec, ∫ 1/√(1 − x²) = arcsin and ∫ 1/(1 + x²) = arctan, each + C. And ∫ tan x dx = −ln |cos x| + C.

\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = -\frac{(\cos x)'}{\cos x} @@ the slope of cos is −sin (21g)
\int \frac{g'}{g} = \ln|g| + C @@ area-under-g′/g (17i), where g ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\int \tan x\,dx = -\ln|\cos x| + C} \quad \blacksquare @@ g = cos x

21n2Powers of Sine and Cosine

Odd-or-even technique: for an odd power, keep one factor and turn the rest into the other coordinate with the circle technique; for an even power, halve the arc.

\int \sin^3 x\,dx = \int (1 - \cos^2 x)\sin x\,dx @@ odd: keep one sin, the circle technique on the rest
= \int (1 - u^2)(-du) = -u + \tfrac{u^3}{3} + C,\ \ u = \cos x @@ substitution (16d), du = −sin x dx
\int \cos^2 x\,dx = \int \tfrac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}\,dx @@ even: half arcs (21k2)
\htmlClass{new}{\int \cos^2 x\,dx = \tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac{\sin 2x}{4} + C} \quad \blacksquare @@ straight-inside (16d) on cos 2x

21n3The Area of the Disc

Disc technique: the disc of radius r has area πr². Put x = r sin θ: as θ runs from 0 to π/2, x runs from 0 to r, and the root becomes r cos θ.

\text{area} = 4\int_0^r \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\,dx @@ four equal quarters (flips over both axes keep area)
x = r\sin\theta,\ \ dx = r\cos\theta\,d\theta,\ \ \sqrt{r^2 - x^2} = r\cos\theta @@ substitution (16d) with g = r sin; cos θ ≥ 0 on [0, π/2]
= 4r^2\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = 4r^2\Big[\tfrac{\theta}{2} + \tfrac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\Big]_0^{\pi/2} @@ odd-or-even (21n2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = 4r^2 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{4} = \pi r^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ sin π = sin 0 = 0

The same trade gives the slice of a disc between two rays an arc θ apart: its area is ½ r² θ. A whole turn, θ = 2π, gives πr² again.

21n4Putting x = a tan θ

Tan-trade technique: with x = a tan θ, a² + x² becomes a² sec² θ; so ∫ dx/(a² + x²) = (1/a) arctan(x/a) + C. And with x = a sin θ, ∫ dx/√(a² − x²) = arcsin(x/a) + C, for a > 0.

x = a\tan\theta,\ \ dx = a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta @@ the slope of tan (21g)
\int \frac{a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{a^2\sec^2\theta} = \int \frac{d\theta}{a} = \frac{\theta}{a} + C @@ 1 + tan² = sec² (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{\int \frac{dx}{a^2 + x^2} = \frac1a\arctan\frac{x}{a} + C} \quad \blacksquare @@ θ = arctan(x/a)

The accepted names

The substitutions x = a sin θ and x = a tan θ are trigonometric substitutions. The slice between two rays is a sector, with area ½r²θ.

Who said it first

In 21n.4.5, the needle is the Comte de Buffon’s (stated 1733, published 1777), and the 3408 drops are Mario Lazzarini’s (1901), whose 355/113 is now generally read as a result chosen in advance.
