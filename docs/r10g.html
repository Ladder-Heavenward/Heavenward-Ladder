10g · technique

Playing the challenge game

10a watched rounds won; 10f mostly avoided the game by rewriting. Here you play it yourself, against any challenge, with a recipe for the answer. The skill is the one the ladder is for: not spotting a move but running an inequality backwards on purpose. You start from where you must end, the output within the gap of the value, and rewrite it, one licensed move at a time, until it says something about how near the input is. Then you turn round and write it forwards for a reader. And you learn to lose on purpose: to play the challenger, with the denial of the definition worked out by rung 7's techs, and refute a claim.

Write the output's distance as the input's distance times a leftover. Promise first to stay near enough that the leftover has a ceiling. Then ask for less than the gap over the ceiling. Answer with the lesser of the two promises.

10g1Two directions, one argument

A finished proof that f heads to L reads forwards: “Let ε > 0. Put δ = (something). Suppose 0 < |x − w| < δ. Then … < ε.” Read cold, the δ seems to come from nowhere. It came from scratch work done in the other direction, starting at the goal. That is the way 4e said proofs are found: backwards from what you want, asking what would make it true, then written forwards with every line licensed. Both directions are legitimate; only the forward one is the proof. This step shows both every time.

The scratch work always has the same three beats, the method of this step. (Here a beat is a step of the method; 6·phys's beats, the swelling and fading of two close tones, keep their meaning.) Expose the displacement: rewrite |f(x) − L| so that |x − w| appears as a factor, |f(x) − L| = |x − w| · W(x). Call W(x) the leftover. Tame the leftover. If it is a constant, there is nothing to do. If not, make a first promise, |x − w| < r, chosen so that W(x) has a ceiling B on that stretch. Read off δ: then |f(x) − L| ≤ B|x − w|, and asking for |x − w| < ε/B finishes. Both promises at once: δ = min(r, ε/B).

10g2The linear function: the leftover is a constant

|f(x) - 3| \lt \varepsilon,\ \ f(x) = 5 - 2x,\ a = 1 @@ the goal; at 1 the output is 3
|\htmlClass{new}{2 - 2x}| \lt \varepsilon @@ substitute for the expression; collect
\htmlClass{new}{2}\,|x - 1| \lt \varepsilon @@ 2 − 2x = −2(x − 1); size-of-a-product; |−2| = 2
|x - 1| \lt \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}} @@ multiply by ½, positive, so the order is kept (8g). Read off δ = ε/2
  
\text{Let } \varepsilon \gt 0.\ \text{Put } \htmlClass{new}{\delta = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}}. @@ the δ, now with its origin known
\text{Suppose } 0 \lt |x - 1| \lt \delta. @@ any input the promise covers
|f(x) - 3| = |2 - 2x| = 2\,|x - 1| @@ the rewrite, forwards
\lt 2\delta = \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition, then the choice of δ

Each line of the write-up is a line of the scratch work run the other way. The leftover was 2, the size of the step (the linear function falls by 2 per step across; the bars took the sign). In general |(mx + b) − (mw + b)| = |m||x − w| and δ = ε/|m|. The linear function stretches distances by |m|, and the answer undoes the stretch. A step of 0 is the one case where the recipe divides by 0, and it does not need it: every δ wins.

10g3A square: tame the leftover with a first promise

Take x² + 3x near 1, heading to 4. One way is 10c's budget: give x² half and 3x half, and take the lesser of their answers. The other is to play it directly, and it shows the three beats cleanly.

\begin{gathered}|x^2 + 3x - 4| = |\htmlClass{new}{(x - 1)(x + 4)}|\\ = |x - 1|\,\htmlClass{keep}{|x + 4|}\end{gathered} @@ expose: x² + 3x − 4 is 0 at 1, so x − 1 is a factor (factor; sum-and-product: product −4, sum 3); size-of-a-product
\htmlClass{new}{|x - 1| \lt 1} \Rightarrow 0 \lt x \lt 2 \Rightarrow 4 \lt x + 4 \lt 6 @@ tame: a first promise of 1, unpacked by the stretch (8h), then add 4 throughout (8g)
|x + 4| \lt \htmlClass{new}{6} @@ the ceiling B = 6 on that stretch
|x^2 + 3x - 4| \lt 6\,|x - 1| \lt \varepsilon \;\Leftarrow\; |x - 1| \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{6} @@ read off
\htmlClass{new}{\delta = \min\!\big(1, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{6}\big)} @@ both promises

The tempting shortcut is illegal. δ = ε/|x + 4| has x in it, and the line says ∃δ ∀x, so δ is named before any x is handed over (2b: the order of the signs is not decoration). Lose on purpose, as 10a6 did, and the first promise earns its place: drop it, answer ε = 30 with ε/6 = 5, and x = 5.9 is in the window with its output 48.51 from 4, because out there the leftover |x + 4| is 9.9. The ceiling was only true inside the promise. With both promises, ε = 30 gets δ = 1, and every input in (0, 2) other than 1 has its output within 30 of 4, comfortably.

10g4One over x: keep off 0

\left|\tfrac1x - 2\right| = \tfrac{|1 - 2x|}{|x|} = |x - \tfrac12| \cdot \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{2}{|x|}} @@ expose: one bottom; 1 − 2x = −2(x − ½); size-of-a-product

The first promise has a second job here, the one 10a7 met: keep x away from 0, where the leftover has no ceiling at all. Size the promise by the distance to the trouble: half of it is always safe.

10g5Roots: the conjugate exposes the displacement

|\sqrt x - 2| = \tfrac{|x - 4|}{\sqrt x + 2} @@ times the conjugate over itself (√x + 2 > 0); difference-of-squares on the top
\le \tfrac{|x - 4|}{\htmlClass{new}{2}} @@ the leftover 1/(√x + 2) is at most ½, since √x ≥ 0: no promise needed for a ceiling
\htmlClass{new}{\delta = \min(4, 2\varepsilon)} @@ the 4 keeps the window inside the domain [0, ∞): it is the reach on which √x is defined near 4

So a first promise can do either job: bound the leftover, or keep the window where the function is defined. Near 0 itself no multiple of ε wins for √x: the inputs must come within ε² (10e's root-at-0), because the root climbs faster than any linear function there.

10g6Combining recipes

10c's proofs are instructions for building answers out of answers. For a sum, give each piece half the gap and take the lesser answer. For x² + 3x near 1, x² answers ε/2 with square-limit's min(1, (ε/2)/3) = min(1, ε/6). And 3x answers ε/2 with line-limit's (ε/2)/3 = ε/6. The lesser is min(1, ε/6), the same recipe the three beats found directly. For a product, limits-multiply's recipe is min(δ₁, δ₂, δ₃): a first promise for the wild factor and two budgets. For a quotient, away-from-zero's promise and reciprocal-limit's budget. You rarely need these by hand, since the techniques give the limit without any δ. But when a later proof asks for an explicit answer, this is how one is built, piece by piece, from answers you already have.

10g7A write-up, complete

Here is the full forward proof for 1/x near ½, as it would be written for a reader, every sentence a move: Let ε > 0. Put δ = min(¼, ε/8). Suppose 0 < |x − ½| < δ. Since δ ≤ ¼, x > ½ − ¼ = ¼, so x is positive and 1/|x| < 4. Then |1/x − 2| = |1 − 2x|/|x| = 2|x − ½|/|x| < 2 · 4 · |x − ½| = 8|x − ½|. Since δ ≤ ε/8, 8|x − ½| < ε. So |1/x − 2| < ε. ∎ Check it against the scratch work above: every bound used was earned by one of the two promises, and the order of the sentences is the order of the moves.

A checklist for any write-up. Open with “let ε > 0” (speak of everything). Name δ before any x appears. Suppose 0 < |x − w| < δ, the 0 < included. Spend each promise exactly where it is needed, saying which. End with |f(x) − L| < ε. If a line uses a bound, the line above must have earned it.

10g8Losing on purpose: the challenger’s sentence

To refute a claimed limit you play the challenger. What must the challenger show? The denial of the definition, pushed inward by rung 1's and rung 7's techs. Not-heading-to technique: for f defined near w, ¬(lim_{x→w} f(x) = L) exactly when ∃ε > 0 ∀δ > 0 ∃x ∈ dom f (0 < |x − w| < δ ∧ ε ≤ |f(x) − L|).

\neg\,\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists \delta \gt 0\ \forall x\,(A \Rightarrow B) @@ the claim denied; A is 0 < |x − a| < δ, B is |f(x) − L| < ε
\htmlClass{new}{\exists \varepsilon \gt 0}\ \neg\,\exists \delta \gt 0\ \forall x\,(A \Rightarrow B) @@ not-every, bounded (7d): not every gives some not. Back: counterexample (2b)
\exists \varepsilon \gt 0\ \htmlClass{new}{\forall \delta \gt 0}\ \neg\,\forall x\,(A \Rightarrow B) @@ not-some (2b): not some is every not; both ways
\exists \varepsilon \gt 0\ \forall \delta \gt 0\ \htmlClass{new}{\exists x}\ \neg(A \Rightarrow B) @@ not-every again. Back: counterexample
\exists \varepsilon \gt 0\ \forall \delta \gt 0\ \exists x\,(A \;\htmlClass{keep}{\land}\; \neg B) @@ negated-if forward (7d): denying an if is an and. Back: negated-if (1f)
\exists \varepsilon \gt 0\ \forall \delta \gt 0\ \exists x\,(A \land \htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon \le |f(x) - L|}) \quad \blacksquare @@ three-way-order on ℝ: ¬(p < ε) is ε ≤ p

Read it as a strategy: the challenger names one gap, and then, for every reach you might answer, produces an input in your window whose output escapes. The leftward direction, from the strategy to the denial, needs no either-way principle: counterexample, not-some and negated-if are rung 1's. The rightward direction, from a bare denial to a strategy, is where rung 7 earns its keep. In practice you always prove the strategy, and the denial follows.

\varepsilon = \tfrac14 @@ the true value is 4, half a unit from 4.5; name a gap less than that
\text{any } \delta \gt 0:\ \ x = 2 + \tfrac12\min(\delta,\ 0.01) @@ in your window, and within 0.01 of 2 for the challenger's own arithmetic
4 \lt x^2 \lt 2.01^2 = 4.0401 @@ powers-keep-order (8f), since 2 < x < 2.01
|x^2 - 4.5| \gt 4.5 - 4.0401 = 0.4599 \ge \tfrac14 @@ unpack-the-bars; so x escapes. Every δ loses: by not-heading-to's leftward direction, the claim is refuted

A jump falls the same way, with no true value to hide behind. Claim any L for |x|/x at 0. The challenger names ε = ½. For any δ, both δ/2 and −δ/2 are in the window, with outputs 1 and −1, two apart. By 10b's two-targets no L is within ½ of both, so one of them escapes. That is not-heading-to's strategy for every L at once, and it is 10e's two-sides-differ seen from the challenger's chair.

Notice the challenger uses a min too: an input inside your window, and close enough to 2 for their own bound. 10d's heads-nowhere and 10e's no-side-at-0 were this same play, with a fraction and an irrational, or with an input far enough in. The warning below, from a well-known calculus book, applies to both sides of the game.

The book observes that a finished proof of this kind hides the scratch work that found its δ, which is why such proofs can look like magic. Its advice is to see first, informally, why the statement is true, and only then write the proof.

10g10Running it backwards

Handed a recipe, recover the game: min(1, ε/9) for x² came from a first promise of 1 and a ceiling 9 = 1 + 2|w|, so w = ±4. Handed a write-up with a blank, fill it from the scratch work. Handed a claim you think false, find the true value and name a gap less than the distance to it. Found upward; written downward.

Special challenges

Harder challenges that rely on your own making. The step is complete without them; the rung's star needs them.

10g.4.1buildBuild write-ups of your own, and lose one on purpose.
  1. Find a recipe for f near w, and write it up.
  2. Write a recipe for f near w, sized to the distance from w to the trouble, and write it up.
  3. By not-heading-to, prove that f does not head to L at w.
10g.4.2edge Find a larger δ that still wins, and the largest.
10g.4.3converse If that recipe wins every round, is the bound true on the reach?
10g.4.4seamA great mathematician, it is said, believed that a finished building should not show its scaffolding, and printed his proofs with the traces of their finding removed. A younger one complained that he was like the fox who wipes out its tracks in the sand with its tail. The oldest geometry book that has come down whole runs only forwards. A later Greek geometer described both directions, and called the backward one analysis. Should a written proof show how it was found?

Who said it first

The calculus book is Michael Spivak’s. In 10g.4.4, the builder who hid his scaffolding is Carl Friedrich Gauss, and the fox remark is Niels Henrik Abel’s; the forward-only book is Euclid’s Elements, and the geometer who named analysis is Pappus of Alexandria.

Spivak observes that a finished proof of this kind hides the scratch work that found its δ, which is why such proofs can look like magic; his advice is to see first, informally, why the statement is true, and only then write the proof.
