5·chem · chemistry

How do you count what is too small to see?

How do you count what is too small to see? Once you can solve several equations at once, work with ratios, and carry units through a chain, chemistry's bookkeeping is nothing but those three things. A chemical change takes molecules apart and puts their atoms together again in new ones, with no atom made or lost. So the number of atoms of each element going in is the number coming out, and balancing a change is solving a few whole-number equations. Nobody can count atoms one by one, so chemists count them by weighing, through a unit called the mole. Every “how many grams” question after that is a unit chain from 5j.

Atoms are neither made nor destroyed, so each element's count is the same on both sides. Write the unknown coefficients, one equation per element, solve, and scale to the least whole numbers: the answer is a ratio. Then weigh instead of counting, through the mole, and push grams through the ratio with a unit chain.

The weighing ledger

One of the founders of chemistry ran his laboratory in Paris the way he ran the tax accounts he also worked on: everything was weighed going in and weighed coming out. Burning, rusting, fermenting, he sealed the changes in closed vessels, set them on a balance, and the totals matched.

He laid it down as a rule needing no proof that in every operation of art or of nature nothing is created. The same amount of matter is there before the experiment and after it, and only its combinations change.

A word about units before any numbers, because science is fussy about them, and this is the first page since 5j where they come back. Every quantity on this page carries a unit: grams of a substance, litres of a gas, and moles, which are coming. The units must balance exactly as the numbers do. An answer in grams per litre to a question about grams is wrong however good the arithmetic. A step whose units don't cancel the way you meant is a step that went wrong. 5j's chains are how you keep them honest, and every chain below is written with its units on show.

Atoms, elements, molecules, formulas

The atomic theory of 1808 said why those totals matched. In plain words:

  • An atom is the smallest piece of a chemical substance, a piece that no chemical change splits, makes or destroys. Burning, rusting and digesting only rearrange atoms.
  • An element is a substance made of one kind of atom. There are about a hundred kinds. Each has a symbol: H hydrogen, C carbon, N nitrogen, O oxygen, Fe iron (Latin ferrum), Hg mercury (Latin hydrargyrum).
  • A molecule is a small group of atoms held together: the smallest piece of a substance such as water that is still that substance.
  • A formula says what is in one molecule: element symbols, each with a count. In a formula, a small number written low after a symbol counts that element's atoms in one molecule. So H₂O is two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; no number means one. (These are 3d's low marks, given a job of their own.)
  • A chemical change, or reaction, takes molecules apart and joins their atoms into new molecules.

A chemical change is written with its starting substances on the left and its products on the right, joined by 5a’s results in: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. As in 5a, + is read “together with”, and the line conforms when each kind of atom is counted the same on both sides. Neither is 4d's arrow or adding of numbers, nor 1e's “gives” in a working; those keep their meanings elsewhere. The products of a change are the substances it makes: 4g's product of numbers and 2g's product of sets keep their meanings. The number in front of a formula is its coefficient (5i's word): how many molecules of it take part.

So the weighing ledger is really a ledger of counts: for every element, the number of atoms going in is the number coming out. That one statement is the whole of balancing. The ledger's finding is the law that mass is kept. Its accepted name is the law of conservation of mass, from the Latin conservare, “to keep”.

Balancing is a few whole-number equations

Take propane, the gas in a camping stove, burning in oxygen to carbon dioxide and water. The formulas are fixed by what the substances are: C₃H₈ has 3 carbons and 8 hydrogens, and you may not touch them. What you choose is how many of each molecule take part. Call the coefficients p, b, c, d, unknowns in 5g's sense, and count each element on each side.

\text{C:}\ \ 3p = c @@ carbon: 3 per propane on the left; 1 per CO₂ on the right
\text{H:}\ \ 8p = 2d @@ hydrogen: 8 per propane; 2 per water
\text{O:}\ \ 2b = 2c + d @@ oxygen: 2 per O₂; 2 per CO₂ and 1 per water
c = 3p,\quad d = 4p @@ the first as it stands; multiplying-keeps-answers with ½ on the second
2b = 6p + 4p = 10p,\quad \htmlClass{new}{b = 5p} @@ substitution (5g) in the third; collect (5i); multiplying-keeps-answers with ½
(p, b, c, d) = (p, 5p, 3p, 4p) @@ every answer is a scaling of 1 : 5 : 3 : 4
\mathrm{C_3H_8} + 5\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 3\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2O} @@ p = 1. Check: C 3 = 3; H 8 = 8; O 10 = 6 + 4

The answers here are lists of four rationals (4i's lists), and the column shows what they all are: (p, 5p, 3p, 4p), for any rational p. Three equations in four unknowns leave one unknown free. Every equation in the column only ever says “so many of this per so many of that”. So any answer scaled by k is an answer too (multiplying-keeps-answers on each equation). The answers other than all zeros are one ratio, 1 : 5 : 3 : 4, a class in 5j's sense. A balanced change is that ratio written by its least whole-number member. If the free choice p = 1 leaves fractions, scale by a common denominator (5h). Ethane, C₂H₆, does this. With p = 1, c = 2 and d = 3 and b = 7/2, so double everything: 2 C₂H₆ + 7 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 6 H₂O.

Every such system also has the answer p = b = c = d = 0: nothing reacts, nothing forms, and every count balances. It is true and useless, the chemist's version of 5g's “multiply both sides by 0”. The question is always for the least whole-number answer that is not all zeros.

Two tempting illegal moves. The first is to change a formula to make the count work: turning H₂O into H₂O₂ balances H₂ + O₂ in one stroke, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. The low numbers say what a thing is; the coefficients say how many. The second is to balance one element and stop: fix carbon, adjust oxygen, and the hydrogen fixed earlier quietly comes undone. Solving all the equations together, as above, avoids that, and the left picture shows every element's count at once for the same reason.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

1a set beside the first arguments a second kind of care, the ledger: every line is paid for, and nature keeps its books the same way. Stuff in equals stuff out. The Paris laboratory above was that ledger run on a balance. Balancing a change is the stuff column closed, one element at a time: 3p = c for carbon, 8p = 2d for hydrogen, 2b = 2c + d for oxygen.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every statement in it is true.

The columns close together or not at all. That is why balancing one element and stopping fails: the hydrogen fixed earlier comes undone. Changing a formula is the opposite mistake. The books balance, and the description is of a different substance.

1a asked where a candle's wax goes. A camping stove's canister weighs 30 g less after breakfast. If nothing is created and nothing is lost, where are the 30 g now, and would they weigh more or less than 30 g?

Counting by weighing: masses of atoms

A balanced change counts molecules, but nobody can count molecules. You weigh. The bridge is that the atoms of each element have a characteristic mass, and the mass of a molecule is the sum of its atoms' masses. Chemists measure single atoms in a tiny unit, the dalton (Da), close to the mass of one hydrogen atom. On that scale hydrogen is about 1, carbon 12, nitrogen 14, oxygen 16, iron 56 and mercury 201. (More closely they are 1.008, 12.011, 14.007, 15.999, 55.845 and 200.59, rounded as in 5h; the whole numbers are what this page uses by hand.) So a water molecule has mass 2 · 1 + 16 = 18 Da, and a propane molecule 3 · 12 + 8 · 1 = 44 Da. That sum is the substance's formula mass.

The mole, and a way of writing large numbers

Chemists fixed a counting unit for molecules, the mole (mol). Since 2019 it is an exact count. One mole of a substance is 602 214 076 000 000 000 000 000 of its molecules (or of its atoms, for an element that comes as single atoms). That number, an ordinary number of rung 4, only large, is the mole number, written NA with a low mark. Numbers that size are written in a shorter way.

From here on, a decimal with one digit other than 0 before its point, times a power of ten, as in 6.022 · 10²³, is said to be in scientific notation. The power is 4g's: 10²³ is 23 tens multiplied together. By 4i's reading rule, multiplying by it moves every digit 23 places to the left, which is the same as moving the point 23 places to the right.

So NA = 6.02214076 · 10²³ exactly, and NA ≈ 6.022 · 10²³ to the places this page uses. (6.022 · 10²³ is 6022 · 10²⁰: a whole number, as it must be, since it counts molecules.) The mole was chosen so that a mole of a substance has a mass in grams equal to its formula mass in daltons. The fit was exact by definition before 2019, and is still good to better than a part in a billion. So a mole of water has mass 18 g, a little more than a tablespoon. The molar mass is that fit written as a quantity: water's is 18 g/mol, which means (18 g)/(1 mol) is a one for 5j's chains. A mole is a count, the way a dozen is a count; the molar mass is what lets a balance do the counting.

An Italian physicist proposed that equal volumes of any two gases, at the same temperature and pressure, hold the same number of molecules. Then the volumes in which gases combine are in the same ratio as the numbers of molecules.

A French chemist had found that gases combine in small whole-number ratios by volume: 2 litres of hydrogen and 1 of oxygen make 2 litres of steam. Read with the equal-volumes law, that 2 : 1 : 2 is a ratio of molecule counts. It is the balanced change 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O read off a measuring jug. (It also forces oxygen to come in pairs, O₂; a question asks why.) The pressure of a gas is how hard it pushes on its container. At 20 °C and the pressure of open air at sea level, one mole of any gas fills about 24 L, whether it is hydrogen, oxygen or propane. So for gases the coefficients are also a ratio of volumes: a litre of propane needs 5 L of oxygen. Air is about 21% oxygen by volume, so that is 5 L · (100 L air)/(21 L oxygen) ≈ 23.8 L of air. Starve the flame of air and some carbon leaves as carbon monoxide instead of the dioxide. That is why stoves must be ventilated: it is a different balanced change, with less oxygen on the left.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

2b set beside every and some a second principle of the same care: a crowd and one member of it are different sizes of thing. 2c met it again: a set is its members, and a member is not the set. 5·stat's mean was a crowd-statement that no household paid. The mole is the crowd made countable. A mole of water has a mass of 18 g. One molecule has a mass of 18 daltons, which no kitchen scale will ever show.

Say which size your statement lives at.

“A mole of any gas fills about 24 L” is a crowd-statement. It is true of hydrogen and of propane alike, because it is not about the molecules' size at all. It is about the room the crowd moves in.

A mole of any gas fills about 24 L at 20 °C. How much room does one molecule of the gas take up? Is that a question with an answer?

Mole ratios: the coefficients are a ratio

A ratio survives scaling (5j), so C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O can be read as one molecule, one dozen, or one mole of propane. “5 mol O₂ per 1 mol C₃H₈” is another one for a chain, and every gram question becomes grams to moles, moles to moles, moles to grams:

88\,\text{g C}_3\text{H}_8 @@ how much oxygen does it take to burn this?
\cdot\,\tfrac{1\ \text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8}{44\,\text{g C}_3\text{H}_8} = 2\ \text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8 @@ grams to moles: the molar mass, turned so the grams cancel
\cdot\,\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{5\ \text{mol O}_2}{1\ \text{mol C}_3\text{H}_8}} = 10\ \text{mol O}_2 @@ the mole ratio from the balanced change: this page's one
\cdot\,\tfrac{32\,\text{g O}_2}{1\ \text{mol O}_2} = 320\,\text{g O}_2 @@ moles to grams

The same chain gives the products: 6 mol of CO₂ (264 g) and 8 mol of water (144 g). The weighing ledger checks it: 88 g + 320 g = 408 g in, 264 g + 144 g = 408 g out. When the ledger balances, the change was balanced; when it doesn't, it wasn't. Notice that the units name the substance as well as the gram. “g C₃H₈” and “g O₂” do not cancel each other, and a chain that cancels them has made a mistake the numbers cannot show.

The limiting reagent

Real mixtures are rarely in the exact ratio. Then one starting substance runs out first and stops the change; it is the limiting reagent, and the others are left over. To find it, ask how many times the change's recipe could run on each substance alone: its moles divided by its coefficient. The least of those numbers is the number of runs.

\mathrm{N_2} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2} \to 2\,\mathrm{NH_3};\quad 28\,\text{g N}_2,\ 9\,\text{g H}_2 @@ ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen, not in the 1 : 3 ratio
1\ \text{mol N}_2,\quad 4.5\ \text{mol H}_2 @@ grams to moles: 28/28 and 9/2
\text{runs: } \tfrac11 = 1 \text{ on N}_2,\quad \tfrac{4.5}{3} = 1.5 \text{ on H}_2 @@ moles over coefficient
\htmlClass{new}{\text{1 run: N}_2 \text{ limits}} @@ the lesser number wins
2\ \text{mol NH}_3 = 34\,\text{g};\ \ 1.5\ \text{mol H}_2 = 3\,\text{g left} @@ one run makes 2 mol of ammonia (17 g/mol) and uses 3 mol of hydrogen
28 + 9 = 37 = 34 + 3 \;\checkmark @@ the ledger, in grams: in is out

The tempting slip is to compare grams: there are 28 g of nitrogen and only 9 g of hydrogen, so surely the hydrogen runs out first? It does not. Only moles divided by coefficients answer the question, because the change counts molecules, not grams.

Yield as a percent

The amount the chain predicts is the theoretical yield, everything going perfectly. Real changes lose some product to side changes, to incomplete conversion and to the bottom of the flask. The percent yield is what you got divided by what the chain promised, written as a percent (5h's line).

A blast furnace takes the oxygen out of iron ore with carbon monoxide: Fe₂O₃ + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂. One mole of ore (2 · 56 + 3 · 16 = 160 g) gives at most 2 mol of iron, 112 g. If a small run turns 160 g of ore into 98 g of iron, the yield is 98/112 = 7/8, which is 87.5%. (With the closer masses, 159.69 g of ore gives at most 111.69 g of iron; the whole-number masses are within a tenth of a percent.) A yield above 100% is not a triumph. The weighing ledger says it is impossible for a pure product, so something else, usually water, is still in it. Two steps of 50% yield give 25%, not 0%: yields multiply, like 5h's percent changes.

Running it backwards: from the smoke to the fuel

The ledger runs both ways. Burn one mole of an unknown fuel made only of carbon and hydrogen, catch what comes out, and the products name the fuel. Every carbon atom ends in a CO₂ and every hydrogen atom in a water, so the counts go straight back.

1\ \text{mol C}_x\text{H}_y \to 3\ \text{mol CO}_2 + 4\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O} @@ the measured smoke, per mole of fuel
x = 3 @@ carbon: one per CO₂, three CO₂ per fuel molecule
y = 2 \cdot 4 = 8 @@ hydrogen: two per water, four waters per fuel molecule
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{C_3H_8}} @@ propane. Check: the balanced change above makes exactly 3 CO₂ and 4 H₂O
\text{oxygen: } 3 \cdot 2 + 4 = 10 \text{ atoms} = \htmlClass{new}{5\ \text{O}_2} @@ the oxygen count runs back too: 5 molecules of O₂ per fuel molecule

The same running back answers the grams question the other way round: 36 g of water is 2 mol, which took 2 mol of H₂, which is 4 g. What the ledger cannot tell you is the arrangement of the atoms inside the fuel: two different molecules can share a formula, and only other measurements tell them apart.

Who said it first

The founder who kept the weighing ledger is Antoine Lavoisier. The atomic theory is John Dalton’s. The French chemist who found the whole-number ratios by volume is Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac, and the Italian physicist is Amedeo Avogadro; Jean Perrin named the mole number for Avogadro in 1909. Making ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen, as in the limiting-reagent example, is Fritz Haber’s process. The word stoichiometry, from the Greek for element and measure, was coined by Jeremias Richter in the 1790s for measuring the proportions in which elements combine.

Lavoisier laid it down as a rule needing no proof that in every operation of art or of nature nothing is created: the same amount of matter is there before the experiment and after it, and only its combinations change.

Lavoisier, Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789), Part I, ch. XIII; after Kerr's translation (1790)

Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of any two gases, at the same temperature and pressure, hold the same number of molecules, so that the volumes in which gases combine are in the same ratio as the numbers of molecules.

Avogadro, Journal de Physique 73 (1811), 58–76

Accepted names

The law that mass is kept is the law of conservation of mass. Reckoning amounts from a balanced change, as this page does, is stoichiometry. The mole number is Avogadro’s number (or Avogadro’s constant), and the equal-volumes law is Avogadro’s law.
