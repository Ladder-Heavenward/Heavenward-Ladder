Can a Proof Be Too Long to Check?

Can a proof be too long to check? Take two whole numbers that share no prime, add them, and multiply together the different primes of the two numbers and their sum. The product is almost always bigger than the sum. The abc question, asked in 1985, says that it can be smaller only by a little, in a sense made exact on this page; if true, it would settle at once a long list of questions about whole numbers, among them the old one about powers that 44·now answered by a road of hundreds of pages. In August 2012 a mathematician in Kyoto posted four long papers, several hundred pages of a new theory, that claimed a proof. Few could follow them. In 2018 two mathematicians who had visited Kyoto to discuss the proof wrote that one step does not hold; the author answered that they had misunderstood it. In 2020 the papers were accepted by a journal of the author’s own institute, whose chief editor he is, and they appeared in 2021. In 2023 a supporter offered a million dollars for a paper that shows a flaw. By 2024 rival write-ups had appeared, and in 2026 a project in Japan set out to check the theory by computer, and published an interim report in July. Its readers do not agree on what it shows. This page proves what abc would give, tells each dated event of the dispute from both sides with no verdict, and asks what a proof checker could and could not settle when the readers of a proof disagree.

For coprime x + y = z, let R be the product of the different primes of xyz. Then 3^(2^k) = 1 + (3^(2^k) − 1) has z/R > 2^(k + 1)/3, so z/R is unbounded, and abc asks only that z < K R^(1 + ε). If so, xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has R ≤ xyz < z³, so zⁿ < K z^(3 + 3ε): no answer for large n. A formal check settles whether formal definitions give a formal statement; it cannot settle whether the definitions say what the words meant.

sky35.1The Primes of a Sum

By 4j’s one-prime-list technique, every whole number above 1 has one list of primes. Its radical R(n) is the product of the different primes on the list, each once: R(72) = R(2³ · 3²) = 6. Take x + y = z with x, y sharing no prime; then x, y, z share none, and R(xyz) = R(x)R(y)R(z).

1 + 2 = 3:\ \ R(6) = 6 > 3 @@ the usual case
1 + 8 = 9:\ \ R(72) = 6 < 9 @@ a hit: the sum beats the radical
3 + 125 = 128:\ \ R(3 \cdot 5^3 \cdot 2^7) = 30 < 128 @@ another
\htmlClass{new}{2 + 3^{10} \cdot 109 = 23^5 = 6\,436\,343:\ \ R = 15\,042} @@ z ≈ R^1.63, the highest power known

Endless-hits technique: for every k ≥ 1, z = 3^(2^k), x = 1, y = z − 1 gives z/R(xyz) > 2^(k + 1)/3; so z can beat its radical by any factor.

3^{2^{k+1}} - 1 = (3^{2^k} - 1)(3^{2^k} + 1),\ \ 2 \mid 3^{2^k} + 1 @@ odd plus 1 is even (4j)
2^{k+2} \mid 3^{2^k} - 1 @@ by induction from 9 − 1 = 8 = 2³, one more 2 each time
R(y) \le 2 \cdot \tfrac{y}{2^{k+2}} = \tfrac{y}{2^{k+1}},\ \ R(xyz) = 3R(y) < \tfrac{3z}{2^{k+1}} @@ the 2s count once
\htmlClass{new}{z/R(xyz) > 2^{k+1}/3} \quad \blacksquare @@ k = 3: 6561/1230 ≈ 5.33

So the radical can lose by any factor. The abc question, 1985, asks that it lose by only a power a hair above 1: for every ε > 0 there is a K with z < K · R(xyz)^(1 + ε) for every coprime x + y = z. The endless-hits technique shows the ε cannot be dropped.

sky35.2What abc Would Give

44·now proved by hand that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no answer for n = 3 and named the 1995 road for the rest. Here the same statement for all large n falls out of abc in two lines, with no curves at all.

Bounded-exponent technique: if abc holds with ε and K, then xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no answer in positive whole numbers for n ≥ 3 + 3ε + log₂ K; and if z < R(xyz)² always, none for n ≥ 6.

R(x^n y^n z^n) = R(xyz) \le xyz < z^3 @@ a coprime answer (divide out any common prime first); x, y < z
z^n < K\,R^{1 + \varepsilon} < K z^{3 + 3\varepsilon} \Rightarrow z^{\,n - 3 - 3\varepsilon} < K @@ abc
z \ge 2 \Rightarrow n - 3 - 3\varepsilon < \log_2 K @@ x, y ≥ 1, so z > 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{with } z < R^2:\ z^n < z^6,\ \text{so } n < 6} \quad \blacksquare @@ the strong form, itself a guess

Three-powers technique: if abc holds, then xᵖ + y^q = zʳ with x, y, z coprime and 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1 has finitely many answers, all exponents together.

s = \tfrac1p + \tfrac1q + \tfrac1r \le \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac17 = \tfrac{41}{42} @@ the largest sum below 1
c = z^r:\ \ R \le xyz < c^{1/p} c^{1/q} c^{1/r} = c^{s} @@ x < c^(1/p), y < c^(1/q)
c < K c^{s(1 + \varepsilon)},\ \ s(1 + \varepsilon) < 1 \text{ for } \varepsilon = \tfrac1{42} @@ abc
\htmlClass{new}{c < K^{1/(1 - s(1 + \varepsilon))}:\ \text{finitely many}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 2⁵ + 7² = 3⁴ and 7³ + 13² = 2⁹ are two

The 1995 proof does not give the three-powers technique; abc would. It would also make effective a 1955 result on how well a number like ∛2 can be approached by fractions, giving the constants that proof could not; told, not proved. That reach is why a claimed proof of abc drew the eyes it did.

sky35.3The Claim of 2012

In August 2012 four papers were posted, building a theory their author called inter-universal. They state a proof that z < K · R^(1 + ε). The stated method, in one paragraph: a number field and a curve over it are taken apart into their symmetries, several copies of the arithmetic are set side by side as separate “universes”, and a deformation between the copies, which ordinary arithmetic does not allow, is controlled closely enough that a certain inequality of heights, read back, is abc. The central inequality is the third paper’s Corollary 3.12. This page does not judge whether it holds.

2012:\ \ 4 \text{ papers, several hundred pages} @@ counts in the press run from about 400 to over 600 pages, as front matter and later revisions are counted
1976:\ \ \text{four colours, a computer check of about } 1200 \text{ hours} @@ 42·now.4: long, but every step was a step anyone could rerun
\htmlClass{new}{\text{length was not the trouble; following the central step was}} @@ the readers’ own account, on both sides

38·now’s statement-read technique said a checked proof certifies the statement as written and nothing else. A proof in words has no core at all: 38·now2’s two-checks technique says it is certified by readers, and needs the same trust as any person’s proof. By 2018 a small number of readers, most of them close to the author, said they had followed it, and many experts said they could not. Contrast ∞42.5: a short written proof convinces a checker who reads nothing, because its core is arithmetic anyone can rerun; a proof in words has only its readers.

sky35.5What a Checker Could Settle

Put the two-checks technique and the statement-read technique together. A formal check has a core, which certifies lines, and a statement and definitions, which a person must read. A dispute between readers can be of either kind.

Settle-what technique: if two readers agree that a formal statement and formal definitions say what the words of a proof mean, a formal check of the disputed step settles their dispute; if they do not agree on that, no check can, since the core certifies only what it is given.

\text{core passes } D \vdash S @@ the small-kernel technique: the lines follow
\text{readers agree } D, S \text{ mean the words} @@ the statement-read technique, done by both
\Rightarrow \text{the step holds for both} @@ the dispute was about the lines
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no agreement on } D, S \Rightarrow \text{the check moves the dispute, it does not end it}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the core cannot read words
  
\text{four colours: 1976 by computer, a proof assistant in 2005} @@ 42·now: 29 years from claim to a formal check
\text{the grocer’s stack: 1998 by computer, formally checked in 2014} @@ 38·now: 16 years
\text{the simple groups: finished in 2004, some ten thousand pages} @@ 46·now: read by about a hundred authors, a shorter proof still being written
\htmlClass{new}{abc:\ 2012\text{, disputed in 2026, 14 years on}} @@ the only one of the four whose experts disagree on a step

In the first three, nobody disputed a step; the question was trust in a long run or a long text, and checks reduced it. Here the experts disagree on one step, and part of their disagreement is over what the definitions mean. That is the case the technique says a checker alone cannot close.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞35.oq.1Is abc true? With ε fixed, is there a K with z < K · R(xyz)^(1 + ε) for every coprime x + y = z? Outside the disputed claim, the best proved bounds are far weaker: z below an exponential of a power of R. Searches of billions of triples have found no quality above about 1.63, which fits abc and proves nothing.
∞35.oq.2Does Corollary 3.12 hold? The 2018 report and the author disagree on whether its proof can be carried out as written; the author says the report’s simplification loses what makes it work, the report’s writers say the simplification loses nothing. The 2026 computer project reported in July; its readers disagree on what it shows. This page gives no verdict.
∞35.oq.3What should a field do with a proof most experts cannot follow? A proof certified by readers needs readers. When few can read it, publication, prizes and reputation stand in for reading, and each side calls the other’s stand-ins illegitimate. Is there a rule, beyond waiting, that a community could agree on before it knows the answer?
∞35.oq.4Can a formal check settle a dispute about meaning? The settle-what technique says it cannot alone. Could both sides agree in advance on formal definitions, so that a check becomes decisive? Who writes them, and how would either side know the other’s are faithful?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The abc question came out of a comparison. For polynomials the same inequality is a technique, proved in the early 1980s by a short argument with slopes; in 1985 two mathematicians asked whether whole numbers obey it too, with the ε that sky35.1 shows they need. It soon became clear how much would follow, and it became one of the central questions of number theory.

The 2012 papers came from a mathematician known for earlier work that experts had read and used, which is why they were taken seriously. Few could follow them. Some readers, after long study, said they were convinced. Others said that the explanations never reached the decisive step.

The 2018 visit was the first time two experts who doubted the step met the author for several days to argue it. Their report reduced the dispute to one inequality and argued that a simplification shows it cannot hold as proved. The author replied that the simplification removes the very structure the proof needs. Each side felt the other had not engaged with its point. Neither moved.

Publication in 2020 to 2021 was argued about as much as the mathematics. The journal belongs to the author’s own institute and he is its chief editor. Supporters pointed to eight years of refereeing. Critics said a journal’s review cannot do what the field’s experts had not, and that the choice of journal made publication count for less. Most number theorists went on treating abc as open.

Money and new readers came next. In 2023 a businessman who supports the theory offered a million dollars for a paper that shows a flaw; a critic replied that the 2018 report already does. By 2024 a mathematician in the United States had posted a series of write-ups, begun in 2021, that he said repair or clarify the theory; the author rejected them in March 2024, and the exchange continued. In 2026 a centre at a private university in Japan, whose site also hosts the prize, began a project to check the theory by computer, and its July interim report was read in different ways.

What is argued now is less the inequality than the method of deciding. Some hope a formal check will end it. Others answer, with the settle-what technique, that a check can only move the dispute to whether the formal definitions are faithful, and that the same people would have to agree on that. The question is still asked as it was in 2012: is abc proved?

The accepted names

The question is the abc conjecture (Masser–Oesterlé, 1985); R(n) is the radical rad(n), and log z / log R(xyz) the quality of the triple. The polynomial version is the Mason–Stothers theorem. The bounded-exponent technique is the standard derivation of the asymptotic Fermat theorem from abc; the strong form z < rad(abc)² is a conjecture sometimes attributed to Baker’s explicit abc. The three-powers technique is the Fermat–Catalan conjecture’s consequence of abc; the 1955 result is Roth’s theorem, made effective by abc (Bombieri). The best unconditional bound is of Stewart–Yu type, z < exp(C R^(1/3 + ε)). The 2012 theory is inter-universal Teichmüller theory (IUT); the third paper’s Corollary 3.12 is the disputed inequality; the 2018 report is “Why abc is still a conjecture”. The journal is Publications of the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences (PRIMS), Kyoto. The 2023 award is the IUT Challenger Prize; the 2026 computer check is Project LANA of the ZEN Mathematics Center, using a proof assistant.

Who said it first

David Masser and Joseph Oesterlé posed abc in 1985; W. W. Stothers (1981) and R. C. Mason (1984) proved the polynomial case. Éric Reyssat found 2 + 3¹⁰ · 109 = 23⁵. Shinichi Mochizuki posted the four IUT papers in August 2012. Peter Scholze and Jakob Stix visited Kyoto in March 2018 and posted their report in September 2018. The papers were accepted by PRIMS in April 2020 and published in 2021. Nobuo Kawakami funded the 2023 prize; Peter Woit argued it should go to Scholze and Stix. Kirti Joshi posted his write-ups from 2021 (arXiv 2106.11452), the one claiming abc in March 2024 (arXiv 2403.10430), Mochizuki replied in March 2024, and Joshi commented on the LANA interim report on 30 July 2026; the LANA report lists Fumiharu Kato among its authors. Klaus Roth proved the 1955 theorem.

A proof that is not allowed

“The proof was published after years of refereeing, so abc is a theorem.” Not allowed: the two-checks technique puts a proof in words in the hands of its readers, and a journal’s referees are some readers, not all. Equally not allowed: “Most experts cannot follow it, so it is wrong.” Not being able to follow a step is evidence about the readers and the writing, not a proof that the step fails. Only an argument about the step itself, on one side or the other, is a proof.
