Rung 10 · theory

Pushing the limit as close as you can imagine

Rung 9 kept running into a sentence it could not finish. Near 0, the outputs of one divided by the input pass every bound. The square of the input less one, divided by the input less one, has no output at 1, yet its outputs near 1 are near 2. The run of one, a half, a third, a quarter and on never stands still, but it plainly goes somewhere. Far out, twice the input plus one, divided by the input less one, comes as close to 2 as any small fraction you name. Each time the page said what order alone could say and stopped. This rung finishes the sentence. It turns “gets as close as you like” into a statement made of every and some, so that it can be proved, or refuted, by the techs you already have.

You know what “as close as you like” means. You have watched a number creep toward another on a screen. Pinning it down takes a game you have not played before, with two players and a gap. You have the moves for it. Play it a few times and it is yours.

Someone names a gap around the value, as small as they please. You answer with a nearness around the input. If every input that near, other than the input itself, sends the output into their gap, and you can always answer, the claim is proved.

What it means to say it

Take the input that 9a's and 9g's (x² − 1)/(x − 1) lacks. At 1 the bottom is 0, so the function has no output there. Everywhere else it agrees with x + 1, by 5h's difference of squares and a cancel that is licensed because x − 1 is not 0. Try inputs near 1: at 1.1 the output is 2.1, at 0.99 it is 1.99, at 1.0001 it is 2.0001. Everyone who looks says the outputs are “getting close to 2”. The question this rung asks is what that sentence claims, exactly, and how you would prove it.

“Close” is a matter of distance, and rung 8 built distance on the line: the distance between two reals is the size of their difference, |p − q|. So “the outputs get close to 2” is about |f(x) − 2|, and “the inputs get close to 1” is about |x − 1|, the size of 8h's displacement. But how close is close? No single gap will do. If you say “within a hundredth”, someone can ask for a millionth. The phrase “as close as you like” is the answer: whatever gap anyone names, however small, the outputs can be brought inside it.

Brought inside it how? By keeping the inputs near enough. So the full claim has two gaps in it, named by two people in a fixed order. A challenger names a gap around the value. You answer with a nearness around the input. You win the round if every input within your nearness, other than the input itself, has its output within their gap. The claim is that you can win every round: for every gap they might name, there is a nearness you can answer with. Every, then some. Rung 1 taught that the order of every and some is not decoration, and here it is the whole game. Your answer may depend on their challenge, because they move first.

From here on, a challenge and an answer are also the two moves of this game: the gap the challenger names, and the nearness you give back. 5f's answers of a condition, the reals that make it true, keep their meaning; what the word is said of says which.

From here on, a point of the line, or a point, is also a real: the usual word for a member of ℝ seen in 8a's picture of the line. No shape is meant. 5g's decimal point and 7·phys's point of a circuit keep their meanings.

Notice the words “other than the input itself”. The value at the point plays no part. That is not a loophole; it is the point. At 1, (x² − 1)/(x − 1) has no output to consult, and the claim about 2 is still true. If someone defines the function to be 3 at 1 by a separate case, the claim about 2 is still true, because the game never looks at 1. A limit is a statement about the neighbours of a point, never about the point.

Where it comes from

The founders of the calculus, late in the seventeenth century, reasoned with quantities that were small but not nothing. They got right answers by methods nobody could fully defend. One of them came close to the idea of this rung. His great book on motion opens with quantities that, in a finite time, approach each other more nearly than by any given difference, and says they end up equal. “Any given difference” is the challenger's gap. But the sentence leans on time and motion, which mathematics cannot reason about. A bishop and philosopher mocked the small quantities of the calculus in 1734. He called them neither finite, nor infinitely small, nor nothing, and he had a point: the reasoning had a hole.

Filling it took more than a century. A priest teaching in Prague wrote a definition of the right kind in 1817. It spoke of a difference that can be made smaller than any given quantity, and almost nobody read it for decades. A lecturer in Paris put the idea at the start of his course on analysis in 1821. His proofs already used two small Greek letters for the challenger's gap and the answer. A teacher in Berlin, from the late 1850s, stripped out the last words about motion. He wrote the definition in the form of every and some that this ladder uses, and his students carried it into print. The two Greek letters are introduced on 10a, each with its line.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things. First, the definition. A function heads to a value at an input when, however small a gap anyone names around the value, there is a neighbourhood of the input where the function stays inside the gap. The input itself is left out of the neighbourhood. The value at the input plays no part. Second, a function cannot head to two values at once at the same input. Challenge both claims with half the distance between the two values. The function's outputs would have to lie within that of each. Then 8h's triangle inequality makes the distance less than itself. So “the limit” names one thing, and may be written as a name.

Third, limits add. If one function heads to one value and another to another, their sum heads to the sum. Split the challenger's gap in two, give each function half, and the triangle inequality finishes it. Fourth, the squeeze. A function trapped near an input between two others, both of which head to the same value, heads there too, whatever it does in between. Fifth, a limit exists exactly when the approaches from the left and from the right exist and agree. Each side is the same game with only half the neighbourhood.

What follows: products and quotients. Their proofs need more care than the sum's, and this ladder proves them in full. For a product the trick is to add and take away the same amount, so that each piece of the difference has a factor that can be made small. Then the answer is chosen as the least of several. For a quotient, the bottom must be kept away from 0 near the input, which takes a round of the game of its own. From those, every constant, every power and every polynomial heads to its own value: substituting the input is a technique, not a habit. And limits pass over missing inputs. (x² − 1)/(x − 1) heads to 2 as x heads to 1. It agrees with x + 1 on every neighbourhood of 1, and the game never looks at 1.

What you can do because you know it

You can prove that a function heads to a value, and you can refute it. To show a claimed value is wrong, you play the challenger and find one gap that every answer loses to. You find it by negating the every and the some with rung 1's and rung 7's techs. You can compute limits quickly by rewriting. You know why each rewrite is licensed, and which tempting ones are not. 10f is the kit: substitute when the rules allow it; factor and cancel where an input is missing; clear a root with 8i's conjugate; squeeze what you cannot rewrite; split by sides where an absolute value changes its formula. It also builds the game for inputs that grow past every bound, and for sequences. There 9·cb4's run 1, ½, ⅓, ⋯ is proved to head to 0. 10g is where the hands learn to choose an answer by working an inequality backwards on purpose. They also learn to write the proof forwards for a reader.

Then 10·bio aims the rung at a living cell. An enzyme is a molecule a cell makes to speed up one particular chemical change. It works faster when given more of what it acts on, but not without end: its rate heads for a ceiling it never reaches. Two biochemists wrote the function down in 1913. The ceiling is a limit in exactly this rung's sense, with a half-way point that biochemists measure in every laboratory.

That is a beginning, and a good one. Every analyst alive is still learning to choose an answer, and so, for a good while yet, are you.

And 10·econ asks whether interest added more often can grow without end: the year's growth rises with every finer cut yet stays below a ceiling, so it heads to a limit, about 2.718 times the start at 100%. And 10·phys asks whether a runner can ever catch a tortoise: the race's endless stages shrink by a fixed factor, so their times head to a finite total, the very moment uniform motion finds directly.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down. Everything stands on rung 8's line, its distances and its triangle inequality, and on rung 9's functions. The price is care with order. The challenger always moves first. Your answer may depend on their gap, but never on the input you will be handed. And the input itself is always left out. One proof, the second principle's, ends by refuting “the two values differ”. So it needs rung 7's not-not out to say they are the same. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that a function gets as close as you like to a value, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

The founder who came close was Isaac Newton, in the first lemma of his Principia (1687). The bishop was George Berkeley, in The Analyst (1734). The priest in Prague was Bernard Bolzano (1817). The lecturer in Paris was Augustin-Louis Cauchy, at the École Polytechnique (1821). The teacher in Berlin was Karl Weierstrass. The enzyme’s function is Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten’s (1913).

Newton says that quantities, and ratios of quantities, which in any finite time tend steadily to equality, and before the end of that time come nearer to each other than by any given difference, become in the end equal.

Newton, Principia (1687), Book I, Section I, Lemma I, after Motte (1729)

Cauchy says that when the values given one after another to a variable approach a fixed value without end, so as finally to differ from it by as little as one wishes, the fixed value is called the limit of all the others.

Cauchy, Cours d'analyse de l'École royale polytechnique (1821), Préliminaires

Accepted names: the line of this rung is the epsilon-delta definition of the limit.
