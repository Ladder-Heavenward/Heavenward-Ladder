27b · proof

Dimension

The plane has two directions and space has three. But what is a direction in a space of polynomials, or of solutions to a rate statement? This step counts directions without pictures. Some vectors build every vector by adding and stretching. Some vectors waste none of their number. A list that does both at once is a set of measuring directions, and every such list in a space has the same length. That length is the dimension.

If some m vectors build every vector in a space, then no more than m vectors can be free of each other. So every list that builds the space with nothing wasted has the same length, the dimension of the space.

27b1Building and Wasting Nothing

A mix of vectors v₁, …, vₖ is any t₁v₁ + ⋯ + tₖvₖ. The vectors build V if every vector of V is a mix of them. They are free if the only mix equal to 0 is the one with every tᵢ = 0. A list that builds V and is free is a set of measuring directions for V; accepted name, a basis.

Spare-vector technique: if v₁, …, vₖ are not free, and v₁ ≠ 0, then some vⱼ is a mix of the vectors before it, and dropping vⱼ leaves every mix the list can make.

t_1v_1 + \cdots + t_kv_k = 0,\ \text{not all } t_i = 0 @@ not free
j = \text{the last index with } t_j \neq 0;\ j \ge 2 @@ if j were 1, then t₁v₁ = 0 with t₁ ≠ 0, so v₁ = 0
v_j = -\tfrac{t_1}{t_j}v_1 - \cdots - \tfrac{t_{j-1}}{t_j}v_{j-1} @@ move the rest across and stretch by 1/tⱼ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{any mix using } v_j \text{ is a mix of the others}} \quad \blacksquare @@ put that in for vⱼ, and gather

27b2No More Free Than Builders

Exchange technique: if w₁, …, wₘ build V, and u₁, …, uₖ in V are free, then k ≤ m. Swap the free vectors in, one at a time, and each time a builder goes out.

u_1, w_1, \ldots, w_m \text{ builds } V \text{ and is not free} @@ u₁ is a mix of the w’s; u₁ ≠ 0, since the u’s are free
\text{drop a spare } w;\ \text{still } m \text{ vectors, still builds} @@ the spare-vector technique (27b1): the spare is not u₁, which is first
u_1, \ldots, u_i \text{ and } m - i \text{ of the } w\text{’s build } V @@ repeat, by induction on i; the spare is never a u, since the u’s are free
i = m + 1 \le k \Rightarrow u_{m+1} \text{ is a mix of } u_1..u_m @@ if k > m, at i = m no w’s are left, and u₁..uₘ build V
\htmlClass{new}{k \le m} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last line breaks the freedom of the u’s

27b3Every Basis Is the Same Size

Same-size technique: if a space has a basis of n vectors, every basis of it has n vectors. That n is the dimension of the space.

v_1..v_n \text{ and } u_1..u_k \text{ are both bases} @@ given
k \le n @@ the exchange technique: the v’s build, the u’s are free
n \le k @@ the same, the other way round
\htmlClass{new}{k = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines above

Grow-to-basis technique: in a space of dimension n, every free list can be grown into a basis, and every list of n free vectors is already one. Add any vector the list cannot make; the longer list is still free, since a mix equal to 0 could not use the new vector. By the exchange technique this stops by n vectors.

Lists of n numbers have dimension n: the lists with a single 1 in one place build them and are free. Polynomials of degree at most n have dimension n + 1: 1, x, …, xⁿ build them, and are free because a polynomial that is 0 everywhere has every coefficient 0 (9g). The solutions of y″ = −y have dimension 2: cos and sin are free, and by 24h every solution is y(0) cos x + y′(0) sin x.

27b4Two Spaces Inside a Space

Let U and W be spaces inside a space V. What they share is the set of vectors in both, U ∩ W. What they build together is the set of all sums u + w with u in U and w in W, written U + W. Each holds 0 and is kept by adding and stretching, so each is a space inside V by the inside-space technique (27a2). The union of U and W is usually not: in the plane the two axes hold (1, 0) and (0, 1), and their sum (1, 1) is on neither.

Two-spaces count technique: if U and W have finite bases, then dim(U + W) + dim(U ∩ W) = dim U + dim W.

z_1..z_k \text{ a basis of } U \cap W @@ a space inside U, so its dimension is at most dim U (27b2)
z_1..z_k, u_1..u_p \text{ a basis of } U @@ the grow-to-basis technique (27b3)
z_1..z_k, w_1..w_q \text{ a basis of } W @@ the same, for W
\text{the } z\text{’s, } u\text{’s and } w\text{’s build } U + W @@ every u + w is a mix of them
\textstyle\sum a_iz_i + \sum b_iu_i + \sum c_iw_i = 0 \Rightarrow \sum c_iw_i \in U \cap W @@ it is in W, and it is minus the rest, which is in U
\Rightarrow \textstyle\sum c_iw_i = \sum d_iz_i \Rightarrow \text{all } c_i = 0 @@ the z’s build U ∩ W; the z’s and w’s are free
\Rightarrow \text{all } a_i = 0,\ \text{all } b_i = 0 @@ the z’s and u’s are free
\htmlClass{new}{\dim(U + W) = k + p + q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three lists together are a basis of U + W; and k + p + q = (k + p) + (k + q) − k

One-way split technique: every vector of U + W is a sum u + w in exactly one way exactly when U and W share only 0. If u + w = u′ + w′, then u − u′ = w′ − w is in U and in W. When they share only 0 it is 0, so u = u′ and w = w′. When they share some z other than 0, the vector z is z + 0 and also 0 + z, two ways.

In space, two planes through 0 that are not the same plane build all of space together, so the count reads 3 + dim(shared) = 2 + 2, and they share a line. A plane through 0 and a line through 0 that is not in it share only 0, and every vector of space is a vector of the plane plus a vector of the line in one way.

27b5The Numbers of a Vector in a Basis

A basis lets every vector be written down as a list of numbers.

One-list technique: if v₁, …, vₙ is a basis of V, every vector of V is x₁v₁ + ⋯ + xₙvₙ for exactly one list (x₁, …, xₙ); and the list of a sum is the sum of the lists, and the list of t·v is t times the list of v. The xᵢ are the numbers of v in the basis.

v = x_1v_1 + \cdots + x_nv_n @@ the vᵢ build V
v = y_1v_1 + \cdots + y_nv_n \Rightarrow (x_1 - y_1)v_1 + \cdots + (x_n - y_n)v_n = 0 @@ take one line from the other
\Rightarrow x_i = y_i \text{ for every } i @@ the vᵢ are free
u + v = \textstyle\sum (x_i + y_i)v_i,\ \ t\cdot v = \sum (t\,x_i)v_i @@ regroup the sums; stretching spreads (27a1)
\htmlClass{new}{v \mapsto (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \text{ is a matching}} \quad \blacksquare @@ of V with the lists of n numbers: one list for each vector, one vector for each list

Another basis gives the same vector another list. Let w₁, …, wₙ be a second basis, and write each old basis vector in it: vⱼ = p₁ⱼw₁ + ⋯ + pₙⱼwₙ. Then v = x₁v₁ + ⋯ + xₙvₙ is a mix of the w’s, and gathering what stands on each wᵢ gives the new numbers: yᵢ = pᵢ₁x₁ + ⋯ + pᵢₙxₙ. The n·n numbers pᵢⱼ, set out with pᵢⱼ in row i and column j, are the change table from the first basis to the second. Its column j is the list of vⱼ in the new basis.

In the plane take the basis (1, 0), (0, 1) and the basis (1, 1), (1, −1). Since (1, 0) = ½(1, 1) + ½(1, −1) and (0, 1) = ½(1, 1) − ½(1, −1), the change table has rows (½, ½) and (½, −½). The vector (3, 1) has numbers 3 and 1 in the first basis, and ½·3 + ½·1 = 2 and ½·3 − ½·1 = 1 in the second: (3, 1) = 2(1, 1) + 1(1, −1).

27b6A Space With No Finite Basis

Every technique of this step so far began from a finite list that builds the space. Some spaces have none. Take all the polynomials together, of every degree. They add and stretch, and they are a space of vectors by 27a2’s reasons.

No-finite-list technique: no finite list of polynomials builds all the polynomials.

p_1, \ldots, p_k \text{ any finite list of polynomials} @@ suppose it builds them all
d = \text{the greatest of their degrees} @@ a finite set of numbers with a member has a greatest member (4k)
\text{every mix of the list has degree at most } d @@ adding and stretching polynomials never raise the degree
x^{d+1} \text{ is not a mix of the list} @@ its degree is d + 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the list does not build the polynomials}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above breaks the supposition

The other half of a basis is still there. The powers 1, x, x², …, xᵐ are free for every m, because a polynomial that is 0 everywhere has every coefficient 0 (9g). So this space holds free lists of every length. By the exchange technique (27b2) a space built by m vectors holds no free list longer than m, which is a second proof that no finite list builds it.

So the same-size technique and the grow-to-basis technique speak only of spaces that some finite list builds. From here on, “a space of dimension n” means there is such a list. The reals as a space over ℚ (27a3) are another space with no finite basis, but that takes the counting of rung 35 to prove.

27b7Moves to Watch Out For

\text{three vectors in the plane are free}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> the plane has dimension 2: at most two free vectors
v_1, v_2 \text{ not parallel in space} \Rightarrow \text{they build space}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> two vectors build at most a plane through 0; space has dimension 3
\text{each pair free} \Rightarrow \text{the list is free}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> (1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1): every pair is free, the three are not
\text{every space of vectors has a dimension}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> only those with a finite list that builds them; all polynomials together have none
U \cup W \text{ is a space inside } V\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> only when one holds the other: (1, 0) + (0, 1) lies on neither axis. What the two build together is U + W
\text{the list of } v \text{, with no basis named}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> a list belongs to a vector and a basis: (1, 1) has the list (1, 1) in e₁, e₂ and the list (1, 0) in the basis (1, 1), (0, 1)

The accepted names

A mix is a linear combination; vectors that build V span it, and the vectors a list can make are its span. Free vectors are linearly independent; vectors that are not free are linearly dependent. Measuring directions are a basis, and the size of a basis is the dimension. The exchange technique is the Steinitz exchange lemma. A space with no finite basis is infinite-dimensional. The numbers of a vector in a basis are its coordinates, and the change table is the change-of-basis matrix. What two spaces build together is their sum, and when they share only 0 it is a direct sum.

Who said it first

Grassmann used the idea of dimension in 1844. Ernst Steinitz proved the exchange lemma in 1913, in a paper on fields.
