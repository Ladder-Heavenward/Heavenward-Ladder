32·then · newsflash

What can a spectrum tell us?

What can a spectrum tell us? Hold a pinch of salt in a gas flame and the flame turns a deep yellow. Pass that light through a prism and the yellow is two thin lines, side by side, always in the same place, from salt bought in Chico or salt dried out of the sea at Point Reyes. Every element has its own lines, and that is how we know what the Sun is made of without going there. The annexes of this ladder have met waves many times: tuning a bass, light in lumps, the sound in a tube. What they could not do was take a measured signal apart into its frequencies and tell what each one means. This page is the news that rung 32 brings. A spectrum is a list of coefficients, and the rung has proved what such a list keeps and what it loses. The page reads five things off spectra: why a spectral line has a width, how a scanner turns a position into a pitch and back into a picture, what is lost when a file drops part of its spectrum, how the walls of a house filter the day’s heat, and, briefly, how a crystal’s spots are coefficients.

A line in a spectrum is a turning that dies away, and its width is fixed by how long it rings. A scanner gives each place in the body its own frequency, so the signal it hears is the transform of the body, and undoing the transform is the picture. Dropping coefficients loses exactly their energy. A house answers the daily swing of the outdoor air like a circuit, scaled down and late. And a crystal scatters light into spots whose brightnesses are coefficients of the crystal itself.

32then1The width of a line

An atom that has been lifted to a higher state gives its energy back as light, a turning of frequency ν₀, that dies away as the state empties. Its strength falls like e^(−t/τ), so its height like e^(−t/(2τ)), where τ is the life of the state.

Line-width technique: a turning of frequency ν₀ whose strength dies away over a life τ has a spectrum peaked at ν₀, whose width at half height is 1/(2πτ).

f(t) = e^{2\pi i\nu_0 t}\,e^{-t/(2\tau)} \text{ for } t \ge 0,\ \ 0 \text{ before} @@ the light of one atom, from the moment it starts
\hat f(\nu) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-t/(2\tau) - 2\pi i(\nu - \nu_0)t}\,dt = \tfrac{1}{1/(2\tau) + 2\pi i(\nu - \nu_0)} @@ the growth function’s total out to forever (16i, 26b)
|\hat f(\nu)|^2 = \tfrac{1}{1/(4\tau^2) + 4\pi^2(\nu - \nu_0)^2} @@ the size of a quotient (25c)
\text{half its peak where } 2\pi|\nu - \nu_0| = \tfrac{1}{2\tau} @@ the two terms of the bottom are equal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{width at half height} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi\tau}} \quad \blacksquare @@ twice 1/(4πτ)

The yellow light of salt comes from sodium, with τ = 16.2 ns, so each line is 9.8 MHz wide, out of about 509 million MHz. The two lines are 589.0 and 589.6 nm, and telling them apart takes a spectrometer that splits light by one part in 589/0.6 ≈ 982 or finer. A line that lives longer is narrower: 32f’s squeeze, read in an atom.

32then2A place becomes a pitch

30·phys found that protons in a magnet turn at a frequency set by the field: about 42.58 MHz for each tesla. A scanner adds a slope to the field along one direction, so that the field at x is B₀ + Gx, and every place sings at its own pitch.

Place-as-frequency technique: in a field with slope G, the signal the scanner hears at time t is the transform of the density of protons, read at the frequency γ̄Gt; so undoing the transform gives the density, place by place. Here γ̄ = 42.58 MHz per tesla.

\text{protons at } x \text{ turn at } \bar\gamma(B_0 + Gx) @@ 30·phys: frequency in proportion to the field
S(t) = \int\rho(x)\,e^{-2\pi i\bar\gamma(B_0 + Gx)t}\,dx @@ the signal: every place’s turning, weighted by how many protons are there
e^{2\pi i\bar\gamma B_0t}S(t) = \int\rho(x)\,e^{-2\pi i x(\bar\gamma Gt)}\,dx @@ the machine turns back the common frequency
\htmlClass{new}{e^{2\pi i\bar\gamma B_0t}S(t) = \hat\rho(\bar\gamma Gt)} \quad \blacksquare @@ so ρ is found by the undo-transform technique (32e3)

In a 1.5 T scanner in a Portland hospital, protons sing at 63.87 MHz. A slope of 10 mT per metre spreads them by 425.8 Hz for every millimetre. Listening for a time T reads frequencies to about 1/T, so for millimetre places the scanner listens a few milliseconds per line, and a picture is built line after line. Where the patient moves, the frequencies smear, and the picture blurs.

32then3What a file loses

Dropped-energy technique: if a signal is rebuilt from only some of its coefficients, the energy of the difference is exactly the sum of the energies of the dropped ones.

f - \textstyle\sum_{n \in A} c_ne_n \text{ has coefficients } c_n \text{ for } n \notin A,\ 0 \text{ for } n \in A @@ coefficients subtract (32a2)
\htmlClass{new}{\big\|f - \textstyle\sum_{n \in A}c_ne_n\big\|^2 = \sum_{n \notin A}|c_n|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy-kept technique (32b3), for the difference

So the best way to drop k coefficients, in energy, is to drop the k smallest. The sawtooth kept up to speed 10 loses Σ over |n| > 10 of 1/n², about 0.19 out of π²/3 ≈ 3.29: under six per cent of its energy, all of it in the high speeds. 32·comp shows what a photo keeps, and why the eye does not miss the rest.

32then4A house filters the day

A house in Redding gains and loses heat through its walls at a rate in proportion to the gap between inside and outside. Its inside temperature y obeys τy′ + y = f, where f is the outdoor temperature and τ is the time it takes the house to close most of a gap, about 5 hours for a wooden house.

Thermal-lag technique: a daily swing of the outdoor air, of period P, reaches the inside scaled by 1/√(1 + (2πτ/P)²) and late by the turn whose tangent is 2πτ/P.

\hat y = \tfrac{1}{1 + 2\pi i\xi\tau}\,\hat f @@ the transfer technique (32j3), with k = 1/τ and the input scaled by k
\xi = \tfrac1P = \tfrac{1}{24} \text{ per hour},\ \ \tau = 5 \text{ h}:\ \ 2\pi\xi\tau \approx 1.309 @@ the daily speed
|H| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 1.309^2}} \approx 0.61 @@ the size of a quotient (25c)
\text{turn} = \text{the angle whose tangent is } 1.309 \approx 52.6^\circ @@ out-and-round form
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an outdoor swing of } 16\ ^\circ\text{C reaches the inside as about } 10\ ^\circ\text{C, about 3.5 hours late}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 52.6/360 of 24 hours

A heavy stone house with τ = 20 hours scales the swing to about 0.19 and delays it by over five hours: the hottest hour indoors comes in the evening, when the air outside is already cooling. A thermostat is a second, faster loop on top of this one, and the language of engineers who design it is the transfer function of 32j.

32then5Spots are coefficients

A crystal is a pattern of atoms that repeats in space. Its density of electrons is a repeating function, and so a sum of turnings in space. X-rays scattered by it add up, and in certain directions only, one turning of the density at a time, they add up strongly: a bright spot. The brightness of each spot is the energy of one coefficient.

So a photograph of the spots is a list of |cₙ|², 32b’s shares. What the photograph cannot hold is the turn of each cₙ, and without the turns the density cannot be rebuilt by 32b alone. 32·chem works through a crystal of salt, and how the turns are found.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A spectral line’s width from the life of its state is the natural linewidth, and the shape 1/(1/(4τ²) + 4π²(ν − ν₀)²) is a Lorentzian. The widening by atoms rushing to and fro is Doppler broadening. The scanner is magnetic resonance imaging; γ̄ is the gyromagnetic ratio of the proton over 2π, the slope of the field is a gradient, and the plane of transforms the scanner fills is k-space. The dropped-energy technique is Parseval’s identity for truncation. A house’s scaled and delayed swing is the decrement factor and time lag of building physics. The spots of a crystal are Bragg reflections.

Who said it first

Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped the dark lines of the Sun in 1814. Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen matched them to elements in 1859–60, and helium was found in the Sun’s spectrum by Jules Janssen and Norman Lockyer in 1868, on Earth only in 1895. Auguste Comte gave the stars’ make-up as unknowable in his Course of Positive Philosophy (1835). Victor Weisskopf and Eugene Wigner derived the natural linewidth in 1930. Paul Lauterbur made the first image with a field gradient in 1973, and Peter Mansfield the fast methods; they shared the Nobel Prize in 2003. Max von Laue, and William Henry and William Lawrence Bragg, found X-ray spots from crystals in 1912–13.

A proof that is not allowed

“The scanner’s frequencies are exact, so its pictures are exact.” Not allowed: the scanner listens for a finite time, and by 32f a signal heard for a time T fixes its frequencies only to about 1/T. Every picture is the body blended with a weight whose width is set by how long the scanner listened.
