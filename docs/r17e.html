17e · proof

exp and e

17d proved that ln is a bijection from the positive numbers to all the reals, so it can be run backwards. This step names the function that runs it backwards, exp, and turns ln's techniques around: ln turns multiplying into adding, so exp turns adding into multiplying. The number the exponential sends one to gets a name of its own, e. It is the number past one where the area under one over the input reaches exactly one. The step traps it between 2 and 3, and then between two runs of numbers that close in on it from both sides.

The exponential undoes the logarithm, sending every real number to a positive one. The exponential of a sum is the product of the exponentials. The number e is the exponential of one, so its logarithm is one, and it lies between two and three. One plus a small fraction, raised to as many powers as the fraction’s bottom, closes in on e from below.

17e1The Function That Undoes ln

For every real x, exp x is the one positive number whose ln is x. Its accepted name is the exponential function. exp is an operator: carried out on x, it produces one positive number.

It exists, and is one function, by log-onto-ℝ (17d). By 9d's inverse-is-a-bijection, exp(ln y) = y for y > 0 and ln(exp x) = x for every real x. By 13l's inverse-is-continuous it is continuous. It rises, since ln does: if x < x′ and exp x ≥ exp x′, then log-rises would give x ≥ x′. And exp 0 = 1, since ln 1 = 0.

Adding-becomes-multiplying technique: for all real x and y, exp(x + y) = exp x · exp y; exp(−x) = 1/exp x; and exp(rx) = (exp x)ʳ for rational r.

a = \exp x,\ \ b = \exp y @@ so ln a = x and ln b = y (inverse-is-a-bijection)
\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b = x + y @@ log-of-a-product (17c)
\htmlClass{new}{\exp(x + y) = ab = \exp x \cdot \exp y} @@ ab is the positive number whose ln is x + y
\ln\tfrac1a = -x \Rightarrow \exp(-x) = \tfrac{1}{\exp x} @@ log-of-a-quotient
\ln(a^r) = rx \Rightarrow \exp(rx) = (\exp x)^r \quad \blacksquare @@ log-of-a-power, r rational

17e2The Number e

e = exp 1: the one number with ln e = 1, the place past 1 where the area under 1/t from 1 reaches 1. Its accepted name is simply e, or Euler's number. e is a name, for one number.

e-bounds technique: 2 < e < 3.

\ln 2 \lt 2 - 1 = 1 = \ln e @@ log-bounds (17a), strict away from 1
2 \lt e @@ log-rises: ln rises, so the smaller log has the smaller number

For rational r, adding-becomes-multiplying gives exp r = exp(r · 1) = (exp 1)ʳ = eʳ. So on the rationals, exp is rung 8's power of e. From here on eˣ is written for exp x at every real x. For irrational x, that is a definition: rung 8 gave eˣ no meaning there. 17g does the same for every base.

17e3Closing in on e

e-as-a-limit technique: for every whole n ≥ 1, (1 + 1/n)ⁿ ≤ e ≤ (1 + 1/n)ⁿ⁺¹; so (1 + 1/n)ⁿ → e.

1 - \tfrac{1}{1 + h} \le \ln(1 + h) \le h,\ \ h = \tfrac1n @@ log-bounds at x = 1 + 1/n
\tfrac{1}{n + 1} \le \ln\big(1 + \tfrac1n\big) \le \tfrac1n @@ 1 − n/(n + 1) = 1/(n + 1)
n\ln\big(1 + \tfrac1n\big) \le 1 \le (n + 1)\ln\big(1 + \tfrac1n\big) @@ multiply the right bound by n, the left by n + 1

\big(1 + \tfrac1n\big)^n \to e \quad \blacksquare @@ squeeze (10d)

17e4Interest Added Ever More Often

In 1683 a mathematician asked what becomes of a sum lent at interest, if the interest is added not yearly but at every moment. He showed that a whole year's interest of the same amount, added in this way, gives more than two and a half times the sum, and less than three times.

Who said it first

Jacob Bernoulli asked what becomes of a sum lent at interest if the interest is added not yearly but at every moment, and showed that a whole year’s interest of the same amount added in this way gives more than two and a half times the sum, and less than three times.

Jacob Bernoulli, on a question of interest (1690, posed 1683); paraphrase

The letter e, and the name Euler’s number, are after Leonhard Euler, who named the number from 1728.
