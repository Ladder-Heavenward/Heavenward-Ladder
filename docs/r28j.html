28j · proof

Flux and Circulation

Picture water moving across a pond, with a speed and a direction at every point. Draw a loop on the surface. Two questions can be asked of the loop. How much water flows out across it? And how much does the water push along it, going round? The first is the flux, the second the circulation. This step proves that each can be found without walking the loop at all: add up, over the inside, how much the water spreads out at each point, or how much it swirls. Rung 16 traded a total along a line for two values at its ends. This step makes the same trade one dimension up, and then once more in space.

Round the edge of a rectangle, the circulation of a smooth field equals the total of its swirl inside, and the flux out equals the total of its spread inside. Over a box in space, the flux out equals the total of the spread inside.

28j1Totals Along a Path

A field F = (P, Q) gives an arrow at each point of the plane. A path is a moving point r(t) = (x(t), y(t)) for α ≤ t ≤ β, with slopes (18i). The circulation of F along the path totals the part of F that points along the way:

\(\int_r F \cdot dr = \int_\alpha^\beta \big(P\,x^{\prime}(t) + Q\,y^{\prime}(t)\big)\,dt.\)

The flux of F across the path totals the part of F that crosses it, to the right of the direction of travel: ∫ (P y′(t) − Q x′(t)) dt. Round a loop travelled with the inside on the left, that is the flux out.

Uphill-total technique: if F = ∇φ for a smoothly sloped φ, the circulation of F along any path is φ at the end minus φ at the start.

P\,x'(t) + Q\,y'(t) = \varphi_x x'(t) + \varphi_y y'(t) = \tfrac{d}{dt}\varphi(r(t)) @@ F = ∇φ, and the roads-add technique (28c3)
\textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta \tfrac{d}{dt}\varphi(r(t))\,dt = \varphi(r(\beta)) - \varphi(r(\alpha)) @@ the area-is-a-change technique (16c)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_r \nabla\varphi \cdot dr = \varphi(\text{end}) - \varphi(\text{start})} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines

So round any closed loop the circulation of an uphill field is 0: you end where you started. A field with nonzero circulation round some loop is not uphill from anything. Nothing in these lines used the plane: in space, with F = (P, Q, S) and a third road z(t), the circulation is ∫ (P x′ + Q y′ + S z′) dt, and the uphill-total technique holds word for word, since the roads-add technique (28c3) takes any number of roads.

28j2Swirl Inside, Circulation Round

The swirl of F at a point is Qx − Py. Accepted name: the curl, in the plane. The spread is Px + Qy. Accepted name: the divergence.

Swirl technique: if P and Q are smoothly sloped on a rectangle R, the circulation of F round the edge of R, travelled with R on the left, equals ∬R (Qx − Py).

\textstyle\iint_R Q_x = \int_c^d \Big(\int_a^b Q_x(x,y)\,dx\Big)dy = \int_c^d \big(Q(b,y) - Q(a,y)\big)\,dy @@ the either-order technique (28h2), then the area-is-a-change technique (16c) inside
\textstyle\int_c^d Q(b,y)\,dy - \int_c^d Q(a,y)\,dy = \text{the } Q \text{ part of the circulation} @@ up the right side y′ = 1, down the left side y′ = −1; on the top and bottom y′ = 0
\textstyle\iint_R P_y = \int_a^b \big(P(x,d) - P(x,c)\big)\,dx @@ the same, in the other order
-\textstyle\iint_R P_y = \text{the } P \text{ part of the circulation} @@ along the bottom x′ = 1, back along the top x′ = −1
\htmlClass{new}{\oint_{\partial R} F\cdot dr = \iint_R (Q_x - P_y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the two parts

The same lines run on any region that is slanted both ways, between two graphs in x and between two graphs in y, with 28i’s slanted totals in place of the rectangle’s. Regions that can be cut into such pieces follow by adding: the circulations along shared cuts cancel, since each cut is walked once each way.

28j3Spread Inside, Flux Out

Spread technique: if P and Q are smoothly sloped on R, the flux of F out of R equals ∬R (Px + Qy). For a box B in space and a field F = (P, Q, S) smoothly sloped on it, the flux out through the six faces equals the triple total of Px + Qy + Sz.

G = (-Q, P) @@ turn every arrow of F a quarter turn
\text{circulation of } G = \textstyle\int (-Q\,x' + P\,y')\,dt = \text{flux of } F @@ 28j1’s two totals
(P)_x - (-Q)_y = P_x + Q_y @@ the swirl of G is the spread of F
\text{flux of } F \text{ out of } R = \textstyle\iint_R (P_x + Q_y) @@ the swirl technique (28j2) for G
\textstyle\iiint_B P_x = \iint \big(P(\text{front}) - P(\text{back})\big) @@ in space: totals over a box in any order (28h2, with three cuts), and 16c along x
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flux out of } B = \textstyle\iiint_B (P_x + Q_y + S_z)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for Q along y and S along z, then add

So there are three trades of the same kind. On a line, a total of slopes is the change between the two ends (16c). On a patch of the plane, a total of swirl or spread is a total round the edge. In a box of space, a total of spread is the flux through the faces. Each says that what happens inside, added up, shows on the boundary, because the inside bits cancel in pairs.

The accepted names

A field is a vector field. The circulation is the line integral ∮ F · dr, and the flux the flux integral ∮ F · n ds. An uphill field is a conservative (or gradient) field, and φ its potential; the uphill-total technique is the fundamental theorem for line integrals. Swirl is curl (in the plane, the scalar curl), and spread is divergence, div F. The swirl technique is Green’s theorem; the spread technique is the divergence theorem (Gauss’s theorem, or Ostrogradsky’s theorem).

Who said it first

Lagrange (1762) and Gauss (1813) used special cases of the divergence theorem in work on gravity. Mikhail Ostrogradsky proved it in general in 1826. George Green, a miller’s son from Nottingham who taught himself mathematics, published an essay in 1828, sold to 51 subscribers, whose identities in space hold this kind of trade; the plane form on this page was stated later. Augustin-Louis Cauchy stated it in 1846, and Bernhard Riemann proved it in 1851. The circulation version on curved surfaces is due to William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, in a letter to George Stokes in 1850.

In 28j.4.5, the miller is George Green of Sneinton, Nottingham. His Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism (1828) went to 51 subscribers. He entered Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, in 1833 and died in 1841. The young graduate is William Thomson, who found the essay in 1845 and had it reprinted in Crelle’s journal from 1850. The mathematician of 1826 is Mikhail Ostrogradsky, who presented the divergence theorem to the Paris Academy that year and printed it in St Petersburg in 1831.
