How Sour Is It, and How Long Until Half Is Gone?

How sour is it, and how long until half is gone? Chemistry meets the log twice. Acidity runs over a range so wide, from a strong acid to a strong base about a hundred trillion times, that it is measured on a scale of logs. And take a sample in which each molecule or nucleus falls apart at random, with the same chance each second as every other. It shrinks at a rate proportional to what is left: 17f's equation, with a minus sign. This page reads both, and solves the two simplest laws of how fast a reaction runs. It uses the steady decay of atoms to date a bone, a sea floor and a meteorite.

The acidity scale counts powers of ten in the amount of free hydrogen, with the sign turned. When a reaction runs at a rate fixed by how much is left, what is left shrinks by a fixed factor in equal times, and the half-life never changes. When it runs at a rate fixed by the square of what is left, one over what is left climbs in a straight line, and each half-life is longer than the last. Radioactive decay shrinks by a fixed factor in equal times, so the logarithm of how much has gone tells the age. A buffer’s acidity is the acid’s own mark plus the logarithm of the ratio of its two forms. A chain reaction multiplies by the same factor in each generation.

The pH scale

For a solution in water with hydrogen ion concentration [H⁺] in mol/L, pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Its accepted name is the pH; pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻] likewise.

Tenfold technique: multiplying [H⁺] by 10 lowers the pH by exactly 1; and in water at 25 °C, where [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴, pH + pOH = 14.

-\log_{10}([\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]) = -\log_{10} 10^{-14} = 14 @@ the measured product; log-of-a-power
\htmlClass{new}{\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14} \quad \blacksquare @@ log-of-a-product splits the left side
  
[\text{H}^+] = 0.0025 \Rightarrow \text{pH} = -\log_{10} 0.0025 \approx 2.60 @@ a strong acid, fully given up
\text{pH} = 8.3 \Rightarrow [\text{H}^+] = 10^{-8.3} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-9} @@ seawater off Florence; undo the log (17h)
\text{pH } 4 \text{ against pH } 6 @@ 100 times the [H⁺], not 1.5 times

Weak acids and buffers

A strong acid gives up all its H⁺ in water. A weak acid HA gives up only some. The reaction runs both ways until the amounts stop changing, and a double arrow says so: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Measurement shows that, at a given temperature, the number [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] then settles at the same value Ka whatever the starting mix. This is the acid's acid dissociation constant, its accepted name; pKa = −log₁₀ Ka. A⁻ has the accepted name the conjugate base of HA. A mix of a weak acid and its conjugate base has the accepted name a buffer.

Buffer-equation technique (the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation): in such a solution, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]). So pH = pKa exactly when [A⁻] = [HA].

K_a = [\text{H}^+]\cdot\tfrac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]} @@ the measured constant
-\log_{10} K_a = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+] - \log_{10}\tfrac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]} @@ log-of-a-product for log₁₀ (log-base-p)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log_{10}\tfrac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}} \quad \blacksquare @@ rearrange; log₁₀ 1 = 0 gives the second claim

Buffer technique: let a buffer hold acid at m and base at n mol/L, and add strong acid at x mol/L, so m becomes m + x and n becomes n − x. The pH changes at the rate −(1/m + 1/n)/ln 10 per unit x at x = 0. For a fixed total m + n = c, that rate is smallest in size when m = n.

\text{pH}(x) = \text{p}K_a + \tfrac{1}{\ln 10}\big(\ln(b - x) - \ln(a + x)\big) @@ buffer-equation; log-base-p
\text{pH}'(0) = -\tfrac{1}{\ln 10}\Big(\tfrac1b + \tfrac1a\Big) @@ area-under-1/x and chain for slopes
\tfrac1a + \tfrac1b = \tfrac{c}{ab},\ \ ab = \tfrac{c^2}{4} - \big(a - \tfrac c2\big)^2 @@ completing the square (8i)
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{ab \text{ is largest, the rate smallest, at } a = b }\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac c2} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ a square is never negative
  
K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5},\ \ \text{p}K_a \approx 4.74 @@ the acid of vinegar
0.10\ \text{M alone}:\ [\text{H}^+]^2 \approx K_a \cdot 0.10,\ \ \text{pH} \approx 2.87 @@ so little is given up that [HA] stays near 0.10 and [A⁻] = [H⁺]
0.10\ \text{M acid},\ 0.10\ \text{M acetate} + 0.01\ \text{M HCl}:\ \ 4.74 + \log_{10}\tfrac{0.09}{0.11} \approx 4.66 @@ buffer-equation: the same HCl in pure water would take the pH from 7 to 2

Titration

To find how much acid a sample holds, add base of known concentration until it has all reacted, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, reading the pH as you go. Its accepted name is titration. The point where the base added exactly matches the acid at the start is the equivalence point. On the log scale of pH, the last drops before it and the first after it make a cliff.

49.9\ \text{mL}:\ [\text{H}^+] = \tfrac{0.0050 - 0.00499}{0.0999} \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-4},\ \text{pH} \approx 4.0 @@ moles of acid left over the total volume
50.0\ \text{mL}:\ \text{pH} = 7.0 @@ only water's own ions
50.1\ \text{mL}:\ [\text{OH}^-] \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-4},\ \text{pH} \approx 10.0 @@ tenfold: pH = 14 − pOH
\text{a weak acid, half the base added}:\ \text{pH} = \text{p}K_a @@ buffer-equation: half the HA has become A⁻; this is how K_a is measured

Acid in rain and in the sea

Clean rain is slightly acid, near pH 5.6, from carbon dioxide dissolved out of the air. Sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen, from burning coal and from engines, make sulfuric and nitric acid in clouds: its accepted name is acid rain. Rain at pH 4.2 carries 101.4 ≈ 25 times the H⁺ of clean rain. It leaches calcium from soils and acidifies lakes. The surface ocean has fallen from about pH 8.2 before 1750 to about 8.1 now, as it takes up carbon dioxide. That is 100.1 ≈ 1.26 times the H⁺, and its accepted name is ocean acidification. The extra H⁺ binds the carbonate that shells are built from. In 2007 and 2008, corrosive upwelled water killed most of the oyster larvae in hatcheries on the Oregon coast.

How fast a reaction runs

For a reaction using up A, the rate is −[A]′. When it is k[A], the reaction is first order; when k[A]², second order. These are their accepted names, and k is the rate constant. The time for [A] to fall to half is written t₁/₂, read “t half”.

First-order technique: if [A]′ = −k[A], then [A] = [A]₀e−kt and the half-life is ln 2 / k, the same at every start. It is proportional-slope and doubling-time. This is the curve 13·chem's readings followed, and its halving time is the same at every stage, as those readings suggested; that flag is closed.

Second-order technique: if [A]′ = −k[A]² with [A] > 0, then 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt, and the half-life from [A]₀ is 1/(k[A]₀).

\Big(\tfrac{1}{[A]}\Big)' = -\tfrac{[A]'}{[A]^2} = -\tfrac{-k[A]^2}{[A]^2} = k @@ chain for slopes (13e) on 1/u
\tfrac{1}{[A]} = \tfrac{1}{[A]_0} + kt @@ same-slopes (14e): same slope as kt; t = 0 fixes the constant
\tfrac{2}{[A]_0} = \tfrac{1}{[A]_0} + kt_{1/2} \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{t_{1/2} = \tfrac{1}{k[A]_0}} \quad \blacksquare @@ [A] = [A]₀/2
  
k = 6.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}^{-1},\ \text{first order}:\ \ t_{1/2} = \tfrac{\ln 2}{k} \approx 1118 \text{ s} @@ first-order
k = 0.50\ \text{L/(mol·s)},\ [A]_0 = 0.20:\ \ t_{1/2} = 10 \text{ s};\ \ [A](30) = \tfrac{1}{5 + 15} = 0.05 @@ second-order; each half-life doubles the last

Atoms that fall apart

Each unstable nucleus has the same chance of decaying in each short time, whatever its age. So the count N of them falls at a rate in proportion to N: N′ = −λN. Here λ, the small Greek letter lambda, is that fixed rate for each nucleus. Its accepted name is the decay constant.

Dating technique: let a sample of N₀ unstable nuclei decay as N = N₀e−λt, with half-life T = ln 2 / λ. Then the time since it held N₀ is t = (T / ln 2) · ln(N₀/N). If each decay leaves one daughter nucleus D, and none was there at the start, t = (T / ln 2) · ln(1 + D/N).

\tfrac{N_0}{N} = e^{\lambda t} \Rightarrow t = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln\tfrac{N_0}{N} @@ 17h; λ = ln 2 / T
N_0 = N + D \Rightarrow \tfrac{N_0}{N} = 1 + \tfrac{D}{N} \quad \blacksquare @@ every lost parent is a daughter

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth, far apart

2b set two sizes of truth beside every and some. 5·chem's mole and 5·bio's 3 : 1 lived at the size of the crowd. Here the two sizes sit as far apart as they ever have. One carbon-14 nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second whatever its age, and nothing anyone knows says when it will. The crowd has a half-life of 5730 years, steady enough to date a bone ten thousand years old.

Don't carry a crowd-truth down to the one: a half-life says nothing about when any one nucleus will go.

The bridge is λ. It is one nucleus's chance per unit of time, and it is also the crowd's rate in proportion to its size. One number is said at both sizes, and it means something different at each.

A sample holds exactly two carbon-14 nuclei. How long until only one is left?

Kinds of decay, and energy from the nucleus

A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons. The number of protons, Z, fixes the element and has the accepted name the atomic number. The total, A, has the accepted name the mass number, so carbon-14 has A = 14 and Z = 6. Kinds of the same element with different A have the accepted name isotopes. An unstable nucleus decays in one of a few ways. Alpha decay throws out a helium nucleus, so A falls by 4 and Z by 2: U-238 becomes Th-234. Beta decay turns a neutron into a proton and throws out an electron, so Z rises by 1 and A stays: C-14 becomes N-14. Gamma decay throws out only energy, as light. Each is first order, so dating applies to all of them.

Splitting a heavy nucleus has the accepted name fission; joining light ones, fusion. One fission of U-235 by a slow neutron gives off about 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J and two or three new neutrons.

Fission-chain technique: if each fission leads, on average, to k further fissions, then generation g holds N₀kᵍ fissions. They die out when k < 1, stay steady when k = 1, and grow without bound when k > 1. It is 9h's steps-that-multiply with b = k, and 15i's small-powers-vanish for k < 1.

\tfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}}{235 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}} \approx 8.2 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J/kg} @@ coal gives about 2.4 × 10⁷ J/kg: over three million times less
k = 1 \text{ kept by rods that soak up neutrons} @@ fission-chain: a reactor runs at k = 1; a bomb needs k > 1
\text{Pu-239},\ T = 24{,}100\ \text{y}:\ \ 2^{-10000/24100} \approx 0.75 @@ three quarters still there after ten thousand years: the waste problem

A nuclear plant gives off no carbon dioxide while it runs. Its costs are the waste, the mining, and the risk of an accident. The Trojan plant on the Columbia near Rainier, Oregon, ran from 1976 to 1992; the Humboldt Bay plant near Eureka from 1963 to 1976. Fusion powers the Sun, but no plant on Earth has yet run on it.

The physicist who first explained these decays told of lecturing on the age of the Earth with the most famous physicist of the day in the audience. That man had set a limit on the Earth's age, on the condition that no new source of heat be found. The lecturer got past the limit by noting that radium was exactly such a source. At which, he said, the old man beamed.

Who said it first

Rutherford recalled that, lecturing on the age of the Earth with Kelvin in the audience, he got past Kelvin’s limit by noting that Kelvin had set it on the condition that no new source of heat be found, and that radium was exactly such a source; at which the old man beamed.

In 17·chem.4.6, the physicist of 1862 is William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, and the naturalist who wanted far more was Charles Darwin. The buffer equation is Lawrence Henderson’s and Karl Hasselbalch’s.
