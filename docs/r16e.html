16e · proof

Parts

Rung 13’s product rule says the slope of a product is the first’s slope times the second, plus the first times the second’s slope: two products, added. 16c makes that a statement about areas. So the areas under those two products add up to the change in the product from end to end. So if one of the two areas is hard and the other easy, the hard one can be traded for the easy one and a difference of two values. This step proves the trade, and shows how to choose which factor to treat as which. Then it uses the trade, with rung 4's induction, to find a whole family of areas at once. These are the areas under a power of the input times a power of one less the input, and they come out as ratios of rung 4’s factorials.

The area under one function times another’s slope is the change in their product less the area under the first’s slope times the second. Choose the first to get simpler when its slope is taken, and the second’s slope to stay no worse when undone. Repeated, it gives each area in the family as a ratio of factorials.

16e1The product rule, backwards

Stands on 13c (product for slopes) and 16c (area-is-a-change).

Parts technique for areas (integration by parts): if f and g have slopes at every point of [p, q], with f′ and g′ continuous there, then ∫ₚ^q f(x) g′(x) dx = f(x)g(x)|ₚ^q − ∫ₚ^q f′(x) g(x) dx.

(fg)' = f'g + fg' \text{ on } [p, q] @@ product for slopes (13c)
f'g \text{ and } fg' \text{ are continuous, so integrable} @@ f and g are continuous (slope-means-no-gap, 13b); 11b; continuous-is-integrable (15f)
\textstyle\int_p^q (f'g + fg') = fg\Big|_p^q @@ area-is-a-change (16c), with G = fg
\textstyle\int_p^q f'g + \int_p^q fg' = fg\Big|_p^q @@ areas-add (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_p^q fg' = fg\Big|_p^q - \int_p^q f'g} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract ∫ f′g from both sides (rung 3's techs)

Without ends: ∫ f g′ dx = fg − ∫ f′g dx. In 16d's bookkeeping, with dg = g′(x) dx and df = f′(x) dx, it reads ∫ f dg = fg − ∫ g df. Its accepted name is integration by parts; many books write it ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du, with letters that this ladder has already spent.

16e2Choosing the parts

Stands on 16e1 and 16d (straight-inside).

\textstyle\int_0^1 x(1 - x)^4\,dx:\ \ f = x,\ g' = (1 - x)^4 @@ f gets simpler when its slope is taken; g′ is easy to undo
g = -\tfrac{(1 - x)^5}{5},\ \ f' = 1 @@ straight-inside (16d) with k = −1
= -\tfrac{x(1 - x)^5}{5}\Big|_0^1 + \tfrac15\textstyle\int_0^1 (1 - x)^5\,dx @@ parts for areas; areas-scale
= 0 + \tfrac15 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac{1}{30} @@ the bar is 0 at both ends; 16d's third substitution
\text{the other choice: } f = (1 - x)^4,\ g' = x,\ g = \tfrac{x^2}{2} @@ legal, and worse: ∫ f′g is ∫ −4(1 − x)³ x²/2, a harder integral than the first

Parts is never wrong; it can be useless. The useful choice is the one that makes the leftover integral simpler than the one you started with. A power of x is the usual f: each slope lowers its power by one.

\textstyle\int_0^3 x\sqrt{x + 1}\,dx:\ \ f = x,\ g' = (x + 1)^{1/2},\ g = \tfrac23(x + 1)^{3/2} @@ straight-inside with fraction-power for slopes
= \tfrac23 x (x + 1)^{3/2}\Big|_0^3 - \tfrac23\textstyle\int_0^3 (x + 1)^{3/2}\,dx @@ parts for areas; areas-scale
= \tfrac23 \cdot 3 \cdot 8 - \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac25 (x + 1)^{5/2}\Big|_0^3 = 16 - \tfrac{4}{15}(32 - 1) = \tfrac{116}{15} @@ 4^{3/2} = 8, 4^{5/2} = 32

16e3A family of areas

Stands on 16e2 and 4c (induction).

Areas-under-xᵐ(1 − x)ⁿ technique: for all whole m, n ≥ 0, ∫₀¹ xᵐ(1 − x)ⁿ dx = m! n! / (m + n + 1)!.

I(m, n) = \textstyle\int_0^1 x^m (1 - x)^n\,dx @@ a name for the area
n = 0:\ \ I(m, 0) = \tfrac{x^{m+1}}{m+1}\Big|_0^1 = \tfrac{1}{m+1} = \tfrac{m!\,0!}{(m+1)!} @@ area-is-a-change with power for slopes; 0! = 1 and (m + 1)! = (m + 1) · m! (4k)
\text{suppose, for one } n \ge 1, \text{ the claim is true for } n - 1 \text{ and every } m @@ induction on n (4c), the claim for all m at once
f = (1 - x)^n,\ g' = x^m,\ g = \tfrac{x^{m+1}}{m+1},\ f' = -n(1 - x)^{n-1} @@ chain for slopes with power for slopes; power for slopes backwards
I(m, n) = \tfrac{(1 - x)^n x^{m+1}}{m+1}\Big|_0^1 + \tfrac{n}{m+1}\textstyle\int_0^1 x^{m+1}(1 - x)^{n-1}\,dx @@ parts for areas; areas-scale
= 0 + \tfrac{n}{m+1}\, I(m + 1, n - 1) @@ at 1, (1 − 1)ⁿ = 0 since n ≥ 1; at 0, 0^{m+1} = 0
= \tfrac{n}{m+1} \cdot \tfrac{(m+1)!\,(n-1)!}{(m + n + 1)!} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{m!\,n!}{(m + n + 1)!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the claim for n − 1 at m + 1; (m + 1)! = (m + 1) · m!, n! = n · (n − 1)!

16e4One area, with no expanding

Stands on 16e3 and 4k (counting choices).

So ∫₀¹ x²(1 − x)³ dx = 2! 3!/6! = 12/720 = 1/60, with no expanding. In rung 4's terms, (m + n + 1) · I(m, n) is one over the number of ways to choose m things from m + n. An area under a curve has turned into a count.

16e.4.4seamIn 1729 two mathematicians asked what 1, 2, 6, 24, 120, … should be between the whole numbers: what is the factorial of ½? A third answered with an area, which gives the factorials at whole numbers and keeps going between them. Others said that n! means the number of ways to put n things in a row, and half a thing cannot be put anywhere.
Is a factorial a count or an area?
If an area agrees with every count, has it found what the count was, or invented something new?

Who said it first

Euler, looking for a meaning for the factorial of a number that is not whole, found that the integral of xᵉ(1 − x)ⁿ from 0 to 1 gives, for whole e and n, a ratio of factorials, and that the integral still has a value when e is a fraction, where the factorials so far had none.

Euler, De progressionibus transcendentibus (written 1729–30, printed 1738); paraphrase

In 16e.4.4, the two who asked were Christian Goldbach and Daniel Bernoulli, and the one who answered with an area was Leonhard Euler.

Accepted names

The areas ∫₀¹ xᵐ(1 − x)ⁿ dx are values of the beta function; the area that carries the factorials between the whole numbers is the gamma function.
