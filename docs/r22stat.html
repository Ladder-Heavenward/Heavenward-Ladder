How many misprints on a page?

How many misprints on a page? A printer sets a few thousand letters on every page, and nearly all of them come out right. Now and then one does not. A proofreader cannot know where the next slip will fall. Yet over a whole book the slips fall into a steady pattern: so many pages clean, so many with one slip, a few with two, hardly any with more. The same pattern turns up in calls to a help line in one minute, and in crashes at one crossing in one year. What makes it hard is that each letter has its own tiny chance, there are thousands of letters, and counting every way the slips could fall is far too much work. This page shows, with 22j’s step from counting to rare events and 22d’s endless sums, that one number, the expected count, fixes the whole pattern. The chances of no slips, one slip, two slips and so on add to exactly one. Counts of this kind add when pages are put together. And there is a quick test for a count that is not of this kind at all.

When many things each have a small chance, and each happens or not apart from the others, the count of the ones that happen follows one law. The chance of none shrinks by a fixed factor for each slip expected. Each larger count has the chance of the one before it, times the expected count, divided by the new count. Those chances make an endless sum, and its total is one. The expected count is also the spread: for a count of rare events the variance equals the mean.

22stat1Many small chances, counted

A page holds N letters. Each slips with the same small chance p, and each slips or not apart from the others. The count of slips is 4·stat’s binomial count. Its expected count is λ = Np (5·stat). Hold λ fixed and let N grow, so each letter’s chance shrinks as the letters crowd in.

P(k) = \binom{N}{k}\,p^k (1 - p)^{N - k} @@ the binomial count (4·stat): k letters slip, N − k do not
p = \tfrac{\lambda}{N} @@ λ = Np, the expected count (5·stat), held fixed
\binom{N}{k}\tfrac{1}{N^k} = \tfrac{1}{k!}\cdot 1\cdot\big(1 - \tfrac1N\big)\cdots\big(1 - \tfrac{k-1}{N}\big) \to \tfrac{1}{k!} @@ k fixed factors, each heading to 1 (10f)
\big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{N - k} = \big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{N}\big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{-k} \to e^{-\lambda}\cdot 1 @@ the rare-event limit (22j)
\htmlClass{new}{P(k) \to e^{-\lambda}\,\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a product’s limit is the product of the limits (10f)

Rare-count technique: when many chances are each small and come apart from one another, the count of hits is k with chance , where λ is the expected count. Only λ is left: N and p have dropped out. That is why misprints, calls and crashes, which have nothing else in common, share one law.

\text{binomial: } P(0) = \big(1 - \tfrac{1}{3000}\big)^{2400} \approx 0.44927 @@ 4·stat
\text{rare count: } e^{-0.8} \approx 0.44933 @@ off in the fifth place

22stat2The chances add to one

A page can hold 0 slips, or 1, or 2, and so on without end, so the chances of the counts make an endless sum. If the rare-count law is a law of chance, that sum must total 1. Write Sₘ for the partial sum 1 + λ + λ²/2! + ⋯ + λᵐ/m!, and S for where the Sₘ settle, if they do.

\tfrac{\lambda^{k+1}/(k+1)!}{\lambda^k/k!} = \tfrac{\lambda}{k+1} \to 0 @@ the ratio test (22e): S settles
\big(1 + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N = \sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{N^k} \le S_N \le S @@ two-term-power (4k); each term at most λᵏ/k!, as in 22·stat.1
e^{\lambda} \le S @@ let N grow: (1 + λ/N)ᴺ → e^λ (17j)
\big(1 + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N \ge \sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{N}{k}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{N^k} \to S_m @@ m fixed, N ≥ m: drop the later terms, none negative
S_m \le e^{\lambda} \text{ for every } m,\ \text{so } S \le e^{\lambda} @@ S is the least ceiling of the rising Sₘ (22a, 22d)
\htmlClass{new}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}\,e^{\lambda} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ S = e^λ; multiply by e^−λ

Count-total technique: the chances of no hits, one hit, two hits and so on make an endless sum whose total is exactly one. 22stat3Mean and spread are the same number

The expected count of slips is a sum over every count, each times its chance. So is the expected square. Both are endless sums, and both fall to 22·stat.2’s total after a shift.

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k\,e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda @@ the k = 0 term is 0; the rest is 22·stat.2’s sum, shifted
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k(k-1)\,e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda^2 @@ the same move, twice
\text{mean of } k^2 = \lambda^2 + \lambda @@ k² = k(k − 1) + k
\htmlClass{new}{\text{variance} = \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda = \text{mean}} \quad \blacksquare @@ mean of the square less the square of the mean (8·stat)

Mean-and-spread technique: a count of rare events has its variance equal to its mean. That gives a quick test of real counts. Take a tally of 200 pages: 94 clean, 70 with one slip, 27 with two, 7 with three, 2 with four. The mean is 0.765 and the variance 0.780, close. The law with λ = 0.765 expects 93.1, 71.2, 27.2, 6.9 and 1.3 pages. A variance well above the mean says the slips are not apart from one another: they cluster.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting, to rare events

4k set beside counting a principle: random things in a crowd make smooth, steady patterns. One page can hold any number of misprints. Two hundred pages pile into a shape, and the shape is fixed by one number, counted before a single page was set.

One count can be anything; many counts make a pile, and the pile is the counting done for you.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it. “About 0.8 a page” is the middle. The law says how often the middle misses, and by how much: nearly half the pages are clean, and about one in a hundred has four or more. A tally whose spread is far wider than its middle is not the same kind of crowd.

A crossing in Tacoma averages 3 reported crashes a year. One year it has 7. Is that the pile, or something else?

22stat4Pages add

Two pages slip apart from one another, with expected counts λ and μ. The total is n when the first holds k and the second n − k, for some k from 0 to n. Those ways do not overlap, so their chances add.

P(\text{total} = n) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}\; e^{-\mu}\tfrac{\mu^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} @@ k on the first page, n − k on the second; apart, so the chances multiply
= e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\,\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\lambda^k\mu^{n-k} @@ n!/(k!(n − k)!) is n choose k
\htmlClass{new}{= e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\,\tfrac{(\lambda+\mu)^n}{n!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two-term-power (4k)

Pages-add technique: rare counts that run apart from one another add up to a rare count, and their expected counts add. A chapter of 12 pages at 0.765 slips a page expects 9.18, and comes out clean with chance . It runs the other way too: a help line at 2.4 calls a minute gets 0.4 calls in ten seconds.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The rare-count law is the Poisson distribution, and λ its rate. The rare-count technique is the Poisson limit of the binomial, also called the law of small numbers (Poisson limit theorem). The sum 1 + λ + λ²/2! + ⋯ is the exponential series. A count whose variance equals its mean is equidispersed; one whose variance is larger is overdispersed. The pages-add technique says that independent Poisson counts add to a Poisson count.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre had the limit of the binomial for small chances in 1711. Siméon Denis Poisson set it down in 1837, in a book on the chances of right and wrong verdicts in French courts. Ladislaus Bortkiewicz (1898) made it famous with the men of ten Prussian cavalry corps killed by horse kicks over twenty years: of the 200 corps-years, 109 had no deaths, 65 had one, 22 had two, 3 had three and 1 had four, against 108.7, 66.3, 20.2, 4.1 and 0.6 from the law with λ = 0.61. William Sealy Gosset (1907) used it for yeast cells counted under a microscope at a Dublin brewery, and Ernest Rutherford and Hans Geiger (1910) for flashes from radium. In 22·stat.6.5, Poisson’s use of chance on verdicts was attacked in the Paris Academy by Louis Poinsot (1836), and John Stuart Mill (1843) called such uses the opprobrium of mathematics. Courts now weigh counts of errors, in DNA evidence and in wrongful convictions, and the quarrel has not closed.
