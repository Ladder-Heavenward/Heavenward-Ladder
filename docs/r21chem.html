What Does It Cost to Turn a Bond?

What does it cost to turn a bond? A jar of butane holds one substance, although its molecules take many shapes. 9chem24 ranked those shapes in words: lowest, a little higher, high, highest. Rung 21 measures an angle as a distance round a circle, so the turn can now be a number, and the energy a function of that number which can be drawn. This page draws it, reads the heights of its hills, and compares them with the knocks a molecule takes in a warm room.

The energy of a molecule is a periodic function of the angle of turn about a single bond, and the height of its hills says whether the shapes can be kept apart. So the question of how many substances are in the jar is answered by reading a curve.

21chem1Energy Against Angle

Mike takes the model ethane of 9chem24, two carbons joined by one peg with three hydrogens on each, and this time clips a paper dial to the peg. Looking down the peg, the dial reads the angle between one hydrogen at the front and one at the back.

At 0° each hydrogen at the back is hidden behind one at the front, and the energy is at its highest: 12.6 kJ/mol above the lowest. At 60° the back hydrogens sit in the spaces, and the energy is lowest. At 120° they are hidden again. The three hydrogens on a carbon are alike, so a third of a full turn brings back the same picture.

That is 21b’s word: the energy is periodic in the angle. Its period is not a full turn but a third of one. Write the angle as θ, measured round the circle as rung 21 measures it, with a full turn 2π. A cosine that runs three times in one turn fits the measured energies closely.

\theta = 0:\ \ 6.3\,(1 + 1) = 12.6 @@ back hydrogens behind front ones
\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\ (60^\circ):\ \ 6.3\,(1 + \cos \pi) = 0 @@ back hydrogens in the spaces
\htmlClass{new}{E(\theta + \tfrac{2\pi}{3}) = E(\theta)} @@ cos has period 2π (21b), so cos 3θ has period 2π/3

Turn-energy technique: the energy of a molecule is a function of the angle of turn about a single bond, and it is periodic: it comes back to itself after a full turn, or sooner when the groups on one end are alike.

21chem2Butane’s Three Hills

Butane’s middle bond has unlike groups on each end: two hydrogens and one carbon. So its energy comes back to itself only after a full turn. The angle is now taken between the two end carbons.

angle between the two end carbonsenergy above the lowest, kJ/mol
180° (far apart)0
120° and 240°15.9
60° and 300° (side by side)3.8
0° (one behind the other)19.2

These are 9chem24’s four positions with their numbers. The curve has three dips and three hills in one turn. The two side-by-side dips, at 60° and 300°, are equal in energy, and each is the other seen in a mirror.

A molecule sits in a dip and is knocked over a hill into the next. At room temperature the knocks carry it over hills of this height many times a second. That is why the three dips are one substance: nobody can pour off the molecules that sit in one of them.

Hill technique: two shapes of a molecule are one substance when the hill between them on the energy curve is low enough to be passed many times a second, and two substances when it is not.

21chem3The Ring’s Flip in Numbers

Claire’s puckered six-ring of 9chem25 flips, every axis position becoming a rim position. The hill between the two flipped shapes is about 42 kJ/mol. That is more than twice butane’s highest hill, and it is still passed constantly at room temperature. Cool the ring far enough and the flip slows until the two shapes can be seen separately.

With one methyl group on the ring, the two dips are no longer level. The shape with the group on the rim is lower by 7.3 kJ/mol, about twice the gap between butane’s far-apart and side-by-side shapes. That is no accident of numbers. A methyl group on the axis sits side by side, in 9chem24’s sense, with two carbons of the ring, and each of those costs what one side-by-side costs in butane.

\text{one side-by-side pair in butane}:\ 3.8 @@ the table above
\text{a methyl group on the axis makes two such pairs}:\ 2 \times 3.8 = 7.6 @@ one with each ring carbon two places along
\htmlClass{new}{\text{measured}:\ 7.3} @@ close to the count

A double bond stands at the other end of the scale. Its hill is so high that no knock in a warm room passes it, which is 9chem26’s reason for calling the same-side and opposite-side forms two substances.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The angle of turn looked at down a bond is the dihedral angle or torsion angle. The cost of hidden-behind positions is torsional strain, and the cost of crowding is steric strain. The hill between two shapes is the barrier to rotation. Butane’s shapes at 180° and 60° are anti and gauche; the two positions on the ring are axial and equatorial, and the crowding of an axial group is a 1,3-diaxial interaction.

Where the numbers come from

The energies on this page (12.6; 3.8, 15.9 and 19.2; 42; 7.3 kJ/mol) are a first course’s values, 3.0; 0.9, 3.8 and 4.6; 10; 1.74 kcal/mol, turned into kJ at 4.184. Other sources give slightly different values. They have not yet been revisited against a second source. Nor have these: the cosine line for ethane, which is the usual first model and not a measurement; that the ring’s flip can be slowed by cooling until the two shapes are seen separately; that a methyl group on the axis counts as two side-by-side pairs.
