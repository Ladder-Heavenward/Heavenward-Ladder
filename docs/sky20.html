How Small Can a Set Be That Holds a Needle in Every Direction?

How small can a set be that holds a needle in every direction? A needle is a segment of length one. A disc of diameter one holds it pointing every way, and so does a triangle with equal sides and height one, which takes less room. In 1917 a question was asked in Japan: what is the least area of a region in which a needle can be turned right round? Within a few years the answer was found to be stranger than anyone expected. A set can hold a needle in every direction and have area zero. The construction cuts a triangle into thin slices from its top and slides them along the base until they pile over one another, and the more slices, the less room the pile takes. Area is then the wrong measure. The right one is dimension, the count of rung 35 of how many small squares it takes to cover a set. In the plane, such a set must have dimension two, as large as a set in the plane can be, even with no area: a count of how thin tubes round the needles overlap proves it, and this page proves it in full. In space the same question asked whether such a set must have dimension three. Every proof fell short, by a little, for decades. In February 2025 two mathematicians posted a proof that it must. In four dimensions and more the question is open. Over a finite field, ∞18 proved the matching statement in a few lines, and why that short proof does not carry over to the real line is part of what makes the question hard.

An equilateral triangle of height 1 holds a unit segment in every direction. Cut it into 2ᵏ slices from the top and slide neighbours together, k rounds, each round keeping a share α² of the cores and adding tops of share 4(1 − α)²: the pile has area at most (α²ᵏ + 4(1 − α)/(1 + α)) times the triangle’s, as small as you like; nest such piles and the limit has area 0. Yet N = ⌈1/δ⌉ tubes 1 × 2δ in separated directions overlap pairwise by at most 4δ²/sin of their angle, so by the product bound their union has area at least 1/(3 + ln(1/δ)), and covering the set takes at least 1/(9δ²(3 + ln(1/δ))) squares of side δ: dimension 2.

sky20.1Needles in Every Direction

A needle set in the plane is a set holding a segment of length 1 in every direction. The question of 1917 asked about turning a needle round inside a region, a stronger demand; holding one in every direction is the version this page answers. A disc of diameter 1 is a needle set of area π/4 ≈ 0.785. Less room will do.

Needle-triangle technique: the triangle with three equal sides and height 1, of area 1/√3 ≈ 0.577, holds a segment of length 1 in every direction.

\text{from the top corner to the opposite side: directions over an angle of } 60^\circ @@ the corner angle of a triangle with equal sides
\text{each such segment has length at least } 1 @@ the height is the shortest way from the corner to the opposite side (18a)
\text{three corners, three ranges of } 60^\circ \text{ turned by } 60^\circ \text{ and } 120^\circ @@ together the half turn: a direction and its reverse are one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every direction holds a segment of length} \ge 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ area: base 2/√3 times height 1, halved

No set without dents does better: a needle set with every chord inside it has area at least 1/√3, stated, not proved here (1920). A script checks the triangle over 3600 directions: the longest chord in each is never below 1.000. With dents allowed, the answer is no longer a shape at all. The next three footings take the triangle apart.

sky20.2Halve and Slide

Take a triangle T with its base on a line, and cut it from the top corner to the middle of the base: two triangles T₁ and T₂ with a common top and bases of equal length side by side. Slide T₂ along the line toward T₁. The two overlap low down, where they are wide, and stick out only near the top, where they are thin.

Halve-and-slide technique: if each half of the base has length 1 and T₂ slides by s toward T₁, 0 < s ≤ 1, then T₁ and the slid T₂ lie inside a triangle S similar to T with ratio α = 1 − s/2, together with the parts of T₁ and T₂ above the share 1 − s of the height; so their union has area at most (α² + 4(1 − α)²)|T|.

T_1:\ py \le x \le 1 + (p - 1)y;\quad T_2':\ 1 - s + (p - 1)y \le x \le 2 - s + (p - 2)y;\quad 0 \le y \le 1 @@ half-base 1, height 1, top (p, 1); stretching along and across the line scales every area alike
S:\ py \le x \le 2 - s + (p - 2)y,\ \ y \ge 0 \text{: corners } (0, 0),\ (2 - s, 0),\ (\alpha p, \alpha) @@ the two sides meet at y = 1 − s/2 = α: T shrunk by α toward (0, 0)
y \le 1 - s:\ \ 1 + (p - 1)y \le 2 - s + (p - 2)y,\ \ 1 - s + (p - 1)y \ge py @@ both say y ≤ 1 − s
\text{below height } 1 - s\text{, both } T_1 \text{ and } T_2' \text{ lie in } S @@ 1 − s ≤ α
\text{above it: the tops of } T_1 \text{ and } T_2'\text{, each its triangle shrunk by } s\text{: area } s^2|T|/2 \text{ each} @@ similar shapes: areas go as the square of the ratio (18)
\htmlClass{new}{|T_1 \cup T_2'| \le \alpha^2|T| + s^2|T| = \big(\alpha^2 + 4(1 - \alpha)^2\big)|T|} \quad \blacksquare @@ s = 2(1 − α)

With s = 0.2, α = 0.9: the bound is 0.81 + 0.04 = 0.85 of |T|, and slicing the union by height in a script gives 0.83, since the two tops share a sliver with S. The gain looks small. The point is that S is again a triangle, and the next footing slides cores, not triangles.

sky20.3A Tree of Triangles

Cut T from its top into 2ᵏ triangles with equal bases. Round 1: pair neighbours and halve-and-slide each pair, leaving a core triangle and two tops. Two neighbouring cores share a direction of side: the right side of one and the left side of the next both run along the cut between their middle pieces. Slid until their bases touch, they share that side and its top corner, and they are a pair again. Round 2 slides the pairs of pairs, and so on, k rounds. Every piece only slides; none turns.

Triangle-tree technique: for 1/2 ≤ α < 1, the 2ᵏ pieces can be slid along the line so that their union has area at most (α²ᵏ + 4(1 − α)/(1 + α))|T|; every piece keeps its base inside the base of T, so it moves at most the length of that base.

\text{round } j:\ \text{the cores add to } \alpha^{2j}|T| @@ halve-and-slide shrinks each pair’s core to α² of the pair
\text{the tops of round } j \text{ add to at most } 4(1 - \alpha)^2\alpha^{2(j - 1)}|T| @@ the halve-and-slide technique on the cores of round j − 1
|\text{union}| \le \alpha^{2k}|T| + 4(1 - \alpha)^2\big(1 + \alpha^2 + \cdots + \alpha^{2(k - 1)}\big)|T| @@ the union lies in the last core and all the tops
\le \alpha^{2k}|T| + \tfrac{4(1 - \alpha)^2}{1 - \alpha^2}|T| @@ the geometric sum (15i)
\text{each slide is toward the left, the leftmost piece stays, and each piece’s base stays in its core’s base} @@ cores only shrink
\htmlClass{new}{|\text{union}| \le \Big(\alpha^{2k} + \tfrac{4(1 - \alpha)}{1 + \alpha}\Big)|T|} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 − α² = (1 − α)(1 + α)

With α = 0.96 and k = 49: 0.96⁹⁸ + 0.16/1.96 = 0.018 + 0.082, below a tenth of |T|, with 2⁴⁹, about 5.6 × 10¹⁴, pieces. For a hundredth the script needs k = 777, with α = 0.996. Slicing by height at k = 10, α = 0.9 gives 0.214 of |T| against the bound 0.332. Each piece still holds its segments from top to base, so the union is a needle set for the 60° of T, and three such trees give every direction in area as small as you like. It looks like a tree of thin branches, and its accepted name says so.

sky20.4Size Zero

Area as small as you like is not area 0. To reach 0, nest: replace each triangle of a pile by a tree of its own thin slices, kept close to it, and repeat; what is left at the end of every stage is the set.

Size-zero technique: some closed and bounded set in the plane has area 0 and holds a segment of length 1 in every direction.

F_1 = \text{three triangles of height } 1 \text{ at } 0^\circ, 60^\circ, 120^\circ;\ \ V_1 = \text{the points within } r_1 \text{ of } F_1 @@ the needle-triangle technique
F_k \to F_{k+1}:\ \text{cut each triangle of } F_k \text{ from its top into slices of base} \lt r_k/2\text{; a tree of each slice} @@ the triangle-tree technique: each slice moves less than r_k/2
|F_{k+1}| \le 2^{-k - 1},\ \ F_{k+1} \subseteq \text{points within } r_k/2 \text{ of } F_k @@ trees of share 2^(−k−1)/|F_k|; the slices keep height 1 and their directions
V_{k+1} = \text{the points within } r_{k+1} \le r_k/4 \text{ of } F_{k+1},\ \ |\overline{V_{k+1}}| \le 2^{-k} @@ closed V within 3r_k/4 of F_k, inside V_k; r small: a strip of width r round each edge adds little
E = \textstyle\bigcap_k \overline{V_k}:\ \ |E| \le |\overline{V_k}| \le 2^{1-k} \text{ for every } k @@ a smaller set is not larger (30a2), in the plane’s sizes (30e1)
\text{direction } \theta:\ \text{unit segments } I_k \subseteq F_k\text{; their midpoints have a settling thread} @@ the halving technique for sequences (22b2), in each coordinate
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the limit segment lies in every closed } \overline{V_j}\text{: in } E\text{, with } |E| = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ I_k ⊆ V_j for k ≥ j; limits of points of a closed set stay in it

The set E has no area, yet it is not small in every sense: it holds a segment in each of uncountably many directions (35b). It is closed and bounded, like 35h’s dust, and like the dust its size and its length tell different stories. The question is now which measure sees it.

sky20.5Dimension Two in the Plane

Use 35h2’s count: cover a set by squares of side δ, let M be the least number that does it, and read the dimension from log M / log(1/δ) as δ shrinks. A square of side 1 gives 2. The dust of 35h gives 0.631. A needle set has area 0. What dimension does it have?

Tube-overlap technique: if E holds a segment of length 1 in every direction, then for 0 < δ < 1 the points within δ of E have area at least 1/(3 + ln(1/δ)); so covering E takes at least 1/(9δ²(3 + ln(1/δ))) squares of side δ, and E has dimension 2.

N = \lceil 1/\delta \rceil,\ \theta_j = j\pi/N;\ \ T_j = \text{the } 1 \times 2\delta \text{ rectangle round a unit segment of } E \text{ in direction } \theta_j @@ each T_j lies within δ of E
|T_i \cap T_j| \le \tfrac{4\delta^2}{\sin \varphi},\ \ \varphi = \tfrac{\pi d}{N},\ d \le \tfrac N2 \text{ the gap between } i \text{ and } j \text{ round the half turn} @@ two strips of width 2δ cross in a shape with sides at angle φ: area 4δ²/sin φ
\sin\tfrac{\pi d}{N} \ge \tfrac{2d}{N} \ \Rightarrow\ \textstyle\sum_{i, j}|T_i \cap T_j| \le 2\delta N + 4\delta^2N^2(1 + \ln N) @@ sine lies above its chord on a quarter turn; for each i, d = 1, 2, … on both sides, and Σ 1/d ≤ 1 + ln N
\big(\textstyle\sum_j |T_j|\big)^2 = \big(\int \sum_j 1_{T_j}\big)^2 \le \big|\bigcup_j T_j\big| \int \big(\sum_j 1_{T_j}\big)^2 = \big|\bigcup_j T_j\big| \sum_{i, j}|T_i \cap T_j| @@ the product-bound technique (31a3), with the indicator of the union
|E_\delta| \ge \tfrac{4\delta^2N^2}{2\delta N + 4\delta^2N^2(1 + \ln N)} \ge \tfrac{1}{3/2 + \ln N} \ge \tfrac{1}{3 + \ln(1/\delta)} @@ E_δ the points within δ of E; 1 ≤ δN ≤ 2
M \text{ squares of side } \delta \text{ cover } E \Rightarrow E_\delta \text{ lies in } M \text{ squares of side } 3\delta:\ \ M \ge \tfrac{|E_\delta|}{9\delta^2} @@ each square with its ring of neighbours
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\log M}{\log(1/\delta)} \ge 2 - \tfrac{\log 9 + \log(3 + \ln(1/\delta))}{\log(1/\delta)} \to 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ and M ≤ a fixed multiple of 1/δ², since E is bounded

The numbers are slow. At δ = 10⁻⁶ the bound gives |E_δ| ≥ 0.0595, at least 6.6 × 10⁹ squares, and log M / log(1/δ) ≥ 1.64; the limit 2 is reached only as δ shrinks without end. A finer measure of dimension, which allows covers by squares of different sizes, also gives 2 in the plane; that was proved in 1971 and is held in trust here. The same count, applied to averages of a function over thin tubes instead of to a set, bounds what is called the maximal function of the needle problem, stated and not proved here.

sky20.6In Space

In space a needle set holds a unit segment in every direction of space. It can have volume 0, by the same trees. Its dimension is at most 3. Is it 3? A first bound comes from its shadow.

Floor-shadow technique: a set in space that holds a segment of length 1 in every direction has dimension at least 2.

\text{shadow } (x, y, z) \mapsto (x, y) @@ straight down onto the floor
\text{a segment in a level direction casts a segment of the same length} @@ its z does not change
\text{the shadow holds a unit segment in every direction of the floor} @@ every floor direction is a level direction of space
M \text{ cubes of side } \delta \text{ cover } E \Rightarrow \text{their } M \text{ shadows, squares of side } \delta\text{, cover the shadow} @@ a cube with level faces casts a square
\htmlClass{new}{M \ge \tfrac{1}{9\delta^2(3 + \ln(1/\delta))}\text{: dimension at least } 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tube-overlap technique on the shadow

Over a finite field the answer is complete in every dimension: a set holding a line in every direction has at least qⁿ/n! points, by sky18.2, a fixed share of the grid. Over the reals, a bound of 1995 raised the dimension in space from 2 to 5/2, and later work went just past 5/2. In February 2025 two mathematicians posted a proof that every needle set in space has dimension 3. Its method, told and not proved here: if the bound failed, the tubes round the needles would have to crowd into thin flat regions, grains, where many tubes meet, and they would have to cling together across scales, nearby directions placed nearby, as in the tree of sky20.3; the proof shows both kinds of crowding force the dimension up.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞20.oq.1Must a needle set in four dimensions, or more, have the full dimension? In the plane it is 2 (1971), in space 3 (2025). From four dimensions on it is open; a claimed proof for four dimensions has been posted and has not been revisited for this page.
Why it is hard: the tubes can crowd in more ways as the dimension grows, and every known method loses a little at each scale.
∞20.oq.2Does the maximal function bound hold past the plane? The bound says that averages of a function over thin tubes, taking the largest average in each direction, cannot be much larger in total than the function itself, with a loss of at most any small power of 1/δ. It implies the dimension statement. In the plane it is proved by sky20.5’s count. Whether the dimension statement and the bound go together, in space and above, is the question this page leaves open; whether the 2025 work also gives the bound in space has not been revisited here. A preprint of June 2025 (Rios and Sawyer) was posted as a proof of the maximal bound in space; by its fourth version, in September 2025, its abstract claims only that the bound is equivalent to a three-way form of itself, with the tubes split into three well-separated bundles. That comes from the preprint alone, one source, and has not been revisited for this page.
∞20.oq.3How far does ∞18’s polynomial method reach into the real question? Over a finite field a few lines settle every dimension (sky18.2); over the reals the answer in space took until 2025 and used other ideas. Is there a real form of the finite-field proof, or do the two questions differ in kind? This is ∞18.oq.3, asked now from the other side.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question was asked in Japan in 1917, about turning a needle in a region of least area, for regions without dents; a three-cornered curved shape that a needle can turn inside was suggested as best for regions with dents. For regions without dents the answer came in 1920: the triangle with equal sides and height 1, of area 1/√3.

About 1920 a set of area 0 holding a segment in every direction was built; the accounts give 1919 or 1920. In 1928 it was shown that a needle can be turned round in a region of area as small as you like, and in the same year a simpler construction by sliding triangles, the one of this page, was published. The intuition that turning a needle needs room had been wrong, and the turning question was closed in a way nobody had wanted.

The question did not die; it changed measure. In 1971 a paper proved that every needle set in the plane has dimension 2. Later the needle sets moved to the centre of harmonic analysis, where the maximal function was studied as a tool for questions about the turnings of 32 in higher dimensions.

In space the progress was by small steps. A bound of 1995 gave 5/2. A theory of how tubes can crowd grew through the 2000s and 2010s, and in 2014 it was shown that a needle set of too small a dimension would have to be grainy. Meanwhile, in 2008 the finite-field question was settled in under a page (∞18), and many hoped the method would carry over; it did not, directly.

In February 2025 two mathematicians posted a proof that needle sets in space have dimension 3, and in March a science magazine called it a once-in-a-century proof. What is argued now is whether its methods reach four dimensions and the maximal function, and whether the related questions of harmonic analysis will follow.

A note on the record: the dates, bounds and constructions told here are checked against two sources. The claimed proof for four dimensions, and whether the 2025 work also gives the bound for the maximal function in space, have not been revisited for this page.

The accepted names

A needle set is a Kakeya set (or Besicovitch set); the turning question is the Kakeya needle problem, and the three-cornered curve suggested for it is the deltoid. The needle-triangle technique, with its converse for convex sets, is Pál’s theorem. The halve-and-slide and triangle-tree techniques are the Perron tree construction, after Perron’s 1928 simplification of Besicovitch’s; the size-zero technique is the existence of a Besicovitch set of measure zero. The tube-overlap technique is Córdoba’s L² argument, giving Minkowski (box) dimension 2; the finer measure is Hausdorff dimension, and the 1971 result is Davies’ theorem. The maximal function is the Kakeya maximal function, and ∞20.oq.2 the Kakeya maximal conjecture. The 1995 bound is Wolff’s hairbrush bound, the grains are from Guth’s polynomial partitioning work, and the 2025 result is the Wang–Zahl theorem, the Kakeya set conjecture in three dimensions.

Who said it first

Sōichi Kakeya posed the question in 1917. Gyula Pál proved the convex case in 1920. Abram Besicovitch constructed a set of measure zero about 1919–20, and showed in 1928 that a needle can be turned in arbitrarily small area. Oskar Perron simplified the construction in 1928. Roy Davies proved the plane case of dimension 2 in 1971. Thomas Wolff proved the 5/2 bound in 1995. Larry Guth showed the graininess in 2014. Zeev Dvir proved the finite-field case in 2008. Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl posted the three-dimensional proof in February 2025.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each tree has area below ε, so let ε go to 0: the trees tend to a set of area 0 holding every direction.” Not allowed: the trees for different ε are different sets, not one shrinking set, and their segments need not settle anywhere. Area 0 needs a nested construction, each stage inside a neighbourhood of the last, and a settling thread to carry each segment to the limit.
