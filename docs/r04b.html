4b · proof

Induction

This is the step the rung is named for. 4a proved that the counting numbers are an inductive set lying inside every inductive set. From that alone comes the principle of induction: whatever is so of 0, and passes from each number to its next one, is so of every number. The step proves it, in a set form and a form for conditions, and turns it into a shape of proof. Then it puts the shape to work at once. Every number is 0 or a next one, so the rest of every cargo chain is thrown out. Every number is a statement that names a set whose members are also its parts. Merging undoes the next-one gesture on every number. And a pair of techniques 4a and 3a proved with foundation are proved again for numbers without it. Last, the dominoes, and a gallery of proofs that look like induction and are not.

To prove something of every number, prove it of 0, then show that whenever it is so of a number it is so of that number's next one. The numbers that have the property make an inductive part of the numbers, and the numbers are inside every inductive set, so the part is the whole.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1i (proof shapes), 2b (sifting, the sift-is-part technique), 2a (the techniques double-inclusion, chain for part of), 2c (⋃), 3a (the techniques next-membership, in-its-next, next-never-empty), 3b (inductive) and 4a (ℕ, the techniques membership-in-ℕ, lies-below, zero-is-a-number, next-of-a-number, strays, different-nexts). Every later step of the rung proves its techniques with this step's technique; 4c uses it to show that defining by repeating works; 4g gives it a stronger form.

4b1The principle, in words

Stands on 4a9 (the smallest inductive set) and 1a (induction over cases).

Take a property of numbers. Suppose it is so of 0. Suppose also that, for any number at all, if the property is so of it then it is so of that number's next one. Then it is so of every number. Said as sets: a part of ℕ that holds 0 and holds the next one after each of its members is all of ℕ.

Example: the property “n is a set”. 0 is a set. And the next one after a set is a set, a merge of two sets. So every number is a set.

Why should that be so? Because such a part is inductive: holding 0 is the base part, and holding the next one after each member is the step part. And 4a's lies-below technique says ℕ lies inside every inductive set, so ℕ lies inside the part. A part of ℕ that ℕ lies inside is ℕ. That is the whole proof, and below it is set out a claim at a time.

A note about the name. This is induction over the series, called mathematical induction, and it is not the induction over cases of 1a, from cases to a rule. Checking 0, 1, 2 and a thousand more numbers proves nothing about the next. The principle proves the rule for every number from two pieces of work, the base and the step, and the step is itself proved for any number at all. It is built from induction over the arrangement, like every other argument on the ladder, so its conclusion cannot fail.

4b2The set form

Stands on 4b1, 4a7 (lies-below) and 2a (double-inclusion).

Induction technique, set form: a part of ℕ that holds 0, and holds the next one after each of its members, is all of ℕ.

The plan: show the part is inductive, then let lies-below and double-inclusion finish.

Suppose A is a part of ℕ. Suppose also that 0 is in A, and that A holds the next one after each of its members. Those two are the base part and the step part. Pack them: A is inductive.

4a’s lies-below technique says ℕ is a part of every inductive set. A is one, so ℕ is a part of A. Now each of A and ℕ is a part of the other. By the double-inclusion technique, A = ℕ.

4b3The form for a condition

Stands on 4b2 (the set form) and 2b (sift).

A short argument, and most of its sentences are given or packing. The work was done on 4a, by sifting the cargo away; induction is what that sift was for.

Induction technique, condition form: a property that is so of 0, and is carried from each number to its next one, is so of every number. Write φ(n) for the property, a condition in 2b's sense with its gap at n.

The plan: sift ℕ by φ, and show the sift holds 0 and the next one after each of its members.

Suppose φ(0). Suppose also that, for every number n, φ(n) gives φ(S(n)). Sift ℕ by φ, and call the sift A: the numbers n with φ(n). A sift of ℕ is a part of ℕ.

The base part. 0 is a number, and φ(0) is true. Pack the sift: 0 is in A.

The step part. Let n be anything, and suppose n is in A. Unpack the sift: n is a number, and φ(n). Use the given every with n: φ(S(n)). And S(n) is a number, by next-of-a-number. Pack the sift: S(n) is in A.

So A holds 0 and the next one after each of its members. The set form says A = ℕ. Now let n be any number. A letter such as n, holding the place of any number at all, is called a variable. Then n is in A, since A is ℕ. Unpack the sift: φ(n). And n was any number, so φ is true of every number.

4b4Base, step, hypothesis

Stands on 4b3 and 1i (proof shapes).

The two pieces of work have names.

The base is φ(0).

The step is ∀n ∈ ℕ (φ(n) ⇒ φ(S(n))). Inside it, the supposed φ(n) is called the induction hypothesis.

The hypothesis is not supposing what you want to prove. It is the hypothesis of an if, opened by 1d's tech and closed by it, for a number n about which nothing else is supposed. What you want to prove is the every, and the every comes from the induction technique.

1i's table of proof shapes said rung 4 would add a shape of its own. Here it is. Proof by induction. Goal: ∀n ∈ ℕ φ(n). Opening: “base: φ(0)”, proved however you can; then “step: let n be a number; suppose φ(n)”. Closing: “… so φ(S(n)); prove an if; speak of everything; by the induction technique, φ(n) for every number n.” Every proof on 4c to 4g has this skeleton, often nested.

4b5Why it is a technique here

Stands on 4b2 and 4a1 (cargo).

Early in the century before this one, a great mathematician called this reasoning by recurrence, and thought it the characteristic form of mathematical reasoning: it holds an endless run of syllogisms condensed into a statement. He judged that it could not be reduced to logic, and took it to be an intuition the mind has before any proof. Another writer listed it among his axioms for the numbers. A third proved it, as a technique about his chains, which is the road this ladder has taken.

Here it is a technique. The endless run of syllogisms has not vanished: it was paid for, and for good, by the infinity axiom and by 4a's sift. And the sift is exactly what the technique needs. Run the set form on an inductive set I that carries cargo, with A = I. The argument reaches “I is inductive” and “ℕ ⊆ I”, and stops, because I ⊆ ℕ is not given and is false. A property can be so of 0, pass along every step, and fail for the cargo. Induction is a technique about ℕ, not about every inductive set.

4b6The dominoes

Stands on 4b4 (base and step).

Stand a row of dominoes, a domino for each number, so that each, falling, knocks over the next. Push the domino at the start. The push is the base, the spacing is the step, and the conclusion is that every domino falls. Take away the push and nothing falls, however well spaced the row is. Leave a gap in the spacing and everything past the gap stays standing, whatever fell before it. The picture lets you do both. What the picture cannot show is the reason it works. The row has no extra pieces lying beside it, no cargo the falls never reach: that is 4a's sift. The fallen dominoes make an inductive set, and the numbers lie inside every inductive set, so every number has fallen.

4b7Every number is 0 or a next one

Stands on 4b4 and 1c (add an option).

The earliest induction.

Zero-or-a-next technique: every number is 0, or is the next one after some number.

Notice that the step never uses its hypothesis: S(n) is the next one after n, whatever is so of n. You still need induction, because the conclusion speaks of every number. Induction is the only road from ℕ's description to a fact about all its members.

The condition φ(n): n is 0, or n is the next one after some number.

The base. 0 = 0, by the self-sameness axiom. Add an option: 0 is 0, or the next one after some number. That is φ(0).

The step. Let n be a number, and suppose φ(n). S(n) = S(n), by self-sameness, and n is a number. So S(n) is the next one after some number, with n as the example. Add an option: that is φ(S(n)). The hypothesis φ(n) was never used.

The induction technique closes it: φ(n) for every number n.

4b8The cargo goes

Stands on 4b7, 4a12 (strays) and 4a13 (different-nexts).

Put zero-or-a-next beside 4a's techniques and the cargo goes.

Back-from-a-next technique: if the next one after x is a number, so is x.

Suppose S(x) is a number. Use zero-or-a-next with S(x): S(x) is 0, or S(x) is the next one after some number. A next is never empty, so S(x) is not 0. The remaining option is true: S(x) = S(m) for some number m. Call it m.

By different-nexts, x = m. And m is a number, so swap: x is a number. That claim does not mention m, so the some closes. Drop the supposition: if S(x) is a number, so is x.

Turn it about by 1d's turn-and-deny technique for If: if x is not a number, neither is S(x). 4a showed {1} is not a number (the strays technique). So S({1}) is not a number, and so S(S({1})) is not, and so on down the chain as far as you write. Whatever cargo chain starts at a stray, the stray is thrown out by strays and every later link by back-from-a-next. The vote in 4a's picture left S({1}) standing because no voter drawn there lacked it; ℕ itself lacks it.

Zero-or-a-next has a companion you will use constantly: every number other than 0 is the next one after exactly one number. There is at least one, by zero-or-a-next and the remaining-option technique. There is at most one, by different-nexts. 4e calls on this companion every time it opens up a number that is not 0.

4b9Transitive sets

Stands on 2a9 (⊆), 2e12 (transitive relations) and 3a7 (each is the set of those before it).

3a noticed that each of 0 to 4 is both a member and a part of each later one, and that every member of 4 is also a subset of 4. That latter fact deserves a name, because it is what makes merging undo S and what makes “less than” behave on 4g.

From here on, the statement “t is a transitive set” means this. Each member of t is also a part of t.

The word is 2e's, used of a set rather than a relation, and both uses agree. Unpack ⊆ and the line says: if x ∈ y and y ∈ t, then x ∈ t. That is 2a's illegal “membership chaining”, which is illegal in general and legal exactly into a transitive set. So a transitive set is one into which ∈ chains, the way a relation is transitive when it chains.

Example: 3 = {0, 1, 2} is transitive, since 0, 1 = {0} and 2 = {0, 1} are all parts of 3. {1} is not transitive: 1 ∈ {1}, and 0 ∈ 1, but 0 ∉ {1}.

4b10Every number is transitive

Stands on 4b9, 4b4 (induction) and 3a4 (in-its-next).

Numbers-are-transitive technique: every number is a transitive set. The proof is by induction.

The base. Suppose y is in 0. But 0 has no members, so that clashes, and the clash permits everything: y is a part of 0. So each member of 0 is a part of 0, and 0 is transitive.

The step. Let n be a number, and suppose n is transitive. That is the induction hypothesis. Let y be anything in S(n). By next-membership, y is in n, or y is n.

Reason by cases. If y is in n, the hypothesis makes y a part of n. And n is a part of S(n), by in-its-next. Chain the two: y is a part of S(n). If y is n, then y is a part of S(n) at once, by in-its-next. Either way, y is a part of S(n). So S(n) is transitive.

The induction technique closes it: every number is a transitive set.

4b11Two techniques from the step

Stands on 4b10.

Look at the step. It never used that n is a number. It showed that the next one after any transitive set is transitive.

Transitive-next technique: the next one after a transitive set is a transitive set.

The base and transitive-next together give numbers-are-transitive through the induction technique. Another technique goes the same way, and 4g leans on it.

ℕ-is-transitive technique: every member of a number is a number.

The condition is ∀y (y ∈ n ⇒ y ∈ ℕ). The base is true because 0 has no members. In the step, a member of S(n) is a member of n, a number by the hypothesis, or is n, a number by supposition.

4b12Merging undoes the next one

Stands on 4b10 and 3a11 (merging back).

3a showed that merging undoes S on 0 to 4, and said why: each member is also a part, so tipping out the members of S(n) gives back nothing beyond n. Numbers-are-transitive makes that true of every number.

Merge-undoes-next technique: for every number n, merging the members of the next one after n gives back n. In marks: ⋃S(n) = n.

The plan: show that each side has every member of the other.

Let n be a number. Suppose first that y is in ⋃S(n). Then y is in some member of S(n); call it z. By next-membership, z is in n, or z is n. If z is in n, numbers-are-transitive makes z a part of n, so y is in n. If z is n, swap: y is in n. Either way, y is in n.

Now suppose instead that y is in n. And n is in S(n), by in-its-next. So y is in a member of S(n), with n as the example: y is in ⋃S(n).

Each way is true, for every y. By the same-members axiom, ⋃S(n) = n.

4b13The other road

Stands on 4b12, 4a13 (different-nexts) and 3a9 (never-its-own-next).

And with merge-undoes-next, another proof that S is one-to-one, for numbers. If m and n are numbers and S(m) = S(n), then ⋃S(m) = ⋃S(n) by the swap axiom. Merge-undoes-next turns each side into m and n: m = n. That is the proof the next-one gesture was built for: S adds the old set as a member, and merging takes the newest member back out.

Compare the roads. 4a's proof of different-nexts works for every set and leans on foundation, through 2f's no-loop. This one works only for numbers and never touches foundation: numbers-are-transitive, merge-undoes-next and the techs are all it uses.

The same goes for 3a's never-its-own-next technique, that nothing is its own next. 3a proved it for every set with foundation (the no-self-membership technique). For numbers it is an induction. Base: S(0) ≠ 0, by next-never-empty. Step: if S(n) ≠ n, then S(S(n)) ≠ S(n). For S(S(n)) = S(n) would give S(n) = n by different-nexts as just proved for numbers (S(n) and n are both numbers), clashing with the hypothesis.

Why prove a thing again? Because the later proof says something the earlier does not: the counting numbers do not need foundation for these techniques. Foundation forbids loops everywhere; for the numbers, induction does the forbidding by itself. In 2f's looped world, where Ω = {Ω} and foundation fails, S(Ω) = Ω, and never-its-own-next fails for Ω. The induction above says nothing about Ω, since Ω is not a number there.

4b16Running it backwards

Stands on 4b1 to 4b14.

Wanting ∀n ∈ ℕ φ(n), you name φ before anything else, as a statement with a gap, so that φ(0), φ(n) and φ(S(n)) are definite statements you can write down.

Then a pair of pieces of scratch work. For the base, φ(0), often by unpacking 0 as ∅. For the step, write φ(S(n)) and look for φ(n) inside it, or for a way to reach it from φ(n). Next-membership is usually the opening move, since it says what is in S(n).

If the step works for every n but 0, check the base with extra care. If it works only from some number on, you have a gap. Find the number where it fails, and see whether the statement fails there too. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Written as axioms, these lines are Peano’s axioms. Reasoning by recurrence is Henri Poincaré’s phrase; the writer who listed induction as an axiom, in 1889, is Giuseppe Peano; the one who proved it about his chains is Richard Dedekind. The looped world is Peter Aczel’s. The horses are usually credited to George Pólya.

Accepted names

The five techniques, taken as axioms, are the Peano axioms. The induction technique is the principle of mathematical induction; the base and the step are the base case and the inductive step.

Wrong moves, worked

The famous fake, told about horses. 3b used the word “matching” in plain words, pairing things off member for member, and this story needs nothing more. Say a herd is matched with a set when its horses can be paired off with that set's members, each member with its own horse and each horse with its own member. The claim: in every herd, all the horses are the same colour. The “proof” is by induction on n, with φ(n): every herd matched with S(n) is all of the same colour.

Base: a herd matched with 1 = {0} is a horse alone, and a horse alone is the same colour as itself. Step: assume every herd matched with S(n) is all of the same colour, and take a herd matched with S(S(n)). Lead a horse, u, out of the barn. The horses left are matched with S(n), so they are all of the same colour. Bring u back and lead out a different horse, v. The horses left are again matched with S(n), so they too are all of the same colour. The horses that stayed in the barn both times belong to both groups. So the colour of the group left the earlier time and the colour of the group left the later time are the same colour. And the whole herd is all of the same colour. By induction, every herd is all of the same colour.

The facts about leading a horse out are granted here (4j proves the counting facts behind them); the flaw is elsewhere. Run the step at n = 0, where a herd matched with S(1) = 2 has a horse for 0 and a horse for 1, call them u and v. Lead out u, and only v is left; lead out v, and only u is left. No horse stayed in both times, and the sentence “the horses that stayed in both times belong to both groups” is about nobody. The step from 0 to 1 fails. From n = 1 on, a herd matched with S(S(n)) has a horse that is neither u nor v, and the step is sound. So the fake is a row of dominoes with a gap right after the domino at the start: nothing past a horse alone is proved.

The horses are an old classroom fake, told to make the point that the step must be true at every number, the smallest included.
