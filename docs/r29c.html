29c · proof

A plane where parallels are many

The sphere made the angles of a triangle add to more than a straight angle, but it did so by breaking one of the other axioms: two opposite points lie on many lines. The sharp case of 29a asks for something harder. It asks for a world that keeps every one of the other axioms and still lets the angles add to less. This step builds one inside an ordinary disc, using the maps of the plane from rung 25. Its lines look curved to us, but inside the disc they behave in every way the other axioms ask. Through a point off a line, endlessly many of them never meet it. That settles the trial of 1733.

Inside the disc, with arcs that meet the edge at right angles as lines and the maps of 25f as moves, every one of the other axioms holds and the parallel axiom fails. So the other axioms can neither prove it nor refute it.

What this stands on, and where it goes

29c1The disc plane

Stands on 25f, 9·cb8.

Take the points z with |z| < 1, the inside of the unit circle. Its lines are the diameters, and the arcs of circles that meet the unit circle at right angles, each taken inside the disc. Call this the disc plane. The unit circle itself is its edge, and is not part of it.

For a point a inside the disc, let fp(z) = (z − p)/(1 − p̄z), where p̄ is the conjugate of a.

Disc-moves technique: for |p| < 1, fp sends the disc onto itself and p to 0, keeps angles, and sends lines of the disc plane to lines of the disc plane.

|1 - \bar p z|^2 - |z - p|^2 = (1 - |z|^2)(1 - |p|^2) @@ expand both sides with |w|² = w w̄ (9·cb8)
|z| < 1 \iff |f_p(z)| < 1,\ \ |z| = 1 \iff |f_p(z)| = 1 @@ the right side is positive, or zero, exactly then (|p| < 1)
f_p(p) = 0,\ \ f_{-p}(f_p(z)) = z @@ substitute and simplify: f₋ₐ undoes fₐ
f_p(z + h) - f_p(z) = h \cdot \frac{1 - |p|^2}{(1 - \bar p z)(1 - \bar p z - \bar p h)} @@ subtract the fractions over one bottom
\text{flat map at } z:\ h \mapsto c\,h,\ \ c = \frac{1 - |p|^2}{(1 - \bar p z)^2} @@ the line above, as h heads to 0 (28b)
\text{multiplying by } c \text{ turns by its angle and stretches by } |c| @@ multiply-and-add (21·cb20): a product multiplies lengths and adds turns
\htmlClass{new}{f_p \text{ keeps the angle between any two curves}} \quad \blacksquare @@ chains (28c): each tangent arrow is multiplied by the same c

And fp is built from z ↦ az + b and z ↦ 1/z, so it sends circles and lines to circles and lines (25f). A line of the disc plane meets the edge at right angles. Its image meets the image of the edge, the edge again, at the same right angles. So lines go to lines. These maps, with the flip z ↦ z̄ and the turns z ↦ eiθz, are the moves of the disc plane. Any point can be moved to the centre.

29c2Through two points, one line

Stands on 29c1.

A line of the disc plane that is not a diameter lies on a circle with centre c and radius r. Meeting the unit circle at right angles means the two radii at a crossing point are at right angles, so |c|² = 1 + r² (18f’s two-sides-and-the-angle technique, with a right angle). Then |0 − c| > r: the centre of the disc is never on such a circle.

One-line technique: through two points of the disc plane passes exactly one line of the disc plane.

p \ne q \text{ in the disc};\ \ f_p(p) = 0,\ \ w = f_p(q) \ne 0 @@ disc-moves: move p to the centre
\text{lines through } 0 \text{ are diameters only} @@ |c|² = 1 + r² > r², so no arc passes through 0
\text{exactly one diameter passes through } 0 \text{ and } w @@ the line through 0 and w (18c)
\htmlClass{new}{f_{-p} \text{ of that diameter is the one line through } p \text{ and } q} \quad \blacksquare @@ disc-moves both ways: lines to lines, and f₋ₚ undoes fₚ

The other axioms can be checked in the same way: move the figure to the centre, where the lines are diameters, and check there. For sizes of segments the disc plane uses d(p, q) = ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) with ρ = |fp(q)|. It grows without bound as q nears the edge, so lengths can be laid off. 29d shows where this distance comes from.

29c3Parallels are many

Stands on 29c1, 29c2.

Many-parallels technique: through a point off a line of the disc plane pass endlessly many lines of the disc plane that never meet it.

P \text{ off the line } \ell;\ \ \text{move } P \text{ to } 0 @@ disc-moves: meeting and not meeting are kept
\ell \text{ on the circle } |z - c| = r,\ \ |c|^2 = 1 + r^2 @@ ℓ does not pass through 0, so it is not a diameter
\text{the diameter along } u,\ |u| = 1:\ \ |t u - c|^2 = r^2 \iff t^2 - 2t(u \cdot c) + 1 = 0 @@ expand, with |c|² − r² = 1
\text{it meets } \ell \text{ inside the disc} \iff |u \cdot c| > 1 @@ the two roots multiply to 1; a root of size below 1 needs (u · c)² > 1
|c| > 1,\ \text{so the directions with } |u \cdot c| \le 1 \text{ fill two arcs of the circle of directions} @@ u · c = |c| cos θ, and |cos θ| ≤ 1/|c| on two ranges of θ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{endlessly many diameters through } 0 \text{ never meet } \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ each direction in those ranges gives one; move back

So the parallel axiom fails in the disc plane. Not by a little: at every point, for every line, there is a whole fan of lines that never meet it.

29c4Angles add to less

Stands on 29c1, 29c3.

Below-straight technique: the angles of every triangle in the disc plane add to less than π.

\text{move the vertex } A \text{ to } 0 @@ disc-moves keep all three angles
\text{sides } AB, AC \text{ are diameters};\ BC \text{ is on a circle } |z-c| = r @@ only diameters pass through 0
\text{ray } t\,u \text{ through a point } w = s\,u \text{ of the chord } BC:\ \text{meets the circle first at } t_1 \le s @@ the chord lies inside the circle, and the ray is inside it exactly for t₁ ≤ t ≤ t₂
\text{the arc } BC \text{ lies inside the straight triangle } 0BC @@ every ray from 0 across the chord meets the arc first
\text{at } B,\ \text{the arc leaves between } B0 \text{ and } BC,\ \text{and not along } BC @@ a circle meets the line BC only at B and C
\angle B < \angle 0BC,\ \ \angle C < \angle 0CB @@ the disc plane’s angle is the angle of the arc’s tangent
\htmlClass{new}{\angle A + \angle B + \angle C < \angle 0 + \angle 0BC + \angle 0CB = \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ angle-sum (18f) for the straight triangle 0BC in ℝ²

So the sharp case of 29a is the geometry of the disc plane. The upright figures there have sharp top angles, and every one of the strange techniques found in 1733 is true there.

29c5The trial settled

Stands on 29a, 29c1 to 29c4, 18c.

Parallel-independent technique: the parallel axiom can be neither proved nor refuted from the other axioms.

\text{the disc plane obeys every one of the other axioms} @@ 29c1, 29c2, checked at the centre and moved
\text{the disc plane is built from } \mathbb{R}^2 @@ it is as sound as ℝ²: a false line there is a false line in ℝ²
\text{suppose the other axioms proved the parallel axiom} @@ to be refuted
\text{then it would hold in the disc plane} @@ a proof from axioms holds wherever the axioms hold
\text{it fails there} @@ many-parallels (29c3)
\text{so no proof} @@ the supposition gave a false line
\text{and no refutation: } \mathbb{R}^2 \text{ obeys the other axioms and the parallel statement} @@ 18c, by the same reasoning
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the parallel axiom is independent of the other axioms}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines above

The trial of 1733 could never end in a refutation. Two thousand years of attempts to prove the fifth postulate were attempts to prove something the other axioms do not say. Geometry is not one thing. There are at least three, flat, round and saddle, each as sound as the numbers it is built from. Which of them the world we live in obeys is not a question the axioms can answer. It is a question for measurement.

29c.4.5seamIn 1781 a philosopher held that the flat geometry of the ancient books, parallel axiom and all, is known before any experience: the mind brings it to the world, so the world cannot break it. In letters he never published, the greatest calculator of the next century worked out a geometry with many parallels, and wondered whether space might obey it. Two younger men published it, in Kazan in 1829 and in Hungary in 1832. In 1902 a French mathematician answered that no measurement can decide: a measured sum below π can always be blamed on bent light rays instead of bent space, so the geometry is a convention, chosen for convenience. In 1915 a physicist built a theory of gravity in which space and time have a geometry that is measured, and that bends near mass.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The disc plane is the Poincaré disc model of the hyperbolic plane; its geometry is hyperbolic geometry (or Lobachevskian, or Bolyai–Lobachevskian geometry). The maps fₐ are disc automorphisms, a kind of Möbius transformation; maps that keep angles are conformal. A model of a list of axioms is a structure in which all of them hold, and the parallel-independent technique is the independence of the parallel postulate. Geometry with the other axioms alone is neutral geometry.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss worked out much of the geometry with many parallels from about 1813 and never published it. Nikolai Lobachevsky published it in Kazan in 1829, and János Bolyai in an appendix to his father’s textbook in 1832. Neither showed that it could not lead to a false line. Eugenio Beltrami did, in 1868, by building a model of it inside ordinary geometry; Felix Klein gave another in 1871, and Henri Poincaré the disc and the half-plane in 1882. In his Critique of Pure Reason (1781) Immanuel Kant had held that Euclid’s geometry is known before any experience; the models showed that logic alone does not force it. In Science and Hypothesis (1902) Poincaré argued that the geometry of space is a convention, chosen for convenience, since a measured angle sum can always be read as bent light rather than bent space. Albert Einstein’s general theory of relativity (1915) made the geometry of space and time something measured, bent by mass.
