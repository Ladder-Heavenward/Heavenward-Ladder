How Can Tides Be Predicted Years Ahead?

How can tides be predicted years ahead? The tide table for Seattle for a year from now is already printed, to the minute and to a few centimetres, and the ferries from the terminal at Colman Dock plan by it. Next year’s weather cannot be predicted beyond a week or two. Why is the tide different? Both are water and air pushed about by the Sun. What makes the question hard is that the tide does not repeat. Two high waters a day, but not quite two; a big range at full and new moon and a small one between; the two daily highs at Seattle often very unequal, and the inequality coming and going through the month and the year. No single period fits. The answer this page gives is that the tide is a sum of turnings whose speeds are all set by the motions of the Moon and the Sun, and those motions are known for centuries. Only the size and the timing of each turning at each port must be measured, and a year of records is enough to find them. Then the turnings are added forward, as far ahead as anyone likes, and for most of a century that adding was done by a machine of pulleys.

The tide at a port is a sum of turnings at speeds fixed by the Moon and the Sun, which are known in advance; only each turning’s size and timing are measured. Over a long record, two turnings whose speeds differ are nearly at right angles, so each size is found as a shadow, once the record is longer than the time it takes the two to drift a whole turn apart. Two turnings close in speed beat, which is the cycle of spring and neap tides. Added forward, the sum predicts the tide for years, with the weather as its exception.

32nav1Speeds Fixed by the Sky

The Moon’s pull raises two bulges of water, one under it and one opposite, and the Earth turns under them. The Moon returns overhead every 24 h 50 min, so the main lunar turning repeats every 12.4206 h. The Sun makes its own two bulges, every 12 h exactly. The tilt of the Moon’s and the Sun’s paths adds turnings near one a day, and the Moon’s changing distance adds others near the main one.

\text{main lunar: } 360/12.4206 = 28.984 @@ twice a lunar day
\text{main solar: } 360/12 = 30.000 @@ twice a solar day
\text{lunar distance: } 360/12.6583 = 28.440 @@ the Moon near and far over its 27.55-day orbit
\text{daily, lunar and solar: } 15.041,\ \ \text{daily, lunar: } 13.943 @@ the tilt of the paths: periods 23.934 h and 25.819 h
\htmlClass{new}{h(t) \approx h_0 + \textstyle\sum_k A_k\cos(\omega_kt - g_k)} @@ the sum of turnings, each ωₖ fixed in advance

No single repeat fits these speeds: 28.984 and 30 are not whole multiples of one speed. The tide is not a periodic function, so 32a does not apply to it as it stands. It is a finite sum of turnings with known speeds, and what is left to find is two numbers, Aₖ and gₖ for each one, at each port.

32nav2Sizes From a Record

A gauge records h(t) for a time T. Take the product ⟨f, g⟩ = (1/T)∫₀ᵀ f ḡ dt over the record. The turnings at the tide’s speeds are not exactly at right angles over a finite record. They are nearly so, if the record is long.

Long-record technique: over a record of length T, the product of two turnings whose speeds differ by Δω has size at most 2/(|Δω|T); so once T is long against 2π/|Δω|, the turnings are nearly at right angles, and each term’s size and timing is its shadow.

32nav3Spring and Neap

At Seattle the main lunar turning has a size of about 1.07 m and the main solar about 0.27 m. They are close in speed, so they beat, by 32·phys’s beat technique, but they are not equal in size.

A_1e^{i\omega_1t} + A_2e^{i\omega_2t} = e^{i\omega_1t}\big(A_1 + A_2e^{i(\omega_2 - \omega_1)t}\big) @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
|A_1 + A_2e^{i\Delta\omega t}| \text{ swings from } A_1 - A_2 \text{ to } A_1 + A_2 @@ the second turning goes round the first
\text{once every } \tfrac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega} = 14.77 \text{ days} @@ 32·nav.2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at Seattle: about } 1.34 \text{ m at springs, } 0.80 \text{ m at neaps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the half-range of the twice-daily tide

Springs come at full and new moon, when the Sun and Moon pull along one line and the two turnings are in step; neaps come at the quarters. The daily turnings add a second beat, which makes Seattle’s two daily highs unequal, most of all when the Moon is far north or south.

32nav4Adding Forward

Once the Aₖ and gₖ of a port are found, the prediction for any time t, next week or in fifty years, is the sum of a few dozen cosines. Before computers, a machine did the sum. Each turning was a wheel turning at its speed, carrying a pulley up and down by Aₖ cos(ωₖt − gₖ); one wire ran over all the pulleys, and its end, holding a pen, rose and fell by the sum. A year of Seattle’s tides took an afternoon to draw.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The sum of turnings is a law: a measured regularity, true over its range, with exceptions that recur. At Seattle the predicted water level misses the measured one by more than about 15 cm on some days each winter, when a storm’s low pressure and wind pile water into the Sound.

A tide table is a law with a known exception rate: the turnings predict for years, and the weather’s surges are counted beside them.

The exceptions are known and counted. The weather adds a surge that no turning holds, and its sizes and how often they come are recorded alongside the tide. A prediction is given as the sum of turnings, with a stated chance of a surge of a given size.

If a harbour master in Seattle wanted to know how often the water will stand more than 30 cm above the table next winter, what record would you ask for?

The accepted names

The tide’s turnings are its harmonic constituents: M2 (main lunar), S2 (main solar), N2 (lunar distance, or larger lunar elliptic), K1 (lunisolar diurnal) and O1 (lunar diurnal), with P1 (solar diurnal) beside K1. Aₖ is a constituent’s amplitude and gₖ its phase lag. Finding them from a record is harmonic analysis; the long-record technique is the Rayleigh criterion for separating constituents, 1/T. The machine is a tide-predicting machine. The unequal daily highs are the diurnal inequality, and a sum of turnings with unrelated speeds is an almost periodic function. The weather’s addition is a storm surge.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton explained the two bulges in 1687, and Pierre-Simon Laplace split the tide into families by speed in the 1770s. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, set up harmonic analysis of tides in 1867 and built the first tide-predicting machine in 1872–73. William Ferrel built an American machine in 1882, and the US Coast and Geodetic Survey’s Machine No. 2, with 37 constituents, predicted the tides of the world’s ports from 1912 to 1965. George Darwin named the constituents. Tide tables for the Pacific coast are published today by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and by the Canadian Hydrographic Service.

A proof that is not allowed

“The tide is periodic with period one lunar month, so its coefficients by 32a give it exactly.” Not allowed: no whole number of solar half-days fits a lunar month exactly, and the Moon’s orbit has its own repeats of 27.55 and 18.6 years. The tide is a sum of turnings with unrelated speeds, and the fit is by shadows over a long record, not by 32a’s repeat.
