14i · technique

When top and bottom both vanish, and growth races

10f found limits of fractions whose top and bottom both head to 0 by factoring, or by multiplying by a partner. It asked whether such a limit needs a rule of its own. Take an input where both top and bottom are 0. Near it, 13k’s linearizations say each is about its slope times the step away. So the fraction should be about the ratio of the two slopes. The “about” needs a technique. 14c's average-slope, run on two functions at once, gives the fraction exactly as a ratio of slopes, at one input in between, with nothing left over. Then the limit of the fraction is the limit of the ratio of slopes, when that exists. The same idea compares how fast two functions grow far out.

Run level-ends on a mix of the two functions built to have equal ends. It gives an input where the ratio of the two changes equals the ratio of the two slopes. When both functions start at 0, the fraction is a ratio of slopes at some input in between, and that input is dragged along as the step shrinks.

14i1Two functions at once

Stands on 14c (average-slope) and 14b (level-ends).

Two-function average-slope technique: let f and g be continuous on [α, β] with slopes at every input of (α, β), and let g′ be 0 at no input of (α, β). Then g(β) ≠ g(α), and for some c with α < c < β,

(f(β) − f(α))/(g(β) − g(α)) = f′(c)/g′(c).

g(\beta) \ne g(\alpha) @@ level-ends (14b) refuted: equal ends would give g′ = 0 inside
h(x) = f(x)\,(g(\beta) - g(\alpha)) - g(x)\,(f(\beta) - f(\alpha)) @@ the helper
h(\alpha) = f(\alpha)g(\beta) - g(\alpha)f(\beta) = h(\beta) @@ multiply out both; the cross terms cancel
\text{call } c:\ h'(c) = f'(c)(g(\beta) - g(\alpha)) - g'(c)(f(\beta) - f(\alpha)) = 0 @@ level-ends: h is continuous with a slope inside (slopes-add, constant-factor for slopes)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{f(\beta) - f(\alpha)}{g(\beta) - g(\alpha)} = \tfrac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by g′(c)(g(β) − g(α)), neither 0

Its accepted name is the extended mean value theorem, from lectures of 1823. With g(x) = x it is average-slope. Read as a motion, let f and g be the two positions of a point moving in the plane. Then some instant has the ratio of the two velocities equal to the ratio of the two total changes.

14i2The rule

Stands on 14i1, 10f (limits of fractions) and 10e (two-sides-agree).

From here on, a limit of f(x)/g(x) as x → α, where f(x) → 0 and g(x) → 0, is said to have the form 0/0. Its accepted name is an indeterminate form: the form alone does not decide the limit. Here 0/0 is a name, of a kind of limit, not a division, which would name nothing.

Slope-ratio technique: let f and g have slopes at every input of a punctured neighbourhood of α, with g′ 0 at none of them. Let f(x) → 0 and g(x) → 0 as x → α. If f′(x)/g′(x) → L as x → α, then f(x)/g(x) → L. Its accepted name is L'Hôpital's rule.

F(\alpha) = 0,\ F = f \text{ elsewhere};\ \ G \text{ likewise} @@ fill the hole: F and G are continuous at α (10b's limit is the value)
\text{given } \varepsilon \gt 0,\ \text{call } \delta:\ 0 \lt |t - \alpha| \lt \delta \Rightarrow \left|\tfrac{f'(t)}{g'(t)} - L\right| \lt \varepsilon @@ the hypothesis, read by 10a's line
\alpha \lt x \lt \alpha + \delta:\ \ \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \tfrac{F(x) - F(\alpha)}{G(x) - G(\alpha)} = \tfrac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} @@ two-function average-slope on [α, x], with α < c < x
0 \lt c - \alpha \lt \delta,\ \ \text{so } \left|\tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} - L\right| \lt \varepsilon @@ c is dragged along with x: the same δ works
\htmlClass{new}{\lim_{x \to \alpha^+} \tfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = L} @@ the line of a one-sided limit (10e)

From the left the same lines run on [x, α], and two-sides-agree (10e) joins the two sides. ∎ The version far out follows by swap-to-one-over-x (10f). Put x = 1/t. By chain for slopes, f(1/t) has slope −f′(1/t)/t², and likewise g, so the ratio of the two slopes is f′(1/t)/g′(1/t). Then slope-ratio at 0 from the right gives: if f, g → 0 as x → ∞ and f′/g′ → L, then f/g → L.

\lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{x^3 - 8}{x - 2} = \lim \tfrac{3x^2}{1} = 12 @@ slope-ratio; 10f's factoring (x² + 2x + 4) agrees
\lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sqrt{1 + x} - 1}{x} = \lim \tfrac{1/(2\sqrt{1 + x})}{1} = \tfrac12 @@ slope-ratio; root-slope and chain for slopes for the top's slope
\lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sqrt{1 + x} - 1 - x/2}{x^2} @@ 0/0: 13k's linearization error over x²
= \lim \tfrac{\frac12(1 + x)^{-1/2} - \frac12}{2x} @@ slope-ratio: still 0/0
= \lim \tfrac{-\frac14(1 + x)^{-3/2}}{2} = \htmlClass{new}{-\tfrac18} @@ slope-ratio again: the error is about −x²/8

The third limit says more than 14g did. 14g proved that √(1 + x) lies below its tangent line 1 + x/2. This says by how much, near 0: about x²/8, which is half of |f″(0)| = ¼ times x². That half is not an accident, and rung 22 says why.

The history 10f told is settled in one line. The rule was found by one man and sent in letters under a contract, and printed by another in 1696 with thanks in the preface. Its proof, the one above, came more than a century later, after the two-function average-slope. A 0/0 limit does not need a rule of its own: it needs average-slope, twice over.

14i3Growth races

Stands on 14i2 and 10f (one-over-x-far-out).

From here on, for f and g with positive values far out, g outgrows f when f(x)/g(x) → 0 as x → ∞. Then f is said to be negligible next to g far out; its accepted mark is f = o(g), read “f is little-o of g”. The whole mark f = o(g) is a statement with two gaps; o(g) names nothing on its own.

Among powers the race is decided by the exponent. For fractions p < q, x^p/x^q = 1/x^(q − p) → 0 (10f's one-over-x-far-out, with fraction-power for slopes's powers), so x^q outgrows x^p. A polynomial is outgrown by every power above its degree. A polynomial of degree n and one of degree m tie when n = m, in the sense that their ratio heads to the ratio of leading coefficients (rational-functions-far-out). The rule earns its keep in races where algebra cannot rewrite the ratio. Those begin when functions that are not built from powers join the field.

\lim_{x \to \infty} x\left(\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x\right) @@ a product of something growing and something shrinking
= \lim_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{\sqrt{x^2 + 1} - x}{1/x} @@ 0/0 far out: √(x² + 1) − x = 1/(√(x² + 1) + x) → 0
= \lim_{t \to 0^+} \tfrac{\sqrt{1 + t^2} - 1}{t^2} @@ swap-to-one-over-x: x = 1/t, and √(1/t² + 1) = √(1 + t²)/t for t > 0
= \lim \tfrac{t/\sqrt{1 + t^2}}{2t} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12} @@ slope-ratio; chain for slopes for the top

14i5Running it backwards

Stands on 14i1 to 14i3.

Handed a limit of a fraction, check its form first: if it is not 0/0, 10f's techniques decide it. If it is, take slopes of the top and bottom separately, and try the limit of their ratio. If that is 0/0 again, repeat, checking the form each time. Far out, swap x for 1/t. Handed a race, write the ratio and find its limit. Handed a claimed value of a 0/0 limit, it may hide a linearization: the first slopes of top and bottom often say it at once. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The two-function average-slope technique is Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s, from his lectures of 1823; its accepted name is Cauchy’s mean value theorem. The slope-ratio rule is Johann Bernoulli’s, sent to Guillaume de l’Hôpital in letters under a contract and printed in l’Hôpital’s Analyse des infiniment petits (1696), with thanks to the Bernoullis in the preface. It is known as L’Hôpital’s rule. In the closer, the textbook of 1821 is Cauchy’s Cours d’analyse; the 1830s argument is Guglielmo Libri’s, and the proof is August Möbius’s.
