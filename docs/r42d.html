42d · proof

Powers cycle

Rung five walked the powers of three round the clock of seven, and the walk came back to one after six steps. Rung nineteen proved, for any finite group, that a member done over and over comes back to doing nothing after a number of steps that divides the size of the group. The units of a clock are a finite group. So the walk always comes back, and the size of the group says when it must have. On a prime clock the size is one less than the prime, and every hour but zero, raised to that power, is one. On any clock the size is the count of units, and the rung has the tools to count them: count them on a clock whose size is a power of a prime, then put the clocks together. With that count, a power with an exponent too large to write can be cut down to one that fits on a page. The step ends with the old puzzle of why one seventh repeats in blocks of six digits.

On a prime clock, any hour but zero to the power one less than the prime is one, by the dividing of rung nineteen. On any clock, a unit to the power of the count of units is one, and the count is found prime by prime. So exponents may be cut on the clock of the count, and the block length of a repeating decimal is a power’s return time.

42d1The little technique

5·cb2 walked [3] round the clock of 7: [3], [2], [6], [4], [5], [1]. Six steps, and the clock has six units. 19d5 said that a member of a finite group G comes back to e after a number of steps that divides |G|, so that a^|G| = e.

Little technique: if p is prime and p does not divide a, then a^(p − 1) ≡ 1 (mod p); and aᵖ ≡ a (mod p) for every integer a.

p \nmid a \Rightarrow [a] \in (\mathbb Z/p)^\times @@ gcd(a, p) = 1 (42b4)
|(\mathbb Z/p)^\times| = p - 1 @@ every hour but [0] is a unit (the prime-clock-field technique)
\htmlClass{new}{[a]^{p - 1} = [1]} @@ order-divides (19d5) in the group of units (42b3)
[a]^p = [a]^{p-1}[a] = [a] @@ times [a]
p \mid a \Rightarrow a^p \equiv 0 \equiv a @@ both sides are multiples of p
\htmlClass{new}{a^p \equiv a \pmod p \text{ for every } a} \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases

So 2¹⁰⁰ on the clock of 101 is 1, though 2¹⁰⁰ has 31 digits. The technique is a fact about the size of a group, and it says nothing about which smaller power comes back first: [2]³ = [1] on the clock of 7, and 3 divides 6.

42d2Counting the units

The units of the clock of n are the hours [k] with 1 ≤ k ≤ n and gcd(k, n) = 1. On the clock of a prime power pᵏ, an hour fails exactly when p divides it, and the multiples of p among 1, …, pᵏ number pᵏ⁻¹.

Unit-count technique: if n is the product of the prime powers p₁^(k₁), …, pᵣ^(kᵣ), then the clock of n has n(1 − 1/p₁)⋯(1 − 1/pᵣ) units.

\gcd(j, p^k) = 1 \iff p \nmid j @@ the common divisors of j and pᵏ are powers of p (4j17’s one prime list)
|(\mathbb Z/p^k)^\times| = p^k - p^{k-1} = p^k(1 - 1/p) @@ take away the pᵏ⁻¹ multiples of p
n = p_1^{k_1} \cdots p_r^{k_r},\ \text{the prime powers sharing nothing} @@ the prime list of n (4j17)
|(\mathbb Z/n)^\times| = \prod_i |(\mathbb Z/p_i^{k_i})^\times| @@ the two-clock technique (42c3), one prime power at a time
\htmlClass{new}{|(\mathbb Z/n)^\times| = n\,(1 - \tfrac{1}{p_1}) \cdots (1 - \tfrac{1}{p_r})} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply the lines

360 = 2³ · 3² · 5, so the clock of 360 has 4 · 6 · 4 = 96 units, and 360 · ½ · ⅔ · ⅘ = 96. The clock of 100 has 40. The clock of a product of two primes pq has pq − q − p + 1 = (p − 1)(q − 1): of the hours 0 to pq − 1, the q multiples of p and the p multiples of q fail, and 0 is counted in both. 42i builds a lock on that count.

42d3Cutting the exponent

42a5 warned that an exponent is a count, and does not carry to the clock of n. It does carry to another clock: the clock of the count of units.

Exponent-clock technique: if gcd(a, n) = 1 and c is the count of units of the clock of n, then a^c ≡ 1 (mod n), and a^k ≡ a^j (mod n) whenever k ≡ j (mod c).

[a]^c = [1] @@ order-divides (19d5) in the units (42b3), of size c
k = j + ct,\ \ k, j \ge 0 @@ ﹀ ≡ on the clock of c, taking k the larger
[a]^k = [a]^j([a]^c)^t = [a]^j @@ powers add their counts and power-of-a-power (4g), carried to the clock
7^{222} \text{ on the clock of } 10:\ \ c = 4,\ \ 222 = 4 \cdot 55 + 2 @@ the unit-count technique: 10 · ½ · ⅘ = 4
\htmlClass{new}{7^{222} \equiv 7^2 = 49 \equiv 9 \pmod{10}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last digit of 7²²² is 9

So the power is cut twice: the base on the clock of n, the exponent on the clock of c. 42·cb7 runs this down a tower of exponents, clock inside clock, and reads the last digits of a number too big to write.

42d4Why one seventh repeats in sixes

8·cb5 proved that a fraction’s decimal repeats, since there are only so many remainders. It did not say how long the block is. 1/7 = 0.142857 142857 …, a block of six; 1/41 = 0.02439 02439 …, a block of five.

Decimal-block technique: if q shares nothing with 10, the decimal of 1/q repeats from the first place in a block whose length is the least k with 10ᵏ ≡ 1 (mod q), and that length divides the count of units of the clock of q.

10^k \equiv 1 \pmod q \iff 10^k - 1 = qM @@ ﹀ ≡
\iff \tfrac1q = \tfrac{M}{10^k - 1} = 0.\overline{M} \text{, } M \text{ written with } k \text{ digits} @@ 8·cb5’s repeating-decimal technique, run backwards
\text{a block of length } k \text{ from the first place} \iff 10^k \equiv 1 @@ the same lines, read both ways
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the least such } k \text{ is the order of } [10] \text{, which divides } |(\mathbb Z/q)^\times|} \quad \blacksquare @@ order-divides (19d5)

On the clock of 7 the order of [10] = [3] is 6, the most it could be. On the clock of 41, 10⁵ − 1 = 99 999 = 41 · 2439, so the block is 02439, of length 5, which divides 40. On the clock of 17 the order is 16, and 1/17 has a block of sixteen digits. A prime q for which the block is q − 1 long is one where [10] walks through every unit. Whether there are endlessly many such primes is open.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The little technique is Fermat’s little theorem. The count of units is Euler’s totient function, written φ(n), and the exponent-clock technique is Euler’s theorem, a^φ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). The least k with aᵏ ≡ 1 is the multiplicative order of a modulo n. A composite n with 2ⁿ⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod n) is a Fermat pseudoprime to base 2, and 341 is the least; 561 is the least Carmichael number (42j). Primes for which 10 has order q − 1 are full reptend primes, and that there are endlessly many is a case of Artin’s conjecture (1927). The numbers 2^(2^k) + 1 are the Fermat numbers.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat stated the little theorem in a letter to Frénicle de Bessy in 1640, without proof. Leonhard Euler proved it in 1736 by the binomial road of 42d.6.1, and defined φ and proved the exponent-clock technique in 1763. Fermat guessed that all Fermat numbers are prime; Euler found 641 · 6 700 417 = 2³² + 1 in 1732. Gauss connected the length of decimal blocks to orders in the Disquisitiones (1801).

A proof that is not allowed

“[a]^(p − 1) = [1] for every unit, so p − 1 is the order of every unit.” Not allowed: order-divides says the order divides p − 1. On the clock of 7, [2] has order 3 and [6] order 2. 42e asks which units have the largest order.
