42d · proof

Unique factoring

Rung four proved that a whole number splits into primes in only one way. The proof leaned on a single fact: a prime that divides a product divides a factor, and that came from the oldest recipe for a greatest common divisor, which is repeated division with remainder. Step 42c gave polynomials over a field the same division. So the same chain of reasons should run there, and it does, once each link is named. Division gives every ideal one maker. One maker turns a member that cannot be split into a member that behaves like a prime. And behaving like a prime gives one factoring. With the chain laid out, polynomials with whole-number coefficients can be brought in too, though they have no division by two: the trick is to look at their coefficients on a prime clock. That gives a test that some polynomials cannot be split at all, and a reason why splitting over the fractions never needs fractions.

In a ring where every ideal has one maker, a member that cannot be split divides a factor of any product it divides, and every member splits into such pieces in one way only, up to order and undoable factors. For whole-number polynomials, the common divisor of the coefficients of a product is the product of theirs. A prime that divides every coefficient but the top, with its square missing the constant, shows a polynomial cannot be split.

42d1Members that cannot be split

A cancelling ring in which every ideal is the multiples of one member is a principal ring. ℤ is one, by 4j’s division-with-remainder technique, and so is F[x] over any field, by 42c’s one-maker technique.

A member q, not 0 and not undoable, is unsplittable when q = wb only if w or b is undoable. It is prime when q dividing wb means q divides w or q divides b. Two members that differ by an undoable factor, as 3 and −3, or x − 1 and 2x − 2 in ℚ[x], are partners.

Unsplittable-is-prime technique: in a principal ring, every unsplittable member is prime.

(q) \subseteq J = (g) \Rightarrow q = gh @@ any ideal above (q) has one maker g
g \text{ undoable, so } J = R;\ \text{ or } h \text{ undoable, so } J = (q) @@ q is unsplittable
(q) \text{ is largest, so } R/(q) \text{ is a field} @@ the largest-ideal technique (42b)
q \mid wb \Rightarrow (w + (q))(b + (q)) = 0 @@ wb lands in (q)
\htmlClass{new}{q \mid w \ \lor\ q \mid b} \quad \blacksquare @@ a field has no zero products

This is 4j’s prime-divides-a-factor technique, with the plus-only combination replaced by the one-maker technique. The two words come apart in rings that are not principal: in 42e, 2 is unsplittable and not prime.

42d2One factoring

One-factoring technique: in a principal ring, every member other than 0 that is not undoable is a product of unsplittables, and any two such products have the same length and can be paired off into partners.

\text{suppose } w_0 \text{ has no factoring; then } w_0 = w_1 b_1,\ w_1 \text{ with no factoring, } b_1 \text{ not undoable} @@ w₀ is not unsplittable, and one factor must lack a factoring
(w_0) \subsetneq (w_1) \subsetneq (w_2) \subsetneq \cdots @@ the same move again and again (6c’s picking in turn)
U = \textstyle\bigcup_k (w_k) = (g),\ \ g \in (w_k) \text{ for some } k @@ a union of a rising chain of ideals is an ideal; one maker
(w_{k+1}) \subseteq (g) \subseteq (w_k) @@ so the chain stops: ⊥, and every member has a factoring
q_1 \cdots q_s = q'_1 \cdots q'_t \Rightarrow q_1 \mid q'_j \text{ for some } j @@ the unsplittable-is-prime technique, on a list (4j’s prime-divides-a-list)
\htmlClass{new}{q'_j = u\,q_1,\ u \text{ undoable; cancel and repeat}} \quad \blacksquare @@ q′ⱼ is unsplittable; cancelling (42a3); induction on s

So in ℚ[x], x⁴ − 4 = (x² − 2)(x² + 2), and no other factoring is possible except partners such as (2x² − 4)(½x² + 1). In ℝ[x] the same polynomial is (x − √2)(x + √2)(x² + 2): one factoring there too, but the unsplittables are different, because the field is.

42d3The divisor of the coefficients

ℤ[x] is not principal (42c4), but it is close. The content of a polynomial with whole coefficients, not 0, is the greatest common divisor of its coefficients. A polynomial with content 1 is bare. 6x² + 24x + 24 has content 6, and x² + 4x + 4 is bare.

Content-multiplies technique: the content of a product of whole-number polynomials is the product of their contents.

p = c(p)\,\bar p,\ \ q = c(q)\,\bar q,\ \ \bar p, \bar q \text{ bare} @@ take out the contents
\text{suppose a prime } \ell \text{ divides every coefficient of } \bar p \bar q @@ to show the product is bare
\bar p \bmod \ell \ne 0,\ \ \bar q \bmod \ell \ne 0 \text{ in } (\mathbb Z/\ell)[x] @@ neither is bare-and-all-multiples-of-ℓ
(\bar p \bmod \ell)(\bar q \bmod \ell) = 0 @@ the clock map ℤ → ℤ/ℓ keeps sums and products, coefficient by coefficient
\bot @@ ℤ/ℓ is a field (42b4), so (ℤ/ℓ)[x] is cancelling (degree-adds)
\htmlClass{new}{c(pq) = c(p)\,c(q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ p̄q̄ is bare

One check: (2x + 4)(3x + 6) = 6x² + 24x + 24, with content 2 · 3 = 6. The use: a whole-number polynomial that splits over ℚ splits over ℤ into factors of the same degrees. Clear the bottoms of each fraction factor, take out contents, and the content-multiplies technique says the leftover number divides out.

42d4A test that a polynomial cannot be split

Prime-wedge technique: let f = aₙxⁿ + ⋯ + a₀ have whole coefficients and n ≥ 1. Let ℓ be a prime. Suppose ℓ divides a₀, …, aₙ₋₁, ℓ does not divide aₙ, and ℓ² does not divide a₀. Then f cannot be split over ℚ into two factors of smaller degree.

f = gh,\ \ g, h \in \mathbb Z[x],\ \ \deg g = k,\ \deg h = n - k,\ \ 0 < k < n @@ suppose it splits; over ℤ, by the content-multiplies technique
f \bmod \ell = a_n x^n,\ \ a_n \not\equiv 0 @@ every lower coefficient is a multiple of ℓ
(g \bmod \ell)(h \bmod \ell) = a_n x^n \Rightarrow g \bmod \ell = b\,x^k,\ h \bmod \ell = c\,x^{n-k} @@ one factoring in (ℤ/ℓ)[x] (one-factoring): the only unsplittable factor is x
\ell \mid g_0 \text{ and } \ell \mid h_0 @@ the constant terms are 0 on the clock, since k > 0 and n − k > 0
\ell^2 \mid g_0 h_0 = a_0 @@ the constant of a product
\htmlClass{new}{\bot:\ f \text{ cannot be split}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ℓ² does not divide a₀

x³ − 2 with ℓ = 2: 2 divides 0, 0 and −2, not the top 1, and 4 does not divide −2. So x³ − 2 cannot be split over ℚ, and ∛2 is a zero of no polynomial over ℚ of degree 1 or 2. 42f needs that. The test only ever says no: x² + 1 passes no prime and still cannot be split over ℚ.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A principal ring is a principal ideal domain. Unsplittable members are irreducible elements; prime members are prime elements; partners are associates. A ring where every member has one factoring is a unique factorisation domain, and the one-factoring technique says every principal ideal domain is one; the chain that must stop is the ascending chain condition. The content-multiplies technique is Gauss’s lemma, and bare polynomials are primitive. The prime-wedge technique is Eisenstein’s criterion.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the lemma on contents in 1801. Theodor Schönemann published the test in 1846 and Gotthold Eisenstein in 1850; it carries Eisenstein’s name. The chain condition is from Emmy Noether’s paper on ideals of 1921, and rings with it are named for her.

A proof that is not allowed

“x⁴ + 4 has no real zero, so it cannot be split over ℚ.” Not allowed: having no zero rules out straight factors only. x⁴ + 4 = (x² + 2x + 2)(x² − 2x + 2), two quadratics with whole coefficients and no real zeros.
