2d · proof

All the subsets

The last tool. From a set, the set of all its parts: every set you could make by keeping some of its members and dropping the rest, gathered into a set of their own. It is the tool whose result you cannot write down by tipping things out or picking things through. You have to run through every way of deciding, member by member, keep or drop. The step gives the tool its mark, and shows the keep-or-drop way of listing its result. Then it proves the techniques the rest of the ladder uses daily. The empty set is part of every set, and every set is part of itself, so both are always on the list. The tool respects inclusion. And it treats common members exactly, and merged members only by an inclusion.

To list every subset, go through the members and decide each: keep or drop. Every way of deciding is a subset; every subset is a way of deciding. The empty set is the way that drops everything, the set itself the way that keeps everything.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1b (when nothing is true, everything is permitted), 2a (⊆, the techniques self-part, chain for part of, double-inclusion), 2b (∅, the empty-set technique) and 2c (gather, ∪, ∩). 2e sifts products out of a power set of a power set; rung 3 and rung 4 build with it; the question of how fast the list grows is rung 4's.

2d1All the subsets, as one set

Stands on 2a9 (⊆) and 2b5 (axioms that say a set exists).

2a said what it is for s to be a subset of u: every member of s is a member of u. Now gather all of them. A set may have subsets in great variety, and each of them is a thing, so the set of all of them is a set you should be able to ask for. By the power axiom, there is one:

Its accepted name is the power set of u. The word “power” here is only a name, and means nothing beyond this line until a later rung gives it work of its own. Read 𝒫(u) as “the subsets of u” or “all the parts of u”. To use a membership in 𝒫(u), unpack it into ⊆ and then into the every-around-an-if. To make one, prove the inclusion and pack. There is nothing else to the mark. It is a name: 𝒫(u) names one set, built from u.

2d2Curly capitals, and collections

Stands on 2d1 (𝒫) and 2a5 (sets of sets).

A set whose members are themselves sets is called a collection. The mark 𝒫 is a capital P with a flourish, said “curly P”. It is the first of a family. The curly capitals, 𝒜, ℬ, 𝒞 and the rest, are said “curly A”, “curly B” and so on, as ∀ and ∃ were named for their shapes. They all behave the same way. A curly capital names a set whose members are themselves sets.

A curly letter is a name like any other letter. The curl only warns the reader what kind of thing is inside: sets, not cats. 𝒫(u) fits the family, since each of its members is a part of u. The difference is that 𝒫 is a mark that builds a collection from a set, while 𝒜 or ℬ simply names a collection you already have.

2d3Listing it: keep or drop

Stands on 2d1 (𝒫) and 2a7 (the same-members axiom).

How do you write down 𝒫({cat, dog})? Think of a subset as a way of deciding, for each member, whether it is in. Go through the members in turn. Each decision so far gives a set. The next member comes back to every set you have made, kept and dropped.

Start with no members decided. There is one way to decide nothing: the empty set. Now decide cat. Drop it, and you have ∅. Keep it, and you have {cat}.

Now decide dog. Each set so far comes back twice: once without dog, and once with it. That gives ∅, {cat}, {dog} and {cat, dog}. Those are all the ways of keeping and dropping, and nothing else. So 𝒫({cat, dog}) = {∅, {cat}, {dog}, {cat, dog}}.

Why is that the whole list? Because a subset of {cat, dog} is fixed by the same-members axiom once you know, of cat and of dog, whether it is in. And those are exactly the decisions made. And why is every set on the list a subset? Because each was made by keeping only members of {cat, dog}.

Spelling the “exactly” out for a particular listed set is a case split on “cat ∈ s or cat ∉ s”. 1g's licence column allows that split for a set whose members are listed, since membership in it is settled.

2d5The empty set is part of every set

Stands on 2b12 (the empty-set technique), 1b (everything permitted) and 1h4 (an every with nothing to check).

Whatever u is, 𝒫(u) has ∅ as a member and u as a member. That u is a member is 2a's self-part technique: u ⊆ u, so u ∈ 𝒫(u) by packing. That ∅ is a member needs a technique of its own:

Empty-part technique: the empty set is part of every set.

Its proof is the standard use of the tech “when nothing is true, everything is permitted”. Everything in ∅ is in b, because nothing is in ∅ that could fail to be: the if's hypothesis is never met. 2b held the empty-part technique in trust, and this step proves it, so that flag is closed. Proof. Let b be any set.

Let x be anything. Suppose x is in ∅, until we drop it. By the empty-set technique, x is not in ∅. That clashes with the supposition. When nothing is true, everything is permitted: so x is in b.

Drop the supposition: if x is in ∅, then x is in b. The line “x is in b” went with the supposition; the if is what leaves. Nothing was assumed about x, so this is true for everything. Pack ⊆: ∅ ⊆ b.

1h had the word for this: the every is true vacuously. There is no member of ∅ to check, so the check passes. People feel cheated by it when they meet it, and lean on it ever after. The whole keep-or-drop listing begins from the empty set, since the empty set is the way of dropping everything.

2d6Always on the list

Stands on 2d5 (the empty-part technique) and 2a9 (the self-part technique).

By the empty-part technique, ∅ ⊆ u. Pack 𝒫: ∅ is in 𝒫(u). By the self-part technique, u ⊆ u. Pack 𝒫 again: u is in 𝒫(u).

So 𝒫(u) is never empty, not even for u = ∅. When u has members, ∅ and u are among the members of 𝒫(u), and they differ: u has a member and ∅ does not, so a member of u is not a member of ∅, and they are not the same set.

A founder of the subject asked a further question. Can the parts of a set all be matched, one each, with its members? Matching is not yet on the ladder; 4j builds it. The step's last challenge takes up what this step can say about it.

2d7How the list grows, said without counting

Stands on 2d3 (keep or drop).

Look again at keep-or-drop. When the set gains another member, every subset you already had comes back as itself and as itself with the newcomer added. Nothing is lost, and every old subset comes back in a version without and a version with. Add a further member and the same happens again to the whole new list. That is why the lists 𝒫 makes grow so quickly. It is why 𝒫(𝒫(u)) is already too long to list by hand even for u = {cat, dog}: it has sixteen members, each a set of sets. How fast, as a matter of counting, is a question for rung 4. Here the fact is structural: each member of u is an independent decision, and the subsets are the ways the decisions can come out.

Said here, not proved; the slides and the picture chase it. In marks, take a set u and a thing c not in u. The subsets of u ∪ {c} split into two kinds. Those that leave c out are exactly the subsets of u. Those that take c in are each a subset of u with c added. The split itself, “c ∈ s or c ∉ s” for an arbitrary s, is 1g's either-way, licensed when membership is settled. For the listed sets of this rung, it is.

2d8The tool respects inclusion

Stands on 2d1 (𝒫) and 2a10 (subsets chain).

Power-keeps-parts technique: if u is part of b, every part of u is a part of b. In marks, 𝒫(u) ⊆ 𝒫(b). The argument is 2a’s chain technique, inside the shape of ⊆.

Let u and b be any sets, and suppose u ⊆ b. Let s be anything. Suppose s is in 𝒫(u), until we drop it. Unpack 𝒫: s ⊆ u. Chain that with u ⊆ b: s ⊆ b. Pack 𝒫: s is in 𝒫(b).

Drop the supposition: if s is in 𝒫(u), then s is in 𝒫(b). Nothing was assumed about s, so this is true for every s. Pack ⊆: 𝒫(u) ⊆ 𝒫(b).

The placeholder is called s because its values are sets; any letter would do. Run backwards, the power-keeps-parts technique also works: from 𝒫(u) ⊆ 𝒫(b), you may write u ⊆ b, because u ∈ 𝒫(u), so u ∈ 𝒫(b), so u ⊆ b. So u ⊆ b and 𝒫(u) ⊆ 𝒫(b) are interchangeable by techs. That is the kind of technique that lets you move a question about sets up to a question about their power sets, and back.

2d10Merged parts, only by an inclusion

Stands on 2d9 (common parts) and 1e (the counterexample technique).

A part of u is a part of u ∪ b (the power-keeps-parts technique with u ⊆ u ∪ b). That u ⊆ u ∪ b takes two lines. Take x in u. Then x is in u or in b, by adding an option, so x ∈ u ∪ b.

The claim 𝒫(u ∪ b) = 𝒫(u) ∪ 𝒫(b) fails the other way, and here is the witness. Take u = {cat}, b = {dog}. Then {cat, dog} ⊆ u ∪ b, so it is in 𝒫(u ∪ b). But it is a part of neither u nor b, so it is in neither 𝒫(u) nor 𝒫(b), and so not in their union. The counterexample technique, and the sameness is dead. This failure is not a licence question like 2c's not-through; the sameness is simply not so, and a set on its own shows it.

2d13Running it backwards

Stands on 2d1 to 2d10.

Wanting s ∈ 𝒫(u), you want s ⊆ u, so you open “let x be anything; suppose x ∈ s” and aim at x ∈ u. Wanting 𝒫(u) ⊆ 𝒫(b), you open “let s be anything; suppose s ∈ 𝒫(u)”, unpack, and need s ⊆ b. So you need u ⊆ b to chain with. Wanting the full list of 𝒫(u) for a listed u, run keep-or-drop. Then check by the same-members axiom that no way of deciding was missed and none written again. Wanting to refute a sameness between power sets, hunt for a set that is a part of one side's set and not of the other's. A power set's membership is an inclusion, so a witness is a set with a stray member. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The founder who showed that the parts of a set outrun its members is Georg Cantor; the fact is Cantor’s theorem.

Cantor showed in 1891 that the parts of a set can never all be matched, one each, with its members: whatever matching you try, some part is left out. He took this as a sign that there is no largest set.

Cantor, “Über eine elementare Frage der Mannigfaltigkeitslehre” (1891); paraphrase
