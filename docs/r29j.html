29j · technique

Flows, and the Order of Two Motions

A field of arrows says, at each point, which way to move. Follow it and every point flows, as 24’s rate statements promised: one path through each start. Two fields give two flows, and the order of following them can matter. A car can move forward and can turn its wheels, but by doing the two in a short loop it moves sideways, which neither can do alone. This step builds the flow of a field, measures how two flows fail to swap by a third field, their bracket, and parks a car with it.

The flow of V moves each point along the solution of x′ = V(x). Flow A for t, B for t, back A for t, back B for t: the point misses its start by t²[A, B] plus smaller terms, where [A, B] = (A·∇)B − (B·∇)A. If [A, B] points in a new direction, the loop reaches it.

29j1Following a Field

Flow technique: if V has continuous slopes, each point p has exactly one path through it with velocity V at every moment, for a while; following these paths for time t is the flow of V, and following for t then s is following for t + s.

29j2Two Flows in a Loop

(0,0) \xrightarrow{A,\,t} (t, 0) \xrightarrow{B,\,t} (t, t^2) @@ B moves up at speed x = t
\xrightarrow{A,\,-t} (0, t^2) \xrightarrow{B,\,-t} (0, t^2) @@ at x = 0, B does not move
\text{the loop misses its start by } (0, t^2) @@ straight up, which neither A nor B is at the start
\htmlClass{new}{[A, B] = (A\cdot\nabla)B - (B\cdot\nabla)A = (0, 1) - (0, 0) = (0, 1)} @@ the miss is t² times this

Bracket technique: following A, B, back A, back B, each for a short time t, moves a point by t²[A, B] plus terms smaller than t², where [A, B] = (A·∇)B − (B·∇)A; the flows swap exactly when the bracket is 0. The general proof expands each flow to second order, as 28l’s hugging polynomials do.

29j3Parking a Car

\text{state: place } (x, y),\ \text{heading } \theta @@ three numbers
\text{drive: } D = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0);\ \ \text{steer: } S = (0, 0, 1) @@ two fields
[S, D] = (S\cdot\nabla)D - (D\cdot\nabla)S = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0) @@ slope of D in θ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a short loop of steer, drive, back, back moves the car sideways}} @@ a third direction from two

Two motions whose bracket points in a new direction reach that direction by short loops, which is how a car with no sideways wheels parks, and how a falling cat turns over with nothing to push on. Robots are steered with brackets of the motions they have.

Accepted names

The flow of a field is its flow or one-parameter group; the bracket is the Lie bracket of vector fields. Reaching new directions by brackets is the Chow-Rashevskii theorem of control theory; the car is a nonholonomic system.

Who said it first

Sophus Lie introduced the bracket in the 1870s. Wei-Liang Chow (1939) and Petr Rashevskii (1938) proved that brackets reach every direction they span.
