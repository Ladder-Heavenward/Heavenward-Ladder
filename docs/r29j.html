29j · technique

Hyperbolic Geometry by Hand

The disc plane looks small on the page, but to anyone living inside it the edge is endlessly far away. This step learns to measure there. First, the distance that rung twenty nine laid down between two points turns out to be the length of the shortest path between them, so it behaves like a distance should. Then the disc is opened out into a different drawing, the upper half of the plane above a floor, where the lines are upright rays and half circles standing on the floor, and distances up a ray are logs. Last, circles. A circle in this geometry is longer than a flat circle of the same radius, and it grows the way a population grows, not the way a fence grows. Triangles have areas too, and their area is how far their angles fall short of a straight angle.

The distance of the disc plane is the length of its shortest path. Opened out onto the upper half of the plane, its ruler is the flat step divided by the height. A circle there grows in length the way a population grows, far faster than a flat circle, and a triangle’s area is how far its angles fall short of a straight angle.

29j1The Distance Is the Shortest Path

29c2 laid down the distance d(p, q) = ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) with ρ = |fp(q)| = |q − p| / |1 − p̄q|, and 29d4 found the ruler ds = 2|dz| / (1 − |z|²) behind it. A distance earns its name when it is the length of the shortest way.

Disc-shortest technique: every path in the disc plane from p to q has ruler length at least d(p, q), and the piece of the line through p and q between them has length exactly d(p, q). So d(p, r) ≤ d(p, q) + d(q, r) for every three points.

f_p(p) = 0,\ \ |f_p(q)| = \rho @@ the disc-moves technique (29c1)
\text{then turn by } e^{-i\theta}:\ \ q \mapsto \rho \text{ on the floor} @@ a turn about 0 is a move of the disc plane (29c1)
\text{moves keep the ruler length of every path} @@ the disc-ruler technique (29d4)
\text{from } 0, \text{ the disc ruler is straight out: } du^2 + g^2\,dv^2 @@ u is the ruler distance from 0 along a diameter (29e5)
\text{any path from } u = 0 \text{ to } u = \ln\tfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho} \text{ has length at least that} @@ curves straight out are shortest (29g2): the length is at least ∫|u′|
\text{the diameter from } 0 \text{ to } \rho \text{ has exactly that length} @@ the disc-ruler technique (29d4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shortest} = d(p, q),\ \text{along the line through } p, q} \quad \blacksquare @@ undo the moves: the diameter goes back to that line (29c1, 29c2); p → q → r is one path from p to r
  
d(0, r) = k \iff r = \tfrac{e^k - 1}{e^k + 1} @@ solve ln((1 + r)/(1 − r)) = k for r (17)
k = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5:\ \ r \approx 0.462,\ 0.762,\ 0.905,\ 0.964,\ 0.987 @@ steps of one unit each, drawn ever shorter
d(0.5, -0.5):\ \ \rho = \tfrac{1}{1 + 0.25} = 0.8,\ \ \ln 9 = 2\ln 3 @@ the formula of 29c2, and two halves of ln 3 each: they agree
d(0.5, 0.5i):\ \ \rho \approx 0.6860,\ \ d \approx 1.681 @@ the flat straight segment between them has ruler length about 1.701
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the flat segment is longer than the line of the disc plane}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the disc-shortest technique: it bows toward the centre, where steps are cheaper

Near the centre the ruler is twice the flat one, so the disc plane looks almost flat there, scaled by 2. Near the edge each flat step costs more and more. A creature walking at a steady pace toward the edge sees it get no closer in any way it can measure.

29j2The Half-Plane

The disc is one drawing of this geometry. Another is often easier. Send the disc onto the upper half-plane, the points w = x + iy with y > 0, so that the edge of the disc becomes the floor y = 0.

Half-plane technique: the map w = i(1 + z)/(1 − z) carries the disc plane onto the upper half-plane and its ruler to ds = |dw| / y. Its lines become the upright half-lines and the half-circles with centres on the floor, and its distance becomes d(w₁, w₂) = ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)) with ρ = |w₁ − w₂| / |w₁ − w̄₂|.

w = \tfrac{iz + i}{-z + 1},\ \ ad - bc = i + i = 2i \ne 0 @@ the four-number technique (25f4); it is undone by z = (w − i)/(w + i)
y = \tfrac{1 - |z|^2}{|1 - z|^2} @@ multiply top and bottom by 1 − z̄; z − z̄ is 2i times the upright part of z; so |z| < 1 exactly when y > 0
\tfrac{dw}{dz} = \tfrac{2i}{(1 - z)^2},\ \ |dw| = \tfrac{2\,|dz|}{|1 - z|^2} @@ complex slopes of quotients (26d)
\tfrac{|dw|}{y} = \tfrac{2\,|dz|}{1 - |z|^2} @@ divide the last two lines: the disc ruler (29d4)
\text{edge} \to \text{floor};\ \text{circles meeting the edge square} \to \text{circles or lines meeting the floor square} @@ 25f4 sends circles and lines to circles and lines; near each point the map multiplies small vectors by its complex slope, a turn and stretch (25f1), so it keeps angles
\tfrac{|z_1 - z_2|}{|1 - \bar z_2 z_1|} = \tfrac{|w_1 - w_2|}{|w_1 - \bar w_2|} @@ put zₖ = (wₖ − i)/(wₖ + i): the top is 2i(w₁ − w₂)/((w₁ + i)(w₂ + i)), the bottom −2i(w₁ − w̄₂)/((w̄₂ − i)(w₁ + i)), and |w̄₂ − i| = |w₂ + i|
\htmlClass{new}{ds = \tfrac{|dw|}{y},\ \ d(w_1, w_2) = \ln\tfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho},\ \ \rho = \tfrac{|w_1 - w_2|}{|w_1 - \bar w_2|}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above, with 29c2
  
z = 0 \mapsto i,\ \ z = 0.5 \mapsto 3i @@ the centre of the disc goes to i
d(iy_1, iy_2) = \ln\tfrac{y_2}{y_1} \ \ (y_2 > y_1) @@ ρ = (y₂ − y₁)/(y₂ + y₁), so (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) = y₂/y₁
d(i, 3i) = \ln 3 = d(0, 0.5) @@ the same distance in both drawings
-1 + i \text{ to } 1 + i:\ \ \text{flat segment } \int_{-1}^{1} \tfrac{dx}{1} = 2 @@ along y = 1 the ruler is the flat step
\rho = \tfrac{2}{|-2 + 2i|} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \ d = \ln\tfrac{\sqrt2 + 1}{\sqrt2 - 1} = 2\ln(1 + \sqrt2) \approx 1.763 @@ the half-plane technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the half-circle of radius } \sqrt2 \text{ about } 0 \text{ beats the flat segment}} \quad \blacksquare @@ it climbs to where steps are cheaper

In the half-plane, height is everything. Doubling the height halves every length, so going up a ray from height 1 to height 2 costs ln 2, and so does going from 1000 to 2000. The maps w ↦ kw + b with k > 0 and b on the floor keep the ruler, since they scale |dw| and y alike. They are moves of the half-plane, and so is w ↦ −1/w, which comes from the turn of the disc by a half turn.

29j3Circles and Triangles

A circle of the disc plane, with centre c and radius ρ, is every point at distance ρ from c. Moving c to the centre of the disc keeps every distance, so it is enough to look at circles about 0.

Disc-circle technique: in the disc plane a circle of radius ρ has length π(e^ρ − e^(−ρ)) and encloses area π(e^(ρ/2) − e^(−ρ/2))².

d(0, z) = \rho \iff |z| = r,\ \ r = \tfrac{e^\rho - 1}{e^\rho + 1} @@ the distance from 0 depends on |z| alone (29c2); solve for r
1 - r^2 = \tfrac{4e^\rho}{(e^\rho + 1)^2} @@ multiply out
\text{length} = \int_0^{2\pi} \tfrac{2r}{1 - r^2}\,d\theta = \tfrac{4\pi r}{1 - r^2} = \pi(e^\rho - e^{-\rho}) @@ the ruler-length technique (29d1) along z = re^{iθ}, of flat speed r
\text{area} = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^r \tfrac{4s}{(1 - s^2)^2}\,ds\,d\theta = 2\pi\Big[\tfrac{2}{1 - s^2}\Big]_0^r = \tfrac{4\pi r^2}{1 - r^2} @@ the ruler-area technique (29d2) in turn and reach: E = 4/(1 − s²)², G = 4s²/(1 − s²)² (29d3)
\tfrac{4\pi r^2}{1 - r^2} = \tfrac{\pi(e^\rho - 1)^2}{e^\rho} @@ the second line
\htmlClass{new}{\text{length } \pi(e^\rho - e^{-\rho}),\ \ \text{area } \pi(e^{\rho/2} - e^{-\rho/2})^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ any other centre: move it to 0 (29c1, 29d4)
  
\rho = 1, 2, 5:\ \ \text{length} \approx 7.38,\ 22.8,\ 466 @@ the disc-circle technique
\text{flat: } 2\pi\rho \approx 6.28,\ 12.6,\ 31.4 @@ the round-quotient technique (21a)
\text{triangle } 0, 0.5, 0.5i:\ \ BC \text{ on the circle about } 1.25 + 1.25i,\ \text{radius}^2 = 2.125 @@ the circle through 0.5 and 0.5i that meets the edge square: |c|² = 1 + r² (29c2)
\text{angle at } 0 \text{ is } \tfrac{\pi}{2};\ \ \text{at } 0.5 \text{ and } 0.5i:\ \tan\beta = \tfrac35,\ \ \beta \approx 0.5404 @@ the angle between the floor and the line square to the radius from 1.25 + 1.25i
\text{area} = -\iint K\,dA = \pi - \tfrac{\pi}{2} - 2\beta \approx 0.4900 @@ the triangle-bend technique (29g4) with K = −1 (29e5)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the shortfall from } \pi \text{ is the area}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a numerical total of the ruler-area integral over the triangle gives 0.4900 too

A flat circle’s length grows like its radius. A disc-plane circle’s length grows like e^ρ, the way a population grows. Its area grows the same way, and nearly all of it lies within one unit of the rim. And no triangle can have area π or more, however large it is drawn: its angles would have to add to 0 or less.

The accepted names

Geometry in the disc plane is hyperbolic geometry, and the disc with its ruler is the Poincaré disc model. The upper half-plane with ds = |dw|/y is the Poincaré upper half-plane model; the map w = i(1 + z)/(1 − z) is a Cayley transform. The disc-shortest technique says the hyperbolic distance is a metric, with the triangle inequality. The distance is often written with the inverse hyperbolic tangent, d(0, r) = 2 artanh r, or in the half-plane as arcosh(1 + |w₁ − w₂|²/(2y₁y₂)). Written with sinh x = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ)/2, the disc-circle technique says a hyperbolic circle has circumference 2π sinh ρ and area 4π sinh²(ρ/2). The shortfall π − (α + β + γ) is the angle defect, and a triangle with all three corners on the edge is an ideal triangle, of area π.

Who said it first

Johann Heinrich Lambert noticed in 1766 that, if the sharp case of 29a held, a triangle’s area would be a fixed multiple of its defect. Nikolai Lobachevsky (1829) and János Bolyai (1832) worked out the formulas for circles and triangles of hyperbolic geometry, including the circumference that grows like e^ρ. Eugenio Beltrami gave models of it in 1868, among them the disc and the half-plane. Henri Poincaré found them again in 1882 from the maps of 25f, while studying differential equations, and his names stuck. M. C. Escher’s woodcuts Circle Limit I to IV (1958 to 1960) tile the disc plane with fish and angels of one size, as a creature inside would see them.
