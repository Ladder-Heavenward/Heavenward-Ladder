26·cb24 · newsflash for 24h

The Damped Oscillator

24h solved the rules on rates that have a second slope and constant numbers in front, by trying a growth function and asking which growth rates fit. When the rates that fit were real numbers, it found every solution. When they were not real, it stopped, and held that case in trust. That case is the one the world uses most. A spring with a brake on it, a car on its springs after a bump, a swing slowing down, a guitar string fading: each swings and dies at once. The growth rates that fit them are complex, and rung twenty-six has just carried growth into the complex numbers. This page finishes what 24h started. It finds every solution when the rates are complex, reads off how fast the swing shrinks and how its pace slows, and then reads a real spring’s brake off two of its swings.

Growth at a complex rate is growth and turning together: the real part of the rate sets how fast it grows or shrinks, and the imaginary part sets how fast it turns. So when the trial equation has complex roots, every solution is a swing inside a shrinking envelope. A spring with a light brake swings a little slower than without one, and each swing is the same share of the last. A heavier brake stops the swinging altogether, and past a certain brake it only creeps back.

26·cb24.1Growth at a Turning Rate

A function of a real x with complex values is two real functions: its real part and its imaginary part. Its slope is taken part by part. Take a complex rate r = α + iβ, with α and β real.

e^{rx} = e^{\alpha x}e^{i\beta x} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
= e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x + i\,e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x @@ the turn-growth technique (26a); together, 26b’s growth-and-turn technique
\text{slope} = \big(\alpha e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x - \beta e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x\big) + i\big(\alpha e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x + \beta e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x\big) @@ part by part: the product rule, exp its own slope (17f), the slopes of sin and cos (21g)
= (\alpha + i\beta)\,e^{\alpha x}(\cos\beta x + i\sin\beta x) @@ multiply out the right side, with i² = −1, and compare
\htmlClass{new}{\big(e^{rx}\big)' = r\,e^{rx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same rule as for a real rate

Complex-rate technique: for a complex rate r = α + iβ, e^(rx) is e^(αx)(cos βx + i sin βx), and its slope is r e^(rx), just as for a real rate. The real part α grows or shrinks the size, which is e^(αx). The imaginary part β turns it, β radians for each unit of x.

26·cb24.2The Roots Held in Trust

24h tried y = e^(rx) in y″ + by′ + cy = 0, with b and c real, and found the trial equation r² + br + c = 0. When b² < 4c the quadratic formula gives no real root. With 9·cb8’s i it gives two: r = α ± iβ, with α = −b/2 and β = √(c − b²/4). The complex try finds the solutions. A shift proves there are no others.

\big(e^{rx}\big)'' + b\big(e^{rx}\big)' + c\,e^{rx} = (r^2 + br + c)\,e^{rx} = 0 @@ the complex-rate technique, twice; r is a root
u + iv \text{ solves the rule, with } u = e^{\alpha x}\cos\beta x,\ v = e^{\alpha x}\sin\beta x @@ the parts of e^{rx} (26·cb24.1)
(u'' + bu' + cu) + i\,(v'' + bv' + cv) = 0 @@ b and c are real, so each bracket is real
\htmlClass{new}{u \text{ and } v \text{ each solve the rule}} @@ a complex number is 0 only when both its parts are 0
  
y = e^{\alpha x}w \Rightarrow y'' + by' + cy = e^{\alpha x}\big(w'' + (2\alpha + b)\,w' + (\alpha^2 + b\alpha + c)\,w\big) @@ the product rule, twice
= e^{\alpha x}\big(w'' + \beta^2 w\big) @@ α = −b/2: 2α + b = 0, and α² + bα + c = c − b²/4 = β²
y \text{ solves the rule} \iff w'' = -\beta^2 w @@ e^{αx} is never 0
w = C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x,\ \text{one pair } C_1, C_2 \text{ for each start} @@ the steady-swing technique (24h2)
\htmlClass{new}{y = e^{\alpha x}\big(C_1\cos\beta x + C_2\sin\beta x\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ shift back

Decaying-turn technique: if b² < 4c, every solution of y″ + by′ + cy = 0 is e^(αx)(C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx), with α = −b/2 and β = √(c − b²/4); and a height and a slope at one input pick C₁ and C₂ exactly. That is what 24h held in trust.

y'' + 2y' + 5y = 0:\ \ r^2 + 2r + 5 = 0,\ \ r = -1 \pm 2j @@ the trial equation; the quadratic formula with i
y = e^{-x}\big(C_1\cos 2x + C_2\sin 2x\big) @@ the decaying-turn technique
y(0) = 1,\ \ y'(0) = 0:\ \ C_1 = 1,\ \ -C_1 + 2C_2 = 0 @@ the height and the slope at 0
\htmlClass{new}{y = e^{-x}\big(\cos 2x + \tfrac12\sin 2x\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 24h found e^{−x} cos 2x fits; now every solution is known

26·cb24.3A Spring With a Brake

A mass m hangs on a spring of stiffness κ, and an oil-filled damper pushes back on it. A measured law says that a damper of this kind pushes back with a force γ times the speed, against the motion. Mass times acceleration is the sum of the forces: my″ = −κy − γy′. Divide by m and write ω² = κ/m and 2b = γ/m.

y″ + 2by′ + ω²y = 0, and the trial equation r² + 2br + ω² = 0 has r = −b ± √(b² − ω²).

From here on, b is the damping, and a spring with b > 0 is damped.

b < \omega:\ \ y = e^{-bt}\big(C_1\cos\omega_d t + C_2\sin\omega_d t\big),\ \ \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega^2 - b^2} @@ the decaying-turn technique: it swings, and shrinks
b = \omega:\ \ y = (C_1 + C_2t)\,e^{-bt} @@ 24h3, one repeated root: the edge of swinging
b > \omega:\ \ y = C_1e^{r_1t} + C_2e^{r_2t},\ \ r_1, r_2 < 0 @@ 24h3, two real roots: 24h’s heavy door closer
E = \tfrac12\big(y'^2 + \omega^2y^2\big),\ \ E' = y'\big(y'' + \omega^2y\big) = -2b\,y'^2 \le 0 @@ the chain rule; the rule. 24h’s flat energy now leaks
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one period } \tfrac{2\pi}{\omega_d} \text{ multiplies the swing by } e^{-2\pi b/\omega_d}} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^{−b(t + 2π/ω_d)} = e^{−bt}e^{−2πb/ω_d}, and the bracket repeats

Light-brake technique: a spring with a light brake, b < ω, swings at the slower pace ω_d = √(ω² − b²), and every swing is e^(−2πb/ω_d) of the one before.

m = 400 \text{ kg},\ \ \kappa = 20\,000 \text{ N/m},\ \ \gamma = 1414 \text{ N s/m} @@ one corner of the truck: its share of the mass, its spring, its damper
\omega = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07,\ \ b = \tfrac{1414}{800} \approx 1.77 @@ per second; ω/2π ≈ 1.13 bounces a second without the damper
\omega_d = \sqrt{50 - 3.13} \approx 6.85,\ \ \tfrac{2\pi}{\omega_d} \approx 0.92 \text{ s} @@ the light-brake technique
\htmlClass{new}{e^{-2\pi b/\omega_d} \approx e^{-1.62} \approx 0.20} @@ each bounce a fifth of the last; two bounces in, under 4% is left

A damper of 2mω ≈ 5657 N s/m would put this corner exactly at the edge of swinging. This one is a quarter of that, so the truck bounces once or twice and settles.

26·cb24.4Reading the Brake off Two Swings

The brake is hard to measure directly. The swings measure it for you. Two peaks one period T apart have heights in the ratio e^(bT), so b is the log of that ratio over T.

h_1 = 10 \text{ cm},\ \ h_2 = 6 \text{ cm},\ \ T = 0.8 \text{ s} @@ two peaks in a row, and the time between them
b = \tfrac{1}{T}\ln\tfrac{h_1}{h_2} = \tfrac{\ln(5/3)}{0.8} \approx 0.639 @@ per second: the log of the ratio over the period
\omega_d = \tfrac{2\pi}{0.8} \approx 7.854,\ \ \omega = \sqrt{\omega_d^2 + b^2} \approx 7.880 @@ the light-brake technique, turned round
\omega_d = \omega\sqrt{1 - \tfrac{b^2}{\omega^2}} \approx \omega - \tfrac{b^2}{2\omega} @@ the binomial series (23h), first two terms
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{b^2}{2\omega} \approx 0.026:\ \text{the pace drops by a third of a percent}} \quad \blacksquare @@ while the swing loses 40% each period

Two-peaks technique: two peaks one period T apart, of heights h₁ and h₂, give the damping b = ln(h₁/h₂)/T. A light brake shrinks the swing a lot and slows the pace very little, since the pace changes only by the square of b.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The rule y″ + 2by′ + ω²y = 0 is the damped harmonic oscillator. A spring with b < ω is underdamped, with b = ω critically damped, and with b > ω overdamped. The share b/ω is the damping ratio, ω_d the damped angular frequency, and ln(h₁/h₂) the logarithmic decrement. The decaying-turn technique solves linear equations with constant coefficients whose characteristic equation has complex roots. A damper is a shock absorber, or a dashpot in diagrams.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler solved rules with constant numbers in front in 1743, complex roots included, by turning e^((α ± iβ)x) into e^(αx) times cos and sin. In 26·cb24.6.5, Isaac Newton tried resistance in proportion to the speed, to its square, and to both, in Book II of the Principia (1687), and found air near the square. George Gabriel Stokes (1851) found the straight law for a small sphere moving slowly through a thick fluid. Osborne Reynolds (1883) found the number that says which of the two a flow is near.
