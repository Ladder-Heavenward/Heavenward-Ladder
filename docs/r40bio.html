How Do You Line Up Two Proteins?

How do you line up two proteins? A protein is a chain of amino acids, written as a word in an alphabet of twenty letters, and the chain folds into the shape that does its work. A human protein and a yeast protein that do the same job often spell nearly the same word, with letters changed, lost or added over a billion years. To see the likeness, the two words must be lined up, with gaps where letters were lost. The ways to line them up are past counting, yet a table filled in order, a loop inside a loop, finds the best in a few seconds. This page writes proteins as words, scores a lining-up, counts the ways, and fills the table, using 39o’s remembering of answers.

Score a lining-up: +1 for a match, −1 for a mismatch, −1 for each gap. The best score for the first i letters of one word against the first j of the other comes from one of three smaller cases: both letters paired, or one against a gap. Fill the table row by row, each cell from its three neighbours; read the best lining-up back from the corner.

40bio1Proteins as Words

Each amino acid has a one-letter name: A for alanine, G for glycine, W for tryptophan, twenty in all. Three letters of DNA name one amino acid, as 4·bio read the code.

\text{protein} = \text{chain of amino acids} @@ joined end to end
\text{written as a word: } \texttt{MVLSPADKTN}\ldots @@ the start of human haemoglobin’s α chain
\text{the word sets the fold} @@ Anfinsen, 1960s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{like words} \Rightarrow \text{like shapes and jobs, usually}} @@ so comparing words compares proteins

From here on, on this page, an alignment of two words writes them one above the other, adding gaps “−” so that each column pairs two letters or a letter and a gap.

40bio2Scoring a Lining-Up

Evita lines up ACGT with AGT, two short words standing in for long ones.

\begin{smallmatrix} \texttt{A} & \texttt{C} & \texttt{G} & \texttt{T} \\ \texttt{A} & - & \texttt{G} & \texttt{T} \end{smallmatrix}:\ 1 - 1 + 1 + 1 = 2 @@ match, gap, match, match
\begin{smallmatrix} \texttt{A} & \texttt{C} & \texttt{G} & \texttt{T} \\ \texttt{A} & \texttt{G} & \texttt{T} & - \end{smallmatrix}:\ 1 - 1 - 1 - 1 = -2 @@ match, two mismatches, gap
\text{score} = \text{matches} - \text{mismatches} - \text{gaps} @@ a sum over columns
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the best alignment has the highest score}} @@ here 2

Real scores reward each pair of amino acids by how often one is swapped for the other in proteins known to be kin: two letters of like chemistry score well even when unequal.

40bio3Too Many Ways to Try

\text{an alignment} = \text{a path through a grid, steps right, down or across} @@ gap, gap or pair
\text{paths with only right and down steps: } \tbinom{2n}{n} @@ choose which steps go down (4·stat)
n = 100:\ \tbinom{200}{100} \approx 9 \times 10^{58} @@ a lower bound
\htmlClass{new}{\text{trying every alignment is hopeless}} @@ even for short proteins

Most proteins are hundreds of letters long, so listing alignments one by one would outlast the universe. But the best alignment of two words is built from best alignments of their beginnings, the shape 39o and 42n exploit.

40bio4The Table Filled in Order

Let F(i, j) be the best score for the first i letters of ACGT against the first j of AGT. The last column of a best alignment pairs the i-th and j-th letters, or puts one against a gap.

F(1,1) = \max(0 + 1, -2, -2) = 1 @@ A with A

Alignment technique: fill a table of best scores for every pair of beginnings, row by row, each cell the best of its three neighbours plus one column’s score. A loop inside a loop (40b); each cell is done once.

40bio5Reading the Answer Back

\text{from the corner, step to the neighbour that gave its value} @@ remembered while filling
(4,3) \to (3,2) \to (2,1) \to (1,1) \to (0,0) @@ pair, pair, gap, pair
\text{work} = (n+1)(m+1) \text{ cells} @@ two 300-letter proteins: 90 601 cells
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a database of millions: search tools skip most cells}} @@ first find short exact matches, then fill near them

The table turns more than 10⁵⁸ alignments into n × m cells. A newly read gene is compared with every known protein this way, and a good match says what the new one probably does.

40bio7How Surprising a Match Is

Shared ancestry leaves shared letters, but two unrelated proteins also share some by chance. Scored in bits, a lining-up of score S happens by chance about once in 2^S tries, and lining one protein against a whole library is many tries.

\text{tries} \approx m\,n = 300 \times 10^8 = 3 \times 10^{10} @@ every place against every place
\text{chance matches scoring } S \text{ or more} \approx m n\,2^{-S} @@ 
S = 40 \text{ bits}:\ 3 \times 10^{10} \times 2^{-40} \approx 0.03 @@ surprising
\htmlClass{new}{S = 30:\ \approx 28 \text{ expected by chance}} @@ ten bits, a thousand times less surprising

Surprise technique: a match scoring S bits is expected about mn2^(−S) times by chance; below about 0.01, shared ancestry is the better explanation, and a bigger library needs a higher score. Shown on a computer: a newly read protein lined up against a library finds, at a score expected by chance far less than once, the known stretch it shares with a protein from yeast, and nothing comparable among the shuffled controls.

Accepted names

This is sequence alignment; the table method for whole words is the Needleman-Wunsch algorithm, and for the best matching pieces the Smith-Waterman algorithm. Filling a table of answers to smaller cases is dynamic programming. Scores for amino acid pairs come from substitution matrices such as BLOSUM62; the fast database search is BLAST. Proteins of shared descent are homologous.

Who said it first

Saul Needleman and Christian Wunsch published the table method in 1970; Temple Smith and Michael Waterman the local version in 1981; BLAST came from Altschul and colleagues in 1990. Christian Anfinsen won the 1972 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for showing that a protein’s sequence sets its fold.
