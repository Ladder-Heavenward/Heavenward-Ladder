Can a Lock Be Built on a Hard Problem?

Can a lock be built on a hard problem? Every lock in use is a bet. The secrets of rung 43 and of the curves of 44·crypt bet that one clock problem is slow, and ∞1 showed that a quantum computer would win that bet against them. The codes standardised in the United States in August 2024 bet on a different problem: a list of equations on a clock, each knocked off by a small error, cannot be solved. This page makes that problem into geometry. The equations become a grid of points in space, regular in every direction, and solving them becomes finding the point of the grid nearest to a given place. In two dimensions a method two centuries old finds the shortest step of any such grid, and the page proves it. In hundreds of dimensions nobody knows a fast way, and the best general method of 1982 can be off by a factor that doubles with every two dimensions. The page then proves when the nearest point is the right answer, and shows that guessing one unknown at a time is no easier than telling the noisy equations from pure chance. A bet is still a bet. What made this one different, in 2005, was a proof that breaking the lock on random equations, even a little of the time, would solve the grid problem for every grid, including the worst one anyone could choose. That proof needs one quantum step, and its key move is that a bell curve laid over a grid blurs it to almost perfectly even. The page proves that move. In April 2024 a paper claimed a quantum method for the problem itself, and for eight days the field held its breath, until a flaw was found in one step. Nobody has proved the problem hard, and nobody knows whether the quantum reach of ∞1 stops at grids.

The points y ≡ As (mod q) form a lattice whose box has volume q^(m − n). A reduced basis of the plane, |q| ≤ p ≤ r, has p as the least value of its form, so its first vector is shortest. If the error e is shorter than half the shortest vector, As is the only nearest point to b = As + e. Adding r to a coordinate of a and rg to b keeps the samples noisy exactly when g is right, so deciding gives searching. The bell of width s summed over the integers is s(1 + 2Σ e^(−πs²m²) cos 2πmc), flat to within 2e^(−πs²): the blur at the heart of the 2005 reduction.

sky40.1Equations on a Clock Become a Grid

44·now2.3 hid a secret s in b = As + e on the clock of q. Read the clock as a fold. A whole-number list y in ℤᵐ is congruent to As when y − As has every entry a multiple of q. Collect every such y, for every s.

A lattice is the set of whole combinations of a few vectors of ℝᵐ that are free (27a): it adds and takes away like arrows, but stretches only by whole numbers. Its volume is the volume of the box its vectors span, |det| of the grid with those vectors as columns (27e); every basis of one lattice gives the same volume, since the change from one basis to another is a whole grid with a whole undo, of det ±1.

Clock-lattice technique: for an m by n grid A on the prime clock of q, the set L of y in ℤᵐ with y ≡ As (mod q) for some s is a lattice; if As takes qⁿ different values on the clock, its volume is qᵐ⁻ⁿ.

y, y' \in L \Rightarrow y - y' \equiv A(s - s') \pmod q @@ L takes away within itself, and holds qℤᵐ
L = \textstyle\bigcup_{\text{values } v \text{ of } As} (v + q\mathbb Z^m) @@ one shift of qℤᵐ for each value
q\mathbb Z^m \text{ has volume } q^m,\ \ L \text{ holds } q^n \text{ of its points per box} @@ the shifts do not meet: different v differ on the clock
\htmlClass{new}{\text{volume of } L = q^m / q^n = q^{m-n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the box is shared among qⁿ points
  
a = (1, 3, 6):\ \ L = \{y : y \equiv t\,a \pmod{17}\} @@ n = 1, m = 3; t a takes 17 values, since the first entry is t
\text{basis } (1, 3, 6),\ (0, 17, 0),\ (0, 0, 17) @@ every y in L is y₁ a plus multiples of 17 in the last two places
\det = 1 \cdot 17 \cdot 17 = 289 @@ a triangular grid: the product of the corners
\htmlClass{new}{289 = 17^{3-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clock-lattice technique, checked

So the clock is gone. Equations on a clock are a lattice in ordinary space, and a secret is a point of it.

sky40.2The Shortest Step, in the Plane

The shortest-vector problem: given a basis, find a nonzero point of the lattice nearest to 0. In the plane, a basis b₁, b₂ gives the form |xb₁ + yb₂|² = px² + qxy + ry² with p = |b₁|², q = 2 b₁·b₂, r = |b₂|² (27i). The form-reduction technique of ∞12.2 changes the basis until |q| ≤ p ≤ r. Here the difference: that reduced basis already holds the answer.

Least-value technique: if a positive form has |q| ≤ p ≤ r, its least value at a whole point other than (0, 0) is p; so in a reduced basis of a plane lattice, b₁ is a shortest nonzero point.

px^2 + qxy + ry^2 \ge px^2 - p|x||y| + py^2 @@ |q| ≤ p and r ≥ p
= p\big((|x| - |y|)^2 + |x||y|\big) @@ multiplied out: x² − 2|x||y| + y² + |x||y|
xy \ne 0 \Rightarrow |x||y| \ge 1;\ \ xy = 0 \Rightarrow (|x| - |y|)^2 \ge 1 @@ whole numbers, not both 0
\htmlClass{new}{px^2 + qxy + ry^2 \ge p,\ \text{reached at } (1, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ b₁ is shortest
  
b_1 = (1, 5),\ \ b_2 = (0, 17):\ \ p = 26,\ r = 289 @@ a basis; volume 17
(0, 17) - 3(1, 5) = (-3, 2),\ \ |{\cdot}|^2 = 13 @@ take away the nearest whole multiple: 85/26 rounds to 3
(1, 5) - (-3, 2) = (4, 3),\ \ |{\cdot}|^2 = 25 @@ 7/13 rounds to 1
p = 13,\ \ q = 2(-12 + 6) = -12,\ \ r = 25 @@ |−12| ≤ 13 ≤ 25: reduced; det = −9 − 8 = −17
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shortest nonzero length } \sqrt{13}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the least-value technique; a search of the plane agrees

Each step takes away a whole multiple of the shorter vector from the longer, like 4j’s greatest common divisor, and the lengths fall until the form is reduced. In many dimensions no step of this kind is known to finish fast with the shortest vector. A method of 1982 finishes fast with a vector at most 2^((n − 1)/2) times the shortest; for n = 500 that factor has 76 digits. The lattices of 44·now2 have hundreds of dimensions.

sky40.3When the Nearest Point Is the Secret

With errors, b = As + e is not a point of L but near one. 27j’s row moves look for an exact point and smear e over the answer, as 44·now2.3 found. The geometric question is the right one: which point of L is nearest to b? Call λ₁ the length of a shortest nonzero point of L.

Unique-nearest technique: if |e| < λ₁/2, then As is the only point of L nearest to b = As + e.

y \in L,\ y \ne As \Rightarrow |y - As| \ge \lambda_1 @@ y − As is a nonzero point of L
|b - y| \ge |y - As| - |b - As| \ge \lambda_1 - |e| @@ the triangle inequality (27i)
\lambda_1 - |e| > \lambda_1/2 > |e| @@ since |e| < λ₁/2
\htmlClass{new}{|b - y| > |b - As|} \quad \blacksquare @@ every other point is further
  
As = (7, 21, 42) \equiv (7, 4, 8),\ \ b = (8, 3, 8) @@ the noisy equations
\lambda_1^2 = 41,\ \text{at } \pm(6, 1, 2) = 6a - (0, 17, 34) @@ a search of every point with entries below 35
|e| = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.41 < \sqrt{41}/2 \approx 3.20 @@ the condition holds
\htmlClass{new}{\text{nearest point } (7, 4, 8) = 7a,\ \text{the next at distance } \sqrt{33}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the secret 7 is read off

Now the grid of 44·now2.3, two unknowns and three equations on the clock of 17. Its first two rows can be undone on the clock (det 1·4 − 2·3 = −2), so its lattice holds every y whose third entry is fixed by the first two; it holds (1, 0, 0), and λ₁ = 1. The point (14, 12, 3) of L is nearer b = (14, 11, 3) than As = (13, 12, 3) is. Three equations in two unknowns hide nothing: they admit a second secret with a smaller error. The volume qᵐ⁻ⁿ grows with every extra equation, so with enough equations λ₁ is large and the secret is the nearest point. The lock is then a bet that finding the nearest point is slow.

sky40.4Guessing One Unknown at a Time

Learning with errors, stated: a secret s on the clock of q, and as many samples as asked, each a fresh random a and b = a·s + e with e a small error drawn from a narrow bell. The search problem is to find s. The decision problem is to tell such samples from pairs (a, u) with u uniform on the clock. Deciding looks easier. It is not.

One-guess technique: on a prime clock, a method that decides gives a method that searches: to test a guess g for the first entry of s, replace each sample (a, b) by (a + r·(1, 0, …, 0), b + rg) with r fresh and uniform; the new samples are noisy samples for the same s if g is right, and uniform pairs if g is wrong. So qn runs of the decider find s.

b + rg = a \cdot s + e + rg = (a + r\mathbf 1_1)\cdot s + e + r(g - s_1) @@ regroup
g = s_1:\ \ b + rg = a' \cdot s + e @@ the same secret, the same error: still a noisy sample
g \ne s_1:\ \ r(g - s_1) \text{ is uniform on the clock} @@ g − s₁ can be undone on a prime clock (43b), and r is uniform
\htmlClass{new}{\text{right guesses keep the noise; wrong ones give pure chance}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the decider tells which
  
a' = (1 + 5, 2) = (6, 2),\ \ a' \cdot s = 18 + 10 = 28 \equiv 11 @@ the shifted sample
g = 3:\ \ b' = 14 + 15 = 29 \equiv 12,\ \ b' - a' \cdot s = 1 @@ the old error, small
g = 4:\ \ b' = 14 + 20 = 34 \equiv 0,\ \ b' - a' \cdot s \equiv 6 @@ not small: and over fresh r it takes every value alike
\htmlClass{new}{17 \times 2 = 34 \text{ guesses find } s} \quad \blacksquare @@ q n tests

So the lock may rest on the decision problem, which is what a lock needs: a locked message that a listener cannot tell from chance. What it cannot rest on, yet, is a proof that either problem is hard.

sky40.5A Bell Blurs a Grid to Even

Most locks are tested on random keys, while a hardness proof speaks of the worst case. The 2005 reduction joined the two: a method that solves learning with errors for random samples would give a quantum method that finds short vectors in every lattice, to within a factor that grows like a power of n. Its engine, held in trust here, builds samples of ever narrower bells on the lattice, using the solver on the classical side and a quantum step of ∞1’s kind to turn each sample round. One move in it is pure 32: a bell wide enough, laid over a lattice and folded back into one box, is almost flat. Here it is for the lattice of whole numbers.

Blur-flat technique: for every width s > 0 and every c, Σₖ e^(−π(k + c)²/s²) = s(1 + 2 Σ_{m ≥ 1} e^(−πs²m²) cos 2πmc), the sums over whole k and m; so the folded bell differs from s by at most about 2s e^(−πs²).

f(c) = \textstyle\sum_k e^{-\pi(k + c)^2/s^2} \text{ repeats every } 1 @@ c → c + 1 only renames k
\textstyle\int_0^1 f(c)\,e^{-2\pi i m c}\,dc = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\pi x^2/s^2} e^{-2\pi i m x}\,dx @@ the coefficient of 32a; the pieces of the line join up, since e^(−2πimk) = 1
= s\,e^{-\pi s^2 m^2} @@ 32e2’s own-transform technique, stretched by s
f(c) = \textstyle\sum_m s\,e^{-\pi s^2 m^2} e^{2\pi i m c} @@ 32b: the coefficients fall so fast the sum of turnings is f itself
\htmlClass{new}{f(c) = s\big(1 + 2\textstyle\sum_{m \ge 1} e^{-\pi s^2 m^2}\cos 2\pi m c\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ pair m with −m
  
s = 1:\ \ f(0) = 1.0864,\ \ f(\tfrac12) = 0.9136 @@ 1 ± 2e^(−π) = 1 ± 0.0864, to four places
s = 2:\ \ 2e^{-4\pi} = 6.97 \times 10^{-6} @@ f within seven millionths of 2, relatively 3.5 × 10⁻⁶
\text{in } \mathbb Z^n:\ \ \text{a product of } n \text{ such sums} @@ the bell in n places is a product of bells (30e)
n = 256,\ s = 2:\ \ (1 + 6.97 \times 10^{-6})^{256} - 1 \approx 0.0018 @@ the ripples multiply
\htmlClass{new}{s = 3:\ \ 2e^{-9\pi} \approx 1.05 \times 10^{-12}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a little more width, and the blur is flat in any dimension in use

For a general lattice the same computation runs with its dual, the vectors whose dot with every lattice point is whole, in place of m; the width at which the blur goes flat is set by the dual’s shortest vector, held in trust. Flatness is what lets a random-looking sample be made from any lattice at all, and so a solver for random samples speaks for the worst lattice.

sky40.6Eight Days in April

The 2005 reduction runs one way. A fast method for random samples would find short vectors in every lattice to within a factor near a power of n, and nobody can do that, on any machine. On 10 April 2024 a paper claimed to do it on a quantum computer, for a factor near n^4.5, through a new quantum step for learning with errors. ∞1’s period finder had broken the clock secrets in 1994; this would have been the first quantum crack in the lattice side.

n = 500:\ \ 2^{(n - 1)/2} = 2^{249.5} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{75} @@ the factor of the 1982 method
n^{4.5} = 500^{4.5} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{12} @@ the factor claimed
1.3 \times 10^{75} / 1.4 \times 10^{12} \approx 10^{63} @@ sixty-three powers of ten closer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a power of } n \text{ against a doubling}} \quad \blacksquare @@ what nobody had done

The claim did not reach the parameters of the 2024 standards directly; it would have left them, as one reader put it, in a much more precarious position. Readers across the field went through it at once. On 18 April the author posted that step 9 of the method contained a bug he did not know how to fix, found by two readers independently, and that the claim did not hold. The paper stays up, with that note at its head.

Two things were learned. A proof that is wrong can be found wrong in days when a field is ready to read it, which ∞35 asks of proofs that are long. And the eight days showed how little stands between the standards and a surprise: the bet of sky40.3 is a bet on the absence of a method, and an absence cannot be checked.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞40.oq.1Is any lattice problem provably slow? Nobody can prove that finding a shortest vector, even roughly, takes more than a power of n steps. A proof would settle more than 41·now2 asks. Worse for the hope, finding vectors within a factor √n of the shortest is known since 2004 to be a problem whose yes and no answers both have short certificates, so it is unlikely to be among the hardest problems of 41h at all. The standards rest on factors of that size or larger.
∞40.oq.2Does the quantum reach of ∞1 stop at lattices? The period finder works in groups whose moves can be done in either order. A 2002 result showed that a fast quantum method for one hidden-period problem in the turnings and flips of a polygon would find unusually short vectors in lattices. No such method is known, and the April 2024 attempt took another road and failed. Whether some quantum road reaches the lattices of the standards is open.
∞40.oq.3Does the ring of 44·now2 make the lock weaker? The standards use the ring with a sign to make keys short, and the 2005 reduction speaks of plain lattices. In 2015 to 2016 a quantum method was found that breaks a few schemes built on one special kind of ideal in rings of this family, those made by one member. It does not reach the module lattices of the standard. Whether the extra structure can ever be turned against the standard is open.
∞40.oq.4How many steps does the best attack on the standard take? The cost of the best known attacks is estimated by models of how lattice reduction behaves in hundreds of dimensions, which nobody can run at that size. In 2023 a critic argued in public that the count for the smallest size had been made wrongly. The estimates rest on guesses about the methods, and the guesses are argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Lattices entered codes as a weapon first. A lock of 1978 rested on the problem of choosing, from a list of whole numbers, some that add to a given total, a problem among the hardest of 41h. In 1982 it was broken, and the same year a method was published that finds short vectors in any lattice fast, to within the factor of sky40.2. The lesson drawn then was that a problem hard in its worst case can be easy on the cases a lock actually uses.

In 1996 that lesson was turned round. A paper proved that a certain problem on random lattices was as hard as a lattice problem in its worst case: for the first time, breaking random instances would break every instance. In 2005 a paper introduced learning with errors and proved the stronger link of sky40.5, with one quantum step, and built a simple lock from it. The quantum step troubled some readers, since it means the evidence is the belief that even quantum computers cannot find short vectors. The 2005 paper received the leading prize for a paper in theoretical computing in 2018.

The urgency came from ∞1. If a quantum computer is ever built, every secret sent today on the clocks of 43l can be read then by whoever retained a copy. In 2016 the US standards agency opened a contest for locks that would survive. One candidate, built on curves of another kind, was broken in 2022 on an ordinary computer in about an hour. The lattice candidates survived, and on 13 August 2024 two of the three new standards rested on learning with errors on the ring of 44·now2. In March 2025 the agency chose a backup built on codes rather than lattices, in case the lattice bet fails.

Not everyone trusted the counting. In 2023 a cryptographer argued that the agency had overstated how hard the smallest standard size is to break, and asked in public what part the intelligence agency had played in the choices. The argument was bitter, and it ran in the open, on the contest’s own mailing list and in the press. Its subject was not the mathematics of sky40.5, which nobody disputed, but how many steps an attack takes in practice.

Then came the eight days of April 2024 (sky40.6). A respected author claimed a quantum method; readers worked through it at once; two of them found the bug in step 9 independently; the author said so in public and withdrew the claim. Many took it as the system working. Others noted that the standards had come within one bug of a serious scare, and that nothing in the episode made the bet safer, only that this one attack did not work.

What is argued now is less whether lattice locks will be used than how much to rely on them alone. The first large uses, in a web browser in August 2023 and in a messaging app the next month, laid a lattice lock and a curve lock on top of each other, so that both must fail. How long the old curve should stay beside the new lattice, and whether it is a safeguard or a cost, is argued; nobody has a proof either way.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The clock-lattice is the q-ary lattice Λ_q(A), here in its image form; λ₁ is the first successive minimum. The shortest-vector problem is SVP, and its approximate form GapSVP or SVP_γ; finding the nearest point under a promise is bounded distance decoding (BDD), and the unique-nearest technique is the reason BDD has one answer below λ₁/2. The least-value technique is the correctness of Lagrange–Gauss reduction. The 1982 method is the LLL algorithm. Learning with errors is LWE, with search and decision forms; the one-guess technique is the search-to-decision reduction for prime modulus polynomial in n. The blur-flat technique is Poisson summation for the Gaussian, and the width at which the blur is flat is the smoothing parameter η_ε(L), defined through the dual lattice L*. The 2005 reduction is Regev’s quantum worst-case to average-case reduction, to GapSVP and SIVP with factor about n/α; the 1996 problem is the short integer solution problem, SIS. The 2024 standards are FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, from CRYSTALS-Kyber) and FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium); the 2025 backup is HQC; the curve candidate broken in 2022 was SIKE. The 2002 result reduces the unique shortest-vector problem to the dihedral hidden subgroup problem. The 2015–16 attack recovers short generators of principal ideals in cyclotomic rings. The 1978 lock is the Merkle–Hellman knapsack cryptosystem.

Who said it first

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1773) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1801) reduced two-dimensional forms. Arjen Lenstra, Hendrik Lenstra and László Lovász published their reduction in 1982, and Adi Shamir broke the Merkle–Hellman knapsack in 1982. Miklós Ajtai gave the first worst-case to average-case link in 1996. Oded Regev introduced LWE and its quantum reduction at STOC 2005 (journal version 2009), and received the Gödel Prize in 2018; Regev’s 2002 paper linked lattices to the dihedral hidden subgroup problem; Dorit Aharonov and Regev placed √n-approximate lattice problems in NP ∩ coNP (2004–05). Ronald Cramer, Léo Ducas, Chris Peikert and Oded Regev gave the short-generator attack (2015–16). Yilei Chen posted the April 2024 claim on 10 April; Hongxun Wu and Thomas Vidick independently found the bug, and Chen withdrew the claim on 18 April. Daniel J. Bernstein raised the 2023 objection to the Kyber-512 security count.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every attack anyone has tried on learning with errors takes more than 2¹⁶⁰ steps, so the lock cannot be broken faster.” Not allowed: a list of failed attacks is not a proof about all attacks. The 2005 reduction gives something better than the list, a link to a problem studied for two centuries, but a link to a belief is still a belief, and April 2024 showed how fast a new road can appear.
