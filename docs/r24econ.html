Why Do Booms End?

Why do booms end? In a boom, firms hire, shops are busy and work is easy to find. Then hiring slows and people are laid off, with no flood, no war and no failed harvest to blame, and some years later the boom comes round again. What makes the question hard is that nothing outside the economy need have changed. This page uses rung twenty-four’s rules on rates. It shows why some share of workers is out of work even in good times, as the balance of two flows. It shows why prices rise at the rate by which money outruns goods. And it shows how two rates, each one steering the other, make a cycle with no push from outside, and what makes such a cycle die away.

Workers lose jobs at rate s and find them at rate f, so the share out of work heads to s/(s + f) from every start. The growth rate of a product is the sum of the growth rates, so prices grow at the rate of money plus its speed less output. When jobs steer the workers’ share of output and that share steers jobs, x′ = −py and y′ = qx, both swing for ever with period 2π/√(pq), and qx² + py² is kept. A pull back to rest makes that kept quantity fall, and the swings die.

24econ1Losing Jobs and Finding Them

A town has 10 000 workers. Each month, 1 in 100 of those with a job lose it, and 19 in 100 of those without a job find one. Let u be the share of workers out of work. The share with jobs is 1 − u. Write s = 0.01 for the rate of losing and f = 0.19 for the rate of finding, both per month. Two flows change u, one in and one out.

Two-flows technique: if u′ = s(1 − u) − fu with s and f above 0, then u = u* + (u₀ − u*)e−(s + f)t with u* = s/(s + f). So the share out of work heads to s/(s + f) from every start.

u' = s(1 - u) - fu @@ in: a share s of those with jobs; out: a share f of those without
u' = -(s + f)\Big(u - \tfrac{s}{s + f}\Big) @@ gather the u
\htmlClass{new}{u = u^* + (u_0 - u^*)\,e^{-(s + f)t},\quad u^* = \tfrac{s}{s + f}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shift-to-rest technique (24h1), with k = −(s + f) below 0
  
u^* = 0.01/(0.01 + 0.19) = 0.05 @@ 500 of the 10 000 out of work, at rest
0.01 \times 9500 = 95 = 0.19 \times 500 @@ at rest, 95 lose a job each month and 95 find one
u_0 = 0.10:\quad u' = 0.01(0.9) - 0.19(0.1) = -0.01 @@ after a slump: 90 lose, 190 find
\htmlClass{new}{u(5) = 0.05 + 0.05\,e^{-1} \approx 0.068} @@ five months on; half the gap closes every 3.5 months

The state u* holds (24k1): above it more find than lose, and below it more lose than find. It is not 0, and no one is to blame for that. Even at rest, with 500 out of work, the 500 are not the same people from month to month: 95 leave and 95 arrive. To lower the share at rest, change a rate. Doubling f to 0.38 brings u* down to about 0.026.

24econ2Money and Prices

In a year, a country’s stock of money M changes hands V times on average. It buys an output Y, at a level of prices P. Both MV and PY count the year’s spending, once from the side of the money and once from the side of the goods. So MV = PY. This is bookkeeping: V is counted as PY/M, and the sameness cannot fail.

The growth rate of a quantity x is x′/x: its rate of change as a share of itself. A stock of money growing at 0.07 a year grows by about 7 in each 100.

Growth-rates-add technique: where x and y are above 0, the growth rate of a product is the sum of the growth rates: (xy)′/(xy) = x′/x + y′/y.

(xy)' = x'y + xy' @@ the product technique for slopes (13c)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{(xy)'}{xy} = \tfrac{x'}{x} + \tfrac{y'}{y}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by xy
  
\tfrac{M'}{M} + \tfrac{V'}{V} = \tfrac{P'}{P} + \tfrac{Y'}{Y} @@ the growth-rates-add technique on both sides of MV = PY
\tfrac{P'}{P} = \tfrac{M'}{M} + \tfrac{V'}{V} - \tfrac{Y'}{Y} @@ prices grow as money and its speed outrun output
\htmlClass{new}{0.07 + 0 - 0.03 = 0.04} @@ money up 7 in 100 a year, speed steady, output up 3: prices up 4

The rates add exactly as slopes. Jumps of a whole year add only nearly: 1.07/1.03 ≈ 1.039, not 1.04.

The sameness says nothing about which quantity moves which. Read with V steady, it says that prices follow money. Whether V is steady is a matter for measurement, and it has held in some decades and failed in others.

24econ3Two Rates, Each Steering the Other

Now watch two numbers together. One is the share of workers with jobs. The other is the workers’ share of output: the part of what is made that is paid out in wages, the rest going to profit. Let x be how far the share with jobs stands above its resting value, and y how far the workers’ share stands above its own. Time t is in years.

  • When jobs are plentiful, x above 0, workers are hard to replace and can bargain, and their share rises: y′ = qx.
  • When the workers’ share is high, y above 0, profits are thin, firms build less, and hiring falls: x′ = −py.

Each number sets the rate of the other. Neither rule mentions anything outside the two.

Each-steers technique: if x′ = −py and y′ = qx with p and q above 0, then x″ = −pqx. So x and y swing for ever, repeating every 2π/√(pq), and qx² + py² never changes.

x'' = -p\,y' = -pq\,x @@ take the rate of the first rule, and feed in the second
x = A\cos(nt),\quad n = \sqrt{pq} @@ the steady-swing technique (24h2), from x(0) = A, y(0) = 0, so x′(0) = 0
y = -x'/p = (An/p)\sin(nt) @@ the first rule, solved for y
(qx^2 + py^2)' = 2qx\,x' + 2py\,y' = -2pq\,xy + 2pq\,xy = 0 @@ the two rules, fed in
\htmlClass{new}{qx^2 + py^2 \text{ is kept, and both repeat every } 2\pi/\sqrt{pq}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a slope of 0 everywhere gives no change (14c2)
  
p = 0.5,\quad q = 0.8:\qquad n = \sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.632 @@ per year
2\pi/n \approx 9.93 \text{ years} @@ one full swing
A = 0.02:\quad y \text{ swings } 0.02 \times 0.632/0.5 \approx 0.025 \text{ each way} @@ jobs start 2 points above rest
\htmlClass{new}{y \text{ peaks } 2.5 \text{ years after } x} @@ sin peaks a quarter swing after cos

So the boom ends itself. At the top of the boom in jobs, the workers’ share is still climbing, because y′ = qx is at its greatest there. That climbing share is what turns hiring down. Jobs then fall, the share stops climbing a quarter swing later, and the fall in the share is what turns hiring up again. Nothing outside pushed.

The rules here are straight, which is right only near rest. Far from rest the two rates bend. 28·bio meets a pair of that kind, foxes and rabbits, and 27·cb24 takes up pairs of rules on rates in general.

24econ4Why the Swings Die

The first footing gave jobs a pull of their own toward rest, at the rate s + f. Put a pull of that kind into the cycle: x′ = −py − cx, with c above 0, and y′ = qx as before.

Dying-swing technique: if x′ = −py − cx and y′ = qx with p, q and c above 0, then qx² + py² never rises, and it falls whenever x is not 0. And x″ + cx′ + pqx = 0.

(qx^2 + py^2)' = 2qx(-py - cx) + 2py(qx) @@ the two rules, fed in
\htmlClass{new}{(qx^2 + py^2)' = -2cq\,x^2 \le 0} @@ the terms in xy cancel as before; the pull leaves −2cqx²
x'' = -p\,y' - c\,x' = -pq\,x - c\,x' \quad \blacksquare @@ the rate of the first rule, with the second fed in

With no pull, the path of (x, y) goes round one closed curve, qx² + py² = a constant, for ever. With a pull it can never get back out to a curve it has left, so each swing is narrower than the last.

A strong pull stops the swinging altogether. The tried-growth technique (24h3) solves x″ + cx′ + pqx = 0 when the trial equation r² + cr + pq = 0 has real roots, which is when c² ≥ 4pq.

p = 0.5,\ q = 0.8,\ c = 1.3:\qquad r^2 + 1.3r + 0.4 = (r + 0.5)(r + 0.8) @@ c² = 1.69 ≥ 1.6 = 4pq
x = C_1 e^{-0.5t} + C_2 e^{-0.8t} @@ the tried-growth technique (24h3)
C_1 e^{-0.5t} + C_2 e^{-0.8t} = 0 \iff e^{0.3t} = -C_2/C_1 @@ at most one t
\htmlClass{new}{x \text{ crosses rest once at most, then creeps back to it}} @@ no cycle

When c² is below 4pq the trial equation has no real roots, and the swings go on while they narrow. Their full form waits for 26·cb24.

This gives two readings of the cycles that are seen. In one, the economy makes its own swings, as in the last footing. In the other, the swings would die, and they are seen only because knocks from outside keep setting them off again: a harvest, a war, a new machine, a change in the rate of interest.

The accepted names

s is the separation rate and f the job-finding rate; u is the unemployment rate, and u* = s/(s + f) is its steady state, often called the natural rate of unemployment. The two-flows picture is the flow model of the labour market, and the people passing through at rest are frictional unemployment. MV = PY is the equation of exchange, V the velocity of money, P the price level and Y real output; the growth rate of P is inflation. Read with V steady, the equation is the quantity theory of money. The growth-rates-add technique is logarithmic differentiation: x′/x is the slope of ln x. The workers’ share of output is the wage share, or labour share, and the cycle of the third footing is the Goodwin model, here in its straight form near rest. In full it is a pair of Lotka–Volterra, or predator–prey, equations, with the wage share as the predator. The kept quantity is a conserved quantity and its curves are closed orbits. A pull to rest is damping; c² ≥ 4pq is the overdamped case and c² < 4pq the underdamped. Cycles made inside the economy are endogenous; cycles set off by knocks from outside are exogenous, the knocks being shocks. The steady trade of 24·econ.6.6 is the Phillips curve, and the answer to it the natural rate hypothesis.

Who said it first

Richard Goodwin gave the cycle of jobs and the wage share in 1967, in a paper called “A Growth Cycle”, and noted that its equations are those written for predators and prey. The equation of exchange is tied to the name of Irving Fisher. The steady trade between unemployment and rising wages was read from British records by A. W. Phillips in 1958, and answered by Milton Friedman in 1968 and by Edmund Phelps in 1967 and 1968.
