42c · proof

Walking Every Link Once

In the town of Königsberg on the Baltic, the river Pregel split the town into four parts: two banks and two islands. Seven bridges joined them. The townspeople asked whether a Sunday walk could cross every bridge exactly once. Nobody found one, and in 1736 it was proved that nobody could, by an argument that never looked at the map again once it had counted the bridges at each part. That argument is the start of this whole rung. A walk that crosses every link once leaves each place it passes through by a different link from the one it came in on, so links come in pairs at every place except where it starts and ends. Counting is enough to say no, and, more surprising, counting is enough to say yes. Then the step asks the question that looks the same and is not: a walk through every dot exactly once. No count of that kind settles it. A strong enough count still forces such a walk, and the step proves one; but in general the question is one of the hard ones of rung 41, where checking an answer is quick and finding one is not.

A walk that uses every link once pairs the links at every dot it passes through. So a closed one exists in a graph in one piece exactly when every dot has an even count of links, and an open one exactly when two dots have odd counts. A walk through every dot once has no such test; when every dot has links to at least half the dots, one exists.

42c1The Seven Bridges

Two bridges of Königsberg joined the same two parts. So for this step a graph may have more than one link between two dots: the links are a list, each joining two different dots, and two links with the same dots are still two links. The ends-count technique’s proof did not care, so it still holds. Call a walk that uses every link exactly once a tour of the links, closed if it ends where it began.

\text{parts: the north bank } N,\ \text{the south bank } S,\ \text{the island } K,\ \text{the east island } L @@ the four dots
d(K) = 5,\ \ d(N) = 3,\ \ d(S) = 3,\ \ d(L) = 3 @@ two bridges from K to each bank, one from K to L, one from L to each bank
\text{a tour of the links enters and leaves each dot it passes through, by two different links} @@ each passing uses a pair
\text{a dot that is neither the start nor the end has an even count} @@ its links are used in pairs
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four dots have odd counts; at most two can be start and end: no tour}} \quad \blacksquare @@

42c2Counting Says Yes

Even-tour technique: a graph in one piece, with at least one link, has a closed tour of the links exactly when every dot has an even count of links.

\text{every count even; induction on the number of links, strong (4h)} @@ the one way was 42c1
\text{from a dot } u \text{, walk along unused links, never stopping while a link is free} @@ links are finite, so the walk stops
\text{the walk stops only at } u @@ entering any other dot uses one link of an even count; one is free to leave by
\text{the closed walk } W \text{ uses some links; what is left has every count even} @@ W took two at each passing, and two at u
\text{each piece of what is left has a closed tour of its links} @@ induction: fewer links
\text{each such piece meets } W @@ the graph is in one piece
\htmlClass{new}{\text{splice each piece’s tour into } W \text{ at a dot they share: a closed tour of all the links}} \quad \blacksquare @@

Open-tour technique: a graph in one piece has a tour of the links that is not closed exactly when exactly two dots have odd counts; it runs from one of them to the other. Add one new link between the two odd dots, take the closed tour that the even-tour technique gives, and take the new link out.

Counting said no to Königsberg, and here it says yes everywhere it can. A snowplough in Truckee that must clear every street once and come back, or a postal round, is a closed tour when the counts are even; when they are not, the cheapest streets to cover twice is the question that is left, and it has a recipe too.

42c3Through Every Dot Once

A cycle through every dot of G exactly once is a round of the dots. The two questions sound alike. One asks about links, one about dots. The first is settled by a count at each dot. For the second no such count is known, and finding one would answer the open question of 41h: whether a recipe that checks a proposed answer quickly can always be matched by one that finds an answer quickly. Deciding whether a graph has a round of the dots is one of the questions 41h named as hardest in that family.

A strong count still forces a round. Half-the-dots technique: a graph with n ≥ 3 dots, each with links to at least n/2 dots, has a round of the dots.

\text{any two dots not joined share a neighbour} @@ their counts add to at least n, among n − 2 other dots (pigeonhole, 8·cb5); so G is in one piece
P = v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k \text{ a longest path} @@ it exists: lengths are at most n − 1
\text{every neighbour of } v_0 \text{ and of } v_k \text{ is on } P @@ else P grows
A = \{i < k : v_0 \sim v_{i+1}\},\ \ B = \{i < k : v_k \sim v_i\},\ \ |A| + |B| \ge n > k @@ each has at least n/2 members, among the k numbers 0 to k − 1
\text{some } i \in A \cap B:\ \ v_0, v_{i+1}, v_{i+2}, \dots, v_k, v_i, v_{i-1}, \dots, v_1, v_0 \text{ is a cycle on all of } P @@ two sets too big to miss each other (pigeonhole)
\text{if a dot is off the cycle, one is joined to it; open the cycle there: a path longer than } P @@ G is in one piece
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the cycle holds every dot: a round of the dots}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against P longest

The count n/2 cannot be lowered to (n − 1)/2: two copies of Kₘ sharing one dot have 2m − 1 dots, each with at least m − 1 links, and no round, since the shared dot would be passed twice. So a count gives a yes for crowded graphs and says nothing about the thin ones, where the question lives.

The accepted names

A graph with repeated links is a multigraph. A closed tour of the links is an Eulerian circuit, an open one an Eulerian trail, and the even-tour technique is Euler's theorem (its yes half was first proved by Carl Hierholzer, published 1873). A round of the dots is a Hamiltonian cycle; the half-the-dots technique is Dirac's theorem (1952) and the weaker count of 42c.5.3 is Ore's theorem (1960). The cheapest street cover is the Chinese postman problem (Meigu Guan, 1960). Deciding whether a Hamiltonian cycle exists is NP-complete (Richard Karp, 1972).

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler wrote on the bridges of Königsberg in 1736, and gave the count without the yes half's proof. William Rowan Hamilton sold a puzzle in 1857, the Icosian game, asking for a round of the dots of the twelve-sided solid. Gabriel Andrew Dirac proved the half-the-dots technique in 1952.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every dot has an even count, so start anywhere and keep walking on unused links: you never get stuck except at the start, so the walk uses every link.” Not allowed: you get stuck only at the start, but you may get stuck there with links still unused elsewhere. The splice is the step this skips.
