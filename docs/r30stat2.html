Can You Test a Difference Without Trusting the Bell Curve?

Can you test a difference without trusting the bell curve? Most tests on rung 30 compare averages, and their chances come from the bell curve. One wild value can drag an average anywhere, and small samples give no reason to believe in a bell. Keep only the order of the numbers, and the chances can be counted exactly, by listing arrangements, whatever shape the numbers came from. This page tests with signs alone, then with ranks, counts the chances exactly, finds the bell curve again for large samples, and asks what is given up.

Replace each number by its sign, or by its rank among all the numbers. If the treatment does nothing, every pattern of signs, or every way of sharing the ranks between the groups, is equally likely, so the chance of a result at least as strong is a count over a total. For large groups the rank sum has a known average and spread, and the bell curve gives the chance.

30stat21One Wild Value

Claire tests a fertiliser on 8 pots, 4 treated and 4 not. Growth in millimetres:

growth
treated12, 15, 18, 400
untreated10, 11, 13, 14

The treated average is 111, the untreated 12. But 400 is a pot where a seed of a different plant sprouted. Drop it and the averages are 15 and 12; keep it and it decides everything. A test built on averages and a bell curve inherits that. From here on, on this page, a test is by order when it uses only which numbers are larger than which.

30stat22Signs Alone

Ten patients are measured before and after a treatment, and nine improve. If the treatment does nothing, each patient is as likely to get better as worse, by the same coin toss, separately.

\text{no effect: each sign } + \text{ or } - \text{ with chance } \tfrac12 @@ like a fair coin (30f)
\text{at least 9 of 10 plus: } \binom{10}{9} + \binom{10}{10} = 11 \text{ patterns} @@ counting (4)
11 / 2^{10} = 11/1024 \approx 0.011 @@ of 1024 equally likely
\htmlClass{new}{\text{either way: } \approx 0.021} @@ nine or more, up or down

Sign technique: if a treatment does nothing, each change is up or down with chance ½, so the chance of so many ups is a count of coin patterns, exactly. Nothing was assumed about how big the changes are or what shape they come from.

30stat23Ranks, and Every Way to Share Them

Back to the pots. Rank all 8 numbers from smallest, 1, to largest, 8. The wild 400 becomes just rank 8, one more than 18.

\text{treated ranks: } 3, 6, 7, 8;\ \text{sum } 24 @@ 12, 15, 18 and 400 among all eight
\text{no effect: every 4 of the 8 ranks equally likely} @@ shuffle the labels (30stat3)
\binom{8}{4} = 70 \text{ ways};\ \text{sum} \ge 24:\ \{3678\}, \{4578\}, \{4678\}, \{5678\} @@ list them
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chance} = 4/70 \approx 0.057} @@ exactly, whatever the shape

Rank technique: replace the numbers by their ranks; if the labels do not matter, every way of sharing the ranks is equally likely, so the chance of a rank sum so large is a count over the total. One wild value moves the result by at most one rank. Here the evidence is suggestive, not strong: 4 ways in 70.

30stat24Large Groups: the Bell Again

With 30 in each group, listing every share is out of the question. But the rank sum is a sum of many draws, and its average and spread can be found by the techniques of rung 30.

\text{each rank in a group of } n_1 \text{ averages } (N + 1)/2 @@ ranks 1 to N, equally likely
\text{average sum} = n_1 (N + 1)/2 @@ averages add (30g)
\text{spread}^2 = n_1 n_2 (N + 1)/12 @@ drawing without putting back
\htmlClass{new}{n_1 = n_2 = 4:\ \text{average } 18,\ \text{spread}^2 = 12} @@ the pots: 24 is 1.7 spreads up

For large groups, the rank sum follows the bell curve (30j) with this average and spread, whatever the numbers’ own shape, since the ranks are always 1 to N. Tests by order get their bell curve from the ranks, not from the data.

30stat25What Order Gives Up

Throwing away sizes costs something when the data really do follow a bell. The cost has been measured: how many more patients a test by order needs to see what the average test sees.

\text{bell-shaped data: ranks need about } \pi/3 \text{ times the patients} @@ about 5% more
3/\pi \approx 0.955 @@ the efficiency
\text{heavy tails or wild values: ranks need fewer} @@ sometimes far fewer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{never less than } 0.864 \text{ of the average test}} @@ whatever the shape

A test by order costs about 5% of the sample when the bell is true, and can save a great deal when it is not. The same idea gives a rank correlation, which asks whether two measurements rise together in order, not in a straight line.

Accepted names

Tests by order are nonparametric or distribution-free tests. Signs alone give the sign test; signs with the sizes ranked give the Wilcoxon signed-rank test; the rank sum is the Wilcoxon rank-sum or Mann-Whitney U test. The 3/π and 0.864 are its asymptotic relative efficiency. The rank correlation is Spearman’s rho; Kendall’s tau counts pairs in order.

Who said it first

Frank Wilcoxon published the rank tests in 1945, and Henry Mann and Donald Whitney the two-group form in 1947. Joseph Hodges and Erich Lehmann showed in 1956 that the efficiency never falls below 0.864.
