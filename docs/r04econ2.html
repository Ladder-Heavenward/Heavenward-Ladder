Can Where the Lines Fall Decide the Winner?

Can where the lines fall decide the winner? A club of 25 members chooses its committee by groups: the members are split into five groups of five, each group votes, and the side that wins more groups wins. Side X has 13 supporters and side Y has 12. Who wins should depend on the votes. This page writes each group’s result as a statement, finds that the same 25 votes split two ways make different statements true, and counts wasted votes, with rung 4’s adding, to measure how much the split, and not the votes, decided the result.

Let dᵢ be the statement “side X wins group i”. Side X wins the club when at least three of d₁ to d₅ are true: add their truth values, 1 or 0, and ask whether the sum is at least 3. A vote is wasted when it does not help its side win: every vote on the losing side of a group, and every winning vote past the three a group needs. Compare the two sides’ wasted votes.

4econ21Each Group’s Vote Is a Statement

The club splits its 25 members into five groups of five. In each group the side with more votes, 3 or more of the 5, wins that group. For group i, let dᵢ be the statement “side X wins group i”.

From here on, on this page, a split is a way of putting the 25 members into five groups of five. A split, with each member’s vote, settles each of d₁, d₂, d₃, d₄, d₅: each is true or false.

Side X has 13 supporters and side Y has 12, and nobody changes their vote on this page. Only the split changes.

4econ22The Result Is a Sum of Truth Values

Count a true statement as 1 and a false one as 0. Side X wins the club when it wins at least three groups, so the club’s result is a statement about a sum, made with 4f’s adding.

[d_i] = 1 \text{ if } d_i \text{ is true, } 0 \text{ if false} @@ a truth value as a number
w = [d_1] + [d_2] + [d_3] + [d_4] + [d_5] @@ the groups X wins, by adding (4f)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{X wins the club} \;\Leftrightarrow\; w \ge 3} @@ more than half of five

Written out with and and or, “at least three of five” is an or of every and of three letters: ten of them. The sum says the same thing in one line, and it is the line a club’s rules actually use.

4econ24Wasted Votes

A vote helps its side only if its side wins the group and the vote is one of the 3 the group needs. So call a vote wasted when it is on the losing side of its group, or when it is a winning vote past the third.

\text{group 1, 5 to 0: X wastes } 5 - 3 = 2,\ \text{Y wastes } 0 @@ past the third
\text{group 2, 4 to 1: X wastes } 1,\ \text{Y wastes } 1 @@ 
\text{group 3, 2 to 3: X wastes } 2,\ \text{Y wastes } 0 @@ all of the losing side
\text{groups 4, 5, each 1 to 4: X wastes } 1,\ \text{Y wastes } 1 @@ twice
\htmlClass{new}{\text{X wastes } 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 7,\ \text{Y wastes } 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 1 = 3} @@ adding (4f)

Do the same for split one: in each 3 to 2 group X wastes none and Y wastes 2, and in each 2 to 3 group X wastes 2 and Y none. X wastes 4 and Y wastes 6.

4econ25The Gap in Wasted Votes

A split that treats the sides alike makes them waste about the same number of votes. So take the difference in wasted votes as a share of all the votes cast.

\text{gap} = \dfrac{\text{X’s wasted} - \text{Y’s wasted}}{25} @@ one number for a split
\text{split one: } (4 - 6)/25 = -2/25 @@ X wastes fewer: the split leans to X
\htmlClass{new}{\text{split two: } (7 - 3)/25 = 4/25} @@ X wastes more: the split leans to Y

Wasted-vote technique: count each side’s wasted votes in each group and add; the difference, over all the votes, measures how far the split, and not the votes, pushed the result toward one side. A gap near 0 means the sides wasted about the same. Split two’s gap of 4 in 25 is what let 12 votes beat 13.

A club officer handed the two splits works out both gaps in a few minutes with this table, and sees at once that split two hands the club to the side with fewer votes. The count needs nothing but the votes in each group.

Accepted names

Drawing group lines to help one side is gerrymandering. Putting one side’s supporters into a few groups it wins by far is packing, and spreading them thinly where they lose is cracking. The wasted-vote gap is the efficiency gap.

Who said it first

The word gerrymander dates from 1812. Nicholas Stephanopoulos and Eric McGhee proposed the efficiency gap in 2015, in “Partisan gerrymandering and the efficiency gap”.
