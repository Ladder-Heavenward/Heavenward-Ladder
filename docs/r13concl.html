13·∎ · conclusion

Slope at a Point Is a Limit of Chord Slopes

How fast a function is changing at one input is where its chord slopes head as the second input closes in. With that limit as the definition, every technique for slopes is proved from it once and used ever after.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 13f a joint has no slope is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 13 promised a way to say how fast a function changes at a single input, where there is nothing to divide by: take a second input, and watch where the chord slopes head as it closes in. 13a gave the words point, chord and slope, named the change h, and laid down the slope at a point as a limit, with its mark f′(w). 13b proved that a slope means no gap. 13c proved the product technique from 5i's grid of cells. 13d climbed the powers by induction, then added constants, sums and every polynomial.

13e proved the chain technique with a helper that never divides by the inside's change. 13f showed that a joint has no slope unless its pieces arrive with one slope, and built the quotient technique from the product and chain techniques. This page puts the title's claim together in one place: the definition, the fact that a slope means no gap, the techniques built on both, and the plainest place where a slope is missing.

The claim, in full

The slope of f at w is the limit of the chord slopes (f(w + h) − f(w))/h as h heads to 0, and every technique for slopes on this rung follows from that one limit.

Let f be a function whose inputs and outputs are reals, defined on an open interval around w.

f has a slope at w when the chord slopes have a limit as h → 0. That limit is f′(w), and it is one real.

For a linear function x ↦ mx + b, the slope at every input is m.

If f has a slope at w, then f is continuous at w.

If f and g have slopes at w, so do f + g and fg, and (fg)′(w) = f′(w)g(w) + f(w)g′(w).

If g has a slope at w and f has a slope at g(w), then (f ∘ g)′(w) = f′(g(w)) · g′(w).

So every polynomial has a slope at every real, and a quotient of polynomials has one wherever its bottom is not 0.

Not backwards: |x| is continuous at 0 and has no slope there.

The Proof

The definition is laid down on 13a, not proved. Two things about it are proved: that it names one real, and that it agrees with the old use of “slope” for linear functions.

f'(w) = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} @@ 13a's line for the slope: the chord slope has a hole at h = 0, and the limit ignores the hole
\text{at most one real is that limit} @@ one-limit (10b)
f(x) = mx + b:\ \ \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \dfrac{mh}{h} = m @@ spread on ℝ, and the ma and b cancel; reciprocal on ℝ, licensed because h ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{f'(w) = m} \quad \blacksquare @@ agreeing-near and line-limit: slope-of-a-line, so the two uses of “slope” agree

The second part is 13b's column, in short.

0 \lt |h| \lt r:\ \ f(w + h) - f(w) = \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} \cdot h @@ multiply and divide by h: regroup for times on ℝ, reciprocal on ℝ, times-one on ℝ, licensed because h ≠ 0
\dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} \to f'(w),\ \ \ h \to 0 @@ the hypothesis; line-limit (10a)
f(w + h) - f(w) \to f'(w) \cdot 0 = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ limits-multiply (10c), with agreeing-near; anything-times-zero
\lim_{x \to w} f(x) = f(w) @@ limits-add, adding back the constant f(w); two-ways-to-write-w-limit (13a)
f \text{ is continuous at } w \quad \blacksquare @@ 11a's line: slope-means-no-gap

The third line is change-heads-to-0. The product technique spends it on its leftover cell, and the chain technique spends it to let the helper be read at a change heading to 0.

The techniques were each proved once on 13c to 13f. Here they are joined to prove one statement of the claim: a quotient of polynomials p/q has a slope at every input where its bottom q is not 0.

(x^n)'(w) = n\,w^{n-1},\ \ n \ge 1 @@ power for slopes (13d): product for slopes climbing the powers, by induction
p'(w) \text{ and } q'(w) \text{ exist} @@ polynomial-slope (13d): slopes-add, constant-factor for slopes and constant-has-slope-0, term by term
\Big(\dfrac{1}{q}\Big)'(w) = -\dfrac{q'(w)}{q(w)^2} @@ reciprocal for slopes (13f): chain for slopes with slope-of-one-over-x at q(w) ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\Big(\dfrac{p}{q}\Big)'(w) = \dfrac{p'(w)\,q(w) - p(w)\,q'(w)}{q(w)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ quotient for slopes (13f): product for slopes on p · (1/q), then one bottom

Each line leans on the ones beneath it on the rung. The quotient technique leans on the chain technique. The chain technique leans on the helper and on change-heads-to-0, and the product technique leans on change-heads-to-0 for its leftover cell. Nothing was assumed beyond the definition and rung 10's limit techniques.

h \gt 0:\ \ \dfrac{|0 + h| - |0|}{h} = \dfrac{h}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{1} @@ 8h's line for |a| by cases; reciprocal on ℝ
h \lt 0:\ \ \dfrac{|0 + h| - |0|}{h} = \dfrac{-h}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{-1} @@ |h| = −h when h < 0
\text{the chord slopes have no limit at } 0 @@ agreeing-near side by side, then two-sides-differ (10e): 1 ≠ −1
|x| \text{ is continuous at } 0 \text{ and has no slope there} \quad \blacksquare @@ size-is-continuous (11e); size-has-no-slope-at-0 (13f)

So the second part runs one way only. Continuity at a point is what a slope there pays for. It does not buy one.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

13a set beside the definition a principle of the second kind of care. Things do not jump between states. They pass through a middle that belongs to neither, as a river goes from flowing to frozen through slush.

The slope at a point is that middle, measured. Every chord has a before and an after, and the slope at w is where the chords settle as the two close in. Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after. The hole at h = 0 means the middle is never caught on its own, and the limit still measures it exactly.

That is the slope at a point, defined and put to work, with every technique proved once. You have only just begun with slopes, and there is a great deal of room ahead of you.
