44·phys · physics

What are particles built from?

What are particles built from? A smoke detector in a hallway in Seattle holds a speck of americium that has been throwing off pieces of its nuclei for decades, and will go on for centuries. Every nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, and in the 1950s accelerators turned up dozens of other heavy particles, so many that a physicist joked that finding one should earn a fine rather than a prize. The way out was symmetry. The proton and the neutron have almost the same mass and feel the nuclear pull alike, as if they were two settings of one particle. Widen that symmetry from two settings to three, and the heavy particles fall into families of eight and ten, exactly the sizes of the pieces of rung 44’s representations. One family had a gap, the mass of the missing member was predicted from the spacing, and it was found in 1964. The pieces are explained if every heavy particle is made of smaller ones, quarks, with charges in thirds. This page starts from the deeper rule that a symmetry of the laws keeps an amount, counts the families, builds particles from quarks, weighs nuclei, and checks which decays the kept amounts allow.

Each symmetry of the laws keeps an amount: a shift in time keeps energy, a slide keeps momentum, a turn of the phase of a wave keeps charge. A symmetry that turns the proton into the neutron sorts particles into pieces of its representations, of two, then of eight and ten when strangeness is added. Quarks with charges in thirds build them. A nucleus weighs less than its parts by its binding energy, and a decay must keep charge and the count of heavy particles.

43phys1A symmetry keeps an amount

29·then’s symmetry-keeps technique got momentum from the slide of a path and energy from a shift in time, and the planet’s equal areas from a turn. A third symmetry lives in the waves of 35·phys. The wave law iħ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m) ∂²ψ/∂x² + Vψ is unchanged when ψ is turned by a fixed phase, ψ ↦ e^(iθ)ψ: both sides are multiplied by the same e^(iθ). The amount that this symmetry keeps is the total of |ψ|², the chance of finding the particle somewhere, and for a charged particle its charge q times that.

Phase-keeps technique: if ψ obeys the wave law and dies away at both ends, the total of |ψ|² does not change in time, because its rate is a flow out of the ends.

\partial_t |\psi|^2 = \bar\psi\,\partial_t\psi + \psi\,\partial_t\bar\psi @@ |ψ|² = ψ̄ψ, the product rule
= \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\bar\psi\,\psi'' - \psi\,\bar\psi''\big) @@ the wave law and its flip; the Vψ terms cancel because V is real
= -\partial_x j,\ \ j = \tfrac{\hbar}{2mi}\big(\bar\psi\,\psi' - \psi\,\bar\psi'\big) @@ the derivative of j gives back the line above; the ψ̄′ψ′ terms cancel
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{d}{dt}\textstyle\int |\psi|^2\,dx = -\big[j\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ 16’s undoing, with ψ dying away at the ends
  
\psi = A e^{ikx}:\ \ j = \tfrac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2 = v|A|^2 @@ the chance flows at the particle’s speed
\text{an electron of } 1 \text{ eV}:\ v = \sqrt{2E/m} = \sqrt{2 \cdot 1.602 \times 10^{-19} / 9.109 \times 10^{-31}} @@ ½mv² = E
\htmlClass{new}{v \approx 5.93 \times 10^5 \text{ m/s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ times the charge −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and |A|² per metre, it is a current in amperes

So three symmetries, three kept amounts: shift in time, energy; slide in place, momentum; turn of phase, charge. Each is a law, measured never to fail; the symmetry says why it holds, and a failure of the symmetry would show as a failure of the law.

43phys2Two settings of one particle

The proton weighs 938.272 MeV (in energy units, mc²) and the neutron 939.565 MeV, a difference of 0.14%. Nuclei with protons and neutrons swapped, like tritium (one proton, two neutrons) and helium-3 (two protons, one neutron), are bound by 8.482 and 7.718 MeV. Treat the proton and neutron as the up and down arrows of a two-direction space, like 38·phys’s spin, and suppose the nuclear pull is unchanged by every grid of ℂ² that keeps lengths and has determinant 1, written SU(2). That is isospin. Its pieces, by 44·cb27, have 1, 2, 3, 4, … members; a piece of 2I + 1 members has isospin I.

8.482 - 7.718 = 0.764 \text{ MeV} @@ helium-3 is less bound: its two protons push each other
\tfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \tfrac{1.44 \text{ MeV fm}}{r} @@ the electric energy of two protons r apart (7·phys), with 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m
r \approx 1.9 \text{ fm}:\ \ \tfrac{1.44}{1.9} \approx 0.76 \text{ MeV} @@ the size of helium-3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the whole difference is the electric push: the nuclear pull is the same}} \quad \blacksquare @@ isospin is a symmetry of the nuclear pull, broken by charge

The three pions, π⁺ at 139.570, π⁰ at 134.977 and π⁻ at 139.570 MeV, are a piece of 3: isospin 1. The proton and neutron are a piece of 2: isospin ½. Isospin is kept by the nuclear pull and not by the electric push, so it is an amount that keeps nearly, to the size of the push.

43phys3Families of eight and ten

In the 1950s a new kept amount turned up, strangeness, kept by the nuclear pull and not by the weak one that makes slow decays. Particles with strangeness, like the Λ at 1115.7 MeV, are made fast and decay slowly. Widen SU(2) to SU(3), the length-keeping grids of ℂ³ with determinant 1, with the third direction strange. Its smallest pieces have 1, 3, 8 and 10 members, and the light heavy particles fit them exactly: the proton and neutron sit in a piece of 8 with six others; the Δ’s, Σ*’s, Ξ*’s and one more sit in a piece of 10.

\Delta:\ 1232,\ \ \Sigma^*:\ 1384.6,\ \ \Xi^*:\ 1531.8 \text{ MeV} @@ strangeness 0, −1, −2: rows of 4, 3, 2
1384.6 - 1232 = 152.6,\ \ 1531.8 - 1384.6 = 147.2 @@ nearly even steps, one strange quark’s extra mass each
\text{the row of 1 at strangeness } {-3}:\ 1531.8 + 150 \approx 1682 \text{ MeV} @@ the next step
\htmlClass{new}{\text{found in 1964 at } 1672.45 \text{ MeV, within 0.6\%}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the Ω⁻, with charge −1 and strangeness −3

The rows of 4, 3, 2, 1 make a triangle of 10: that shape is the piece, and no other shape of SU(3)’s pieces has 10 members in those rows. The prediction was from the representation alone, before anyone knew what the particles were made of.

43phys4Quarks, and a third label

The piece of 3 of SU(3) is not seen as particles. Suppose it is what they are made of: three quarks, up u, down d and strange s, with charges +2/3, −1/3 and −1/3. A heavy particle like the proton is three quarks; a pion is a quark with an antiquark, of opposite charge. The pieces of three of 3s split as 27 = 10 + 8 + 8 + 1, and a quark with an antiquark as 9 = 8 + 1, by 44·cb27’s splitting technique.

p = uud:\ \ \tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = 1 @@ the proton
n = udd:\ \ \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0 @@ the neutron
\Omega^- = sss:\ \ -1;\ \ \Delta^{++} = uuu:\ \ +2 @@ the ends of the piece of 10
\htmlClass{new}{\pi^+ = u\bar d:\ \ \tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the antiquark d̄ has charge +1/3

The Δ⁺⁺ is three up quarks with their spins all up, in the same orbital: three identical particles in one state. 38·phys’s swap sign says that state is zero. Unless each quark carries a third label with three values, so that the three can differ in it, the Δ⁺⁺ cannot exist, and it does. The label is called colour, a name with nothing to do with rung 36, and its symmetry is another SU(3), exact this time. Quarks are never seen alone.

43phys5What holds a nucleus together

A nucleus weighs less than its protons and neutrons apart. The missing mass, times c², is the binding energy B, the energy needed to pull it apart.

\text{deuteron: } 938.272 + 939.565 - 1875.613 = 2.224 \text{ MeV} @@ one proton, one neutron
\text{helium-4: } 2(938.272) + 2(939.565) - 3727.379 = 28.295 \text{ MeV} @@ 7.07 MeV for each of its 4 pieces
\htmlClass{new}{B(A, Z) \approx 15.8A - 18.3A^{2/3} - 0.714\tfrac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}} - 23.2\tfrac{(A - 2Z)^2}{A} \text{ MeV}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a drop of nuclear matter: the bulk, less the surface, less the push of Z protons, less the cost of unequal numbers
  
\text{iron-56}, A = 56, Z = 26:\ \ 884.8 - 267.9 - 126.2 - 6.6 = 484.2 \text{ MeV} @@ 56^(2/3) ≈ 14.64, 56^(1/3) ≈ 3.826
484.2/56 \approx 8.65 \text{ MeV each;\ measured } 492.3,\ 8.79 @@ within 2%
\text{uranium-238}, A = 238, Z = 92:\ \ B \approx 1798 \text{ MeV},\ 7.56 \text{ each} @@ the push of 92 protons
\htmlClass{new}{\text{split into two of } A \approx 119:\ \text{about } (8.5 - 7.6) \times 238 \approx 200 \text{ MeV released}} \quad \blacksquare @@ fission; joining light nuclei toward iron releases energy too

43phys6Which decays are allowed

A decay must keep energy, momentum and charge, by the symmetries of footing 1. Two counts are also kept in every reaction ever measured: the heavy count, the number of three-quark particles less the number of their antiparticles, and the light count, of electrons and their neutrinos less their antiparticles. These are laws, from guessing under the small case, with no known exception.

n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu @@ one d quark becomes a u
\text{charge } 0 = 1 - 1 + 0;\ \text{heavy } 1 = 1;\ \text{light } 0 = 1 - 1 @@ all kept
939.565 - 938.272 - 0.511 = 0.782 \text{ MeV} @@ the energy shared by the electron and the antineutrino
\htmlClass{new}{\tau \approx 879 \text{ s}:\ \ e^{-3600/879} \approx 0.017 \text{ left after an hour}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 17’s decay; half-life τ ln 2 ≈ 609 s
  
p \to e^+ + \pi^0 @@ charge 1 = 1 + 0, energy allowed
\text{heavy count } 1 \to 0 @@ broken
\htmlClass{new}{\text{not seen: the proton lives longer than } 10^{34} \text{ years}} \quad \blacksquare @@ tanks of 50 000 tonnes of water, watched for decades

Americium-241 in the smoke detector throws off helium-4 nuclei with a half-life of 432 years, and in 50 years e^(−50 ln 2/432) ≈ 92% of it is left. What made a symmetry from the heavy count, if anything, is open.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

That every continuous symmetry of the action keeps an amount is Noether’s theorem; the kept amount of the phase is the conserved current of a U(1) symmetry, and j is the probability current. Isospin is isotopic spin; the families of 8 and 10 are the baryon octet and decuplet, and the scheme is the eightfold way. The heavy and light counts are baryon number and lepton number. The mass rule is the semi-empirical mass formula of Weizsäcker and Bethe; colour and its SU(3) are quantum chromodynamics. The weak pull is the weak interaction.

Who said it first

Emmy Noether proved the link between symmetries and kept amounts in 1918. Werner Heisenberg treated the proton and neutron as two states of one particle in 1932, and Eugene Wigner named isospin in 1937. Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne’eman proposed the eightfold way in 1961, and Gell-Mann predicted the Ω⁻ in 1962; it was found at Brookhaven in 1964. Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed quarks in 1964, and Oscar Greenberg colour the same year. Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker gave the mass rule in 1935. Enrico Fermi’s theory of beta decay dates from 1933–34. The proton limits are from the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan.

A proof that is not allowed

“The wave law is unchanged by ψ ↦ e^(iθ)ψ, so ψ itself does not change in time.” Not allowed: the symmetry keeps the total of |ψ|², not ψ. The wave moves and spreads; only how much of it there is stays fixed.
