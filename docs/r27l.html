27l · technique

Eigenvectors by Hand

The stretch test turns the hunt for the vectors a grid only stretches into two smaller jobs. First find the stretches, as the roots of one polynomial. Then, for each stretch, find the vectors it loses when that stretch is taken off its middle, by elimination. For a grid of two rows the polynomial needs only two numbers: the sum down the middle and the determinant. Once the vectors are found, a grid done a hundred times is no harder than a grid done once, because in their directions it only stretches. The largest stretch wins in the long run, and that decides where a population settles. And the rabbit counts of an earlier rung, each the sum of the two before, are the powers of one small grid. Its two stretches are the two numbers in their closed form.

The stretches of a grid are the roots of its stretch polynomial, and for a grid of two rows that polynomial is read off the sum down the middle and the determinant. The vectors come from elimination. Written in those vectors, a high power of a grid is a power of each stretch, and the largest stretch wins. The rabbit counts are powers of one grid.

27l1Stretches From the Middle and the Determinant

The middle sum of a square grid is a₁₁ + a₂₂ + ⋯ + aₙₙ, the sum down its middle.

Middle-sum technique: det(A − λI) = (−λ)ⁿ + (a₁₁ + ⋯ + aₙₙ)(−λ)ⁿ⁻¹ + ⋯ + det A. So the n stretches of A in ℂ, each counted as often as its factor appears, add to the middle sum and multiply to det A. For a 2 × 2 grid the stretches are the roots of λ² − (a₁₁ + a₂₂)λ + det A. And P⁻¹AP has the same stretch polynomial as A, for every undoable P. Here λ (little lambda) means a number tried as a stretch of A.

\det(A - \lambda I) = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}\sigma\prod_j\big(a_{\sigma(j)j} - \lambda\,[\sigma(j){=}j]\big) @@ the three-conditions formula (27e2), as in the stretch-test technique (27f1)
\sigma \ne \text{doing nothing moves at least two places} @@ a shuffle that moves one place moves the place it lands on
\text{so } \lambda^n,\ \lambda^{n-1} \text{ come only from } (a_{11} - \lambda)\cdots(a_{nn} - \lambda) @@ every other term has at most n − 2 factors holding λ
\det(A - \lambda I) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n - \lambda) @@ the root technique (25d), with first number (−1)ⁿ
\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n = a_{11} + \cdots + a_{nn},\ \ \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n = \det A @@ compare the λⁿ⁻¹ and the plain numbers (9g); at λ = 0 the left side is det A

27l2High Powers, and the Largest Stretch

Leading-stretch technique: if A, n × n, only stretches the free vectors v₁, …, vₙ, by λ₁, …, λₙ, and x = c₁v₁ + ⋯ + cₙvₙ, then Aᵏx = c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + ⋯ + cₙλₙᵏvₙ. If |λ₁| is larger than every other |λᵢ|, and c₁ ≠ 0, then Aᵏx/λ₁ᵏ heads to c₁v₁ as k grows.

A^k v_i = \lambda_i^k v_i @@ induction on k (4c): A(λᵢᵏvᵢ) = λᵢᵏAvᵢ = λᵢᵏ⁺¹vᵢ
A^kx = c_1\lambda_1^kv_1 + \cdots + c_n\lambda_n^kv_n @@ Aᵏ keeps mixes (27c1)
\tfrac{A^kx}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1v_1 + c_2\big(\tfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_2 + \cdots + c_n\big(\tfrac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_n @@ divide by λ₁ᵏ ≠ 0
\big|\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\big|^k \to 0 \text{ for } i \ge 2 @@ the standard-sequences technique (22h2): rᵏ → 0 for |r| < 1
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{A^kx}{\lambda_1^k} \to c_1v_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ place by place, every other piece heads to 0
  
A = PDP^{-1},\ P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix},\ D = \begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} @@ the vectors of 27l1 as columns; the straighten-out technique (27f3)
P^{-1} = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} @@ the 2 × 2 undo, by stand-ins (27g2): divide by det P = −3
A^k = PD^kP^{-1} = \tfrac{5^k}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{2^k}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-2&2\end{pmatrix} @@ multiply out, the 5ᵏ and 2ᵏ parts apart
\htmlClass{new}{A^{10} = \tfrac{5^{10}}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{2^{10}}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ check k = 1: the sum is A; for large k the 5ᵏ part wins, as the leading-stretch technique says

A grid that cannot be straightened needs another road. The slide with rows (1, 1), (0, 1) has only the stretch 1, and its powers are not powers of 1: by induction its k-th power has rows (1, k), (0, 1). Its powers grow, slowly, along the floor.

27l3The Rabbit Counts Again

Step-grid technique: if uₙ₊₁ = p uₙ + q uₙ₋₁ for every n ≥ 1, then (uₙ₊₁, uₙ) = Mⁿ(u₁, u₀), where M has rows (p, q), (1, 0). The stretches of M are the roots of λ² = pλ + q, and when they are two different numbers λ and μ, uₙ = cλⁿ + dμⁿ for two numbers c, d fixed by u₀ and u₁.

(u_{n+1}, u_n) = M(u_n, u_{n-1}) = \cdots = M^n(u_1, u_0) @@ row 1 is the technique; row 2 copies uₙ; induction (4c)
\det(M - \lambda I) = (p - \lambda)(-\lambda) - q = \lambda^2 - p\lambda - q @@ the middle-sum technique (27l1)
M(\lambda, 1) = (p\lambda + q, \lambda) = (\lambda^2, \lambda) = \lambda(\lambda, 1) @@ λ² = pλ + q
(u_1, u_0) = c(\lambda, 1) + d(\mu, 1) @@ different stretches give free vectors (27f2); two free vectors build the plane (27b3)
(u_{n+1}, u_n) = c\lambda^n(\lambda, 1) + d\mu^n(\mu, 1) @@ the leading-stretch technique (27l2), first sentence
\htmlClass{new}{u_n = c\lambda^n + d\mu^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ read the second place
  
F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1},\ \ R = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} @@ p = q = 1
\lambda^2 = \lambda + 1:\ \ \varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2},\ \ \psi = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt5}{2} @@ the stretches of R
(F_1, F_0) = (1, 0) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\big((\varphi, 1) - (\psi, 1)\big) @@ φ − ψ = √5
\htmlClass{new}{F_n = \tfrac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the step-grid technique: the rabbit closed-form technique of 23j2, reached without storing the list

The grid gives more than the closed form. Rⁿ has rows (Fₙ₊₁, Fₙ), (Fₙ, Fₙ₋₁), by induction. Its det is (det R)ⁿ = (−1)ⁿ by the det-product technique, so Fₙ₊₁Fₙ₋₁ − Fₙ² = (−1)ⁿ for every n ≥ 1: at n = 10, 89 · 34 − 55² = 3026 − 3025 = 1. Neighbouring rabbit counts nearly make a square, and miss by exactly one.

27l4AB and BA Have the Same Middle Sum

Write s(A) for the middle sum of a square grid A. AB and BA are mostly different grids (27d7). Their middle sums never differ.

Swap-sum technique: s(AB) = s(BA) whenever A is m × n and B is n × m. So s(P⁻¹AP) = s(A) for every undoable P, and AB − BA is never I for n × n grids of numbers.

s(AB) = \textstyle\sum_i (AB)_{ii} = \sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}b_{ji} @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d2)
s(BA) = \textstyle\sum_j (BA)_{jj} = \sum_j\sum_i b_{ji}a_{ij} @@ the same, for BA
s(AB) = s(BA) @@ the same products, added in another order (5d)
s(P^{-1}AP) = s\big((AP)P^{-1}\big) = s(A) @@ swap the two factors P⁻¹ and AP
s(X + Y) = s(X) + s(Y),\ \ s(cX) = c\,s(X) @@ s is a reading on the grids (27h1)
\htmlClass{new}{s(AB - BA) = 0 \neq n = s(I)} \quad \blacksquare @@ so AB − BA = I cannot be

So the middle sum belongs to a map and not to the basis it is read in, as the determinant does (27e5): every grid of the map has the same one. With A = rows (0, 1), (0, 0) and B = rows (0, 0), (1, 0), AB − BA has rows (1, 0), (0, −1): not 0, but its middle sum is 0.

On a space with no finite basis the story changes. On the polynomials (27b6), let D take the slope and X multiply by x. Then D(X(p)) − X(D(p)) = (xp)′ − xp′ = p for every p: DX − XD is doing nothing. No grids can copy that, by the technique, and none have to: those maps have no grids.

27l5The Only Reading That Lets Products Swap

The middle sum is a reading on the n × n grids with s(AB) = s(BA) (27l4). Is there any other? Write Eᵢⱼ for the grid with 1 in place i, j and 0 everywhere else. Then EᵢⱼEₖₗ is Eᵢₗ when j = k, and the zero grid otherwise.

Only-reading technique: if f is a reading on the n × n grids with f(AB) = f(BA) for all A and B, then f = c · s for one number c.

i \neq j:\ E_{ij} = E_{ii}E_{ij},\ \ E_{ij}E_{ii} = 0 @@ the products of the Eᵢⱼ
f(E_{ij}) = f(E_{ii}E_{ij}) = f(E_{ij}E_{ii}) = f(0) = 0 @@ f lets products swap; a reading is 0 at 0
E_{ii} = E_{ij}E_{ji},\ \ E_{jj} = E_{ji}E_{ij} @@ the products again
f(E_{ii}) = f(E_{jj}) = c @@ one number c for every place down the middle
A = \textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}E_{ij} @@ the Eᵢⱼ are a basis of the grids (27d6)
\htmlClass{new}{f(A) = \textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}f(E_{ij}) = c\,s(A)} \quad \blacksquare @@ f is straight; only the places i, i are left

The same products show what the grids AB − BA build. Each Eᵢⱼ off the middle is EᵢᵢEᵢⱼ − EᵢⱼEᵢᵢ, and each Eᵢᵢ − Eⱼⱼ is EᵢⱼEⱼᵢ − EⱼᵢEᵢⱼ. Together those build every grid of middle sum 0, a space of dimension n² − 1 (27c2: s loses it and reaches the numbers).

Every reading on the grids comes from the middle sum too. Put bᵢⱼ = f(Eᵢⱼ). Then f(A) is the sum of all aᵢⱼbᵢⱼ, which is s(BᵀA). And s(AᵀA) is the sum of the squares of the entries of A, so for a real grid it is 0 only when A = 0.

27l6A Small Push Away From I

I scales every volume by 1. Push it a little: I + tA, for a small number t. How fast does the volume scale leave 1?

Push-from-I technique: det(I + tA) = 1 + s(A) t + ⋯ + det A · tⁿ, a polynomial in t. It is (1 + tλ₁)⋯(1 + tλₙ), with λ₁, …, λₙ the stretches of A in ℂ. Here s(A) is the middle sum (27l4).

\det(A - \lambda I) = (-\lambda)^n + s(A)(-\lambda)^{n-1} + \cdots + \det A @@ the middle-sum technique (27l1)
\det\big(A + \tfrac1tI\big) = \tfrac{1}{t^n} + \tfrac{s(A)}{t^{n-1}} + \cdots + \det A @@ put λ = −1/t, for t ≠ 0
\det(I + tA) = t^n\det\big(A + \tfrac1tI\big) @@ det is straight in each of its n columns (27e1)
\det(I + tA) = 1 + s(A)\,t + \cdots + \det A\ t^n @@ multiply by tⁿ; at t = 0 both sides are 1
\htmlClass{new}{= (1 + t\lambda_1)\cdots(1 + t\lambda_n)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same, from det(A − λI) = (λ₁ − λ)⋯(λₙ − λ) (27l1)

For the grid with rows (4, 1), (2, 3): det(I + tA) = (1 + 4t)(1 + 3t) − 2t² = 1 + 7t + 10t², and that is (1 + 5t)(1 + 2t). The slope at t = 0 is s(A). So a small push tA changes volumes by about s(A) t, and the entries off the middle do not count at first. A push with middle sum 0 keeps volumes, as nearly as a first guess can tell.

The accepted names

The middle sum is the trace, tr A; the middle-sum technique says the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant. A grid P⁻¹AP is similar to A, and similar matrices share their characteristic polynomial. The leading-stretch technique is the idea behind the power method, and the largest stretch is the dominant eigenvalue. A rule uₙ₊₁ = puₙ + quₙ₋₁ is a linear recurrence, and M is its companion matrix. Fₙ₊₁Fₙ₋₁ − Fₙ² = (−1)ⁿ is Cassini’s identity. A grid of shares moving between places, like the two towns, is a stochastic (or Markov) matrix, and the vector it heads to is its steady state. AB − BA is the commutator of A and B. The Eᵢⱼ are the matrix units.

Who said it first

Giovanni Domenico Cassini found his identity in 1680. Abraham de Moivre found the closed form of the rabbit counts in 1730, and Jacques Binet rediscovered it in 1843. Andrey Markov studied chains of shares that move step by step from 1906. Richard von Mises and Hilda Pollaczek-Geiringer described the power method in 1929.
