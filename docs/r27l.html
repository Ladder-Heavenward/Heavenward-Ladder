27l · technique

Eigenvectors by hand

The stretch test turns the hunt for the arrows a grid only stretches into two smaller jobs. First find the stretches, as the roots of one polynomial. Then, for each stretch, find the arrows it loses when that stretch is taken off its middle, by elimination. For a grid of two rows the polynomial needs only two numbers: the sum down the middle and the determinant. Once the arrows are found, a grid done a hundred times is no harder than a grid done once, because in their directions it only stretches. The largest stretch wins in the long run, and that decides where a population settles. And the rabbit counts of an earlier rung, each the sum of the two before, are the powers of one small grid. Its two stretches are the two numbers in their closed form.

The stretches of a grid are the roots of its stretch polynomial, and for a grid of two rows that polynomial is read off the sum down the middle and the determinant. The arrows come from elimination. Written in those arrows, a high power of a grid is a power of each stretch, and the largest stretch wins. The rabbit counts are powers of one grid.

27l1Stretches from the middle and the determinant

Stands on 27f1 (the stretch-test technique), 27e2 (the three-rules technique), 27e3 (the det-product technique), 25d (the root technique), 9g (the same-coefficients technique) and 27j2 (elimination).

The middle sum of a square grid is a₁₁ + a₂₂ + ⋯ + uₙₙ, the sum down its middle.

Middle-sum technique: det(A − λI) = (−λ)ⁿ + (a₁₁ + ⋯ + uₙₙ)(−λ)ⁿ⁻¹ + ⋯ + det A. So the n stretches of A in ℂ, each counted as often as its factor appears, add to the middle sum and multiply to det A. For a 2 × 2 grid the stretches are the roots of λ² − (a₁₁ + a₂₂)λ + det A. And P⁻¹AP has the same stretch polynomial as A, for every undoable P.

\det(A - \lambda I) = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}\sigma\prod_j\big(a_{\sigma(j)j} - \lambda\,[\sigma(j){=}j]\big) @@ the three-rules formula (27e2), as in the stretch-test technique (27f1)
\sigma \ne \text{doing nothing moves at least two places} @@ a shuffle that moves one place moves the place it lands on
\text{so } \lambda^n,\ \lambda^{n-1} \text{ come only from } (a_{11} - \lambda)\cdots(a_{nn} - \lambda) @@ every other term has at most n − 2 factors holding λ
\det(A - \lambda I) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n - \lambda) @@ the root technique (25d), with first number (−1)ⁿ
\lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_n = a_{11} + \cdots + a_{nn},\ \ \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n = \det A @@ compare the λⁿ⁻¹ and the plain numbers (9g); at λ = 0 the left side is det A
\htmlClass{new}{\det(P^{-1}AP - \lambda I) = \det\big(P^{-1}(A - \lambda I)P\big) = \det(A - \lambda I)} \quad \blacksquare @@ λI = P⁻¹λIP; the det-product technique (27e3), with det P⁻¹ det P = 1
  
A = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}:\ \ \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) @@ middle sum 7, det 12 − 2 = 10
\lambda = 5:\ \ A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ v = (1, 1) @@ lowest terms (27j2): x = y, y free
\lambda = 2:\ \ A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix}1&\tfrac12\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ v = (1, -2) @@ x = −y/2; take y = −2

27l2High powers, and the largest stretch

Stands on 27f3 (the straighten-out technique), 27l1, 22h2 (the standard-sequences technique) and 4b (induction).

Leading-stretch technique: if A, n × n, only stretches the free arrows v₁, …, vₙ, by λ₁, …, λₙ, and x = c₁v₁ + ⋯ + cₙvₙ, then Aᵏx = c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + ⋯ + cₙλₙᵏvₙ. If |λ₁| is larger than every other |λᵢ|, and c₁ ≠ 0, then Aᵏx/λ₁ᵏ heads to c₁v₁ as k grows.

A^k v_i = \lambda_i^k v_i @@ induction on k (4b): A(λᵢᵏvᵢ) = λᵢᵏAvᵢ = λᵢᵏ⁺¹vᵢ
A^kx = c_1\lambda_1^kv_1 + \cdots + c_n\lambda_n^kv_n @@ Aᵏ keeps mixes (27c1)
\tfrac{A^kx}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1v_1 + c_2\big(\tfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_2 + \cdots + c_n\big(\tfrac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_n @@ divide by λ₁ᵏ ≠ 0
\big|\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\big|^k \to 0 \text{ for } i \ge 2 @@ the standard-sequences technique (22h2): rᵏ → 0 for |r| < 1
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{A^kx}{\lambda_1^k} \to c_1v_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ place by place, every other piece heads to 0
  
A = PDP^{-1},\ P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix},\ D = \begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} @@ the arrows of 27l1 as columns; the straighten-out technique (27f3)
P^{-1} = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} @@ the 2 × 2 undo, by stand-ins (27g2): divide by det P = −3
A^k = PD^kP^{-1} = \tfrac{5^k}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{2^k}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-2&2\end{pmatrix} @@ multiply out, the 5ᵏ and 2ᵏ parts apart
\htmlClass{new}{A^{10} = \tfrac{5^{10}}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{2^{10}}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ check k = 1: the sum is A; for large k the 5ᵏ part wins, as the leading-stretch technique says

A grid that cannot be straightened needs another road. The slide with rows (1, 1), (0, 1) has only the stretch 1, and its powers are not powers of 1: by induction its k-th power has rows (1, k), (0, 1). Its powers grow, slowly, along the floor.

27l3The rabbit counts again

Stands on 27l2, 27f2 (the different-stretches technique), 27e3 and 23j2 (the rabbit closed-form technique).

Step-grid technique: if uₙ₊₁ = p uₙ + q uₙ₋₁ for every n ≥ 1, then (uₙ₊₁, uₙ) = Mⁿ(u₁, u₀), where M has rows (p, q), (1, 0). The stretches of M are the roots of λ² = pλ + q, and when they are two different numbers λ and μ, uₙ = cλⁿ + dμⁿ for two numbers c, d fixed by u₀ and u₁.

(u_{n+1}, u_n) = M(u_n, u_{n-1}) = \cdots = M^n(u_1, u_0) @@ row 1 is the rule; row 2 copies uₙ; induction (4b)
\det(M - \lambda I) = (p - \lambda)(-\lambda) - q = \lambda^2 - p\lambda - q @@ the middle-sum technique (27l1)
M(\lambda, 1) = (p\lambda + q, \lambda) = (\lambda^2, \lambda) = \lambda(\lambda, 1) @@ λ² = pλ + q
(u_1, u_0) = c(\lambda, 1) + d(\mu, 1) @@ different stretches give free arrows (27f2); two free arrows build the plane (27b3)
(u_{n+1}, u_n) = c\lambda^n(\lambda, 1) + d\mu^n(\mu, 1) @@ the leading-stretch technique (27l2), first sentence
\htmlClass{new}{u_n = c\lambda^n + d\mu^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ read the second place
  
F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1},\ \ R = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} @@ p = q = 1
\lambda^2 = \lambda + 1:\ \ \varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2},\ \ \psi = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt5}{2} @@ the stretches of R
(F_1, F_0) = (1, 0) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\big((\varphi, 1) - (\psi, 1)\big) @@ φ − ψ = √5
\htmlClass{new}{F_n = \tfrac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the step-grid technique: the rabbit closed-form technique of 23j2, reached without storing the list

The grid gives more than the closed form. Rⁿ has rows (Fₙ₊₁, Fₙ), (Fₙ, Fₙ₋₁), by induction. Its det is (det R)ⁿ = (−1)ⁿ by the det-product technique, so Fₙ₊₁Fₙ₋₁ − Fₙ² = (−1)ⁿ for every n ≥ 1: at n = 10, 89 · 34 − 55² = 3026 − 3025 = 1. Neighbouring rabbit counts nearly make a square, and miss by exactly one.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The middle sum is the trace, tr A; the middle-sum technique says the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant. A grid P⁻¹AP is similar to A, and similar matrices share their characteristic polynomial. The leading-stretch technique is the idea behind the power method, and the largest stretch is the dominant eigenvalue. A rule uₙ₊₁ = puₙ + quₙ₋₁ is a linear recurrence, and M is its companion matrix. Fₙ₊₁Fₙ₋₁ − Fₙ² = (−1)ⁿ is Cassini’s identity. A grid of shares moving between places, like the two towns, is a stochastic (or Markov) matrix, and the arrow it heads to is its steady state.

Who said it first

Giovanni Domenico Cassini found his identity in 1680. Abraham de Moivre found the closed form of the rabbit counts in 1730, and Jacques Binet rediscovered it in 1843. Andrey Markov studied chains of shares that move step by step from 1906. Richard von Mises and Hilda Pollaczek-Geiringer described the power method in 1929.
