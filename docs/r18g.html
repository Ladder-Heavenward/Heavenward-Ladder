18g · technique

Lines in Coordinates

18c proved what a line is. This step is about writing lines down and using them: the five usual ways to give one, which side of it a point is on, the regions cut out by several, and the best point of such a region. Then it proves, by a line or two of algebra each, facts about triangles and four-sided figures that take the old geometry a page: the diagonals of a parallelogram, the middles of any four sides, the three medians and the three heights of a triangle, and the area of any triangle from its corners.

A line rises by its slope times how far it runs from a known point. The sign by which a point misses a line’s equation says which side of the line it is on. On a segment, a first-degree function is largest at an end, so on a region cut out by lines it is largest at a corner. Midpoints are averages. The area of a triangle is half the size of a cross product of two of its side vectors.

18g1Five Ways to Write a Line

Every line is αx + βy = c with (α, β) ≠ (0, 0) by two-descriptions-agree, and the other forms are this one rearranged. Each has an accepted name. Slope-intercept: y = mx + q, with slope m and y-intercept q, for every line that is not x = constant. Point-slope: y − y₀ = m(x − x₀), the line through (x₀, y₀) with slope m. Two-point: through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) with x₁ ≠ x₂, take m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) and use point-slope; line-through-two-points says it is the only one. Intercept: x/p + y/q = 1, through (p, 0) and (0, q). Standard, or general: αx + βy = c.

18g2Sides of a Line

For a line ℓ: αx + βy = c, write f(X) = αx + βy − c, so ℓ is where f = 0. Sides-of-a-line technique: for points P and Q not on ℓ, the segment from P to Q meets ℓ exactly when f(P) and f(Q) have opposite signs. Moving from any point along (α, β) raises f. The two sets {f > 0} and {f < 0} have the accepted name half-planes, and (α, β) points into the first.

f(P + t(Q - P)) = (1 - t)f(P) + tf(Q) @@ adding-keeps-answers: f is of the first degree, so the c splits as (1 − t)c + tc
\text{a zero with } 0 \lt t \lt 1 \iff f(P), f(Q) \text{ of opposite signs} @@ the only zero is t = f(P)/(f(P) − f(Q)), in (0, 1) exactly then
\htmlClass{new}{f(P + s(\alpha, \beta)) = f(P) + s(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ adding-keeps-answers; α² + β² > 0

So a set cut out by several inequalities of the first degree has a simple shape. If X and Y both satisfy αx + βy ≤ c, so does every point between, by sides-of-a-line's first line, and so for any number of such inequalities. A set with that property, that it holds the whole segment between any two of its points, has the accepted name convex. When it is bounded, it is a polygon, and its corners are where two of its edge lines meet (two-lines-meet).

Best-corner technique: on a bounded convex polygon, a function g(x, y) = px + qy takes its largest and its smallest values at corners. On a segment, g is (1 − t)g(P) + tg(Q) by the same line as sides-of-a-line, so it is never beyond both ends. Every point of the polygon lies on a segment between two edge points, from any corner through it; and every edge point lies between two corners. So no point beats every corner. The accepted name for finding that corner is linear programming; a way to walk from corner to better corner was given in 1947.

x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0,\ x + y \le 40,\ x + 3y \le 60 @@ the corners: (0, 0), (40, 0), (30, 10), (0, 20)
g = 300x + 500y:\ \ 0,\ 12\,000,\ 14\,000,\ 10\,000 @@ best-corner: the best is at (30, 10)

18g3Midpoints Are Averages

By part-of-the-way the midpoint of a segment from A to B is (A + B)/2, and the point a fraction t of the way is (1 − t)A + tB. That one fact proves four techniques.

Parallelogram-diagonals technique: the diagonals of a parallelogram ABCD meet at the midpoint of each. Conversely, a four-sided figure whose diagonals meet at the midpoint of each is a parallelogram. By parallelogram, D = A + C − B, so the midpoint of BD is (B + A + C − B)/2 = (A + C)/2, the midpoint of AC. Conversely, (A + C)/2 = (B + D)/2 gives D − A = C − B and D − C = A − B, so both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.

Midpoint-parallelogram technique: for any four points A, B, C, D, the midpoints of AB, BC, CD and DA are the corners of a parallelogram, with sides parallel to the diagonals AC and BD and half as long. The midpoints of BC and AB differ by (C − A)/2, and so do the midpoints of CD and DA; the other pair differ by (D − B)/2.

The segment from a corner of a triangle to the middle of the opposite side has the accepted name median. Centroid technique: the three medians of a triangle meet in one point, G = (A + B + C)/3, two thirds of the way along each from its corner. The point two thirds of the way from A to (B + C)/2 is A/3 + (2/3)(B + C)/2 = (A + B + C)/3, and the letters enter alike. G has the accepted name centroid.

A line through a corner perpendicular to the opposite side's line has the accepted name height, or altitude. The rung's survey promised that the three heights meet in one point, and that the claim is one of algebra. Heights-meet technique: the three heights of a triangle meet in one point H.

(X - A)\cdot(B - C) + (X - B)\cdot(C - A) + (X - C)\cdot(A - B) = 0 @@ adding-keeps-answers and the dot technique: for every X, the X terms add to X · 0, and the rest cancel in pairs
\text{the heights from } A, B \text{ meet at one } H @@ their normals B − C and C − A are not parallel (else A, B, C collinear); two-lines-meet
\htmlClass{new}{(H - C)\cdot(A - B) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first two terms vanish at H; so H is on the height from C (perpendicular-lines)

H has the accepted name orthocentre. A triangle now has four centres from this rung: the circumcentre O of 18d, the incentre I, the centroid G and the orthocentre H.

18g4The Area From the Corners

Triangle-area technique: for a triangle with u = B − A and v = C − A, the area is ½|u₁v₂ − u₂v₁|. With corners (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃) it is ½|x₁y₂ − x₂y₁ + x₂y₃ − x₃y₂ + x₃y₁ − x₁y₃|, and it equals ½ · |AB| · d(C, line AB).

\text{below a side from } (x_i, y_i) \text{ to } (x_j, y_j):\ \ \tfrac12 (y_i + y_j)(x_j - x_i) @@ area-between: the integral of a function of the first degree; signed by the direction of travel
\text{area} = \Big|\tfrac12 \textstyle\sum (y_i + y_{i+1})(x_{i+1} - x_i)\Big| @@ going round, the top sides count once forward and the bottom sides once back (16g); move the triangle up first if need be, which changes no area
(y_i + y_{i+1})(x_{i+1} - x_i) = (x_{i+1}y_i - x_iy_{i+1}) + (x_{i+1}y_{i+1} - x_iy_i) @@ adding-keeps-answers
\textstyle\sum (x_{i+1}y_{i+1} - x_iy_i) = 0 @@ round the loop, a sum of differences collapses (collapsing-sum)

Who said it first

The way of walking from corner to better corner, given in 1947, is George Dantzig’s. In 18g.4.4, the Greek geometer is Euclid and the French philosopher is René Descartes.

Accepted names: midpoint-parallelogram is Varignon's theorem.
