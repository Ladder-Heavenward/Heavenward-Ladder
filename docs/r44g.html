Finite Fields

The prime clocks are fields with finitely many members. Are there others? A field with four members cannot be the clock of four, which has two times two equal to nothing. Yet there is one, and it is easy to build: put into the clock of two a root of a polynomial that cannot be split there, as step 44f put the cube root of two into the fractions. Every finite field is built that way, and the counting is strict. A finite field holds a prime clock, it is a space of vectors over that clock, and so its count of members is a power of a prime. Six members, or ten, or twelve, can never make a field. And for each power of a prime there is a field of that size: the zeros of one polynomial, gathered in a big enough field, are closed under adding and multiplying, because on a prime clock raising to the prime keeps sums. Last, as on the prime clocks, one member’s powers run through every member but zero.

A finite field holds a prime clock and is a space over it, so its size is a power of that prime. Dividing the polynomials of a prime clock by one that cannot be split, of degree n, gives a field of pⁿ members. For every power of a prime there is one: the zeros of x to that power minus x. Raising to the prime keeps sums there. The members other than 0 are the powers of one of them.

44g1The Size of a Finite Field

In a finite field F, add 1 to itself: 1, 1 + 1, 1 + 1 + 1, …. Two of these must agree, so some count of 1s is 0. Let p be the least count with p · 1 = 0. If p = ab with a, b < p, then (a · 1)(b · 1) = 0, and a field has no zero products, so p is prime. The p sums of 1 are a copy of ℤ/p inside F. This p is the field’s bottom prime.

Prime-power-size technique: a finite field with bottom prime p has pⁿ members, where n = [F : ℤ/p].

\mathbb Z/p \subseteq F @@ the sums of 1
F \text{ is a space over } \mathbb Z/p \text{ with a basis } u_1, \ldots, u_n @@ the field-as-space technique; finite, so the degree is finite
z = c_1 u_1 + \cdots + c_n u_n,\ \ c_i \in \mathbb Z/p,\ \text{one way} @@ a basis builds and is free (27b)
\htmlClass{new}{|F| = p^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ p choices for each of n coefficients (4k’s product rule)

So there is no field with 6 members, or 10, or 12. ℤ/6, ℤ/10 and ℤ/12 have zero products, and no other plus and times on 6 members fix that.

44g2Building Small Ones

Unsplittable-builds technique: if m of degree n cannot be split over ℤ/p, then (ℤ/p)[x]/(m) is a field with pⁿ members. It is a field by 44b’s largest-ideal technique, as in 44f2, and its members are the remainders of degree less than n, pⁿ of them, by 44c’s division.

m = x^2 + x + 1 \text{ on } \mathbb Z/2:\ m(0) = 1,\ m(1) = 1 @@ no zero, so no straight factor; degree 2, so it cannot be split
\text{members } 0,\ 1,\ x,\ x + 1;\ \ x^2 = x + 1 @@ the remainders, and x² ∼ −x − 1 = x + 1 on ℤ/2
x(x + 1) = x^2 + x = 1 @@ so x and x + 1 are each other’s reciprocals
\htmlClass{new}{F_4 = \{0, 1, x, x + 1\},\ \ 1 + 1 = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a field of four, not the clock of four

On ℤ/2, x³ + x + 1 has no zero, so it cannot be split, and it gives a field of 8. Its powers of x run x, x², x + 1, x² + x, x² + x + 1, x² + 1, 1: all seven members other than 0. On ℤ/3, x² + 1 has no zero (0, 1, 1 are the squares), and it gives a field of 9, written ℤ/3 with i put in. In it 1 + i has powers 1 + i, 2i, 1 + 2i, 2, 2 + 2i, i, 2 + i, 1.

44g3One for Each Prime Power

Power-keeps-plus technique: in a ring that turns about with p · 1 = 0 for a prime p, (w + b)ᵖ = wᵖ + bᵖ. By 4k’s binomial technique (w + b)ᵖ is the sum of C(p, k) wᵏ bᵖ⁻ᵏ, and for 0 < k < p, C(p, k) = p!/(k!(p − k)!) is a multiple of p: p divides the top and no factor of the bottom (4j’s prime-divides-a-list). Those terms are 0.

Every-prime-power technique: for every prime p and every n ≥ 1 there is a field with q = pⁿ members.

f = x^q - x \text{ over } \mathbb Z/p @@ the polynomial whose zeros will be the field
E \supseteq \mathbb Z/p \text{ in which } f \text{ splits into straight factors} @@ put in a root of an unsplittable factor (the root-field technique), and repeat, at most q times
f' = q\,x^{q-1} - 1 = -1 @@ the slope of a power read on coefficients (13d’s rule, which uses only adding and multiplying); q · 1 = 0
\text{no factor } (x - r)^2 @@ f = (x − r)²g would give f′ = (x − r)(2g + (x − r)g′), zero at r
F = \{r \in E : r^q = r\} \text{ has exactly } q \text{ members} @@ q different straight factors
r^q = r,\ s^q = s \Rightarrow (r + s)^q = r + s,\ (rs)^q = rs,\ (r^{-1})^q = r^{-1} @@ the power-keeps-plus technique, n times; powers of a product
\htmlClass{new}{F \text{ is a field with } q \text{ members}} \quad \blacksquare @@ it holds 0, 1, sums, products, negatives and reciprocals

And every field F with q members is of this kind: its q − 1 members other than 0 form a group under times, so each has rᵠ⁻¹ = 1 by 19d’s order-divides technique, so every member is a zero of x^q − x. That makes any two fields with q members copies of each other. Held in trust: that two fields in which one polynomial splits, each made by its zeros, are copies. 46c proves it. One thing is not the same: a field with q = pⁿ members has n matchings with itself that keep sums and products, the powers of r ↦ rᵖ, not one. That there are no others is held in trust.

44g4One Member Reaches Every Other

43e proved that on a prime clock some hour’s powers reach every hour but 0. The proof counted members of each order, and spent one fact about the clock: a polynomial of degree d has at most d zeros there. 44c3 showed that fact holds in every cancelling ring, so in every field.

Whole-hand technique: in every finite field, the members other than 0 are the powers of one of them. The proof is 43e’s, word for word, with F in place of ℤ/p and 44c3’s at-most-n-zeros in place of the clock’s.

(1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 2i @@ i² = −1 = 2 on ℤ/3
(1 + i)^4 = (2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4 = 2 @@ on ℤ/3, −4 = 2
(1 + i)^8 = 2^2 = 1 @@ and no smaller power is 1: 2i, 2, and the fourth power is 2, not 1
\htmlClass{new}{1 + i \text{ has order } 8:\ \text{its powers are all eight members other than } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the order divides 8 (19d); it is not 1, 2 or 4

Not every member reaches every other. i has order 4 in the field of 9: i, −1, −i, 1. In the field of 8, every member other than 0 and 1 has order 7, since 7 is prime.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Finite fields are Galois fields, written F_q or GF(q). The bottom prime is the characteristic, and ℤ/p inside F is the prime field. The power-keeps-plus technique is the Frobenius map’s additivity, sometimes called the freshman’s dream. The every-prime-power technique and the uniqueness held in trust give the existence and uniqueness of finite fields, and F_q is the splitting field of x^q − x. The whole-hand technique says the multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic; a member that reaches every other is a primitive element.

Who said it first

Évariste Galois built fields of pⁿ members in 1830, with imaginary roots on a prime clock. Carl Friedrich Gauss had worked out much the same in unpublished notes from about 1797. E. H. Moore proved in 1893 that every finite field is one of Galois’s. Georg Frobenius studied raising to p in 1896.

A proof that is not allowed

“A field of 8 has 7 members other than 0, and 7 is prime, so it is ℤ/7 with 0 put back in.” Not allowed: the members other than 0 form a group of size 7 under times, and that group is a copy of ℤ/7 under adding. The field itself has bottom prime 2, since 1 + 1 = 0 in it, and no copy of ℤ/7 sits inside it.
