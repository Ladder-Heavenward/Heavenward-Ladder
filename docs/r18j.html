18j · technique

Constructions with straightedge and compass

The Greeks drew with two tools: a straightedge with no marks on it, and a compass. This step lays down what the tools may do. It builds the constructions of the first book of the old geometry with them: the equilateral triangle, the midpoint, the perpendiculars, the bisected angle, the parallel, and the hexagon and the square in a circle. It checks each one in coordinates, by solving for the points where the lines and circles meet. Then it finds which points the tools can reach at all.

A straightedge draws the line through two drawn points. A compass draws the circle with a drawn centre through a drawn point. New points are where drawn lines and circles meet. Every point the tools reach has coordinates built from the starting ones by adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing and square roots.

18j1The rules of the game

From here on, a construction starts from some drawn points and makes moves of three kinds. Straightedge: draw the line through two drawn points. Compass: draw the circle whose centre is a drawn point, through another drawn point. Mark: a point where two drawn lines or circles meet is drawn. Nothing else is allowed. The straightedge has no marks on it. The compass closes when it is lifted, so it cannot carry a length from one place to another. A point reached this way has the accepted name constructible.

These are the rules of a game, not axioms: nothing new is assumed about the plane. Each construction claims that certain moves reach a certain point, and its proof is in coordinates. Most checks below use one device from 18c. For a displacement u = (u₁, u₂), write n = (−u₂, u₁). Then u · n = 0 by the dot technique, and |n| = |u| by distance-laws.

18j2A triangle and a midpoint

Let A ≠ B be drawn. Write u = B − A, s = |u|, n = (−u₂, u₁) and M = (A + B)/2. Equilateral technique: the circle with centre A through B and the circle with centre B through A meet at exactly two points, and each makes an equilateral triangle with A and B. The two points are C = M ± (√3/2)n.

\text{both radii are } s = d(A, B),\ \ |s - s| \lt s \lt s + s @@ two-circles-meet: exactly two meeting points
C - A = \tfrac12 u \pm \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}n @@ adding-keeps-answers, with M − A = u/2
|C - A|^2 = \tfrac14 s^2 + \tfrac34 s^2 = s^2 @@ right-triangle: u · n = 0 and |n| = s
|C - B|^2 = s^2 @@ the same, with C − B = −u/2 ± (√3/2)n
\htmlClass{new}{d(C, A) = d(C, B) = d(A, B)} \quad \blacksquare @@ so both points are on both circles, and two-circles-meet says there are no others

Midpoint technique: the line through the two points of equilateral is the perpendicular bisector of AB, and it meets AB at the midpoint. Each of the two points is s from A and s from B, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector (perpendicular-bisector). By line-through-two-points, the line through them is that bisector. Their average is M, by part-of-the-way, and M is on the line AB.

    18j3Perpendiculars, bisected angles and parallels

    Perpendicular technique: through any drawn point P, on a drawn line ℓ or off it, the line perpendicular to ℓ can be constructed. Let ℓ be drawn through Q and R. The moves are below. The idea in both cases is to find two points of ℓ equally far from P, and bisect the segment between them.

    \begin{aligned}&P \text{ on } \ell:\ \text{the circle centre } P \text{ through } Q \text{ }\\ &\quad \text{meets } \ell \text{ again at } Q' = 2P - Q\end{aligned} @@ line-meets-circle: the distance from P to ℓ is 0 < |PQ|; Q′ is on ℓ, and d(P, Q′) = d(P, Q)
\text{the bisector of } QQ' \text{ passes through } P = \tfrac{Q + Q'}{2} @@ midpoint; it is perpendicular to ℓ (perpendicular-bisector)
\begin{aligned}&P \text{ off } \ell:\ \text{the circle centre } P \text{ through } Q \text{ }\\ &\quad \text{meets } \ell \text{ at } Q \text{ and at } Q'\end{aligned} @@ line-meets-circle; if it only touches at Q, then PQ is already perpendicular to ℓ
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{the bisector of } QQ' \text{ passes through } P \text{ and }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{is perpendicular to } \ell} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ perpendicular-bisector: d(P, Q) = d(P, Q′); it meets ℓ at the foot of nearest-point

    Let A, B and C be drawn, with the rays from A through B and through C neither one ray nor opposite. Write r = |AB|, e₁ = (B − A)/r and e₂ = (C − A)/|C − A|. 18d called the line through A with direction e₁ + e₂ the angle bisector at A. Angle-bisector technique: to bisect the angle at A, mark the point B′ on the ray through C with AB′ = AB, and bisect the segment from B to B′.

    \begin{aligned}&\text{the circle centre } A \text{ through } B \text{ meets}\\ &\quad\text{the line through } A, C \text{ at } A \pm re_2\end{aligned} @@ line-meets-circle, with δ = 0; take B′ = A + re₂, on the ray
d(A, B) = d(A, B') = r @@ so A is on the bisector of BB′ (perpendicular-bisector); B ≠ B′, since e₁ ≠ e₂
\tfrac{B + B'}{2} = A + \tfrac r2(e_1 + e_2) \ne A @@ part-of-the-way; e₁ + e₂ ≠ 0, since the rays are not opposite
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{the bisector of } BB' \text{ is the line through } A \text{ }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{with direction } e_1 + e_2} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ line-through-two-points: through A and the midpoint

    Let a line ℓ be drawn through Q and R, and P be a drawn point off ℓ. Write r = |PQ| and e = (R − Q)/|R − Q|. Parallel technique: three circles give the parallel to ℓ through P: the circle centre Q through P marks S on ℓ, and the circles centre S and centre P, both through Q, meet again at a point D, and the line through P and D is parallel to ℓ. The four points Q, S, D, P have equal sides; the accepted name for such a figure is a rhombus.

    S = Q + re @@ line-meets-circle, with δ = 0: S is on ℓ, and d(Q, S) = r
D = P + S - Q:\ \ d(D, S) = d(P, Q) = r,\ \ d(D, P) = d(S, Q) = r @@ distance-laws; so D is on both circles, whose radii are both r
0 \lt d(S, P) \lt 2r @@ triangle in the plane, with equality only if Q were on the segment from S to P, which would put P on ℓ (equality-on-the-segment)
\begin{aligned}&\text{the two circles meet at exactly } Q \text{ and } D,\\ &\quad \ \ D \ne Q\end{aligned} @@ two-circles-meet; D = Q would put P = 2Q − S on ℓ
\htmlClass{new}{D - P = S - Q = re} \quad \blacksquare @@ so the line through P and D has ℓ's direction: it is the parallel (parallel-through-a-point)

    18j4Polygons in a circle

    A polygon whose corners all lie on one circle, with all its sides equal, has the accepted name regular. Let a circle with centre O through A be drawn. Write w = A − O, r = |w| and n = (−w₂, w₁).

    Hexagon technique: stepping round the circle with the compass, each step a circle through the centre, gives six corners that close up into a regular hexagon. The circle centre A through O meets the first circle at O + w/2 ± (√3/2)n, by equilateral with the triangle on OA. Each next step does the same from the corner just found. The six corners are O + w, O + w/2 + (√3/2)n, O − w/2 + (√3/2)n, O − w, O − w/2 − (√3/2)n and O + w/2 − (√3/2)n. Each is r from O, and each is r from the next, by right-triangle as in equilateral. The sixth step lands back on A. Every second corner gives the equilateral triangle in the circle, with side r√3.

    Square technique: the diameter through A and the diameter perpendicular to it meet the circle at the four corners of a square. The line OA meets the circle again at A′ = O − w. Its perpendicular bisector passes through O with direction n, by perpendicular, and meets the circle at O ± n. The corners O ± w and O ± n have sides of length r√2, and (n − w) · (−w − n) = |w|² − |n|² = 0, so each corner is a right angle.

    A = (0, 0),\ B = (6, 0):\ \ C = (3, \pm 3\sqrt3) @@ equilateral: n = (0, 6), C = (3, 0) ± (√3/2)(0, 6)
P = (2, 5),\ \ell: y = 0 \text{ through } Q = (-2, 0),\ R = (4, 0) @@ perpendicular: the circle centre P through Q, (x − 2)² + (y − 5)² = 41, meets y = 0 again at Q′ = (6, 0); the bisector of QQ′ is x = 2
\text{the same } P, \ell:\ \ S = (-2 + \sqrt{41}, 0),\ \ D = (2 + \sqrt{41}, 5) @@ parallel: D − P = S − Q, level
O = (0, 0),\ A = (5, 0):\ \ (2.5,\ \pm 2.5\sqrt3),\ (-2.5,\ \pm 2.5\sqrt3),\ (-5, 0) @@ hexagon; the square's corners are (±5, 0) and (0, ±5)

    18j5Which points the tools reach

    Let K be a set of reals that holds 0 and 1, and holds g + h, g − h, gh and, for h ≠ 0, g/h whenever it holds g and h. Let every drawn point have both coordinates in K. Root-and-ratio technique: each point the tools mark next has coordinates of the form p + q√k, with p, q and k in K.

    \text{the line through } (x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2):\ \ (y_2 - y_1)x - (x_2 - x_1)y = c,\ \ c \in K @@ two-descriptions-agree, with c = (y₂ − y₁)x₁ − (x₂ − x₁)y₁
\text{the circle: } x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0,\ \ D, E, F \in K @@ circle-equation expanded, from a centre and a point in K
\text{two lines: } x, y \in K @@ two-lines-meet: its formulas use only +, −, × and ÷
\text{a line and a circle: } \alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma = 0,\ \ \alpha, \beta, \gamma \in K @@ solve the line for one letter and substitute; if the line is upright, swap the letters
x = \tfrac{-\beta}{2\alpha} \pm \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\sqrt{\beta^2 - 4\alpha\gamma} @@ quadratic-formula: p + q√k, with k = β² − 4αγ in K; y from the line
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{two circles: subtract, and it is a line and a }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{circle}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ as in two-circles-meet

    So, from (0, 0) and (1, 0), every coordinate the tools reach is built from 0 and 1 by +, −, ×, ÷ and square roots, taken finitely often. The converse is true too. Products and quotients come from parallel-cut, and square roots from one more construction. Square-root technique: for k > 0, the circle with diameter from (−1, 0) to (k, 0) meets the line x = 0 at (0, ±√k). By diameter, its equation is (x + 1)(x − k) + y² = 0, and at x = 0 that is y² = k.

    So the tools reach exactly the numbers built by square roots. Is ∛2 one of them? A cube with twice the volume of a unit cube needs it as its side. In 1837 it was proved that the tools cannot reach ∛2 from 0 and 1, and that they cannot cut the angle of an equilateral triangle into three equal angles. The proof needs more algebra than this ladder has built, so here it is reported, not proved.

    Who said it first

    Let it be granted to draw a straight line from any point to any point, to extend a finite straight line continuously in a straight line, and to describe a circle with any centre and distance.

    Euclid, Elements, Book I, postulates 1 to 3 (about 300 BCE); paraphrase

    The old geometry is Euclid’s Elements. Equilateral is Euclid's first proposition; carrying a length with a closing compass is his second, and 18j6 is it. The bisected angle, the midpoint and the perpendiculars are his propositions 9 to 12, and the parallel through a point is his 31. The hexagon in a circle is Book IV, proposition 15.

    In 18j.4.4, the geometer with the marked straightedge is Archimedes; the philosopher is Plato, as Plutarch tells it of Eudoxus, Archytas and Menaechmus. The proof of 1837 is Pierre Wantzel's. Carl Friedrich Gauss had shown in 1796, at nineteen, that the regular polygon with 17 sides can be constructed. Georg Mohr (1672) and Lorenzo Mascheroni (1797) showed that the compass alone reaches every constructible point, which 18j.4.2 begins.

    Accepted names: the marked straightedge's move is a neusis; the three old problems are doubling the cube, trisecting the angle, and squaring the circle, the last shown impossible by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882.

    \text{the chord from } (1, 0) \text{ to } (0, 1) \text{ is cut at } (\tfrac23, \tfrac13),\ (\tfrac13, \tfrac23) @@ part-of-the-way: three equal pieces
\text{so the rays through the cuts cut the right angle in three} @@ not allowed: equal chords cut off equal angles only on one circle about the vertex (18f), and the cuts are not on one
