Why Is Carbon Fibre Stiff One Way and Soft the Other?

Why is carbon fibre stiff one way and soft the other? A bicycle frame or an aircraft wing made of carbon fibre is a bundle of very stiff threads held in a soft glue. Pull along the threads and it barely stretches. Pull across them and it gives far more. This page finds the stiffness along the threads as a weighted average, the stiffness across them as a different average, writes both as functions of the share of fibre with rung 9’s functions, shows that every real layup lies between the two, and finds the measure that tells a designer which material to choose.

Along the fibres, fibre and glue stretch by the same amount, so their stiffnesses add in proportion to their shares. Across the fibres, they carry the same pull, so their softnesses, one over stiffness, add instead. Both are functions of the fibre share f, and the first is always at least the second.

9eng1Stretched Together: A Weighted Average

A material’s stiffness, written E, is how hard you must pull to stretch it by a given share of its length: pull per area over stretch per length. Stiff steel has E about 200 gigapascals (GPa); the carbon fibres Terry uses have E about 230 GPa, and the epoxy glue about 3.5 GPa.

From here on, on this page, f is the share of the material’s cross-section that is fibre, and 1 − f the share that is glue. Terry’s layup has f = 0.6. Pull along the fibres: fibre and glue are bonded side by side, so they stretch by the same share, and each carries pull in proportion to its own stiffness and its own area.

\text{pull} = E_f \cdot f \cdot \text{stretch} + E_m (1 - f) \cdot \text{stretch} @@ same stretch, shares of area
E_\parallel = f\,E_f + (1 - f)\,E_m @@ a weighted average (5j’s ratios)
\htmlClass{new}{E_\parallel = 0.6 \cdot 230 + 0.4 \cdot 3.5 = 139.4 \text{ GPa}} @@ the fibres carry nearly all of it

9eng2Pulled Across: The Softnesses Add

Pull across the fibres. Now fibre and glue lie one after the other in the path of the pull, like links in a chain: each carries the whole pull, and their stretches add. Stretch is pull over stiffness, so it is the softnesses, 1/E, that add in proportion to the shares.

\dfrac{1}{E_\perp} = \dfrac{f}{E_f} + \dfrac{1 - f}{E_m} @@ softnesses add
= \dfrac{0.6}{230} + \dfrac{0.4}{3.5} \approx 0.0026 + 0.1143 = 0.1169 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{E_\perp \approx 8.6 \text{ GPa}} @@ sixteen times softer than along

Across the fibres the soft glue dominates: it is only 40% of the material, but it does nearly all the stretching. The two directions differ by a factor of sixteen, from the same fibres and glue.

9eng3Two Functions of the Fibre Share

Both stiffnesses depend only on f, once the fibre and glue are chosen. So each is a function of f on the interval from 0 to 1 (9a), and each can be drawn (9e).

E_\parallel(f) = E_m + (E_f - E_m)\,f @@ a straight line from E_m to E_f
E_\perp(f) = \dfrac{E_f E_m}{E_m f + E_f (1 - f)} @@ a rational function (9g)
E_\parallel(0) = E_\perp(0) = E_m,\ \ E_\parallel(1) = E_\perp(1) = E_f @@ they meet at the ends
\htmlClass{new}{E_\perp(0.5) \approx 6.9,\ \ E_\parallel(0.5) \approx 117} @@ far apart in between

The first is a straight line; the second hugs the glue’s value until f is nearly 1 and then shoots up. Adding fibre helps the along-stiffness steadily and the across-stiffness hardly at all until the glue is almost gone.

9eng4Every Layup Lies Between Them

Real materials are not perfectly side by side or perfectly in a chain: fibres wander, layers cross. But the two cases are the extremes. Stiffness is greatest when every part stretches together, and least when every part carries the whole pull.

E_\perp(f) \le E \le E_\parallel(f) @@ for any arrangement with fibre share f
E_\parallel - E_\perp = \dfrac{f(1 - f)(E_f - E_m)^2}{E_m f + E_f (1 - f)} \ge 0 @@ a square over a positive: never negative
\htmlClass{new}{\text{equal only at } f = 0,\ f = 1, \text{ or } E_f = E_m} @@ one material, or two the same

Two-bounds technique: a mix of two materials with shares f and 1 − f has a stiffness no greater than the weighted average of their stiffnesses and no less than the weighted average of their softnesses, turned back into a stiffness. An engineer checking a supplier’s figure for a new layup checks first that it falls between the bounds; one that falls outside is wrong.

9eng5Stiffness per Weight

A wing must be stiff and light. The measure that matters is stiffness over density, ρ: how stiff each kilogram is. Compare Terry’s layup along its fibres with steel and aluminium.

materialE (GPa)ρ (tonnes per cubic metre)E/ρ
steel2007.8about 26
aluminium702.7about 26
carbon layup, along1391.6about 87
carbon layup, across8.61.6about 5

Along its fibres, carbon layup is more than three times as stiff per kilogram as steel or aluminium; across them, it is five times worse. So designers lay sheets at several angles, 0°, 90° and ±45°, so every direction gets some fibres, and they point most fibres the way the load runs. A materials engineer, testing a strip cut along the fibres, measures close to the 139 GPa this page found.

Accepted names

Stiffness E is Young’s modulus, and a material that is stiffer one way than another is anisotropic. The weighted average along the fibres is the rule of mixtures or Voigt bound, and the average of softnesses is the inverse rule of mixtures or Reuss bound. E/ρ is the specific modulus. The glue is the matrix, and f the fibre volume fraction.

Who said it first

Woldemar Voigt set out the equal-stretch average in 1889 and András Reuss the equal-pull average in 1929, both for crystals; Rodney Hill showed in 1952 that they bound the true value.
