28k · technique

Partials and gradients by hand

The proof steps of this rung turned a hillside into a flat tilted sheet near each spot, and found the arrow that points straight uphill. This step puts those tools in your hands. First the sheet itself: over any point of a smooth surface there is exactly one flat sheet that hugs it, and its tilt is read off the two partial slopes. Then the slope in any direction, and a trick a surveyor can use: walk two different trails, measure how steep each one is, and the uphill arrow is fixed, so every other direction is known without walking it. Last, the lines of equal height on a map, and the surfaces of equal value in space. The uphill arrow stands square across them, so it hands you the line that just touches a curve and the flat sheet that just touches a surface.

Over a smooth surface the touching sheet is read off the partial slopes. Slopes along two different trails fix the uphill arrow, and with it the slope along every other trail. The uphill arrow stands square across lines of equal height, so it gives the touching line and the touching sheet at once.

28k1The flat sheet over a point

Partial slopes by hand need nothing new: the one-knob technique of 28a2 treats every other input as a fixed number, and rung 13 does the rest. What the hand can now add is the sheet those slopes describe. The graph of f(x, y) is the surface z = f(x, y) in space. A plane through the point (w, b, f(w, b)) that is not upright is z = f(w, b) + p(x − w) + q(y − b) for two numbers p and q, its tilts along x and along y.

Tangent-plane technique: if f is smoothly sloped near (w, b), then z = f(w, b) + fx(w, b)(x − w) + fy(w, b)(y − b) is the one plane through (w, b, f(w, b)) whose height gap from the surface shrinks faster than the distance from (w, b). It is the tangent plane, the flat sheet that touches the surface there.

L = \begin{pmatrix} f_x(w,b) & f_y(w,b)\end{pmatrix} @@ the slope-grid technique (28b2): a single row
\text{gap of the plane with tilts } p, q:\ \ f(w,b) + Lh - \big(f(w,b) + ph + qk\big) + r(h,k) @@ subtract the plane’s height at (w + h, b + k)

The sheet is a guess about the surface near one point, and the guess is good in a precise sense: its error is small even next to the step. Ten metres east and five south, the hill has bent away from the sheet by fifteen centimetres. A hundred metres east it has bent away by ten metres. 28l measures that bending.

28k2Slopes in every direction from two

By 28b2 and 28g1 the slope of f at w along an arrow u of length 1 is ∇f(w) · u. That one dot holds every direction at once. Turned round, it means a few slopes are enough to know them all.

Two-directions technique: let f be smoothly sloped near a point in the plane. If its slopes along two arrows u and v of length 1 that are not along one line are s and t, then ∇f is the one arrow g with g · u = s and g · v = t; and then the slope along every other arrow w of length 1 is g · w.

\nabla f \cdot u = s,\ \ \nabla f \cdot v = t @@ the slope along an arrow of length 1 is a dot with ∇f (28b2, 28g1)
u_1 g_1 + u_2 g_2 = s,\ \ v_1 g_1 + v_2 g_2 = t @@ two samenesses in the two places of g = ∇f
\det\begin{pmatrix} u_1 & u_2 \\ v_1 & v_2 \end{pmatrix} \ne 0 @@ u and v are not along one line, so they are free: the nonzero-det technique (27e4)
\text{exactly one } g \text{ solves them} @@ the det-ratio technique (27k3)
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla f = g,\ \ \text{slope along } w = g\cdot w} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∇f solves them, so it is that one g
  
u = (1, 0),\ \ s = -0.10;\ \ \ v = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1),\ \ t = 0.05 @@ a trail due east falls 10 cm per metre; a trail to the northeast climbs 5 cm per metre
g_1 = -0.10,\ \ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(g_1 + g_2) = 0.05 @@ the two samenesses
g = (-0.10,\ 0.05\sqrt2 + 0.10) \approx (-0.10,\ 0.171) @@ solve the second for g₂
|g| \approx 0.198 @@ the steepest slope (28g1): about 20 cm per metre
\htmlClass{new}{\text{due north: } g\cdot(0, 1) \approx 0.171} \quad \blacksquare @@ a third trail’s slope, never walked

With three inputs, three slopes along three free arrows fix ∇f the same way, by three samenesses. Two arrows along one line are not enough: a trail and the same trail walked back give s and −s, the same fact twice.

28k3Lines and sheets that touch level sets

A level curve of F(x, y) is the set where F keeps one value c. A level surface of F(x, y, z) is the set in space where F keeps one value. 28g1 proved that the uphill arrow stands at right angles to any path along a level curve. Here that fact becomes a recipe.

Level-plane technique: if F is smoothly sloped near p and ∇F(p) ≠ 0, then the line or plane touching the level set of F through p is ∇F(p) · (x − p) = 0. In the plane it is a line; in space, a plane.

F_z(p) \ne 0 \ \ (\text{or another place, renamed}) @@ ∇F(p) ≠ 0
\text{near } p,\ F = c \text{ is the graph } z = g(x, y) @@ the fixed-by-an-equation technique (28f1), with m = 1 equation and two inputs x, y (28f2)
g_x = -F_x/F_z,\ \ g_y = -F_y/F_z @@ the implicit-slope technique (28f2), one knob at a time
z - p_3 = g_x\,(x - p_1) + g_y\,(y - p_2) @@ the tangent-plane technique (28k1) for g
\htmlClass{new}{F_x(x - p_1) + F_y(y - p_2) + F_z(z - p_3) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply by −F_z and gather: ∇F(p) · (x − p) = 0
  
F = \tfrac{x^2}{4} + y^2,\ \ p = (2, 1):\ \ F(p) = 2,\ \ \nabla F(p) = (\tfrac{x}{2},\ 2y) = (1, 2) @@ an ellipse through p
(x - 2) + 2(y - 1) = 0:\ \ x + 2y = 4 @@ the level-plane technique; check by 28f2: y′ = −(x/2)/(2y) = −½
F = x^2 + y^2 + z^2,\ \ p = (1, 2, 3):\ \ \nabla F(p) = (2, 4, 6) @@ the sphere of radius √14
\htmlClass{new}{2(x - 1) + 4(y - 2) + 6(z - 3) = 0:\ \ x + 2y + 3z = 14} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plane at right angles to the radius

On a map with lines of equal height every hundred metres, the lines crowd where the ground is steep. Between the line at height c and the line at c + 100, the distance across is about 100/|∇f|, because along the uphill arrow the height grows at |∇f| per metre. Lines a centimetre apart on a map of scale one to fifty thousand are five hundred metres apart on the ground, a slope of about one in five.

The accepted names

The tangent-plane technique gives the tangent plane, and its formula is the linearization (or linear approximation) of f at (w, b). The slope along u is the directional derivative Duf. Level curves are contour lines (or isolines); on weather maps, the isotherms and isobars. Level surfaces are level sets in space. A vector at right angles to a surface is a normal vector, so ∇F(p) is normal to the level surface through p. The distance between contour lines on a map, against the height between them, is the map’s gradient in the surveyor’s sense, and its steepest value is |∇f|.

Who said it first

Edmond Halley drew lines of equal magnetic variation over the Atlantic in 1701. Philippe Buache drew lines of equal depth for the English Channel in 1752, and Charles Hutton drew lines of equal height in 1778 to work out the volume of the Scottish mountain Schiehallion, in an experiment to weigh the earth led by Nevil Maskelyne. Alexis Clairaut and Leonhard Euler wrote the tangent plane of a surface in the 1730s and 1760s, and Gaspard Monge made the geometry of surfaces by partial derivatives a subject in his lectures at the École polytechnique around 1795.
