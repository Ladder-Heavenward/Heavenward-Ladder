Why Not Just Round the Best Plan?

Why not just round the best plan? 18·logi found the best plan for a bakery at a corner of the region its limits draw. Loaves can be counted in halves on paper, but crates, trucks and people cannot: a plan for three and a half crates is no plan. This page loads a van with whole crates, finds that rounding the corner breaks the limits or throws profit away, and that the best whole plan can sit far from the corner. Then it finds the best whole plan by splitting the region in two and using the corner of each part as a ceiling, without listing every plan.

Find the best corner as in 18·logi. Its value is a ceiling: no whole plan earns more. If the corner is not whole, split the region on a fractional count, x ≤ the count rounded down or x ≥ it rounded up, and find each part’s best corner. A part whose ceiling is below the best whole plan found so far can be dropped unexplored.

18logi21Whole Crates Only

Claire drives a van to a market. It has room for 5 crates and can carry 1100 kg. A crate of apples weighs 100 kg and earns 10 dollars; a crate of honey weighs 500 kg and earns 40. With x crates of apples and y of honey, the van’s limits and the earnings are these.

18logi22The Best Corner Is Not Whole

Leave wholeness aside for a moment and find the best plan in the region, as 18·logi did.

\text{corners: } (0,0),\ (5,0),\ (0, 2.2),\ \text{and where the limits cross} @@ the region’s corners
x + y = 5,\ x + 5y = 11 \Rightarrow 4y = 6 @@ two lines meet (18c)
(3.5,\ 1.5):\ E = 3.5 + 6 = 9.5 @@ against 5 and 8.8 at the others
\htmlClass{new}{\text{best corner } (3.5, 1.5),\ E = 9.5} @@ the best-corner technique (18g)

Ceiling technique: every whole plan lies in the region, so none earns more than the best corner. No whole plan earns more than 95 dollars. The question is how close a whole plan can come.

18logi23Rounding Fails

Round the corner to the nearest whole plan, or round it down to be safe, and compare with the best whole plan found by listing all 13.

\text{round: } (4, 2):\ 4 + 2 = 6 > 5 @@ breaks the room limit
\text{and } 4 + 10 = 14 > 11 @@ and the weight limit
\text{round down: } (3, 1):\ E = 7 @@ allowed, and 25 dollars below the ceiling
\htmlClass{new}{\text{best whole plan: } (1, 2),\ E = 9} @@ two from the corner in apples, by listing

Rounding the best corner can break the limits, and rounding down can leave much behind; the best whole plan can lie away from the corner. Listing every whole plan works for a van. For a hundred trucks it does not: each yes-or-no choice doubles the list.

18logi24Split, and Use the Corners as Ceilings

Every whole plan has y ≤ 1 or y ≥ 2, since no whole number lies strictly between 1 and 2. So split the region on y = 1.5 into two parts, and find the best corner of each.

y \le 1:\ \text{best corner } (4, 1),\ E = 8 @@ whole already: a plan in hand
y \ge 2:\ x + 5y \le 11 \Rightarrow x \le 1 @@ the weight limit squeezes
\text{corners } (0, 2),\ (1, 2),\ (0, 2.2):\ E = 8,\ 9,\ 8.8 @@ the best-corner technique (18g)
\htmlClass{new}{(1, 2),\ E = 9 \ge 8} @@ whole, and it beats the other part’s ceiling

The part y ≤ 1 has ceiling 8, below the 9 in hand, so nothing there needs looking at again. Split technique: split on a fractional count into its two whole sides, take each part’s best corner as its ceiling, and drop any part whose ceiling is below the best whole plan found.

18logi25Why the Ceilings Save Work

A part that is dropped takes every plan inside it with it, unlisted. Here two parts settled what listing 13 plans did. With fifty yes-or-no choices there are 250 plans, about a million billion.

\text{part ceiling } < \text{best in hand} @@ no plan inside can win
\text{drop it: every plan inside, unlisted} @@ one corner found in place of the list
2^{50} = (2^{10})^5 > (10^3)^5 = 10^{15} @@ the list for fifty choices
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a good plan early makes more parts drop}} @@ so start from a rounded-down plan

There is no promise. Some problems force a split after a split down to almost every plan, and no one knows a way that never does. In practice, ceilings close to the truth and a good plan found early drop most of the parts, and the splitting finishes in seconds where the list would take ages.

\text{round down: always allowed, so always best}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> allowed here, and 20 dollars short of the best whole plan (18logi23)
\text{split on } y \le 1.5 \text{ and } y \ge 1.5\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> split on the whole sides, y ≤ 1 and y ≥ 2, so the corner is cut away (18logi24)

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

A best plan with some counts forced whole is an integer program; leaving wholeness aside gives its linear relaxation, whose best value is a bound. Splitting and dropping parts by their bounds is branch and bound; the parts are branches, and dropping one is pruning. Integer programs are NP-hard: no way that is quick on every one is known.

Who said it first

Ailsa Land and Alison Doig set out branch and bound in Econometrica, 1960. Ralph Gomory showed in 1958 how to cut fractional corners away with extra limits.
