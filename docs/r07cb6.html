7·cb6 · newsflash for 6d

The top of every chain gives choice

6d proved that the maximal-member principle gives, for each collection of non-empty sets, a largest partial picking. It proved that for each set of the collection it is not so that the set is missing from it. The column stopped there, at a double negative, and 6d's table marked the last line as rung 7's. This page writes that line, so the maximal-member principle gives choice. Then it goes the other way. 6d sketched “choice gives the maximal-member principle” in words. The obvious proof climbs past every counting number, and the ladder has not built a recursion that runs that far. There is a proof that needs no such run: it takes all the climbs that keep one rule, and their union is the long run. With either-way in hand, every line of it can be written, and this page writes them.

A largest partial picking misses nothing, not not; so it misses nothing. And the other way: if nothing were maximal, a choosing function could always step higher. Then the union of every run that keeps its rule would be a run it could still extend.

The maximal-member principle gives choice

6d set up ℱ, the set of partial choosing functions for a collection 𝒜 of non-empty sets, ordered by ⊆. It proved that every chain in ℱ has an upper bound, its union (bounded-chains). And, with the maximal-member principle, it took a maximal member m and proved for every A ∈ 𝒜 that ¬¬(A is an input of m) (chains-give-choice). Chains-give-choice technique, in full (Zorn's): if the maximal-member principle is true, every collection of non-empty sets has a choosing function.

\text{call } m \text{ a maximal member of } \mathcal F @@ the maximal-member principle, with bounded-chains for its hypothesis; use the some
\forall A \in \mathcal A\ \neg\neg(A \text{ is an input of } m) @@ the chains-give-choice technique: where 6d stopped
  
\text{let } A \in \mathcal A:\ \ \neg\neg(A \text{ is an input of } m) @@ use the every
\htmlClass{new}{A \text{ is an input of } m} @@ not-not-out (7d): the missing line
\exists y\,((A, y) \in m) @@ ﹀ “input”: every input has an output
\begin{aligned}&m \subseteq \mathcal A \times \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A,\ \ \text{no input has two outputs},\\ &\quad \ \ \text{each output in its input}\end{aligned} @@ m ∈ ℱ, unpacked
m : \mathcal A \to \textstyle\bigcup\mathcal A,\ \ m(A) \in A @@ ︿ 4c's line for a function; the value line
\htmlClass{new}{m \text{ is a choosing function for } \mathcal A} \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ 6a's line; speak of everything

Look at what the column cites. Not-not-out is the either-way principle, and nothing in the column calls on the choosing axiom. On this ladder either-way came from Choice, on 7c. So the honest reading is this: from the principles of rungs 2 to 4 together with either-way, the maximal-member principle gives choice. Without either-way, as 6d's basement reported, no cleverer column exists; with it, the gap is one line. The flag 6d left on that line is closed.

Choice gives the maximal-member principle: the words

The idea of 6d's sketch was to climb. Start anywhere, choose something strictly higher, and again, and at the end of every run take an upper bound of the run and climb on. If nothing were maximal, the climb could never stop. That is impossible, because a climb through a set cannot go on longer than the set allows. The trouble was “at the end of every run”. After all the counting numbers, a run goes on, and the recursion that carries it there was not built. The way round is to fix one rule for climbing and take every run that keeps the rule. It was found in 1950 and made short in 1991. Those runs line up, each the beginning of the next, so their union is a run too: the long run, with no recursion needed. Then the rule can extend even that, which is totally false.

From here on, when a partial order ≼ on a set a is in hand, means x ≼ y ∧ x ≠ y. The mark is a statement with two gaps, like ≼; read it “x is strictly below y”. And x ⊀ y means ¬(x ≺ y), with a stroke through the mark as in ∉ and ≠. A strict upper bound of a part C is a u ∈ a with x ≺ u for every x ∈ C. For a part W of a and x ∈ a, names {y ∈ W : y ≺ x}, the members of W strictly below x (4e's low mark, carrying x). W is an initial part of V when W ⊆ V and every member of V strictly below a member of W is in W.

Fix a partial order ≼ on a set S in which every chain has an upper bound. Suppose, for contradiction, that S has no maximal member. Write 𝒯 for the set of chains of S, a sift of 𝒫(S).

Strict-upper-bounds technique: if no member of S is maximal and every chain has an upper bound, then every chain has a strict upper bound.

C \in \mathcal T;\ \ \text{call } u \text{ an upper bound of } C @@ the hypothesis on chains; use the some
\neg(u \text{ is maximal}):\ \ \neg\forall x \in a\,(u \preccurlyeq x \Rightarrow x = u) @@ the supposition, used with u; 6d's line for maximal (u ∈ a is true)
\text{call } v \in a \text{ with } u \preccurlyeq v \land v \ne u @@ not-every, bounded form, then negated-if forward (7d)
y \in C \Rightarrow y \preccurlyeq u \preccurlyeq v,\ \ y \preccurlyeq v @@ u an upper bound; transitive
y = v \Rightarrow v \preccurlyeq u \land u \preccurlyeq v \Rightarrow u = v,\ \bot @@ the swap axiom; antisymmetric; clash with v ≠ u
\htmlClass{new}{y \prec v \text{ for every } y \in C} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; join; speak of everything: v is a strict upper bound

Now the one use of the axiom. For each chain C, its strict upper bounds form a set, sifted from S, with a member by strict-upper-bounds. Gather those sets into a collection, sifted from 𝒫(S). By the choosing axiom there is a choosing function for it; call it c. Define f on 𝒯 by f(C) = c(the set of strict upper bounds of C), by 4c's rule-makes-a-function. Then f(C) is a strict upper bound of C, for every chain C. This f is the rule for climbing.

From here on, on this page, a part W of a is conforming when ≼ well-orders W and every member of W is f's value at the members of W strictly below it: .

W_x is a part of a chain, so a chain, and f(W_x) makes sense. ∅ is conforming, vacuously. So is {f(∅)}: its one member has nothing strictly below it. So is {f(∅), f({f(∅)})}, and so on. A conforming part is a run of the climb that kept the rule f at every step, each member chosen by f from what came before. The picture builds them.

Two conforming parts line up

Least-difference technique: if W and V are conforming, and x is the least member of W that is not in V, then V = W_x. (W ∖ V has a least member because W is well-ordered.)

W_x \subseteq V @@ y ∈ W, y ≺ x, y ∉ V would put y in W ∖ V below its least member: refute; not-not-out
\text{suppose, for contradiction, } \neg(V \subseteq W_x);\ \ y = \text{least of } V \setminus W_x @@ not-every, negated-if forward: V ∖ W_x has a member; V is well-ordered
V_y \subseteq W_x @@ as in the first line, with V, y in the places of W, x
\quad \text{suppose, for contradiction, } \neg(W_x \subseteq V_y);\ \ z = \text{least of } W_x \setminus V_y @@ the same move, inside
\quad W_z \subseteq V_y @@ s ∈ W_z gives s ≺ z ≺ x, so s ∈ W_x; s below z's least, so s ∈ V_y (not-not-out)
\quad t \in V_y:\ \ t \in W,\ \ t \prec z \lor t = z \lor z \prec t @@ V_y ⊆ W_x ⊆ W; W is a chain; a split on t = z
\quad t = z:\ z \in V_y,\ \bot;\ \ \ z \prec t:\ z \in V,\ z \prec y,\ z \in V_y,\ \bot @@ z ∉ V_y; and z ∈ W_x ⊆ V (first line), z ≺ t ≺ y
\quad V_y \subseteq W_z,\ \ \text{so } V_y = W_z @@ the case t ≺ z is all that is left; double-inclusion (2a)
\quad y = f(V_y) = f(W_z) = z @@ V and W conforming; the swap axiom
\quad y \notin W_x,\ \ z \in W_x,\ \ \bot @@ y was picked from V ∖ W_x; the swap axiom; clash
\quad W_x \subseteq V_y,\ \ \text{so } W_x = V_y @@ refute, not-not-out; double-inclusion
\quad x = f(W_x) = f(V_y) = y \in V,\ \ \text{but } x \notin V,\ \ \bot @@ conforming; the swap axiom; x was picked from W ∖ V
V \subseteq W_x,\ \ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{V = W_x} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute, not-not-out; double-inclusion with the first line

Conforming-parts-compare technique: if W and V are conforming, one is an initial part of the other. Split on W ⊆ V (either-way). If not, not-every gives a least x in W ∖ V, and least-difference gives V = W_x. That is an initial part of W: a member of W strictly below a member of W_x is strictly below x. If so, split on V ⊆ W. If that is true too, W = V. If not, least-difference with the two exchanged gives W = V_y. ∎

The long run, and the contradiction

Let 𝒲 be the set of conforming parts of S, sifted from 𝒫(S), and U = ⋃𝒲. Conforming-union technique: the union of all the conforming parts is itself conforming. In marks: U is conforming, and Uₓ = Wₓ whenever x ∈ W ∈ 𝒲.

x \in W \in \mathcal W:\ \ W_x \subseteq U_x @@ W ⊆ U (2c)
y \in U_x:\ \ y \in V \in \mathcal W,\ \ y \prec x @@ 2c's ⋃ line; use the some
\begin{aligned}&\text{conforming-parts-compare:}\\ &\quad V \text{ initial in } W:\ y \in V \subseteq W;\\ &\quad W \text{ initial in } V:\ y \in W\end{aligned} @@ either way y ∈ W, so y ∈ W_x. So U_x = W_x
U \text{ is a chain} @@ x ∈ W, y ∈ V: conforming-parts-compare puts both in the larger, a chain (well-order-compares)
X \subseteq U,\ e \in X,\ e \in W;\ \ m = \text{least of } X \cap W @@ W well-ordered; X ∩ W has e
z \in X:\ \ z \prec m \Rightarrow z \in U_m = W_m \subseteq W,\ m \preccurlyeq z,\ \bot @@ the first lines; m least in X ∩ W; antisymmetric; so m ≼ z (U a chain; a split on z = m)
x = f(W_x) = \htmlClass{new}{f(U_x)} \text{ for } x \in U \quad \blacksquare @@ x in some W ∈ 𝒲; conforming; U_x = W_x; the swap axiom. U is conforming

U is a chain, so f(U) is a strict upper bound of it. Write u for it. Choice-gives-chains technique (Zorn's lemma): with the choosing axiom, if every chain in a partial order on a set S has an upper bound, then S has a maximal member.

\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose, for contradiction,}\\ &\quad\text{no member of } a \text{ is maximal}\end{aligned} @@ the denial of what is wanted
\quad f,\ \ \mathcal W,\ \ U \text{ as above};\ \ U \text{ conforming} @@ strict-upper-bounds; the choosing axiom; conforming-union
\quad u = f(U);\ \ x \prec u \text{ for every } x \in U @@ U is a chain; f gives a strict upper bound
\quad U \cup \{u\} \text{ is well-ordered} @@ a part meeting U has its least there, since u is above all of U; otherwise the part is {u} (a split, either-way)
\quad (U \cup \{u\})_x = U_x \text{ for } x \in U;\ \ (U \cup \{u\})_u = U @@ u ⊀ x, since x ≺ u (antisymmetric); every x ∈ U is ≺ u, and u ⊀ u
\quad U \cup \{u\} \text{ is conforming} @@ x = f(U_x) for x ∈ U (conforming-union); u = f(U) by its name
\quad u \in U \cup \{u\} \subseteq U,\ \ u \prec u,\ \ u \ne u,\ \ \bot @@ it is in 𝒲, so a part of the union U; strict upper bound; the self-sameness axiom; clash
\begin{aligned}&\neg\neg\exists m\,(m \text{ maximal}),\\ &\quad \ \ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{a \text{ has a maximal member}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ refute; not-not-out

Notice where the long run went. 6d said choosing from what is left must go past every counting number, along runs the ladder had not built. U is that run. It was never built step by step. It was gathered all at once, as the union of every run that keeps the rule. The lining-up in the conforming-parts-compare technique is what makes the union a run. f was made from the one choosing function the axiom says exists; either-way did the rest. 6d's sketch of this direction is now a proof.

The last arrow, in words. Given a set X, look at the pairs (Y, r) with Y a part of X and r a well-order of Y. Put (Y, r) below (Y′, r′) when Y is an initial part of Y′ under r′ and r′ agrees with r on Y. A chain of such pairs has an upper bound: the union of the parts, ordered by the union of the orders. The proof that this is a well-order is conforming-union's column again. So the maximal-member principle, now proved from Choice, gives a maximal pair (Y, r). If Y were not all of X, a member of X outside Y, put above all of Y, would make a larger pair. So Y is X, by refute and not-not-out, and r well-orders X. Every step is of a kind this page has written out, and 7·cb6.4.1b asks for the chain step. With that, the three principles are one, given the other axioms and either-way, as 6d said they would be.

Who said it first

The way round the long run was found by Hellmuth Kneser in 1950 and made short by Jonathan Lewin in 1991. The finding that without either-way no cleverer column exists is John Bell’s.

Lewin presented the argument as a simple proof of Zorn's lemma from the axiom of choice, avoiding the ordinals entirely: call a well-ordered chain conforming when each of its members is the chosen strict upper bound of the members below it, show that any two conforming chains are one an initial part of the other, and take the union of them all.

Lewin, “A simple proof of Zorn's lemma”, American Mathematical Monthly 98 (1991)

Accepted names

The maximal-member principle is Zorn’s lemma. Runs longer than ℕ are the ordinals, which this proof avoids. The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
