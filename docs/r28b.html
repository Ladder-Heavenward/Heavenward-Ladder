28b · proof

The best flat map

On rung 13 a slope was a number. Near the point, the function looked like a straight line, and the slope was how steep the line was. With two inputs, a hillside near one spot looks like a flat tilted sheet. This step says what it means for a function to look like a flat sheet near a point, in every direction at once. The flat sheet is a straight map, so rung 27 can describe it with a grid of numbers. The step then proves that the grid is made of the partial slopes, and that partial slopes which change smoothly are enough to give the sheet.

A function has a slope at a point when one straight map matches it there better than any gap shrinks. That map is unique, its grid is the partial slopes, and continuous partial slopes guarantee it.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 28a (partial slopes), 27c and 27d (straight maps are grids) and 14c (the average-slope technique). 28c chains these maps together.

28b1A flat map that matches

Stands on 28a1, 27c1, 27d1.

Let f send lists of n numbers to lists of m numbers. f has a slope at w when there is a straight map L with

\(f(w + h) = f(w) + L(h) + r(h), \quad \text{where } \dfrac{|r(h)|}{|h|} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0.\)

L is the best flat map of f at w. Accepted name: the derivative of f at w, written Df(w). The leftover r(h) shrinks faster than the step h. With n = m = 1, L(h) = f′(w)h and this is rung 13’s slope, read through 13k’s nearby line.

One-flat-map technique: a function has at most one best flat map at a point.

f(w+h) = f(w) + L(h) + r(h) = f(w) + M(h) + s(h) @@ two best flat maps L and M
(L - M)(h) = s(h) - r(h) @@ subtract
(L - M)(tu) = t\,(L-M)(u) \text{ for a fixed } u,\ t > 0 @@ L − M is straight (27c)
|(L-M)(u)| = \dfrac{|s(tu) - r(tu)|}{t} = |u|\,\dfrac{|s(tu) - r(tu)|}{|tu|} \to 0 @@ as t → 0, ﹀ “best flat map”: r and s shrink faster than the step
\htmlClass{new}{(L - M)(u) = 0 \text{ for every } u, \text{ so } L = M} \quad \blacksquare @@ a fixed number heading to 0 is 0

28b2The grid of the flat map

Stands on 28b1, 28a2, 27d1.

Slope-grid technique: if f has a best flat map L at w, then every partial slope of every output exists there, and the grid of L has ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ (w) in row i, column j. The grid is the slope grid of f at w. Accepted name: the Jacobian matrix.

f(w + t e_j) = f(w) + t\,L(e_j) + r(te_j) @@ ﹀ “best flat map”, with h = t·eⱼ, a step along knob j
\dfrac{f(w + te_j) - f(w)}{t} = L(e_j) + \dfrac{r(te_j)}{t} @@ divide by t ≠ 0
\dfrac{|r(te_j)|}{|t|} = \dfrac{|r(te_j)|}{|te_j|} \to 0 @@ |eⱼ| = 1
\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(w) = L(e_j) @@ the left side heads to the partial slope (28a2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{column } j \text{ of the grid of } L \text{ is } \partial f/\partial x_j (w)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the columns technique (27d)

The same lines with any arrow u of length 1 in place of eⱼ give the slope of f along u: it is L(u). So the best flat map gives a slope in every direction, and the slope grid hands it over by one multiplication.

Flat-means-continuous technique: if f has a best flat map at w, f is continuous at w. For f(w + h) − f(w) = L(h) + r(h), and both terms head to 0: L(h) is a mix of the places of h (27d), and r(h) = |h| · (r(h)/|h|).

28b3Continuous partials are enough

Stands on 28b1, 28a3, 14c.

28a3 showed partial slopes alone do not give a best flat map: that function was not even continuous. Partial slopes that change continuously do.

Smooth-knobs technique: if every partial slope of f exists near w and is continuous at w, then f has a best flat map at w. Shown here for two inputs; with n inputs the same walk takes n legs.

f(w+h, b+k) - f(w,b) = [f(w+h,b+k) - f(w,b+k)] + [f(w,b+k) - f(w,b)] @@ walk along x, then along y
f(w+h,b+k) - f(w,b+k) = f_x(w + sh,\, b+k)\,h,\ \ 0
  
A function whose partial slopes all exist and are continuous is called smoothly sloped here. Every formula built from places by sums, products, quotients, powers, exponentials and the trig functions is smoothly sloped wherever its bottoms are not 0.

The accepted names

The best flat map is the (total, or Fréchet) derivative Df(w). The slope grid is the Jacobian matrix, and its determinant, when the grid is square, the Jacobian. The slope along an arrow u of length 1 is the directional derivative. A function whose partial derivatives exist and are continuous is continuously differentiable, or of class C¹.

Who said it first

Karl Weierstrass taught the derivative as a best linear approximation in Berlin in the 1860s. Otto Stolz wrote it down for functions of several variables in 1893, and William Henry Young made it the centre of the subject in 1910. Maurice Fréchet carried the idea to spaces of functions in 1911. Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi studied the determinant of the grid of partial derivatives in 1841.
