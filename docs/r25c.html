25c · proof

The size of a product

With no larger and smaller, the new numbers need something else to measure how close two of them are. Size does that job. The size of a product is the product of the sizes, which the turning picture showed by stretching and this step proves by arithmetic alone. The size of a sum is never more than the sum of the sizes, which is the old fact that one side of a triangle is shorter than the other two. With those two facts, everything the number line did with distances, the plane can do too.

The size of a product is the product of the sizes. The size of a sum is at most the sum of the sizes. So distance in the plane works as it did on the line.

25c1Sizes multiply

Product-size technique: for all z and w in ℂ, |zw| = |z||w|; and when z ≠ 0, |1/z| = 1/|z|. The flip technique turns it into arithmetic on the floor.

|zw|^2 = (zw)\,\overline{zw} @@ z z̄ = |z|², for the number zw (25a3)
= z\,w\,\overline{z}\,\overline{w} = (z\overline{z})(w\overline{w}) @@ the flip of a product (25a3); turn-about and regroup for times
= |z|^2|w|^2 @@ 25a3 twice
|zw| = |z||w| @@ both sides are sizes, never negative; take the square root (8f)
1 = |1| = |z \cdot \tfrac1z| = |z|\,|\tfrac1z| @@ the line above with w = 1/z
\htmlClass{new}{|zw| = |z||w|,\ \ |\tfrac1z| = \tfrac{1}{|z|}} \quad \blacksquare

21·cb20 saw the same fact as stretching: multiplying by w stretches by |w|. Here no angle is used. The proof works for every pair, and does not need the out-and-round form at all. Two more fall out: |zⁿ| = |z|ⁿ, and |z̄| = |z|, since a² + (−b)² = a² + b².

25c2The triangle in the plane

Plane-triangle technique: for all z and w in ℂ, |z + w| ≤ |z| + |w|, and ||z| − |w|| ≤ |z − w|. One side of a triangle is never longer than the other two together.

|z+w|^2 = (z+w)(\overline{z}+\overline{w}) @@ size squared, and the flip of a sum (25a3)
= |z|^2 + |w|^2 + (z\overline{w} + \overline{z\overline{w}}) @@ share out; z̄w is the flip of z w̄
= |z|^2 + |w|^2 + 2\,\text{(floor part of } z\overline{w}) @@ u + ū is twice the floor part (25a3)
\text{floor part of } u \le |u| @@ a ≤ |a| ≤ √(a² + b²) (8h, the sandwich technique)
|z+w|^2 \le |z|^2 + |w|^2 + 2|z||w| = (|z|+|w|)^2 @@ |z w̄| = |z||w̄| = |z||w| (25c1)
|z+w| \le |z| + |w| @@ square roots keep order for numbers ≥ 0 (8g)
|z| = |(z-w)+w| \le |z-w| + |w| @@ the line above, with z − w for z
\htmlClass{new}{|z+w| \le |z|+|w|,\ \ \big||z|-|w|\big| \le |z-w|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same with z and w swapped

With d(z, w) = |z − w|, which is 18a’s distance, the plane has every distance technique the line had. A list zₙ in ℂ settles at L when |zₙ − L| heads to 0, and it settles exactly when both its floor parts and its upright parts settle, because each part is at most the size, and the size is at most the sum of the parts’ sizes.

25c3The size of a polynomial’s value

Value-fence technique: for p(z) = u₀ + u₁z + ⋯ + uₙzⁿ, |p(z)| ≤ |u₀| + |u₁||z| + ⋯ + |uₙ||z|ⁿ; and |p(z) − p(w)| ≤ C|z − w| whenever |z|, |w| ≤ ρ, with C = Σₖ k|uₖ|ρᵏ⁻¹. So a polynomial is continuous on the plane.

|p(z)| \le \textstyle\sum |u_k z^k| = \sum |u_k||z|^k @@ the triangle, n times (25c2); sizes multiply (25c1)
z^k - w^k = (z-w)(z^{k-1} + z^{k-2}w + \cdots + w^{k-1}) @@ share out: the middle terms cancel
|z^{k-1} + \cdots + w^{k-1}| \le k\rho^{k-1} @@ k terms, each of size at most ρᵏ⁻¹
|p(z)-p(w)| \le \textstyle\sum |u_k||z^k - w^k| \le \big(\sum k|u_k|\rho^{k-1}\big)|z-w| @@ the triangle; sizes multiply
\htmlClass{new}{|p(z)-p(w)| \le C|z-w| \text{ on } |z|,|w| \le \rho} \quad \blacksquare

That is continuity, in the strong form 24d called tame: a nearby input gives a nearby value, with a fixed rate. 25d needs it to carry rung 12’s lowest values into the plane.

The accepted names

The product-size technique says the absolute value is multiplicative. The plane-triangle technique is the triangle inequality in ℂ, and its second half is the reverse triangle inequality. The value-fence technique says a polynomial is Lipschitz on every disc, and so continuous.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy used the size of a complex number in 1821. Jean-Robert Argand used the triangle in the plane in his 1814 proof that every polynomial has a root.
