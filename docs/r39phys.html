39·phys · physics

Are faraway particles secretly connected?

Are faraway particles secretly connected? Make two photons together in a crystal, send one to a lab in Victoria and the other to a lab in Seattle, and pass each through a polarizing filter. Whether each one gets through looks like a coin toss. But the two tosses agree far more than chance, and how much they agree depends on the angle between the two filters, chosen at the last moment, too late for any signal at the speed of light to pass between them. One explanation is ordinary: the photons left with a plan, a list of answers for every angle, the way two gloves of a pair are left and right from the start. In 1964 it was shown that every such plan obeys an inequality, a bound on how much the answers can agree, and that quantum mechanics breaks it. Experiments since 1982, and without loopholes since 2015, side with quantum mechanics. This page builds the physics: a two-way state as an arrow of size one, two particles in one state that is no pair of states, the bound and its breaking with the numbers worked, the description of half a pair, gates that keep size, how a real gate is made and why it must be cold, and a recipe that factors numbers with such gates.

A qubit is an arrow of size one in two turning dimensions, and its readings come with chances that are squared sizes. Two qubits can share a state that is no pair of states. Every plan of hidden answers keeps a sum of agreements within 2; the shared state reaches 2√2, and experiments find it. Half a pair alone is an even mix, so nothing is sent. Gates keep size, real gates need cold, and a period found by a transform factors a number.

39phys1A qubit

37·phys gave an electron a spin with two directions, up and down, at right angles. 31·phys gave light a shaking along a line, and a filter that keeps the part along its axis. Each is a two-way system. Its state is an arrow ψ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ in the space of pairs of complex numbers (25), of size one: |a|² + |b|² = 1. Here |0⟩ and |1⟩ are a right-angled pair, level and upright for a photon, up and down for a spin. Such a system is a qubit.

35·phys found that a wave’s size squared is a chance, a law measured and never broken. For a qubit it reads like this. Square-chance law, as used here: measured along a right-angled pair d₀, d₁, a qubit in state ψ gives d₀ with chance |⟨ψ, d₀⟩|², and afterwards it is in state d₀. The two chances add to 1, by 31h’s energy sum on a basis of two.

\psi = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac12|1\rangle @@ a photon shaking at 30° from level
\text{level }\allowbreak \text{or }\allowbreak \text{upright: } \tfrac34 \text{ and } \tfrac14 @@ |√3/2|² and |1/2|²; 31·phys’s cos² 30° and sin² 30°
d_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\text{, }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{filter }\allowbreak \text{at } 45° @@ another right-angled pair
|\langle \psi, d_0\rangle|^2 = \big(\tfrac{\sqrt3 + 1}{2\sqrt2}\big)^2 = \tfrac{2 + \sqrt3}{4} @@ multiply out: (3 + 2√3 + 1)/8
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 0.933 = \cos^2 15°} \quad \blacksquare @@ the filter is 15° from the shaking

A qubit is not a bit that is secretly 0 or 1. The state at 30° gives 0 three times in four along one pair, and passes a 45° filter 93% of the time; no single hidden value of 0 or 1 says both.

39phys2Two qubits, one state

Two qubits have states in a space of four dimensions, with the right-angled basis |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩, as 37·phys gave two electrons one joint state. If the first is a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ and the second c|0⟩ + d|1⟩, the pair is ac|00⟩ + ad|01⟩ + bc|10⟩ + bd|11⟩. Not every state of four dimensions is such a pair.

No-split technique: the state Φ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is not a state of the first qubit paired with a state of the second.

ac = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \allowbreak \ ad = 0,\ \allowbreak \ bc = 0,\ \allowbreak \ bd = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} @@ suppose Φ is a pair, and match the four parts
ac \ne 0 \text{ and } bd \ne 0 \Rightarrow a, b, c, d \text{ all } \ne 0 @@ a product is 0 only when a factor is (25)
\htmlClass{new}{ad \ne 0:\ \bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ against ad = 0

Such a state is entangled. Pass both photons of Φ through filters at angles α and β from level. Both pass with chance |⟨cos α|0⟩ + sin α|1⟩ ⊗ cos β|0⟩ + sin β|1⟩, Φ⟩|² = (cos α cos β + sin α sin β)²/2 = cos²(α − β)/2, and both are stopped with the same chance. So they agree with chance cos²(α − β), and the average of the product of their answers, counting pass as +1 and stop as −1, is E(α, β) = cos² − sin² = cos 2(α − β). Each photon alone passes half the time, whatever the angles.

39phys3What a plan of answers allows

Suppose each pair leaves with a plan: answers A, A′ for the first photon at the angles a, a′, and B, B′ for the second at b, b′, each +1 or −1, drawn by whatever chances the source likes. Each lab picks its angle after the photons have left, too late for any signal at light speed to reach the other.

Hidden-answers technique: for every plan, the averages of products obey |E(a, b) + E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) − E(a′, b′)| ≤ 2.

S = AB + AB' + A'B - A'B' = A(B + B') + A'(B - B') @@ for one pair, one plan
B = B' \Rightarrow S = 2AB = \pm 2;\ \allowbreak \ B = -B' \Rightarrow S = 2A'B = \pm 2 @@ the two cases of B and B′
\text{every }\allowbreak \text{plan }\allowbreak \text{gives } S = \pm 2 @@ so its average lies between −2 and 2
E(S) = E(a, b) + E(a, b') + E(a', b) - E(a', b') @@ averages add (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{|E(a, b) + E(a, b') + E(a', b) - E(a', b')| \le 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average of numbers in [−2, 2]
  
a = 0°,\ \allowbreak a' = 45°,\ \allowbreak b = 22.5°,\ \allowbreak b' = -22.5° @@ chosen angles
E(0, 22.5) = E(0, -22.5) = E(45, 22.5) = \cos 45° \approx 0.707 @@ E = cos 2(α − β)
E(45, -22.5) = \cos 135° \approx -0.707 @@ the last term enters with a minus
\htmlClass{new}{0.707 + 0.707 + 0.707 + 0.707 = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83 \gt 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plan’s bound is broken

Measured: in 1982 a team in Paris found the bound broken with the angles switched while the photons flew. In 2015 three teams, in Delft, Vienna and Boulder, closed the remaining gaps, and the Delft test, with electrons 1.3 km apart, found S ≈ 2.42 with an uncertainty of about 0.20. So no plan of hidden answers fits. Something is lost: either the answers did not exist before the measurement, or the choice at one lab reached the other faster than light. The physics does not decide which picture to keep; it rules out the ordinary one.

39phys4Half a pair, and no message

A system that is in state ψᵢ with chance pᵢ is described by its density grid ρ = Σ pᵢ ψᵢ ψᵢ*, a symmetric grid (31d) with stretches pᵢ when the ψᵢ are at right angles. The chance of reading d is ⟨d, ρd⟩ = Σ pᵢ|⟨ψᵢ, d⟩|². Two mixtures with the same grid give the same chances for every measurement, so nothing can tell them apart.

Half-pair technique: whatever the Seattle lab measures on its photon of Φ, the Victoria photon alone has the density grid ½I; so no choice in Seattle changes any chance in Victoria.

\text{Seattle }\allowbreak \text{measures }\allowbreak \text{at } \beta\text{: }\allowbreak \text{pass }\allowbreak \text{with }\allowbreak \text{chance } \tfrac12\text{, }\allowbreak \text{and }\allowbreak \text{then }\allowbreak \text{Victoria’s }\allowbreak \text{photon }\allowbreak \text{is } d_\beta @@ the state Φ, read with the square-chance law: both agree at the same angle
\text{stop }\allowbreak \text{with }\allowbreak \text{chance } \tfrac12\text{, }\allowbreak \text{and }\allowbreak \text{then }\allowbreak \text{Victoria’s }\allowbreak \text{photon }\allowbreak \text{is } d_\beta^\perp @@ the other arrow of the pair
\rho = \tfrac12 d_\beta d_\beta^* + \tfrac12 d_\beta^\perp d_\beta^{\perp *} @@ Victoria does not know which happened
\htmlClass{new}{\rho = \tfrac12 I\text{, }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{same }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{for }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{every } \beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ any right-angled pair d, d⊥ has dd* + d⊥d⊥* = I

½I is 31·phys’s mixed light: it passes any filter half the time. So the agreement of Φ shows up only when the two labs compare their lists, by an ordinary signal. The connection is real and cannot carry a message. That is why it breaks no rule of 29·phys about light speed.

39phys5Gates, and why they must be cold

Left alone, a qubit’s state changes by a grid that keeps every product, 31d’s product-keeper: sizes stay one, and so chances still add to one. A quantum gate is such a grid, run on purpose. Two are enough for this page. H = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]] turns |0⟩ into (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. The copy gate of 39·comp, run on arrows, sends |x, y⟩ to |x, x ⊕ y⟩.

|00\rangle \xrightarrow{H \text{ on }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{first}} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle + |10\rangle) @@ H keeps products: H*H = I
\xrightarrow{\text{copy}} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) @@ |10⟩ becomes |11⟩; the gate forgets nothing
\htmlClass{new}{= \Phi} \quad \blacksquare @@ two gates make an entangled pair

Cold-qubit technique: a qubit whose two states differ in energy by hf, left at temperature T, is found in the upper state with chance about e^(−hf/kT) when kT is far below hf. It is 30·phys’s energy-share technique on two states, and its frozen-shake technique says the same in words.

\text{a }\allowbreak \text{superconducting }\allowbreak \text{qubit }\allowbreak \text{at } f = 5\ \text{GHz}\text{: } hf = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 5 \times 10^9 \approx 3.31 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{J} @@ the gap between its two states
T = 300\ \text{K}\text{: } kT \approx 4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J} \approx 1250\, hf @@ the room shakes it at random
T = 0.02\ \text{K}\text{: } hf/kT \approx 12.0 @@ the cold of a dilution refrigerator
\htmlClass{new}{e^{-12.0} \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}} \quad \blacksquare @@ about six in a million found upper by heat alone

A gate is made by shining microwaves at the gap’s frequency for a set time: the state turns from |0⟩ toward |1⟩ at a steady rate, and a pulse of the right length is H, or a full flip. An electron’s spin in a field of 1 tesla has a gap of about 28 GHz, and is driven the same way. Such qubits keep their state for about a tenth of a millisecond, and a gate takes about twenty billionths of a second, so some five thousand gates fit before the state is lost to its surroundings. That is the engineering limit of today’s machines, and the reason they check their own errors.

39phys6Factoring by a period

To factor N, pick a below N with no common factor, and look at the remainders of a, a², a³, … by N. They come back to 1 after r steps; r is the period.

Period-factor technique: if the period r of a is even, and the remainder of a^(r/2) by N is not N − 1, then the gcd of a^(r/2) − 1 and N, and that of a^(r/2) + 1 and N, are factors of N other than 1 and N.

a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2} - 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) \text{ is }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{multiple }\allowbreak \text{of } N @@ the remainder of aʳ is 1
N \text{ divides }\allowbreak \text{neither }\allowbreak \text{factor} @@ the first since r is the least period; the second since the remainder of a^(r/2) is not N − 1
\text{so } N \text{ shares }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{factor }\allowbreak \text{other }\allowbreak \text{than } 1 \text{ and } N \text{ with }\allowbreak \text{each} @@ the one-prime-list technique (4j): N’s primes are among the two factors’ primes
\htmlClass{new}{\gcd(a^{r/2} \pm 1, N) \text{ are }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{proper }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{factors}} \quad \blacksquare @@ found fast by 4j’s gcd
  
7, 4, 13, 1 @@ the remainders of 7, 7², 7³, 7⁴ by 15
r = 4,\ \allowbreak \ 7^2 = 49\text{, }\allowbreak \text{remainder } 4 @@ even, and 4 is not 14
\htmlClass{new}{\gcd(3, 15) = 3,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \ \gcd(5, 15) = 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ 15 = 3 · 5

Finding the period is the slow part: without a quantum machine no fast way is known. With one, put a register of m qubits into an even mix of every x below 2ᵐ, compute the remainder of aˣ by N into a second register, and apply 32k’s fast transform to the first register, as a circuit of about m² gates. The size squared of the result is large only near multiples of 2ᵐ/r, so a measurement gives r after a few tries. The whole costs about n³ gates for an n-digit N. The best known method without such gates takes a number of steps that grows faster than every power of n. The questions a quantum circuit of a power of n gates answers with chance of error at most 1/3 form the class BQP. It holds BPP and sits inside PSPACE; whether it holds anything outside BPP is not proved, and factoring is the best evidence that it does.

The accepted names

A qubit’s state is written in Dirac’s notation, |ψ⟩; the square-chance law is the Born rule. An entangled state is one that is not a product state, and Φ is a Bell state, Φ⁺. The hidden-answers technique is the CHSH inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969), a form of Bell’s inequality, and its quantum value 2√2 is Tsirelson’s bound (1980). A plan of answers is a local hidden-variable theory. The density grid is the density matrix, and the half of a pair is the reduced density matrix, here the maximally mixed state; the half-pair technique is the no-signalling theorem. H is the Hadamard gate and the copy gate is CNOT. The microwave pulse drives Rabi oscillation. The period-factor technique is the classical half of Shor’s algorithm, and the transform is the quantum Fourier transform. BQP is bounded-error quantum polynomial time.

Who said it first

Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen (1935) argued quantum mechanics is incomplete; Erwin Schrödinger named entanglement the same year. John Bell proved his inequality in 1964. Alain Aspect, Philippe Grangier, Jean Dalibard and Gérard Roger tested it with switched angles in Paris in 1982. In 2015 Ronald Hanson’s group in Delft (S = 2.42 ± 0.20), Anton Zeilinger’s in Vienna and Krister Shalm’s at NIST in Boulder closed the loopholes. Aspect, John Clauser and Zeilinger shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2022. Peter Shor gave the factoring algorithm in 1994; Ethan Bernstein and Umesh Vazirani defined BQP in 1993. Max Born gave the rule in 1926.

A proof that is not allowed

“The photons agree with chance cos²(α − β), so each carries an angle λ, and passes a filter at α with chance cos²(α − λ).” Not allowed as a model of Φ: it is a plan of chances, and averaged over λ it gives E(α, β) = ½ cos 2(α − β), half the agreement Φ shows; every such plan obeys the hidden-answers bound, so it can never reach 2.83.
