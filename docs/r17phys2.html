Why Is a Rocket Mostly Fuel?

Why is a rocket mostly fuel? A rocket standing on the pad is nine-tenths propellant or more; what reaches orbit is a sliver. The reason is that a rocket must carry, and speed up, the fuel it will burn later. In 1903 Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, a schoolteacher in a Russian provincial town, found the law. Each moment the rocket throws a little mass backward and gains a little speed, by 14·phys’s momentum; adding up those gains is exactly rung 17’s area under one over x, the logarithm. This page derives the rocket equation, prices a trip to orbit, finds why rockets drop their empty tanks, and why faster exhaust is worth so much.

A rocket of mass m throws a sliver dm backward at speed u and gains dv = u·dm/m. Add the slivers: Δv = u ln(m₀/m₁), the area under 1/m from the empty mass to the full. The needed mass ratio is e to the power Δv/u, exponential in the speed asked for. Dropping empty tanks splits one huge ratio into a product of modest ones, since logs of a product add.

17phys21Throwing Mass Backward

Jean’s rocket, mass m, moving at v, throws a small mass of exhaust backward at speed u relative to itself. Nothing outside pushes; momentum is kept (14·phys).

\text{mass } \delta \text{ thrown back at } u @@ relative to the rocket
(m - \delta)\,\delta v \approx \delta \cdot u @@ momentum before = after, in the rocket’s frame
\delta v \approx u \cdot \dfrac{\delta}{m} @@ small δ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each sliver adds speed } u \text{ times the share of the mass it is}} @@ later slivers count more: m is smaller

From here on, on this page, the mass ratio is the full mass m₀ over the empty mass m₁, and Δv the speed the burn adds.

17phys22Adding Up the Slivers

\Delta v = \sum u \dfrac{\delta}{m} \to u \int_{m_1}^{m_0} \dfrac{dm}{m} @@ strips under 1/m (15c)
\int_{m_1}^{m_0} \dfrac{dm}{m} = \ln m_0 - \ln m_1 @@ the log as an area (17a)
\Delta v = u \ln \dfrac{m_0}{m_1} @@ ln x − ln y = ln(x/y) (17c)
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{m_0}{m_1} = e^{\Delta v / u}} @@ undo the log (17e)

Rocket technique: the speed a burn adds is the exhaust speed times the log of the mass ratio, so the mass ratio needed grows exponentially with the speed asked for. Nothing about how fast the fuel burns enters, only how fast the exhaust leaves.

17phys23The Price of Orbit

\Delta v \approx 9.4 \text{ km/s to low orbit} @@ orbit speed plus losses to air and gravity
u \approx 3.0 \text{ km/s} @@ kerosene burnt in oxygen
\dfrac{m_0}{m_1} = e^{9.4/3.0} = e^{3.13} \approx 23 @@ from the rocket equation
\htmlClass{new}{\text{empty mass } \tfrac{1}{23} \approx 4\% \text{ of the full}} @@ tanks, engines and payload together

To reach orbit on kerosene, 96 parts in 100 of the rocket at lift-off must be propellant. The tanks, engines and payload must fit in the other four, and tanks alone tend to weigh more than that.

17phys24Dropping Empty Tanks

Once a tank is empty, it is dead weight that later burns must still speed up. So drop it.

\Delta v = u \ln R_1 + u \ln R_2 = u \ln (R_1 R_2) @@ each stage its own ratio; logs add (17c)
R_1 R_2 = 23:\ R_1 = R_2 = \sqrt{23} \approx 4.8 @@ two modest ratios
\text{a ratio of } 4.8 \text{ leaves } 21\% \text{ for tanks and the stage above} @@ buildable
\htmlClass{new}{\text{stages turn one impossible ratio into a product of possible ones}} @@ nearly every orbital rocket stages

Because the logs of the stages’ ratios add, a ratio no single tank could reach is the product of ratios each stage can. Tsiolkovsky called the stages a rocket train.

17phys25Faster Exhaust

\text{hydrogen and oxygen: } u \approx 4.4 \text{ km/s} @@ lighter exhaust, faster
e^{9.4/4.4} = e^{2.14} \approx 8.5 @@ against 23 for kerosene
\text{ion engine: } u \approx 30 \text{ km/s, but a push of a fraction of a newton} @@ months to build speed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the ratio falls exponentially as } u \text{ rises}} @@ u sits under the exponent

Since the mass ratio is e to the Δv over u, a faster exhaust saves far more than a lighter rocket. Hydrogen is bulky and must be kept at 20 degrees above absolute zero, so engineers trade u against tank size; ion engines carry probes across the solar system on a few hundred kilograms of gas.

Accepted names

Δv = u ln(m₀/m₁) is the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation; u is the effective exhaust velocity, often given as specific impulse (u divided by g, in seconds). The speed budget is the delta-v; dropping tanks is staging; the ion engine is electric propulsion.

Who said it first

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published the equation in 1903, in “Exploration of Outer Space by Means of Reaction Devices”, and the rocket train with stages in 1929. William Moore had derived it for military rockets in Britain in 1813.
