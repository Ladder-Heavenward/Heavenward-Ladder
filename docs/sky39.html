What Do the Passes of a Landscape Say About Its Shape?

What do the passes of a landscape say about its shape? On rung 34 a falling sea uncovered an island from the top down, and peaks minus passes plus pits came out as 1, or 2 for a whole planet. This page takes the same flood to any closed shape, in any dimension. A clean flat point of a height has a number of directions in which the ground falls, and no change of coordinates can alter that number. Stand a doughnut on its end and its height has four flat points: a top, a bottom and two passes, falling two ways, one way, one way and no way. As the water rises past each flat point the dry land gains one cell, of dimension equal to the number of falling directions, so the height cuts the shape into cells, one for each flat point, and ∞15’s signed count of cells turns into peaks minus passes plus pits. The holes give more than a sum: there must be at least as many flat points of each kind as there are holes of that dimension, so no height on the doughnut has fewer than four flat points, and a closed surface with only two flat points is a sphere. In five dimensions and more, flat points can be cancelled in pairs when there is room to untangle the paths between them, and that proved in 1961 that a shape with the loops and holes of a sphere is a sphere, up to stretching. The same flat points gave spheres in seven dimensions that are spheres up to stretching but not smoothly, in 1956. In four dimensions the room runs out: whether a smooth shape that is a sphere up to stretching is a smooth sphere is still open.

At a flat point of h, the grid of second slopes has a fixed number k of falling directions, the index. A torus on its end: (R + r cos v) cos u has flat points of index 2, 1, 1, 0 at heights 3, 1, −1, −3. Passing a flat point of index k adds a k-cell, so Σ(−1)ᵏcₖ = χ: on a surface, pits − passes + peaks = χ. From cₖ = bₖ + dim Bₖ + dim Bₖ₋₁: cₖ ≥ bₖ, and the running sums obey Σⱼ≤ₖ(−1)ᵏ⁻ʲcⱼ ≥ Σⱼ≤ₖ(−1)ᵏ⁻ʲbⱼ. Two flat points: χ = 2, the sphere. Cancel pairs in dimension ≥ 5 (1961); exotic 7-spheres with two flat points (1956); smooth Poincaré in dimension 4 open.

sky39.1Falling Directions

Let h be a smooth height on a shape that looks like flat space of n dimensions near each point. A flat point is where every first slope of h vanishes; it is clean when its grid H of second slopes, in some coordinates, loses no arrow. 28l2’s grid test sorted the clean flat points of a surface into tops, bottoms and saddles, and 31e’s hug-axes technique did it in any dimension by the signs of the stretches of H. Call the number of stretches below 0, the number of directions in which the ground falls, the index.

Down-count technique: at a flat point, the index does not depend on the coordinates used.

x = \varphi(y),\ \ \tilde h = h \circ \varphi,\ \ J = \text{the grid of first slopes of } \varphi @@ new coordinates, undoable, so J is undoable
\tilde h_{y_iy_j} = \textstyle\sum_{k,l} h_{x_kx_l} J_{ki}J_{lj} + \sum_k h_{x_k}\,\partial_{y_iy_j}\varphi_k @@ the chain rule twice
h_{x_k}(p) = 0 \Rightarrow \tilde H = J^{\mathsf T} H J \text{ at } p @@ at a flat point the second term vanishes
y^{\mathsf T}\tilde H y = (Jy)^{\mathsf T} H (Jy) @@ the same form, read through z = Jy
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the counts of signs of } \tilde H \text{ and } H \text{ agree}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sign-count technique (31e2)

Away from a flat point the second slopes do change their signs with the coordinates; the first-slope term is what saves the flat point. The check: h = x² − y² has index 1; in coordinates x = x′ + 2y′, y = y′ it becomes x′² + 4x′y′ + 3y′², whose grid has rows (2, 4), (4, 6) and stretches 4 ± 2√5, one of each sign. Index 1 again. In three dimensions x² − y² + z² has index 1: a pass that falls one way and rises two. Held in trust: near a clean flat point of index k there are coordinates in which h is exactly its value minus k squares plus n − k squares.

sky39.2A Torus on Its End

A torus is the tube of radius r round a circle of radius R > r. Its points are ((R + r cos v) cos u, (R + r cos v) sin u, r sin v), for angles u and v. Laid flat on a table, its height r sin v is highest along a whole circle: those flat points are not clean. Stand it on its end instead and take the height h = (R + r cos v) cos u.

Standing-torus technique: with R = 2 and r = 1, h has exactly four flat points, all clean: (u, v) = (0, 0) of index 2 at height 3, (0, π) of index 1 at height 1, (π, π) of index 1 at height −1, and (π, 0) of index 0 at height −3.

h_u = -(R + r\cos v)\sin u,\ \ h_v = -r\sin v\cos u @@ the first slopes
h_u = 0 \iff \sin u = 0 @@ R + r cos v ≥ R − r > 0
\sin u = 0 \Rightarrow \cos u = \pm 1 \Rightarrow (h_v = 0 \iff \sin v = 0) @@ four points, u and v each 0 or π
h_{uu} = -(R + r\cos v)\cos u,\ \ h_{vv} = -r\cos v\cos u,\ \ h_{uv} = r\sin u\sin v = 0 @@ the second slopes there
(0,0)\!: (-3, -1);\ (0,\pi)\!: (-1, 1);\ (\pi,\pi)\!: (1, -1);\ (\pi,0)\!: (3, 1) @@ the diagonal entries
\htmlClass{new}{\text{indices } 2, 1, 1, 0 \text{ at heights } 3, 1, -1, -3} \quad \blacksquare @@ count the entries below 0; the down-count technique lets the angles serve as coordinates

One peak, two passes, one pit: 1 − 2 + 1 = 0, the χ of the torus on 34f’s list, and the total bend of 29f’s hole-count technique, 4π(1 − 1) = 0, says the same by another road. The pass at height −1 is on the inner ring, at the bottom of the hole; the pass at height 1 is on the inner ring at the top. A script on a grid of 720 by 720 angles finds the four points and no others.

sky39.3A Cell at Each Flat Point

34·cb18.3’s flood count let the sea fall from the top. Let it rise from the bottom instead and watch the dry land below the water line, the set where h ≤ c. Held in trust, as there: between flat points the land below the water changes only by stretching; and passing a clean flat point of index k, it changes, up to stretching, by gaining one cell of dimension k, sewn on along its rim, a sphere of dimension k − 1.

c = -1\text{: index } 1 @@ a 1-cell, both ends on the point: a loop y
c = 1\text{: index } 1 @@ another 1-cell: a second loop x, a figure of eight
c = 3\text{: index } 2 @@ a 2-cell sewn along x y x⁻¹ y⁻¹
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the torus of sky15.4, from its square word}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one corner, two edges, one square

Flat-point-count technique: if a closed shape carries a height whose flat points are all clean, and cₖ of them have index k, then c₀ − c₁ + c₂ − ⋯ is its characteristic; on a closed surface, pits − passes + peaks = χ. The height cuts the shape into cells, cₖ of dimension k. sky15.5’s signed-count technique makes c₀ − c₁ + c₂ − ⋯ the signed sum of the hole counts, which does not see the cutting, and on a surface that is 34f’s χ. 34·cb18.3 had the case of the sphere.

A surface with two holes, χ = −2, needs passes − peaks − pits = 2 more than nothing: a height on it with one peak and one pit has four passes. The count is the same for every clean height. A bump pushed up on a slope adds a peak and a pass, 1 − 1, and changes nothing, as on 34·cb18.3.

sky39.4At Least as Many Flat Points as Holes

The count of sky39.3 is a single sum, and flat points can hide in it in pairs. The holes, dimension by dimension, give more. Work with fractions as weights, as in sky15.5, so that Hₖ has dimension bₖ, the hole count. On the cells cut by the height, Cₖ has dimension cₖ.

Fewest-flat-points technique: for a clean height on a closed shape, cₖ ≥ bₖ for every k: at least as many flat points of index k as holes of dimension k.

Running-count technique: for every k, cₖ − cₖ₋₁ + cₖ₋₂ − ⋯ ± c₀ ≥ bₖ − bₖ₋₁ + bₖ₋₂ − ⋯ ± b₀, with equality when k is the top dimension.

c_j = \dim Z_j + \dim B_{j-1},\ \ b_j = \dim Z_j - \dim B_j @@ sky15.5’s lost-plus-reached line and the free count of sky15.2
c_j = b_j + \dim B_j + \dim B_{j-1} @@ subtract
c_k \ge b_k @@ dimensions are never below 0: the first technique
\textstyle\sum_{j \le k} (-1)^{k-j}(\dim B_j + \dim B_{j-1}) = \dim B_k @@ the sum telescopes; B₋₁ = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{j \le k}(-1)^{k-j}c_j = \sum_{j \le k}(-1)^{k-j}b_j + \dim B_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ ≥, and at the top Bₙ = 0, giving sky39.3 again
  
b_0, b_1, b_2 = 1, 2, 1 @@ sky15.4
c_0 \ge 1,\ c_1 \ge 2,\ c_2 \ge 1 @@ the fewest-flat-points technique
\htmlClass{new}{c_0 + c_1 + c_2 \ge 4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the standing torus is as lean as possible

No height on the torus has three clean flat points. A surface with g holes needs 2 + 2g. The bottle, with b₁ = 1 over fractions, still needs four: it has a highest and a lowest point, so c₀, c₂ ≥ 1, and then c₁ = c₀ + c₂ − χ ≥ 2. The bound counts flat points of a clean height; with flat points that are not clean, fewer may do, and then other counts take over.

sky39.5Two Flat Points Make a Sphere

A closed shape always has a lowest and a highest point, so every height on it has at least two flat points. Two is enough only for one kind of shape.

Two-flat-points technique: a closed surface with a clean height that has exactly two flat points is the sphere.

\text{the two are the lowest point and the highest} @@ both are flat points
c_0 = 1,\ c_1 = 0,\ c_2 = 1 @@ a pit has index 0, a peak index 2
\chi = 1 - 0 + 1 = 2 @@ the flat-point-count technique
\text{on 34f’s list: } \chi = 2 - 2g \text{ for } g \text{ tori},\ 2 - k \text{ for } k \text{ cross-caps} @@ the surface-list technique
\htmlClass{new}{\chi = 2 \Rightarrow \text{the sphere}} \quad \blacksquare @@ g = 0 and no cross-caps

In every dimension the same holds up to stretching: a closed shape with a clean height having exactly two flat points is a sphere up to stretching, since it is a cell of the top dimension sewn onto a point. Held in trust. Smoothly it need not be the round sphere. In 1956 shapes of dimension 7 were built, each with a height having exactly two flat points, so each a sphere up to stretching; a count from four dimensions higher showed that some of them cannot be smoothly the round one. Spheres that are spheres only up to stretching are called strange spheres here. In 1963 the strange spheres of dimension 7 were counted: with the round one, 28.

sky39.6Cancelling Passes, and Dimension Four

34·now told the question of 1904 and its fate: settled in five dimensions and more in 1961, in four in 1982, in three in 2002–03. The proof of 1961 is made of flat points.

Two flat points of indices k and k + 1, joined by exactly one path of steepest descent, can be cancelled: the height can be changed near that path so that both disappear and nothing else changes. Stated, with its idea: the land after the pair is the land before it, a k-cell and a (k + 1)-cell sewn on so that the second fills the first exactly once. The running-count technique of sky39.4 says how many pairs there are to cancel. Making the paths meet exactly once is the hard part: the sheets that flow down from one flat point and up from the other must be pushed apart where they cross wrongly, in pairs. In five dimensions and more there is room to push one sheet off another. Then a shape with the holes and loops of a sphere carries a height with two flat points, and sky39.5 makes it a sphere up to stretching.

n = 2 @@ sky39.5 and 34now1: loops decide
n = 3 @@ settled 2002–03 by the flow of 34now
n \ge 5\text{: room to cancel; up to stretching, settled 1961} @@ smoothly, strange spheres exist: 28 in dimension 7
n = 4\text{: up to stretching, settled 1982, by other means} @@ the sheets cannot be pushed apart; the 1982 proof works around them
\htmlClass{new}{n = 4\text{, smoothly: open}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no strange sphere of dimension 4 is known, and none is ruled out

Dimension four is strange in another way. Flat space of four dimensions itself has smooth structures that are not the usual one: infinitely many were found in 1985. In every other dimension flat space has only one. The tools that found them came from physics, through equations of fields on four-dimensional shapes, and they see smooth structure that cells and holes cannot.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞39.oq.1Is every smooth four-dimensional shape that is a sphere up to stretching smoothly the round sphere? In every dimension but four the answer is known. In four the 1982 proof gives the sphere up to stretching and says nothing about smoothness. Candidates have been proposed, and several have been shown to be round after all; the tools that see smooth structure in four dimensions have not yet told any of them apart.
∞39.oq.2How many smooth structures does the four-sphere carry? It could be one, or infinitely many, as for flat four-space. No one knows a single strange one, and no one can prove there is none.
∞39.oq.3Which four-dimensional shapes can be made smooth? Cells and holes can be listed for shapes that are smooth and for shapes that are only shapes up to stretching, and some lists occur only in the second kind. An old guess says exactly which lists of a certain kind can be smooth; it is proved with a margin (2018) and open in between.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Counting flat points to learn about shape began in the 1920s, with inequalities between the numbers of flat points of each index and the holes, proved for heights on shapes of any dimension.

In 1956 the method was used to build something nobody expected: smooth shapes of dimension 7 that are spheres up to stretching but not smoothly. The existence of a height with two flat points made them spheres; a count of smooth invariants made them strange. In 1963 their number was found to be 28. Smoothness, which had seemed a technical convenience, turned out to carry information of its own.

In 1961 flat points were cancelled in pairs to settle the 1904 question in dimensions five and up, and high dimensions turned out easier than low ones, because there is room. The general method, the cancelling of pairs, became a theory of building shapes from cells that is still the standard tool. Dimension three waited until 2002–03 and a different method (34·now).

Dimension four was settled up to stretching in 1982, by a different method that works around the missing room. The next year, equations from physics showed that many four-dimensional shapes cannot be smooth, and soon that flat four-space has strange smooth structures. Up to stretching four dimensions behaves like five; smoothly it behaves like nothing else. Prizes followed in 1986.

What is argued now is whether the smooth four-sphere has strange twins. Some expect one to be found among the many candidates; others point out that every candidate checked so far has turned out round, and that the tools may be too weak rather than the answer being no. A proof either way would need a new way of seeing smooth structure in four dimensions.

The accepted names

A clean flat point is a nondegenerate critical point; a height whose flat points are all clean is a Morse function, and the index is the Morse index. That it does not depend on coordinates is Sylvester’s law of inertia applied to the Hessian; the held-in-trust normal form is the Morse lemma. The land below the water is a sublevel set, and gaining a cell at each flat point is the handle attachment of Morse theory. The flat-point-count technique is the Morse form of the Poincaré–Hopf count; the fewest-flat-points and running-count techniques are the weak and strong Morse inequalities. The two-flat-points technique is Reeb’s sphere theorem, for surfaces. Strange spheres are exotic spheres; Milnor’s 7-spheres, and the 28 of Kervaire and Milnor. Cancelling pairs is handle cancellation, the pushing apart is the Whitney trick, and the 1961 result is the generalized Poincaré conjecture, proved through the h-cobordism theorem. The four-dimensional question is the smooth Poincaré conjecture in dimension 4; the strange flat spaces are exotic ℝ⁴s; the equations from physics are gauge theory (Yang–Mills, later Seiberg–Witten). The guess of oq.3 is the 11/8 conjecture, proved with 10/8 + 4.

Who said it first

Marston Morse developed the theory in the 1920s. Georges Reeb proved the sphere theorem. John Milnor built the exotic 7-spheres (Annals of Mathematics, 1956), using a height with two flat points; Michel Kervaire and John Milnor counted them (1963). Stephen Smale proved the generalized Poincaré conjecture in dimensions five and up (1961). Michael Freedman settled dimension four up to stretching (1982), and Simon Donaldson’s gauge theory showed the smooth obstructions (1983); both received Fields Medals in 1986. Robert Gompf found infinitely many exotic ℝ⁴s (1985). Michael Hopkins, Jianfeng Lin, XiaoLin Danny Shi and Zhouli Xu proved 10/8 + 4 (2018).
