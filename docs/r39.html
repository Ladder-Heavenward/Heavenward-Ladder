A machine made of switches

Rung 1 wrote tables of true and false, and every rung since has reasoned with them. This rung builds them out of matter. First it writes true as 1 and false as 0, so that both is a product and either the greater, and reads 1 and 0 as a wire that is high or low: the sign of a turning, a wave that is only on or off. The same wave on a wire can carry power or a message, and the two are told apart. Then the rung builds the switches. A one-way valve gives either and both, but no not and no way to clean a weak level. A switch worked by a voltage gives a not with a slope above 1 in the middle, and that slope is what keeps noise from piling up along a chain. From not-and alone every table follows. A not fed into itself has no rest: it is the liar statement, and it is a clock. Two nots in a ring rest in one of two states: it is a memory. With gates, a clock and a memory the rung builds an adder, and then a machine that follows a list of instructions kept as numbers in its own memory. Then it counts the turns the machine takes: how the count grows with the size of the job, how fast a list can be searched and sorted, how a job split in pieces is counted, how data is kept findable, and how answers kept once save work done again. The last steps wire tables and count steps by hand.

True is 1 and false is 0, on a wire high or low. A switch worked by a switch, with a slope above 1, builds a not that cleans its level; from not-and alone, every table. An odd ring of nots is a clock, an even ring a memory. Gates, a clock and a memory make an adder, and then a machine that follows a list of numbers. Its turns are counted, and how they grow with the job decides what can be done.

What it means to say it

To say that a machine is made of switches is to say that the tables of rung 1 can be built in matter: a wire high or low is true or false, a switch worked by another switch is a gate, and gates wired in rings and rows give every table, a clock and a memory. From those, a machine that keeps its instructions as numbers in its own memory follows any list it is given, and the number of turns it takes on a job of size n can be counted and ranked.

Every table of n inputs is built from not-and gates alone, made of switches worked by switches whose slope above 1 keeps every level clean; an odd ring of nots is a clock and an even ring a memory; with them a machine runs any list of instructions stored as numbers, and the turns it takes grow in a family that a change of machine does not change. The rung proves it, and one step of it is held in trust where it says so.

Where it comes from

In 1847 and 1854 the reasoning of rung 1 was written as an algebra of 1 and 0. In 1886 it was noticed that switches in circuits could compute it, and in 1937 a thesis showed that relay circuits are exactly that algebra. The valve of 1904 and the switch of 1947, made of silicon from the 1950s, gave fast switches; in 1918 two valves in a ring were found to hold a bit, and in 1945 a design kept a machine’s instructions in its own memory as numbers. From 1946 onward the counting of a program’s turns grew into a subject, with binary search, merge sort, and in 1968 a long work that made counting turns a discipline. The capacity of a noisy wire was found in 1948. The names are in the basements of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things.

First, with true as 1 and false as 0, both is a product, either the greater, not one take away, and exactly one a remainder by two. A wave read as high or low is the sign of a turning, and its first turning alone keeps every bit; a level read against a threshold is wrong at a rate the noise sets.

Second, a wave on a wire carries power, the sum of the powers of its turnings, or a message, which a known wave lacks however strong; a wire passing frequencies up to B carries at most 2B free values a second.

Third, a one-way valve gives either and both but never a not, and never makes a level cleaner; a switch worked by a voltage gives a not, and a slope above 1 at its middle pulls every level back to 0 or 1, so noise along a chain stays bounded. Not-and alone builds every table, though most tables need many gates.

Fourth, a not fed into itself has no rest: an odd ring is a clock, of period twice the number of gates times their delay. An even ring rests in one of two states and holds a bit; a pair of latches catches a bit at the rise of a clock.

Fifth, gates add: three bits in, two out, a carry that ripples or a tree of carries about log₂ n deep, and subtraction by turning bits over and adding one. A machine whose instructions are numbers in its own memory follows any list, and can change the list it runs.

Sixth, its turns can be counted, and the family of the count survives a change of machine; no search by yes-or-no questions beats halving, a sort need not compare when its items are small whole numbers, and a sort by random pivots, a heap, a search tree and a table of slots each have a count that can be proved.

Seventh, a rule for a count in pieces can be solved, by adding the levels, by shares below one, or by guessing and checking; and answers kept once save calls repeated, while a greedy choice is best for some jobs and fails for others.

Anexact, yet rigorous. One box on this rung. 39b carries laws with a known exception rate: every reading of a wire is a guess, since the voltage is a crowd of causes no one tracks; the bell of the noise does not make the wire exact, it makes the error exact, one misread in a billion at six spreads of margin. The reading is wrong at a rate, and the rate is known.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 39p wires a table described in words into a small circuit of not-ands, and checks it row by row. 39q counts the steps of a program by reading its loops and calls, and says from which size on the fastest term rules. The annexes ask why silicon has a gap, why chips stopped getting faster, whether a neuron is a gate, and how little energy a switch can spend. Rung 40 makes the machine exact and asks what no machine can do.

What you can do because you know it

You can read a wire as true or false and say how often the reading is wrong. You can tell a wire that carries power from one that carries a message. You can say why a not needs a switch with gain and a valve will not do, build any table from not-ands, find the period of a ring and the bit an even ring holds, and add and subtract with gates. You can write a program as numbers for a machine that follows a list, count its turns, and rank the count. You can search, sort and keep data with proved counts, and solve a rule for a count in pieces.

And you can say exactly what those results do not show: not that a faster machine makes every job possible, but that a count growing as 2ⁿ gains only a few items for each doubling of speed.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down that is not measured. The rung spends rung 1’s tables, rung 4’s numerals and sums, rung 17’s growth by equal factors, rung 22’s families of growth and rules for counts, rung 24’s states that hold, rung 30’s chance, and rung 32’s turnings and samples. It takes from measurement the law of a silicon diode and of a switch worked by a voltage. One thing is held in trust: that every reasonable machine mimics every other within a fixed factor or a power, until rung 40 makes machines exact; and 39c uses 30·comp’s capacity of a noisy wire, held in trust there. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say a machine is made of switches, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
