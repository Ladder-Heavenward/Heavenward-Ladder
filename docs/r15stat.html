15·stat · statistics

How Likely Is Landing in a Range?

How likely is landing in a range? 4·stat's chance counted equally likely cases: two heads in 6 of the 16 ways four coins can fall. That works when the cases can be listed. A wait for a bus, a length of trout, the sum of two readings: these land anywhere in an interval, and the cases cannot be listed. Each single value is so unlikely that its chance must be 0, and yet some value always happens. 9·stat drew such data as a histogram and asked, among its Make questions, where its outline heads as the bins grow narrow. This page makes the bins narrow, and finds that the bars' total over a range heads to an area. So the chance of landing in a range is read as the area over it, and rung 15's areas start doing statistics.

Draw each bar so that its area is the share of the data in its bin. As the bins grow narrow, the bars’ tops head to a curve. The chance of landing in a range is then the area under the curve over that range; the curve is never negative, and its whole area is 1. The running total, the area from the left end up to a point, is the share at or below that point, and the chance of a range is the running total at its right end less the total at its left.

Bars that are areas

9·stat drew a histogram with bars as tall as their counts. Draw each bar instead at height count/(n · w), for n values in bins of width w. A bar is a box, so its area is its height times its width (15a): count/n, the share of the data in its bin. The counts add to n (bins), so the areas add to 1. The share of the data in a range made of whole bins is the area of the bars over it (areas-add). Its accepted name is a density histogram.

\text{height} = \tfrac{\text{count}}{n w} @@ n values, bins of width w
\text{area} = \text{height} \cdot w = \tfrac{\text{count}}{n} @@ a box's area (15a): the bin's share of the data
\text{the areas add to } 1 @@ bins (9·stat): the counts add to n

Draw each bar so that its area, not its height, is its share of the data. Then bins of different widths can stand side by side honestly, which 9·stat warned they could not when drawn by count.

Making the bars thin

Take two random numbers from 0 up to 1, and add them. With one decimal place each, a number is one of 0, 0.1, …, 0.9. Take all 100 pairs as equally likely, as 4·stat took the 36 throws of two dice. A sum of s tenths comes from s + 1 pairs when s ≤ 9, and from 19 − s pairs when s ≥ 9. Bin the sums by tenths, w = 0.1, and draw density bars.

\text{one place: height at } x = \tfrac{s}{10} \text{ is } \tfrac{s + 1}{100 \cdot 0.1} = x + 0.1 @@ for s ≤ 9: count over nw
\text{m places: height at } x \text{ is } x + 10^{-m} \text{ for } x \lt 1 @@ 10²ᵐ pairs, s + 1 of them with sum s · 10⁻ᵐ, bins 10⁻ᵐ wide
\text{and } 2 - x - 10^{-m} \text{ for } x \ge 1 @@ the same count, from the other end
\htmlClass{new}{f(x) = x \text{ on } [0, 1],\ \ f(x) = 2 - x \text{ on } [1, 2]} @@ the bars' tops come within 10⁻ᵐ of this curve

The bars over a range [u, v] are a strip sum of a curve that lies within 10⁻ᵐ of f. As the bins narrow, narrow-strips (15h) says strip sums of the continuous f head to ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx. The extra 10⁻ᵐ adds at most 10⁻ᵐ(v − u + 2 · 10⁻ᵐ) to the bars' area, which heads to 0 too. So the chance counted over the pairs heads to the area.

\textstyle\int_{0.5}^{1} x\,dx = \tfrac{1^2 - 0.5^2}{2} = 0.375 @@ area-under-a-power (15i), on [0.5, 1]
\text{one place: pairs with sum } 0.5 \text{ to } 0.9 \text{: } 6 + 7 + 8 + 9 + 10 = 40 @@ 40 of 100: the counted chance, coarse
\begin{aligned}&\text{m places: the counted}\\ &\quad\text{chance heads to } 0.375\end{aligned} @@ narrow-strips

The chance of landing in a range is the area over it.

A curve as a model of chance

From here on, a density on [c, d] is a function f, integrable on [c, d], with f(x) ≥ 0 there and ∫ᶜᵈ f(x) dx = 1. A quantity lands by f when, for c ≤ u ≤ v ≤ d, its chance of landing in [u, v] is ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx. Accepted names: a probability density function; the quantity is a continuous random variable.

The tent f above was counted. For a real quantity, which f it follows is not proved. It is a guess, checked against a histogram, the way 4·stat took cases as equally likely and checked them against tallies.

Range-chance technique: let f be a density on [c, d]. Every range in [c, d] has a chance from 0 to 1. Two ranges that meet only at an end have chances that add. A single value has chance 0.

0 \le \textstyle\int_u^v f @@ smaller-function-smaller-area (15e): f ≥ 0
\textstyle\int_c^d f = \int_c^u f + \int_u^v f + \int_v^d f @@ interval-cut-in-two (15e), twice
\textstyle\int_u^v f \le \int_c^d f = 1 @@ the other two pieces are at least 0
\textstyle\int_u^v f + \int_v^e f = \int_u^e f @@ interval-cut-in-two: ranges meeting at b add
f \le M \text{ on } [c, d] @@ integrable means bounded (15c)
\textstyle\int_u^{u + w} f \le Mw \text{ for every } w \gt 0 @@ smaller-function-smaller-area
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the chance of exactly } u \text{ is } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ it is at most every Mw; and ∫ₐᵃ f = 0 (15e)

So [u, v] and the range without its ends have the same chance. A height of f is not a chance. A density can be taller than 1 where it is narrow: f = 3 on [0, ⅓] is a density.

The area so far

From here on, the running total of a density f on [c, d] is F(x) = ∫ᶜˣ f(t) dt, for c ≤ x ≤ d. It is the chance of landing at most x: 9·stat's running total, divided by n, read as an area. Accepted name: the cumulative distribution function. F is a name, for a function.

Area-so-far technique: F starts at 0 and ends at 1, never goes down, and has no gaps. The chance of [u, v] is F(v) − F(u). Some m has F(m) = ½.

F(c) = 0,\ \ F(d) = 1 @@ ∫ᶜᶜ f = 0 (15e); f is a density
F(v) = F(u) + \textstyle\int_u^v f @@ interval-cut-in-two
F(v) - F(u) = \textstyle\int_u^v f \ge 0 @@ smaller-function-smaller-area: F never goes down
0 \le F(v) - F(u) \le M(v - u) @@ f ≤ M: F is continuous, by the squeeze (10d)
F(c) \lt \tfrac12 \lt F(d) \;\Rightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{F(m) = \tfrac12} \text{ for some } m @@ in-between-value (12a) ∎

For the tent, F(x) = x²/2 on [0, 1], and F(x) = 1 − (2 − x)²/2 on [1, 2], by area-under-a-power on each piece. The chance of [0.5, 1.5] is F(1.5) − F(0.5) = 0.875 − 0.125 = 0.75. The m with F(m) = ½ is 1, the median.

A bus stop in Salinas

A bus comes every 20 minutes, and a rider arrives without the timetable. A guess: the wait lands by f = 1/20 on [0, 20]. The chance of waiting more than 15 minutes is ∫ from 15 to 20 of 1/20, which is ¼.

Now the rider changes to a second bus, also every 20 minutes, and the two waits add. Scale the tent by 20: the total lands by f(x) = x/400 on [0, 20] and (40 − x)/400 on [20, 40]. The scaling is safe: on a range [A, B] inside [0, 20], the tent's area over [A/20, B/20] and this f's area over [A, B] both come to (B² − A²)/800, by area-under-a-power.

\textstyle\int_{30}^{40} \tfrac{40 - x}{400}\,dx = \tfrac{1}{400}\Big(400 - \tfrac{40^2 - 30^2}{2}\Big) @@ areas-add, areas-scale, area-under-a-power
= \tfrac{50}{400} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac18} @@ more than half an hour in all, about one trip in 8

One wait is as likely short as long. Two waits added are likeliest near 20 minutes, and seldom either very short or very long.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting

4k set beside counting a principle of the second kind of care: random things in a crowd make smooth, steady patterns. One rider's total wait can be anything from 0 to 40 minutes. A thousand riders' waits pile into a tent, and the tent was counted before anyone waited.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it.

“About 20 minutes” is the middle. The tent says how often the middle misses, and by how much: one trip in 8 waits more than half an hour.

A rider says the second bus “always” makes her wait half an hour. Of her last 40 trips, 11 waited that long. Is that the tent, or something else?

Who said it first

Simpson argued that astronomers should take the mean of many observations rather than trust one careful one. To show it, he supposed each error lands anywhere in a range, likelier the smaller it is, by a curve rising in a straight line to the middle and falling the same way, and he found by areas the chance that the mean errs by more than a given amount.

Thomas Simpson, “On the advantage of taking the mean of a number of observations” (1756–1757); paraphrase

Abraham de Moivre first read a chance as an area under a curve, in 1733, for the counts of many coins. Karl Pearson named the histogram in his lectures of 1891. In 15·stat.4.4, the mathematician of 1928 is Richard von Mises, in Wahrscheinlichkeit, Statistik und Wahrheit, and the one of 1931 is Bruno de Finetti, who later wrote that probability does not exist.

Accepted names: the density histogram; a probability density function; the cumulative distribution function for F; the uniform distribution for the bus's 1/20; a continuous random variable. That the shares in a long run settle near the chances is the law of large numbers.
