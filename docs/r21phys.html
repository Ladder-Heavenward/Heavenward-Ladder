Can Forces Be Added Like Arrows?

Can forces be added like arrows? Two people pull a stuck boat with two ropes, at an angle to each other. How hard does the boat feel pulled, and which way? Long before anyone could say why, sailors and builders treated a pull as an arrow, and found that two arrows laid end to end give the pull of both. What makes it hard is that a push at a slant does two jobs at once, and only cos and sin can say how much of each. This page shows, with 21’s cos and sin and 21g’s slopes, how a block stays on a slope, why a car on a curve needs friction, and how a tilted road can take its place.

A force at an angle is an arrow whose across part is its size times the cosine and whose up part is its size times the sine, and forces add part by part. On a slope, weight splits into a part along it, by the sine, and a part into it, by the cosine, so a block holds while the slope’s tangent is no more than the grip of the surface. Going round a circle needs a pull toward the centre of the speed squared over the radius, for each unit of mass. A stone thrown at a slant runs on both parts at once, steady across and slowed going up, and over level ground lands farthest at 45°.

21phys1Arrows Add Part by Part

18·eng took as a measured law that forces add as displacements add. A force of size F pointing at angle θ from east has parts F cos θ east and F sin θ north. Arrow technique: to add forces, add their parts across and their parts up; the size of the sum comes from the cosine-side technique.

300 \text{ N at } 0^\circ:\ (300,\ 0) @@ cos 0 = 1, sin 0 = 0
400 \text{ N at } 60^\circ:\ (400 \cdot \tfrac12,\ 400 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) = (200,\ 346.4) @@ the parts (21b)
\text{sum}:\ (500,\ 346.4),\ \ \text{size } \sqrt{500^2 + 346.4^2} \approx 608.3 \text{ N} @@ part by part; distance (18a)
\htmlClass{new}{300^2 + 400^2 + 2 \cdot 300 \cdot 400 \cos 60^\circ = 370\,000 \approx 608.3^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cosine-side technique (21h), with the angle between the arrows laid end to end, 120°

21phys2A Block on a Slope

A block of weight W rests on a slope at angle α. Turn the arrows so one part runs along the slope and one into it. Weight splits into W sin α pulling down the slope and W cos α pressing into it. A measured law says that a dry surface can hold back a slide with a force of at most μ times the press into it, where μ is fixed for the two surfaces.

\text{along}: W\sin\alpha,\ \ \text{into}: W\cos\alpha @@ next-and-facing (21b), on the weight
\text{holds} \iff W\sin\alpha \le \mu\,W\cos\alpha @@ the most friction can hold back
\htmlClass{new}{\text{holds} \iff \tan\alpha \le \mu} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by W cos α, positive for α below a right angle

Slope technique: a block holds on a slope exactly when the tangent of the slope’s angle is at most μ, whatever it weighs. Dry gravel has μ near 0.7, and tan 35° ≈ 0.70. A pile of gravel at a quarry near Stockton stands at about 35°, however high it is heaped.

21phys3Going Round a Circle

A point goes round a circle of radius r, turning ω of a radian each second: at time t it is at (r cos ωt, r sin ωt). Its speed is v = rω. Take slopes twice.

p(t) = (r\cos\omega t,\ r\sin\omega t) @@ the point at time t
p'(t) = (-r\omega\sin\omega t,\ r\omega\cos\omega t) @@ the slopes of sin and cos (21g), and the chain rule
p''(t) = -\omega^2\,(r\cos\omega t,\ r\sin\omega t) = -\omega^2\,p(t) @@ again
\htmlClass{new}{|p''| = r\omega^2 = \tfrac{v^2}{r},\ \text{toward the centre}} \quad \blacksquare @@ −p(t) points at the centre

Round technique: going round a circle at speed v takes a pull of v²/r per unit of mass, toward the centre. On a flat road, only friction can give it. A car at 20 m/s on a curve of radius 50 m needs 400/50 = 8 m/s² toward the centre, and friction gives at most μ times g ≈ 9.8 m/s², so it needs μ ≥ 0.82: a dry road holds it, and a wet one may not.

21phys4A Road Tilted for Its Curve

Tilt the road at angle α toward the inside of the curve. The road pushes on the car square to its surface, with a push N, which has parts N sin α toward the centre and N cos α up. With no friction at all, the up part must hold the weight, and the inward part must give the pull.

N\cos\alpha = mg,\ \ N\sin\alpha = \tfrac{m v^2}{r} @@ up, and toward the centre (21·phys.3)
\tfrac{N\sin\alpha}{N\cos\alpha} = \tfrac{v^2}{r g} @@ divide
\htmlClass{new}{\tan\alpha = \tfrac{v^2}{r g}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the right tilt for speed v, needing no friction

Tilt technique: a curve tilted so that tan α = v²/(rg) holds a car at speed v with no friction at all. The tilt is set for one speed. Slower cars lean on friction to keep from sliding in; faster ones lean on it to keep from sliding out.

21phys6A Stone Thrown at a Slant

A stone leaves the hand at speed v, at an angle θ above level. By the arrow technique (21·phys.1) its speed is two arrows: v cos θ across and v sin θ up. Weight pulls only down, so nothing acts on the across part, and it keeps its size: the uniform motion of 9·phys. The up part is 13·phys’s stone thrown upward, slowed by g ≈ 9.8 m/s² and brought back down. The two parts run on one clock, and neither touches the other.

Slant-throw technique: a stone thrown at speed v and angle θ over level ground lands v² sin 2θ/g away, so it goes farthest at 45°, and two angles that add to 90° land it at the same place.

x = v\cos\theta\ t,\ \allowbreak \ y = v\sin\theta\ t - \tfrac12 g t^2 @@ across: no force; up: 13·phys’s thrown stone
y = 0:\ \ t = \tfrac{2v\sin\theta}{g} @@ the time aloft: the root other than t = 0
x = v\cos\theta \cdot \tfrac{2v\sin\theta}{g} = \tfrac{v^2}{g}\,2\sin\theta\cos\theta @@ put the time into the across part
= \tfrac{v^2}{g}\sin 2\theta @@ the double technique (21d4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{greatest }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{at } 2\theta = 90^\circ:\ \theta = 45^\circ,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{range } \tfrac{v^2}{g}} \quad \blacksquare @@ sin is at most 1; sin(180° − 2θ) = sin 2θ (21e3) gives the pairs

At 20 m/s a throw at 45° lands 400/9.8 ≈ 40.8 m away. Throws at 30° and at 60° both land 35.3 m away, by two very different roads: the 60° throw stays up 3.53 s against 2.04 s, and climbs three times as high, 15.3 m against 5.1 m. Take t out of the two parts and y = x tan θ − gx²/(2v² cos² θ): the path is a parabola, the cut cone of 18·cb16. Air drags on both parts at once and spoils the split. A ball hit hard flies farthest well below 45°, and how far below, this page cannot find without a law for the drag.

The accepted names

The parts of a force are its components, and the sum the resultant. The push of a surface square to it is the normal force, μ is the coefficient of friction, and the steepest slope that holds loose material is its angle of repose. v²/r is the centripetal acceleration; a road tilted for its curve is banked. The outward pull felt on the hand is the centrifugal force. The flight of a thrown body is projectile motion, its across and up parts the horizontal and vertical components of the velocity, and v² sin 2θ/g the range equation.

Who said it first

Simon Stevin’s wreath of balls (1586) gave the slope’s split of weight. Guillaume Amontons (1699) and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1785) gave the laws of dry friction; Leonardo da Vinci had found them in notebooks no one read. Christiaan Huygens (1659, published 1673) found v²/r and called the force centrifugal, fleeing the centre. In 21·phys.7.5, Isaac Newton turned it round (Principia, 1687): the force is centripetal, seeking the centre, and the outward pull is the stone’s tendency to go straight on. Galileo Galilei showed in 1638 that a thrown body’s across and up motions run separately, that its path is a parabola, and that over level ground 45° throws farthest.
