47a · proof

Things and Arrows

Rung nine built functions and the two facts about running them one after another: brackets do not matter, and the function that changes nothing changes nothing on either side. Since then the ladder has met the same two facts again and again, with new things and new maps between them. Groups and the maps that keep products. Spaces and the continuous maps. Spaces of arrows and the maps that keep adding and stretching. Each time the two facts were proved again for the new maps, and each time the proof was the same proof. This step stops proving it again. It writes down the two facts once, as a kind of thing of their own: a collection of things, a collection of arrows from thing to thing, a way to run two arrows in a row, and an arrow that does nothing at each thing. Then a group turns out to be such a collection with only one thing in it, and an order turns out to be one with at most one arrow between any two things. And it asks what it means for two things to be the same when only the arrows are looked at. The answer depends on the arrows, not on the things, and a step of rung thirty four already showed why that matters.

A category is things, arrows between them, a way to run two arrows in a row that does not care about brackets, and a do-nothing arrow at each thing. Sets and functions, groups and the maps that keep products, spaces and continuous maps are categories. A group is a category with one thing; an order is a category with at most one arrow between two things. Two things are matched when an arrow between them can be undone, and which arrows count decides which things are the same.

47a1Arrows That Run in a Row

A category C is four things. A collection of things. For any two things A and B, a collection C(A, B) of arrows from A to B, each written f : A → B, with 4d’s mark. For arrows f : A → B and g : B → D, an arrow g ∘ f : A → D, read “g after f”, as in 9b. And for each thing A an arrow id_A : A → A. Two rules hold. h ∘ (g ∘ f) = (h ∘ g) ∘ f whenever the arrows meet end to start, and f ∘ id_A = f = id_B ∘ f for every f : A → B. Category is this step’s one academy word: from the Greek katēgoria, what can be said of a thing. The arrows need not be functions, and the things need not be sets. The word arrow is reused here: on rung 27 an arrow was a member of a space, and here it is a map.

Sets with functions are a category, written Set: 9b3 gave the brackets rule and 9b4 the one-unit technique. Groups with the maps that keep products, Grp: 34g2 named those maps, and a map that keeps products after one that keeps products keeps products. Spaces with continuous maps, Top: 34b2’s pull-back-chain technique. Spaces of arrows over a field k with linear maps, Vect: 27d’s product-is-doing technique. In each, ∘ and id are 9b’s, and the rules come free. The things of Set are not the members of any set: every part of a set of every set would be one of its members, against 35c’s smaller-than-its-parts technique. So a category is described, as a collection, and nothing below needs its things gathered into one set.

One-do-nothing technique: each thing of a category has exactly one arrow that does nothing on both sides.

e, e' : A \to A,\ \ f \circ e = f \text{ and } e \circ g = g \text{ for every } f \text{ out of } A,\ g \text{ into } A @@ suppose two arrows each do nothing on both sides
e' \circ e = e' @@ e does nothing on the right; take f = e′
e' \circ e = e @@ e′ does nothing on the left; take g = e
\htmlClass{new}{e = e'} \quad \blacksquare @@ chain the two lines (0b)

That is 19b1’s one-identity proof, word for word, with “member” turned into “arrow”. Nothing in it used that the arrows were moves of a group. That is the point of the category: a proof that uses only the two rules is proved once, for every category at once.

47a2A Group Is a Category With One Thing

Take a group G. Make a category with one thing, call it ⋆. Its arrows ⋆ → ⋆ are the members of G. g after h is the product g h. id_⋆ is the member that does nothing. 19a’s brackets rule is the category’s brackets rule, and the member that does nothing is the do-nothing arrow. Every arrow of it has an undoing. Turned round, a category with one thing in which every arrow can be undone is a group: its arrows, multiplied by ∘. A category is a group spread out over many things.

Take a set X with a relation ≤ on it, as 2h11. Make a category with the members of X as things, and one arrow from x to y exactly when x ≤ y, and none otherwise. There is nothing to choose in composing: there is at most one arrow x → z.

Order-category technique: a relation on a set makes a category, with one arrow from x to y exactly when x is related to y, exactly when the relation is reflexive and transitive.

\text{an arrow } x \to y \text{ and one } y \to z \text{ need an arrow } x \to z @@ g ∘ f must exist
x \le y \land y \le z \Rightarrow x \le z @@ that is transitive (2h12)
\text{each } x \text{ needs } \mathrm{id}_x : x \to x,\ \text{so } x \le x @@ that is reflexive (2h12)
\text{at most one arrow between two things: both sides of each rule are the one arrow} @@ the brackets and do-nothing rules ask for two arrows x → w to be equal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reflexive and transitive} \iff \text{a category}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last line runs the other way: the arrows then exist and the rules are automatic

The statements of rung 1, with an arrow P → Q when P ⇒ Q is always true, make such a category: reflexive, since P ⇒ P, and transitive by 1d’s carry an if through, again and again. The divisibility relation on the counting numbers of 4j makes another. Neither need keep things apart: P and ¬¬P point at each other once rung 7 is in hand, and are still two statements.

47a3Matched by an Arrow

An arrow f : A → B is undoable when some g : B → A has g ∘ f = id_A and f ∘ g = id_B. Then A and B are matched in C, and from here on A ≅ B says so. 20d4 wrote ≅ for congruent sets of points; here the mark says that A and B are matched in C. In Set the undoable arrows are the bijections, by 9d3’s there-and-back technique, and ≅ is 9c’s matching. In Grp they are the matchings that keep products. In an order with x ≤ y ≤ x, the two arrows undo each other.

One-undoing technique: an undoable arrow has exactly one undoing, and being matched is a sameness relation among the things of a category.

g, g' \text{ both undo } f : A \to B @@ suppose two
g = g \circ \mathrm{id}_B = g \circ (f \circ g') = (g \circ f) \circ g' = \mathrm{id}_A \circ g' = g' @@ the two rules, and nothing else (19b2’s proof)
\mathrm{id}_A \text{ undoes itself, so } A \cong A @@ reflexive
f \text{ undone by } g \Rightarrow g \text{ undone by } f,\ \text{so } A \cong B \Rightarrow B \cong A @@ the definition is symmetric in f and g
\htmlClass{new}{(f' \circ f) \text{ undone by } (g \circ g'),\ \text{so } A \cong B \cong D \Rightarrow A \cong D} \quad \blacksquare @@ socks and shoes (19c1): undo the last arrow first

Now take spaces. 34e4 found a continuous map that is a matching of members and not a same-shape map: t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) from [0, 1) onto the circle. It is one-to-one and onto, so its undoing exists in Set. In Top no undoing exists, since the backwards map is not continuous at (1, 0). The members are the same members, and the arrow is the same function. Whether it is undoable depends on which arrows the category has. Sameness, in a category, belongs to the arrows.

The accepted names

Things are objects and arrows are morphisms; C(A, B) is the hom-set, often Hom(A, B). g ∘ f is composition and id_A the identity morphism. An undoable arrow is an isomorphism, and matched things are isomorphic. A category with one object is a monoid; one in which every arrow is an isomorphism is a groupoid, and a one-object groupoid is a group. A category with at most one arrow between two objects is a preorder, or thin category. When the objects form a set the category is small; the category of all sets is large, and is handled with classes or with universes.

Who said it first

Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane defined categories in their paper “General theory of natural equivalences” of 1945, written to make exact what “natural” meant in algebraic topology. They said later that they defined categories only to define functors, and functors only to define natural transformations. Aristotle’s Categories, the list of what can be said of a thing, gave the word.

A proof that is not allowed

“[0, 1) and the circle are matched, by t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt), which is a bijection.” Not allowed without naming the category: in Set they are matched, and in Top they are not, since a same-shape map keeps connectedness after one point is taken out (34e3) and the two spaces differ there.
