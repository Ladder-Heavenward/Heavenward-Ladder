11c · proof

Continuous after continuous

Do one continuous function, then another, and the result is continuous. The proof is a chain of three gaps, worked from the outside in. The challenger names a gap for the final output. The outer function, continuous at the inside's value, answers with how near its own input must be: a gap in the middle. The inner function, challenged with that middle gap, answers with how near the input must be. That answer is yours. The middle gap needs a name, and this step gives it a third Greek letter. Then the chain is turned into a licence for carrying a limit inside a continuous function, and two tempting swaps are caught.

Work from the outside in: the outer function says how near its input must be; challenge the inner function with that, and its answer wins the whole round.

11c1The chain, and its domain

9f taught chains by hand: name the inside r = g(x), and read x ↦ f(g(x)) as the outside f after the inside g. 9f's composite-domain gave its natural domain: the inputs x of g whose output g(x) is an input of f. This step writes f ∘ g for that chain, 9b's ring, with g kept (9d) to the inputs whose outputs f can take, so that 9b's line applies. Throughout, g is the inside and f the outside, and the inside's value at the input w gets a name of its own: r₀ = g(w), with 3d's low mark. The accepted name for f ∘ g is the composite function, from the Latin compositus, “put together”.

The challenge game now has three lines to keep track of: x near w, r near r₀, and f(r) near f(r₀). The challenger's gap ε is on the last; your answer δ is on the first. The middle needs a name.

From here on, η (the Greek letter eta, in its small form) names a positive real that is the answer in the middle of a chain: how near the inside's outputs must come to r₀ for the outside to keep its promise. The outer function gives it as an answer; the inner function receives it as a challenge. On this ladder η always names a positive real, and its capital form is not used.

11c2Continuous after continuous

Continuous-after-continuous technique: if g is continuous at w and f is continuous at r₀ = g(w), then f ∘ g is continuous at w. First the chain must be defined near w, with w an input. Then the round itself.

r_0 = g(w) \in \operatorname{dom} f;\ \ \text{a reach } k \text{ for } f \text{ near } r_0 @@ f continuous at r₀: r₀ is an input, f is defined near r₀. Every r with |r − r₀| < k is an input of f
\text{challenge } g \text{ with } k;\ \ \text{call } \delta_0 @@ g is continuous at a; continuity-unpacked: |x − a| < δ₀ gives |g(x) − r₀| < k
|x - w| \lt \min(\delta_0, r_g) \;\Rightarrow\; x \in \operatorname{dom}(f \circ g) @@ r_g a reach for g near w: x is an input of g, and g(x) is an input of f (composite-domain)
w \in \operatorname{dom}(f \circ g) @@ g(w) = r₀ is an input of f. So f ∘ g is defined near w, and w is an input
  
\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0 @@ the challenge is about the final output f(g(x))
\text{call } \htmlClass{new}{\eta}:\ r \in \operatorname{dom} f,\ |r - r_0| \lt \eta \Rightarrow |f(r) - f(r_0)| \lt \varepsilon @@ f is continuous at r₀ (continuity-unpacked): it answers ε with a middle gap
\text{call } \delta:\ x \in \operatorname{dom} g,\ |x - w| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |g(x) - r_0| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\eta} @@ g is continuous at w (continuity-unpacked), challenged with η
\text{let } x \in \operatorname{dom}(f \circ g);\ \text{assume } |x - w| \lt \delta @@ x is an input of g, and r = g(x) is an input of f (composite-domain)
|\htmlClass{new}{g(x)} - r_0| \lt \eta @@ g's promise
|f(g(x)) - f(r_0)| \lt \varepsilon @@ f's promise at r = g(x): substitute for the expression
|(f \circ g)(x) - (f \circ g)(w)| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ 9b's line: (f ∘ g)(w) = f(g(w)) = f(r₀). continuity-unpacked for f ∘ g

Notice there is no “0 <” anywhere. That matters. The inside's output g(x) may be exactly r₀ at inputs x other than w (a constant inside does that everywhere), and then f's promise is asked at r₀ itself. A mere limit of f at r₀ would make no promise there; continuity does. That is why the outside must be continuous, and not just headed somewhere.

Read it backwards to see how it was found. The final gap is about f; so ask f first: how near must its input be? That is η. Then the question becomes one about g: how near must x be for g(x) to be within η of r₀? That is δ. The outer function's answer becomes the inner function's challenge. Found from the outside in; written from the outside in too, because each answer is needed before the next challenge can be named.

\tfrac{1}{x^2} \text{ at } 1:\ \ \eta = \min(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}),\ \ \delta = \min(1, \tfrac{\eta}{3}) @@ outer 1/r at r₀ = 1 by one-over-x-limit's recipe; inner x² at 1 by square-limit's. ε = 0.3: η = 0.15, δ = 0.05
(2x + 1)^2 \text{ at } 0:\ \ \eta = \min(1, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}),\ \ \delta = \tfrac{\eta}{2} @@ outer r² at r₀ = 1 by square-limit; inner 2x + 1 by line-limit. ε = 0.6: η = 0.2, δ = 0.1
\sqrt{x^2 + 1} \text{ at } 2:\ \ \eta = \min(5, \varepsilon\sqrt5),\ \ \delta = \min(1, \tfrac{\eta}{5}) @@ outer √r at r₀ = 5 by 10d's squeezed-by-distance recipe for root-limit; inner x² + 1 at 2. ε = 0.1: η ≈ 0.2236, δ ≈ 0.0447

11c3At the edge of the outside’s domain

|x| is √(x²), by 8h's size-squared. At w ≠ 0 the inside's value w² is positive, √ is continuous there (root-is-continuous), and continuous-after-continuous applies. At 0 the inside's value is 0, and √ is not continuous at 0 in 11a's sense: it is not defined near 0. Yet look at what continuous-after-continuous's round used of f: only the promise “r ∈ dom f, |r − r₀| < η ⇒ |f(r) − f(r₀)| < ε”, over f's inputs. At the edge of a domain with no inputs on the other side, that promise is the one-sided one: for √ at 0, “r ≥ 0 and |r| < η” is “0 ≤ r < η”. Chain-at-an-edge technique: if g is continuous at w, f is continuous from the right at r₀ = g(w) and has no inputs below r₀, and f ∘ g is defined near w, then f ∘ g is continuous at w; likewise with left and above. Continuous-after-continuous's round, word for word, with the one-sided promise in place of f's two-sided one. ∎

|x| = \sqrt{x^2} @@ size-squared (8h)
x^2 \ge 0 \text{ for every } x @@ square-never-negative: the inside's outputs are all inputs of √, so the chain is defined at every real
\begin{aligned}&\sqrt{\ } \text{ continuous from the right at } 0\\ &\quad ;\ \text{no inputs below } 0\end{aligned} @@ root-is-continuous (11a)
|x| \text{ continuous at } 0 @@ chain-at-an-edge, with η = ε² (root-at-0's answer) and δ = √η = ε (|x²| < ε² when |x| < ε, by powers-keep-order)

11e proves |x| continuous a third way, directly from the game, and makes the point of it.

11c4A limit passes inside a continuous function

The chain has a second use: computing limits. Limit-passes-inside technique: if lim_{x→w} g(x) = M and f is continuous at M, and f ∘ g is defined near w, then lim_{x→w} f(g(x)) = f(M). The round is continuous-after-continuous's, with the window 0 < |x − w| < δ in place of |x − w| < δ on the inside's line. g's line is now a limit: the input w is left out, and g need not even have a value at w. f's promise has no “0 <”, because f is continuous at M. ∎

In marks, limit-passes-inside says lim f(g(x)) = f(lim g(x)): the limit passes inside f. It is licensed exactly when f is continuous at the inside's limit.

\lim_{x \to 1} \sqrt{\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}} @@ the inside is 9a's H, which has no value at 1
\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} \to 2 \text{ as } x \to 1 @@ 10a (agreeing-near with x + 1)
\sqrt{\ } \text{ continuous at } 2 @@ root-is-continuous
\lim_{x \to 1} \sqrt{\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}} = \htmlClass{keep}{\sqrt2} @@ limit-passes-inside. The chain is defined near 1: H's outputs near 1 are near 2, inputs of √
\lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{1}{1 + x \cdot q(x)} = \tfrac{1}{1 + 0} = \htmlClass{keep}{1} @@ inside heads to 1 (10d, limits-add); 1/r continuous at 1 (one-over-x-is-continuous); limit-passes-inside

11c5Two swaps that are not allowed

Let v be the function with v(r) = 1 for every r ≠ 0 and v(0) = 0 (7d's cases): a constant with one stray value. v heads to 1 at 0, and is discontinuous there.

g(x) = x^2:\ \ v(g(x)) = 1 \text{ for } x \ne 0 @@ x² ≠ 0 when x ≠ 0 (zero-product); so lim_{x→0} v(g(x)) = 1 (agreeing-near, lines-are-continuous)
v\big(\lim_{x\to0} g(x)\big) = v(0) = \htmlClass{new}{0} \ne 1 @@ the swap lim v(g(x)) = v(lim g(x)) fails: v is not continuous at 0
g(x) = 0 \text{ for every } x:\ \ v(g(x)) = v(0) = 0 @@ a constant inside; lim_{x→0} v(g(x)) = 0
\lim_{r \to 0} v(r) = \htmlClass{new}{1} \ne 0 @@ the swap lim v(g(x)) = lim_{r→M} v(r) fails: the inside sits at M itself

Both swaps are repaired by the same condition: the outside continuous at the inside's limit. Then the first is limit-passes-inside, and the second is true too, since lim_{r→M} f(r) = f(M). The second swap can also be rescued another way, when the inside never takes the value M near w; a question asks for that column.

11c6Longer chains

A chain of three, s after r after x, is two uses of continuous-after-continuous, and 9b's regroup for composing says the brackets do not matter. Take 1/√(x² + 1). The inside x² + 1 is continuous everywhere (lines-are-continuous, continuous-products, continuous-sums) and at least 1. √ is continuous at every positive real (root-is-continuous). 1/r is continuous at every r ≠ 0 (one-over-x-is-continuous). So it is continuous at every real, and its limit at 0 is its value, 1. No round of the game was played, and none needed to be.

11c8Running it backwards

Handed a chain to prove continuous, take it apart from the outside (9f). Check the outside at the inside's value and the inside at the input. Check too that the inside's outputs near the input stay among the outside's inputs. Handed a limit of a chain, find the inside's limit, check the outside is continuous there, and substitute. Handed a recipe min(1, η/3) with η = min(½, ε/2), read off the two functions' recipes and guess the chain. Found from the outside in; written the same way.

Who said it first

The function of 1875 in 11c.4.4 is Carl Johannes Thomae’s, now called Thomae’s function.

Accepted names: continuous-after-continuous is the continuity of composite functions; limit-passes-inside is the limit of a composite function.
