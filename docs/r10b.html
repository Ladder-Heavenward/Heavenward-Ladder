10b · proof

A function can't head two ways

10a wrote “the function heads to this value here” as a whole statement, and refused, for now, to let “the limit” stand alone as a name. A name is honest only when exactly one thing fits it (4d). This step proves that at most one real fits: if a function heads to two values at one input, the two are the same. The proof challenges both claims with one gap, half the distance between the two values. It finds an input whose output would have to be within that gap of both, and 8h's triangle inequality forbids that. With that, “the limit” becomes a name. Two more techniques come from the same half-distance idea: limits keep order, and a function heading to a positive value is positive near the input.

Challenge both claims with half the distance between them. Answer both with the lesser reach. One input in that window would sit within the gap of each value, and no real can.

10b1Why “the limit” needs a proof

4d's rule for names: a description names a thing only when exactly one thing fits it. In 9a's mark, that is ∃!, a some joined to an at-most-one. “The limit of f at w” has two halves to earn. The some can fail: 10d and 10e meet functions with no limit at all, and then the phrase names nothing. The at-most-one cannot fail, and this step proves it. It is the honest licence for every later line that computes with lim_{x→w} f(x) as if it were a real, as 10c does on every line.

Is it obvious? It seems so: how could the outputs crowd around 7 and around 7.5 at once? But the definition never says the outputs crowd around anything. It is a promise game, and a proof that one function cannot win two different games is a statement about every gap and every reach. So prove it, from the line and nothing else.

10b2Two targets that cannot share a member

The whole proof rests on a tech about reals that has nothing to do with functions. Two targets, each of half-width ε, around values L and M: if ε is at most half the distance between L and M, no real sits in both. Two-targets technique: for reals L, M and y, and a positive ε with 2ε ≤ |L − M|, it is not so that |y − L| < ε and |y − M| < ε.

|y - L| \lt \varepsilon \;\land\; |y - M| \lt \varepsilon @@ assume, to refute
|L - M| \le |L - y| + |y - M| @@ triangle in its distance form (8h), with y in the middle
|L - y| = |y - L| @@ size-of-a-negative (8h): distance is the same both ways
|L - M| \lt \htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon + \varepsilon} = 2\varepsilon @@ keep each part; same-direction-add (8g) on the two promises; at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ chains ≤ then <
|L - M| \lt 2\varepsilon \le |L - M| @@ the hypothesis 2ε ≤ |L − M|
\htmlClass{keep}{|L - M| \lt |L - M|} \quad \bot @@ at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ: < then ≤ gives <; and ¬(p < p) (8a's order on ℝ)
\neg\big(|y - L| \lt \varepsilon \land |y - M| \lt \varepsilon\big) \quad \blacksquare @@ refute (1b): the supposition closes

In the plane of 9e: the target rows around L and the target rows around M share no row. On the line of 8a: the interval (L − ε, L + ε) and the interval (M − ε, M + ε) share no real, by 8h's stretch. Why ½? Because the half-widths add. Take L = 1 and M = 4: with ε = 1.5 the intervals are (−0.5, 2.5) and (2.5, 5.5), which share nothing, since 2.5 is in neither. With ε = 3, the challenge of the whole distance, they are (−2, 4) and (1, 7), and 2.5 is in both: |2.5 − 1| = 1.5 < 3 and |2.5 − 4| = 1.5 < 3. Any ε up to half the distance keeps them apart, and half is the largest that does.

10b3At most one limit

One-limit technique: if f is defined near w, lim_{x→w} f(x) = L and lim_{x→w} f(x) = M, then L = M. In plain words: challenge both claims with half the distance between L and M. Each answers with its own reach. Take the least of the two answers and of a reach on which f is defined, and look at one input in that window. Its output must be within the gap of L and within the gap of M, and two-targets says no real is. Its accepted name is the uniqueness of limits.

L \ne M @@ assume, for contradiction (7e)
\varepsilon = \tfrac12\,|L - M| \gt 0 @@ L − M ≠ 0 (adding-keeps-answers), so |L − M| > 0 (8h); half a positive is positive (positive-product on ℝ)
\begin{gathered}\text{call } \delta_1 \text{ for } L,\ \ \delta_2 \text{ for } M,\\ r \text{ with every } x \text{ near } w \text{ in } \operatorname{dom} f\end{gathered} @@ the two lines at this ε (use an every, use a some, twice); “defined near w” (use a some)
\delta = \htmlClass{new}{\min(\min(\delta_1, \delta_2), r)} \gt 0 @@ lesser-below-both twice: positive, and at most each of δ₁, δ₂, r
x = a + \tfrac{\delta}{2}:\ \ 0 \lt |x - a| = \tfrac{\delta}{2} \lt \delta @@ the half of a positive is positive and less than it (order-and-arithmetic-agree: δ − δ/2 = δ/2 > 0)
x \in \operatorname{dom} f @@ δ ≤ r: x is in the neighbourhood of reach r
|f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon \;\land\; |f(x) - M| \lt \varepsilon @@ δ ≤ δ₁ and δ ≤ δ₂: carry each promise through at x; join
\bot @@ two-targets with y = f(x): 2ε = |L − M|
\neg(L \ne M),\ \text{so } \htmlClass{keep}{L = M} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; not-not-out (7d)

Three things in that column are worth slowing down for. The least of the answers: each promise is kept only in its own window, so the input must be in both. Lesser-below-both makes the lesser reach do that. The input itself: the definition promises something about inputs in the window, so the proof must produce one. The input the column picks, δ/2 to the right of w, is in the window and, because δ ≤ r, in the domain. That is why “defined near w” is in the hypothesis. A function with no inputs near w would win every game with nothing in the window, and then every real would be a limit. And the last line: the column refutes L ≠ M, which gives ¬¬(L = M). Rung 7's not-not-out turns that into L = M. This is proof by contradiction proper, and here the either-way principle enters, through not-not-out.

A well-known calculus book proves, before it lets itself write “the limit”, that a function cannot approach two different numbers near w. It remarks that the notation would otherwise be meaningless, and that the proof is a matter of choosing ε to be half the distance between them.

From here on, when f has a limit at w, lim_{x→w} f(x) names it: the one real L with lim_{x→w} f(x) = L (the one-limit technique). Read it “the limit of f(x) as x heads to w”. When f has no limit at w, lim_{x→w} f(x) names nothing, and a line that uses it as a name must first show that the limit exists. That closes the flag 10a opened when it kept this name back.

So lim_{x→2} (3x + 1) is 7, by line-limit, and it may now be written in a chain: lim_{x→2} (3x + 1) + 1 = 7 + 1 = 8. And one-limit is a refutation machine: since 3x + 1 heads to 7 at 2, it does not head to 7.5 there, for if it did one-limit would make 7 = 7.5. Every limit you prove refutes every other value at once.

10b4Limits keep order

The same half-distance gap proves more. Limits-keep-order technique: if f and g are defined near w, f(x) ≤ g(x) for every x in some neighbourhood of w, lim_{x→w} f(x) = L and lim_{x→w} g(x) = M, then L ≤ M.

M \lt L @@ assume, for contradiction: by three-way-order on ℝ, ¬(L ≤ M) is M < L
\varepsilon = \tfrac{L - M}{2} \gt 0 @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: L − M positive; halve it
\begin{gathered}\text{an } x \text{ in every window:}\\ |f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon,\ \ |g(x) - M| \lt \varepsilon,\\ f(x) \le g(x)\end{gathered} @@ as in one-limit: the least of the three reaches and the reach of the hypothesis; x = a + δ/2
g(x) \lt M + \varepsilon = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{L + M}{2}} = L - \varepsilon \lt f(x) @@ unpack-the-bars (8h) unpacks each distance; collect (8d)
g(x) \lt f(x) \le g(x) \quad \bot @@ at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ; ¬(p < p)
L \le M \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; three-way-order on ℝ turns ¬(M < L) into L ≤ M

The middle line is the picture: the halfway real (L + M)/2 separates the two targets, g's below it and f's above it. So f's outputs would sit above g's, against f ≤ g. Notice what limits-keep-order does not say. Strict order is not kept: −|x| < |x| at every x ≠ 0, yet both head to 0 at 0. The order of outputs survives into the limits only as ≤.

And with a constant on one side it says: a function heading to a positive value cannot be at most 0 near w. Turned to say what it does, that is the next technique. Sign-kept-near technique: if lim_{x→w} f(x) = L and L > 0, then f(x) > L/2 > 0 for every x in some neighbourhood of w. Challenge with ε = L/2 and call the answer δ: in that window, |f(x) − L| < L/2, so by unpack-the-bars f(x) > L − L/2 = L/2. ∎ Likewise, L < 0 gives f(x) < L/2 < 0 near w. A limit that is not 0 decides the sign of the outputs near the input; rung 12 leans on this.

10b5A second road, and what the either-way principle is doing

Limits-keep-order gives one-limit again, by a road that never mentions half the distance. Suppose f heads to L and to M at w. Near w, f(x) ≤ f(x), so limits-keep-order with f on both sides gives L ≤ M; and, reading the two claims the other way round, M ≤ L. By 8a's at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ, ≤ on the line is antisymmetric (double-inclusion on the cuts), so L = M. The two roads share their engine. Limits-keep-order's own column refuted M < L with the same kind of gap. It closed with three-way-order on ℝ, which on the line rests on 8b's comparison, and so on rung 7's either-way principle. Neither road avoids the either-way principle.

f(x) \le f(x) \text{ near } a @@ 4h's ≤ read on the line: every real is at most itself (at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ)
L \le M @@ limits-keep-order, with f → L as the lower and f → M as the upper
M \le L @@ limits-keep-order again, the claims exchanged
\htmlClass{keep}{L = M} \quad \blacksquare @@ at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ: ≤ is antisymmetric on ℝ (8a, double-inclusion)

Why does the either-way principle keep turning up? Because sameness of reals is not decidable by looking. 8b's comparing-gives-either-way showed that if any two reals compare, every statement is true or false. A column that ends “so L = M” for any reals L and M has to pass through that principle somewhere, here as not-not-out or as three-way-order on ℝ. What the column proves without it is weaker and still useful: ¬(L ≠ M), that the two values cannot differ. On this ladder, with rung 7 in hand, the two say the same thing.

Limits-keep-order is also a tool for computing. If f(x) ≤ x² near 0 and f has a limit at 0, the limit is at most 0 (limits-keep-order with g(x) = x², which heads to 0 by square-limit, 10a). If moreover f(x) ≥ −x², the limit is at least 0, so it is 0. That last step needed the limit to exist; 10d builds a way to prove that it exists, from the trap alone.

10b7Running it backwards

Handed two claimed limits, find the gap that separates them: any ε up to half their distance; the proof's last line then needs an input in both windows. Handed a claim you suspect is wrong, look for a value you can prove instead: one proof and one-limit refute every other value. Handed an inequality between outputs near w, limits-keep-order passes it to the limits, as ≤. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The calculus book is Michael Spivak’s. The article in 10b.4.4 is Jean le Rond d’Alembert’s, in the Encyclopédie of Diderot and d’Alembert.

Spivak proves, before he lets himself write “the limit”, that a function cannot approach two different numbers near w; he remarks that the notation would otherwise be meaningless, and that his proof is a matter of choosing ε to be half the distance between them.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 5

Accepted names: one-limit is the uniqueness of limits; limits-keep-order is the order limit theorem; sign-kept-near is the sign-preserving property of limits; the either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
