How Thin the Primes Are

The primes are endless, and the count π(x) is at least about x/ln x up to a fixed factor (43g). The same middle count bounds it from above. So near x, about one number in ln x is prime. Gauss guessed this at fifteen from tables, and it took a century and the complex slopes of rung 26 to prove it: ζ, carried over the whole plane (26j), has no zeros on the line where its real part is 1. Where its other zeros lie is the most famous open question in mathematics. This step bounds π(x) on both sides, states the prime count, and says what the zeros of ζ control.

Every prime between n and 2n divides the middle count C(2n, n) < 4ⁿ, so their product is below 4ⁿ; with 43g, π(x) lies between two fixed multiples of x/ln x. The prime count: π(x) ln x / x → 1. Riemann’s guess, all other zeros of ζ on the line with real part ½, would make the error about √x ln x.

43h1A Bound From Above

n < p \le 2n \Rightarrow p \mid \tbinom{2n}{n} = \dfrac{(2n)!}{n!\,n!} @@ p is in the top, not in the bottom
\textstyle\prod_{n < p \le 2n} p \le \tbinom{2n}{n} < 4^n @@ the counts add to 4ⁿ (4k)
\textstyle\sum_{n < p \le 2n} \ln p < n\ln 4 @@ take logarithms
\htmlClass{new}{\pi(x) \le 6\,\dfrac{x}{\ln x} \text{ for every } x \ge 2} @@ add over x, x/2, x/4, …

Two-sided technique: there are fixed numbers A and B above 0 with A·x/ln x ≤ π(x) ≤ B·x/ln x for every x ≥ 2; the lower bound is 43g’s middle count, the upper bound this one.

43h2The Prime Count

\pi(10^6) = 78\,498,\ \ 10^6/\ln 10^6 \approx 72\,382 @@ ratio 1.08
\pi(10^{12}) = 37\,607\,912\,018,\ \ \text{ratio } 1.04 @@ creeping toward 1
\zeta(s) \ne 0 \text{ on the line } s = 1 + it @@ the key fact, with 26j’s carried ζ
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\pi(x)\,\ln x}{x} \to 1} @@ the prime count

Prime-count technique: π(x) ln x / x heads to 1: near x, about one number in ln x is prime. The proof, from the prime-product technique (43g) and the complex slopes of 26, shows that ζ has no zeros where its real part is 1; it is taken as given here.

43h3Where the Zeros Lie

\zeta(-2) = \zeta(-4) = \cdots = 0 @@ zeros on the negative line
\text{every other zero has real part between } 0 \text{ and } 1 @@ proved
\text{the first ten trillion of them have real part exactly } \tfrac12 @@ computed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all of them? } \pi(x) = \textstyle\int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t} + \text{at most a multiple of } \sqrt x \ln x} @@ Riemann’s guess, open since 1859

The zeros of ζ control how far π(x) strays from its smooth guess; that every one off the negative line has real part ½ is believed and unproved. A prize of a million dollars has been offered for it since 2000.

Accepted names

The prime count is the prime number theorem; the two-sided bound is Chebyshev’s; Riemann’s guess is the Riemann hypothesis, one of the Clay Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems. ∫ dt/ln t is the logarithmic integral Li(x).

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss guessed the count about 1792; Pafnuty Chebyshev proved the two-sided bound in 1852; Bernhard Riemann linked the primes to the zeros of ζ in 1859; Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin proved the count in 1896.
