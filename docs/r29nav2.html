Why Does Wind Circle a Storm?

Why does wind circle a storm? Air should rush from high pressure straight into low, like water draining. Instead, on a weather map, the winds run around the low, along the lines of equal pressure, and they circle one way in the north and the other way in the south. This page brings in 27·phys’s sideways push of a turning earth, finds how it grows with latitude on 29b’s sphere, balances it against the pressure push, finds why storms turn opposite ways in the two halves of the earth, and why the balance fails near the equator.

Seen from the turning ground, moving air is pushed sideways by f times its speed, with f = 2Ω sin(latitude). Air that the pressure push starts moving is turned until the two pushes balance: then it runs along the lines of equal pressure at speed (pressure slope)/(ρf). In the north the push is to the right, so wind circles a low anticlockwise; in the south, clockwise. At the equator f = 0, and the balance fails.

29nav21On a Turning Earth, Moving Air Seems Pushed Sideways

Mike flies weather balloons for a forecasting office. Seen from the turning ground, everything that moves feels an extra push, −2Ω × v per kilogram, with Ω the earth’s turning arrow, 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ radians a second (27·phys).

From here on, on this page, the wind is the air’s speed over the ground, flat along the surface, and the turning push is the flat part of −2Ω × v.

\text{push per kg} = -2\,\Omega \times v @@ 27·phys
\htmlClass{new}{\text{square to the wind: it turns the wind, never speeds it}} @@ the cross product is square to v

29nav22The Sideways Push Grows With Latitude

At latitude φ, only the part of Ω pointing straight up out of the ground turns flat motion. On the sphere, that part is Ω sin φ (29b).

\Omega_{\text{up}} = \Omega\sin\varphi @@ the axis meets the local up at 90° − φ
\text{flat turning push} = f\,v,\ \ f = 2\Omega\sin\varphi @@ to the right of the wind in the north
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 45°:\ f \approx 2 \times 7.292 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.707 \approx 1.03 \times 10^{-4} \text{ per second}} @@

29nav23The Pressure Push Against the Turning Push

Air is pushed from high pressure toward low, by the pressure slope over its density. Once moving, it is turned to the right. It keeps turning until the turning push points straight back against the pressure push. Then the wind blows along the lines of equal pressure.

\dfrac{1}{\rho}\,\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{100 \text{ Pa}}{1.2 \times 10^5 \text{ m}} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m/s}^2 @@ ρ = 1.2 kg per m³
f\,V = \dfrac{1}{\rho}\,\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta x} @@ the two pushes balance
\htmlClass{new}{V = \dfrac{8.3 \times 10^{-4}}{1.03 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 8 \text{ m/s},\ \text{along the lines of equal pressure}} @@

Balanced-wind technique: away from the ground and the equator, wind blows along the lines of equal pressure at speed (pressure slope)/(ρ f), faster where the lines crowd together. Mike’s balloons, tracked a kilometre up, drift along the lines on the morning map at the speed this count gives; near the ground, friction slows the wind, and it leans a little across the lines toward low pressure.

29nav24Why Storms Turn Opposite Ways North and South

In the north, sin φ > 0 and the turning push is to the right of the wind. Air heading in toward a low is turned right, and ends up circling it with the low on its left. In the south, sin φ < 0, the push is to the left, and the circling is the other way.

\text{north: around a low, anticlockwise seen from above} @@ 
\text{south: around a low, clockwise} @@ f changes sign at the equator
\htmlClass{new}{\text{stand with your back to the wind in the north: the low is on your left}} @@ a rule for sailors

Storms circle anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the south because the turning push changes side with the sign of sin φ. The pull is far too weak to set the swirl of water draining from a basin, which goes whichever way the water started.

29nav25Near the Equator the Balance Fails

\varphi = 15°:\ f \approx 3.8 \times 10^{-5},\ V \approx 22 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\varphi = 5°:\ f \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-5},\ V \approx 65 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 0:\ f = 0,\ \text{no balance at all}} @@

Near the equator the turning push is too weak to hold a balance, so air flows more directly from high to low, and circling storms cannot form within about 5° of it. A forecaster in the tropics reads winds from other clues, and hurricane warnings start only once a storm drifts far enough from the equator to begin to spin.

Accepted names

The turning push is the Coriolis force, f the Coriolis parameter, and the balanced wind the geostrophic wind. The sailor’s rule is Buys Ballot’s law. A circling low is a cyclone and a circling high an anticyclone.

Who said it first

Christophorus Buys Ballot stated the sailor’s rule in 1857 (not yet revisited for the date); the turning push is named for Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who worked it out in 1835.
