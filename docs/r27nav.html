How does a phone know where it is?

How does a phone know where it is? Stand on a street in Seattle and a small blue dot lands on the map within a few metres of you, with no tower in sight and no one telling the phone anything about where it is. High overhead, about thirty satellites circle the earth, each one calling out the time by its own very good clock and where it is. The phone only listens. What makes this hard is that a voice from the sky says how far away it is only through how long it took to arrive, and light is so fast that a thousandth of a second of delay is three hundred kilometres of distance. The phone’s own clock is a cheap crystal, nowhere near good enough. So the phone cannot simply time the signals. This page shows how it gets round that with rung twenty-seven’s straight samenesses and their grids: four satellites, four unknowns, three of them for where you are and one for how wrong your clock is. It then asks how the way the satellites stand in the sky decides how far the blue dot can be trusted.

Each satellite’s signal gives a distance, but only up to the phone’s unknown clock error, the same for every satellite. Taking the samenesses of the spheres in pairs, one from another, cancels every square and leaves samenesses that are straight. With four satellites there are three straight samenesses in four unknowns, so the answers lie along a line. One sphere then picks a point of that line by a quadratic, and of its two roots, one is on the ground and the other is not. The same small error in the timing moves the answer a little when the satellites are spread across the sky, and a lot when they are bunched, because then the grid is nearly flat and nearly cannot be undone.

27nav1A distance is a delay

A satellite’s signal says, in effect: this left at time t_s, and at that moment I was at the point S. The phone hears it at time t_r. Light covers 299 792.458 km each second, exactly, since 5j set the metre by light. Write c for that speed.

d = c\,(t_r - t_s),\ \ c = 299\,792.458 \text{ km/s} @@ distance is speed times time; the metre is set by light (5j)
\text{straight overhead, } d = 20\,200 \text{ km}: \ t_r - t_s = \tfrac{20\,200}{299\,792.458} \approx 0.0674 \text{ s} @@ GPS satellites fly about 20 200 km above the ground
\text{an error } \delta \text{ in the delay gives an error } c\,\delta \text{ in } d @@ c(t + δ) = ct + cδ: stretching spreads over a sum
\htmlClass{new}{\delta = 10^{-3} \text{ s} \Rightarrow c\delta \approx 300 \text{ km};\ \ \delta = 10^{-9} \text{ s} \Rightarrow c\delta \approx 0.3 \text{ m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a thousandth of a second, and a billionth

Travel-time technique: a distance is the speed of light times the delay, so each billionth of a second of error in the delay is about 30 cm of error in the distance. The satellites carry atomic clocks, kept to a few billionths of a second. The phone carries a quartz crystal. If its clock is a thousandth of a second ahead, every delay it measures is a thousandth of a second too long, and every distance is 300 km too long. Timing alone cannot work.

27nav2Two spheres, one straight sameness

Suppose first that the clock were perfect. Then the phone is at distance r from the satellite at S: it lies on the sphere of radius r about S (18a). Each sphere is a sameness with squares in it. Taken one from another, the squares cancel.

|X - S_i|^2 = |X|^2 - 2\,S_i \cdot X + |S_i|^2 @@ the right-triangle technique (18a), with u = X and v = −Sᵢ
|X - S_i|^2 = r_i^2 \ \text{ and } \ |X - S_1|^2 = r_1^2 @@ X on the sphere about Sᵢ, and on the one about S₁
\htmlClass{new}{2\,(S_i - S_1) \cdot X = |S_i|^2 - |S_1|^2 - r_i^2 + r_1^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract: |X|² cancels, and what is left is straight in X

Sphere-difference technique: taking one sphere’s sameness from another’s cancels every square, and the points on both spheres lie on the straight sameness that is left. In the plane the spheres are circles. Here are three towers on a flat map, in km, and a phone at known distances from each.

x^2 + y^2 = 41,\ \ (x-8)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 13,\ \ (x-3)^2 + (y-9)^2 = 29 @@ towers at (0, 0), (8, 2), (3, 9); distances √41, √13, √29
-16x - 4y + 68 = -28 \ \Rightarrow\ 4x + y = 24 @@ second less first
-6x - 18y + 90 = -12 \ \Rightarrow\ x + 3y = 17 @@ third less first
\det\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = 11 \ne 0 @@ the grid can be undone: one answer (27e)
4\,(17 - 3y) + y = 24 \ \Rightarrow\ y = 4,\ x = 5 @@ clear x, then put y back: elimination (27j)
\htmlClass{new}{(5, 4):\ 25 + 16 = 41} \quad \blacksquare @@ check it on the first circle

The last line matters. Subtracting keeps every point on the spheres, but the straight samenesses alone may hold points on none of them. So the answer is checked on one sphere. The grid can be undone exactly when the three towers are not on one line, since its rows are the steps from the first tower to the other two.

27nav3The fourth satellite is the clock

Now let the phone’s clock be ahead by an unknown time τ. Every delay it measures is τ too long, and every distance cτ too long. Write b = cτ, the clock error as a distance. The phone measures ρᵢ = (true distance to satellite i) + b, the same b for every satellite. The unknowns are four: x, y, z and b.

|X - S_i|^2 = (\rho_i - b)^2,\ \ i = 1, 2, 3, 4 @@ the true distance is ρᵢ − b
|X|^2 - 2S_i\cdot X + |S_i|^2 = \rho_i^2 - 2\rho_i b + b^2 @@ multiply out both sides (18a)
2(S_i - S_1)\cdot X - 2(\rho_i - \rho_1)\,b = |S_i|^2 - |S_1|^2 - \rho_i^2 + \rho_1^2,\ \ i = 2, 3, 4 @@ subtract the first: |X|² and b² both cancel
M X = c - b\,a \ \Rightarrow\ X = M^{-1}c - b\,M^{-1}a = p + b\,q @@ M has rows 2(Sᵢ − S₁); undoable when the four satellites are not on one plane (27d, 27e)
|p + bq - S_1|^2 = (\rho_1 - b)^2 @@ keep one sphere, as in 27·nav.2
\htmlClass{new}{(|q|^2 - 1)\,b^2 + 2\big((p - S_1)\cdot q + \rho_1\big)\,b + |p - S_1|^2 - \rho_1^2 = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out: a quadratic in b, solved by 8i

Clock-unknown technique: with the clock error as a fourth unknown, four satellites give three straight samenesses whose answers lie along a line, and one sphere then picks the point by a quadratic. Three samenesses in four unknowns leave one unknown free, as lost-plus-reached (27c) would have it: the line is p + bq. Here is a fix in Seattle, worked in km, with the earth taken as a ball of radius 6371 km and the axes from its centre, z through the north pole. The satellites stand where four GPS satellites could, 26 560 km from the centre.

S_1 = (-3883, -12676, 23014),\ \rho_1 = 20\,568.079 @@ high in the north-east
S_2 = (8030, -24300, 7105),\ \rho_2 = 23\,317.648 @@ low in the east
S_3 = (-23106, -12765, 2932),\ \rho_3 = 22\,880.118 @@ low in the south-west
S_4 = (-10571, 10061, 22191),\ \rho_4 = 23\,781.984 @@ low in the north-west
p \approx (-2306.60, -3641.77, 4724.52),\ \ q \approx (0.1037, 0.1641, -0.2151) @@ the three straight samenesses, cleared one unknown at a time (27j)
-0.9161\,b^2 + 52\,294.6\,b - 4\,438\,407 = 0 @@ put into the sphere about S₁
b \approx 85.000 \ \text{ or } \ b \approx 57\,000 @@ the quadratic-formula technique (8i)
\htmlClass{new}{X \approx (-2297.8, -3627.8, 4706.2):\ 47.62^\circ \text{N},\ 122.35^\circ \text{W}} \quad \blacksquare @@ from b ≈ 85.000; the other root puts X 10 117 km from the centre, out in space

So the phone is at the Space Needle, and its clock was ahead by 85/299 792.458 s, about 0.28 thousandths of a second. It learned its place and its clock error in one stroke. The second root is not near the ground, so it is thrown out.

27nav4How the satellites stand

Each measured distance carries a small error. How far does it move the fix? The straight samenesses are M X = c, and an error in the distances changes c a little.

M(X + \Delta X) = c + \Delta c \ \Rightarrow\ M\,\Delta X = \Delta c @@ subtract M X = c; grids spread over sums (27d)
\Delta X = M^{-1}\Delta c = \tfrac{1}{\det M}\,S\,\Delta c @@ the stand-in technique (27g): S is the grid of stand-ins
\text{towers } (0,0),\ (8,2),\ (3,9):\ \det M = 264,\ \text{a 10 m error moves the fix at most about } 8 \text{ m} @@ phone at (5, 4) km; one distance 10 m long, each in turn
\text{towers } (0,0),\ (8,0.5),\ (16,2):\ \det M = 32,\ \text{at most about } 112 \text{ m} @@ three towers nearly along one road
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same 10 m error, about thirteen times the miss}} \quad \blacksquare @@ worked exactly, by solving again

Spread-sky technique: an error in the distances moves the fix by the undo of the grid, so when the satellites bunch and the determinant nears 0, the same small error moves the fix much further. With the towers on one line the determinant is 0, and the grid cannot be undone at all (27e). Then the phone and its mirror image across the line of towers are at the same distances from all three, and nothing can tell them apart.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, from the sky

21a said that every measured value is a fence. A satellite’s clock is kept to a few billionths of a second, and the air slows each signal a little, in ways only partly known. So each distance is a fence a few metres wide, never a single number.

A fix is a fence: its width is the distances’ fence, stretched by how the satellites stand, and both are reported.

The fix is the undo of a grid applied to those fences. Satellites spread across the sky give a determinant far from 0, and the fence round the fix stays a few metres wide. Satellites bunched in one patch of sky, as seen from a street between tall buildings, give a determinant near 0, and the same fences make a fence round the fix many times wider. A phone draws both: its dot, and a pale circle round it for how wrong it might be.

A phone on Granville Street in downtown Vancouver sees four satellites, all high in the sky and close together. Which of these would you report: the fix to the metre; the fix with a wide circle round it; no fix at all?

The accepted names

The measured ρᵢ is a pseudorange, and b the receiver clock bias. Finding a place from distances is trilateration (from differences of distances, multilateration). The axes from the earth’s centre that turn with the earth are earth-centred, earth-fixed coordinates. How the satellites’ spread stretches the error is the dilution of precision (GDOP, or PDOP for place alone). The system is the Global Positioning System; with Europe’s Galileo, Russia’s GLONASS and China’s BeiDou, the systems together are GNSS. Solving the four spheres by straight samenesses and one quadratic is Bancroft’s method.

Who said it first

The US Navy’s Transit system (1964) fixed ships from the pitch shift of one satellite’s signal. Roger Easton’s Timation satellites (1967) first flew precise clocks. Bradford Parkinson led the joint program that became GPS from 1973; the first of its satellites was launched on 22 February 1978, and the full system was declared working in 1995. Until 1 May 2000 the signal open to everyone was deliberately blurred to about 100 m; that night it was switched off. Stephen Bancroft published the closed solution in 1985. Gladys West’s models of the earth’s shape went into the satellites’ orbits. In 27·nav.6.5, the satellite NTS-2 (1977) flew with its clock correction switched off for its first days, to measure the gain; it matched the prediction to within about one percent.
