28g · proof

The best point on a constraint

Many questions ask for the best value of something while something else is held fixed. The biggest field a fixed length of fence can hold. The cheapest mix that still meets a recipe. Rung 14 found tops and bottoms where the slope is flat. On a constraint the slope need not be flat, because you are only allowed to walk along the constraint. This step first finds the arrow that points straight uphill, then proves that at the best point on a constraint, the uphill arrow of what you want and the uphill arrow of the constraint line up.

A one-output function has an uphill arrow made of its partial slopes. At a top or bottom of f on the curve g = 0, where g’s uphill arrow is not zero, f’s uphill arrow is a multiple of g’s.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 28b (the slope grid), 28f (an equation fixes a function), 14a (the flat-top technique) and 18b (the dot-bound technique). 28·econ spends it on a budget.

28g1The uphill arrow

Stands on 28b2, 18b1.

For f with one output, the slope grid is a single row. Read as an arrow it is ∇f = (fx, fy), the uphill arrow. Accepted name: the gradient. By 28b2 the slope of f along an arrow u of length 1 is ∇f · u.

Uphill technique: if ∇f(w) ≠ 0, the slope of f at w is greatest along the direction of ∇f(w), and that greatest slope is |∇f(w)|. Along a level curve of f, where f keeps one value, the direction of travel is at right angles to ∇f.

\text{slope along } u = \nabla f(w) \cdot u,\ \ |u| = 1 @@ the slope-grid technique (28b2)
\nabla f(w)\cdot u \le |\nabla f(w)|\,|u| = |\nabla f(w)| @@ the dot-bound technique (18b)
\text{equality} \iff u = \nabla f(w) / |\nabla f(w)| @@ the dot-bound’s equal case: u a positive multiple of ∇f(w)
f(x(t), y(t)) = c \Rightarrow \nabla f \cdot (x'(t), y'(t)) = 0 @@ the roads-add technique (28c3), and a constant has slope 0
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla f \text{ points uphill, with slope } |\nabla f|, \text{ and is at right angles to level curves}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above; a zero dot means a right angle (18a)

A map of a hill with lines of equal height shows this: the steepest way up crosses every contour line square on.

28g2The arrows line up

Stands on 28g1, 28f2, 14a.

Now walk only along the curve g(x, y) = 0. Lines-up technique: if f and g are smoothly sloped, f has a top or a bottom among the points of g = 0 at w, and ∇g(w) ≠ 0, then ∇f(w) = λ∇g(w) for some number λ.

g_y(w) \ne 0 \ \ (\text{or else } g_x(w) \ne 0 \text{ and swap the roles of } x, y) @@ ∇g(w) ≠ 0
\text{near } w,\ g = 0 \text{ is the graph } y = \varphi(x),\ \ \varphi' = -g_x/g_y @@ the fixed-by-an-equation and implicit-slope techniques (28f)
h(x) = f(x, \varphi(x)) \text{ has a top or bottom at } w_1 @@ the points (x, φ(x)) are exactly the points of g = 0 near w
0 = h'(w_1) = f_x + f_y\,\varphi' = f_x - f_y\,g_x/g_y @@ the flat-top technique (14a), the roads-add technique (28c3)
f_x\,g_y - f_y\,g_x = 0 @@ multiply by g_y
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla f(w) = \lambda\,\nabla g(w),\ \ \lambda = f_y(w)/g_y(w)} \quad \blacksquare @@ f_x = λg_x by the line above, and f_y = λg_y by the choice of λ

So the search for the best point on a constraint becomes three equations in three unknowns: fx = λgx, fy = λgy, g = 0. The number λ has a meaning too: it is how fast the best value changes as the constraint is loosened. 28·econ reads it as the worth of one more dollar.

Worked once: the biggest area xy of a rectangle with perimeter 2x + 2y = 40. ∇(xy) = (y, x) and ∇g = (2, 2), so y = 2λ and x = 2λ, so x = y = 10. The best rectangle is a square.

28g3Illegal moves

Stands on 28g1 and 28g2.

\text{best on } g = 0 \Rightarrow \nabla f(w) = 0\,? @@ only the slope along the curve is 0; ∇f may point off the curve
\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g \Rightarrow w \text{ is a top}\,? @@ the arrows also line up at bottoms and at flat-but-neither points: check them
\nabla g(w) = 0 \text{ is allowed}\,? @@ then the curve may have a corner or a cusp, and the technique is silent; test those points by hand
\text{the steepest slope at } w \text{ is } f_x + f_y\,? @@ it is |∇f| = √(f_x² + f_y²), along ∇f

The accepted names

The uphill arrow is the gradient, ∇f, read “del f” or “grad f”. The slope along u is the directional derivative. Level curves are contour lines or isolines. The lines-up technique is the method of Lagrange multipliers, and λ is the Lagrange multiplier.

Who said it first

Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the multiplier method in his Mécanique analytique of 1788, for bodies held on surfaces and curves, and stated it for best values in 1797. The sign ∇ was introduced by William Rowan Hamilton in 1837 and named “nabla”, after a Phoenician harp, by Peter Guthrie Tait in the 1870s.
