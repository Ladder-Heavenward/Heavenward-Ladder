What Is the Best Shape for a Can?

What is the best shape for a can? A drinks maker sells billions of cans, and a gram of metal saved on each is worth a fortune. The can must hold 330 millilitres; everything else is free to choose. Engineers call this design by optimisation: state the goal, state the limits, and let the mathematics find the shape. This page finds the can with least metal by rung 28’s best point on a constraint, reads its multiplier as a price, adds what real ends cost, then lets limits that are inequalities decide a beam, and ends with two goals that cannot both be won.

Write the goal as a function of the sizes, and each limit as an equation or an inequality. With one equation, the best point has the goal’s gradient a multiple λ of the limit’s, and λ is how much the best goal changes per unit of the limit. With inequalities, the best point usually sits where some limits are tight; there the goal’s gradient is a mix of the tight limits’ gradients with signs that push outward. With two goals, list the designs no other beats in both.

30eng1Least Metal for 330 Millilitres

Evita designs a can of radius r and height h, in centimetres. The metal goes as the area of the sides and the two ends; the can must hold V = 330 cubic centimetres. Rung 28’s best point on a constraint sets the gradients side by side.

A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h,\ \ \pi r^2 h = V @@ goal and limit
\nabla A = \lambda \nabla(\pi r^2 h) @@ the best point on a constraint (28g)
4\pi r + 2\pi h = \lambda\, 2\pi r h,\ \ 2\pi r = \lambda\, \pi r^2 @@ the two parts
\lambda = 2/r \Rightarrow 4\pi r + 2\pi h = 4\pi h @@ put the second into the first
\htmlClass{new}{h = 2r:\ r \approx 3.74,\ h \approx 7.49} @@ as tall as it is wide

Best-point technique: at the best design under one equation limit, the goal’s gradient is a multiple λ of the limit’s gradient. The least-metal can is as tall as it is wide, a squat tin, not a drinks can.

30eng2The Multiplier Is a Price

λ came out as 2/r. It has a meaning: how much metal the best can needs for one more cubic centimetre held.

A_{\min}(V) = 6\pi r^2,\ \ r = (V/2\pi)^{1/3} @@ the best can at each V
\dfrac{dA_{\min}}{dV} = 12\pi r \cdot \dfrac{dr}{dV} = 12\pi r \cdot \dfrac{1}{6\pi r^2} @@ the chain technique (28c)
= \dfrac{2}{r} = \lambda @@ the same number
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 0.53 \text{ cm}^2 \text{ of metal per extra cm}^3} @@ the price of a bigger can

Price technique: the multiplier is the rate at which the best goal changes as the limit is loosened. It tells the designer which limits are worth arguing about before redesigning anything.

30eng3What Real Ends Cost

A real can is about 6.6 cm across and 12.2 cm tall: h/r ≈ 3.7, not 2. The goal was wrong, not the method. The ends are pressed thicker to hold the pressure, and the rim is folded. Say an end costs k times as much metal per area as the side.

A = k\, 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h @@ the new goal
4k\pi r + 2\pi h = \lambda\, 2\pi r h,\ \ 2\pi r = \lambda\, \pi r^2 @@ the same limit
h = 2k\,r @@ as before, with k
\htmlClass{new}{h/r \approx 3.7 \Rightarrow k \approx 1.85} @@ ends nearly twice as costly

An optimum is only as good as its goal: when the best design disagrees with practice, look first for a cost the goal left out. Here the real can tells us what its ends cost.

30eng4Limits That Are Inequalities

A beam of rectangle b wide and h deep must carry a bending load: its strength needs bh² ≥ S, a fixed number. It must fit under a floor, h ≤ H, and be at least b0 wide to make. Minimise its area bh.

\text{strength: } bh^2 \ge S;\ \ h \le H;\ \ b \ge b_0 @@ three inequalities
\text{on the strength curve, } bh = S/h @@ deeper is lighter
\text{so push } h \text{ up to } H @@ the depth limit is tight
\htmlClass{new}{h = H,\ b = S/H^2} @@ if S/H² ≥ b₀; otherwise b = b₀, and the strength has room to spare

Tight-limit technique: with inequality limits, the best design sits where some limits are tight, and there the goal’s gradient is a mix of the tight limits’ gradients with signs pushing out of the allowed region. A loose limit has multiplier 0: loosening it is worth nothing.

30eng5Two Goals at Once

Often there are two goals: a bicycle frame should be light and stiff. Making it stiffer adds metal. No design wins both; the question becomes which designs are not beaten in both.

\text{design } P \text{ beats } Q:\ \text{no heavier, no less stiff, better in one} @@ a partial order
\text{keep the designs no other beats} @@ the trade-off curve
\text{weigh the goals: minimise weight} - c \cdot \text{stiffness} @@ one goal per price c
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each } c \text{ picks a point on the curve}} @@ the buyer chooses c

With two goals, the mathematics finds the trade-off curve; choosing a point on it is a judgment about what each goal is worth. Engineers hand the curve to whoever pays.

30eng7Cooling Slowly to Find the Best

Some design problems have a cost with many dips: the order of stops on a delivery round, the layout of parts on a sheet. Walking always downhill stops in the first dip. Instead, step at random, and take a step that makes things worse with a chance that shrinks as a setting T is lowered.

\text{take it with chance } e^{-\Delta/T} @@ a better step is always taken
T = 1,\ \Delta = 2:\ e^{-2} \approx 0.14 @@ early: often climbs out of a dip
T = 0.1,\ \Delta = 2:\ e^{-20} \approx 2 \times 10^{-9} @@ late: almost never
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lowered slowly enough, it ends in the lowest dip with chance near 1}} @@ a chance that steps (30p)

Slow-cooling technique: search by random steps, taking worse ones with chance e^(−Δ/T), and lower T slowly; early it roams over every dip, late it settles in a deep one. Too fast a lowering freezes it in a shallow dip, as metal cooled too quickly freezes in a poor crystal; how slowly to go is the price of the answer.

Accepted names

Finding the best design is design optimisation; the goal is the objective function, the limits constraints, and λ a Lagrange multiplier, also a shadow price. The conditions with inequalities are the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions; a tight limit is active, a loose one inactive. Designs no other beats form the Pareto front, and weighing goals by a price is scalarisation.

Who said it first

Joseph-Louis Lagrange introduced the multipliers in Mécanique analytique, 1788. William Karush stated the inequality conditions in a 1939 thesis, and Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker in 1951.
