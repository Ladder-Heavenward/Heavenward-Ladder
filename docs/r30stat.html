Did the Treatment Work?

Did the treatment work? A seed company tries three mixes of fertiliser on fifteen plots of wheat, five plots to a mix, and weighs the grain from each plot. The three averages are 21, 25 and 23 kg. No two plots give the same weight even under one mix, so three averages that differ are what chance alone would give as well. 30s tested one difference between two samples. Three mixes have three differences, and three tests have three chances to raise a false alarm. This page asks one question of all the groups at once: do the group averages stand further apart than the scatter inside the groups accounts for? Then it turns to the trial itself, before any grain is weighed: how to lay the plots out so that good soil cannot pass for a good mix, and how many plots are enough.

The squared distances from the overall average split into a part between the groups and a part within them: Q = B + W. If the group averages are truly equal, B/(k − 1) and W/(N − k) both average σ²; if not, the first averages more. Their ratio is F. Under a fair draw, shuffling the labels over every split the draw could have made tells how rare the seen F is, with no bell assumed. With each treatment once in each block, the block effects cancel from every comparison. Two groups of n = 15.7σ²/d² find a true difference d about 8 times in 10.

30stat1Three Averages, One Question

\text{mix A:}\ \ 20,\ 22,\ 19,\ 21,\ 23 @@ average 21
\text{mix B:}\ \ 24,\ 26,\ 23,\ 25,\ 27 @@ average 25
\text{mix C:}\ \ 23,\ 21,\ 25,\ 22,\ 24 @@ average 23
\text{all fifteen plots} @@ average 23

The two-sample technique (30s2) could test A against B, A against C and B against C. Each test raises a false alarm with chance 0.05 when the mixes are truly alike. The chance that at least one of the three does is then more than 0.05: at most 0.15 by the chance-rules technique (30f1), and 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 0.143 if the three tests were independent (30l2). Ten mixes would have 45 pairs. So ask one question of all the groups at once.

Let there be k groups. Group i has nᵢ readings yᵢⱼ, with average ȳᵢ. There are N readings in all, with overall average ȳ. Measure three things. The total Q is the sum of (yᵢⱼ − ȳ)² over every reading. The part between the groups, B, is the sum of nᵢ(ȳᵢ − ȳ)² over the groups. The part within the groups, W, is the sum of (yᵢⱼ − ȳᵢ)² over every reading.

Squares-split technique: Q = B + W.

y_{ij} - \bar y = (y_{ij} - \bar y_i) + (\bar y_i - \bar y) @@ add and take away the group’s average
(y_{ij} - \bar y)^2 = (y_{ij} - \bar y_i)^2 + 2(y_{ij} - \bar y_i)(\bar y_i - \bar y) + (\bar y_i - \bar y)^2 @@ square the sum
\textstyle\sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar y_i) = 0 @@ the readings of a group sit around their own average
\textstyle\sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar y)^2 = \sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar y_i)^2 + n_i(\bar y_i - \bar y)^2 @@ add over group i: the middle term is 0, the last is the same nᵢ times
\htmlClass{new}{Q = W + B} \quad \blacksquare @@ add over the groups
  
B = 5(21 - 23)^2 + 5(25 - 23)^2 + 5(23 - 23)^2 = 40 @@ between the mixes
W = 10 + 10 + 10 = 30 @@ within: each mix’s readings sit −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 from its average
Q = 30 + 30 + 10 = 70 @@ every reading against 23, mix by mix
\htmlClass{new}{70 = 40 + 30} @@ the squares-split technique

The split is arithmetic. It holds for any numbers sorted into any groups, with no chance in it yet. Move the fifteen weights among the three groups and Q stays 70, while B and W trade.

30stat2What Chance Alone Gives

Now let chance in. Suppose the readings are independent, that a reading in group i has average μᵢ, and that every reading has the same spread σ². The claim to test is that the treatment does nothing: μ₁ = μ₂ = ⋯ = μk. Write μ̄ for (n₁μ₁ + ⋯ + nkμk)/N.

Between-and-within technique: E W = (N − k)σ², and E B = (k − 1)σ² + n₁(μ₁ − μ̄)² + ⋯ + nk(μk − μ̄)².

E \textstyle\sum_j (y_{ij} - \bar y_i)^2 = (n_i - 1)\sigma^2 @@ the sample-spread technique (30q2), in group i
E\,W = \textstyle\sum_i (n_i - 1)\sigma^2 = (N - k)\sigma^2 @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
B = \textstyle\sum_i n_i \bar y_i^{\,2} - N \bar y^{\,2} @@ expand the squares; the nᵢȳᵢ add to Nȳ
E\,\bar y_i^{\,2} = \mu_i^2 + \sigma^2/n_i,\qquad E\,\bar y^{\,2} = \bar\mu^2 + \sigma^2/N @@ Var X = E X² − μ² (30g2), with the sample-average technique (30q1)
E\,B = \textstyle\sum_i n_i\mu_i^2 + k\sigma^2 - N\bar\mu^2 - \sigma^2 @@ the average-adds technique
\htmlClass{new}{E\,B = (k - 1)\sigma^2 + \textstyle\sum_i n_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same expanding, on the μᵢ

W does not care whether the claim is true: each group is measured around its own average, so W reads the scatter and nothing else. B reads the scatter too, and on top of it the distance between the true averages. Divide each by its count of directions and form the ratio F = (B/(k − 1)) / (W/(N − k)). When the claim is true, the top and the bottom both average σ². When it is false, the top averages more.

B/(k - 1) = 40/2 = 20 @@ three mixes
W/(N - k) = 30/12 = 2.5 @@ fifteen plots
\htmlClass{new}{F = 20/2.5 = 8} @@ the mixes stand 8 times as far apart as the scatter gives on average

A ratio near 1 is what chance alone gives. Is 8 far enough from 1? That depends on how F itself scatters from trial to trial, and the averages above do not say.

30stat3Shuffle the Labels

The plots got their mixes by a fair draw (30v3): every way of handing five plots to each mix was equally likely. There are 15!/(5!·5!·5!) = 756 756 such splits. Take the claim in its strongest form: the mix changes no plot’s weight. Then the fifteen weights would have been the same fifteen whatever the draw gave, and the draw only chose which weight wears which label. So F can be worked out for every split the draw could have made, and the split that was drawn takes its place among them.

Label-shuffle technique: under a fair draw among M splits, if the treatment changes no reading, then the rule “reject when at most αM of the splits give a ratio as large as the one seen” rejects with chance at most α.

\text{the readings are fixed, and each split } s \text{ has chance } 1/M @@ the claim, and the fair draw (30v3)
c(s) = \text{the number of splits } t \text{ with } F(t) \ge F(s) @@ a count for each split
R = \{s : c(s) \le \alpha M\},\qquad s^* \in R \text{ with } F(s^*) \text{ least} @@ the splits that reject; if there are none the chance is 0
t \in R \Rightarrow F(t) \ge F(s^*) @@ s* has the least ratio in R
|R| \le c(s^*) \le \alpha M @@ every member of R is counted in c(s*), and s* is in R
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{reject}) = |R|/M \le \alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-cases technique (30l1)
  
F \ge 8 \iff B \ge 40 @@ Q = 70 on every split, so W = 70 − B, and F rises with B
5754 \text{ of the } 756\,756 \text{ splits have } B \ge 40 @@ counted by machine
\htmlClass{new}{5754/756\,756 \approx 0.0076 \le 0.05:\ \text{reject}} @@ the label-shuffle technique

No bell was assumed, and no model of the readings. The chance is in the draw alone, and the experimenter made the draw. With more plots there are too many splits to count. A computer then draws some thousands of them, and the run-count technique (30v4) says how close its share comes.

Held in trust: if every reading is bell-shaped with one spread σ², and the group averages are equal, then F has a chance law that depends only on k − 1 and N − k. Its proof is not on this page. What it gives is a table, as in 30q4. For 2 and 12 the table says that F ≥ 3.89 has chance 0.05, and that F ≥ 8 has chance about 0.0062, close to the shuffle’s 0.0076.

Rejecting says that the three averages are not all equal. It does not say which mix differs from which.

30stat4Fields as Blocks

The fifteen plots lie in five fields, three plots to a field, and the fields differ: one lies low and wet, one is stony. A fair draw over all fifteen plots may hand one mix two plots in the best field. Over many draws that evens out, by the fair-split technique (30v3). In the one trial that is run, the differences between fields sit inside the scatter and widen it. So lay the trial out another way: every field holds each mix once, and a fair draw inside each field settles which plot gets which mix. Each field is then a block. 30v3 did this with pairs; here a block holds k plots.

Write the reading for mix i in field j as yᵢⱼ = μᵢ + βⱼ + eᵢⱼ. The βⱼ is what field j adds, and it may be any number. The eᵢⱼ are independent, with average 0 and spread σ². Let ȳ₁ and ȳ₂ be the averages of two mixes over the b fields.

Same-field technique: with each treatment once in each of b blocks, ȳ₁ − ȳ₂ has average μ₁ − μ₂ and spread 2σ²/b, whatever the block effects βⱼ are.

\bar y_1 = \mu_1 + \bar\beta + \bar e_1,\qquad \bar y_2 = \mu_2 + \bar\beta + \bar e_2 @@ average over the b blocks; β̄ is the average block effect
\bar y_1 - \bar y_2 = \mu_1 - \mu_2 + \bar e_1 - \bar e_2 @@ β̄ cancels: both treatments met every block once
E(\bar y_1 - \bar y_2) = \mu_1 - \mu_2 @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_1 - \bar y_2) = \sigma^2/b + \sigma^2/b = 2\sigma^2/b} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sample-average technique (30q1); independent spreads add (30g3)

Compare a trial with no blocks, in which every plot lies in a field of its own and the field effects are independent draws of spread σβ². Each reading then has spread σ² + σβ², and the two-sample technique (30s2) gives the difference a spread of 2(σ² + σβ²)/b.

\sigma^2 = 2.5,\quad \sigma_\beta^2 = 8,\quad b = 5 @@ scatter inside a field; fields at −4, −2, 0, 2, 4 kg
\text{no blocks:}\ \ 2(2.5 + 8)/5 = 4.2,\ \ \text{width } \sqrt{4.2} \approx 2.05 \text{ kg} @@ the two-sample technique (30s2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{blocks:}\ \ 2(2.5)/5 = 1,\ \ \text{width } 1 \text{ kg}} @@ the same-field technique

The same number of plots pins the difference down twice as closely. The rule of layout: block on what can be seen beforehand, such as fields, litters or days, and leave to the draw what cannot. The shuffle of the last footing follows the layout. Its splits are the ones this draw could have made, labels moved inside each field only: (3!)⁵ = 7776 of them.

30stat5How Many Plots?

Before the trial comes a question of size. Suppose a difference of d = 2 kg between two mixes would be worth knowing about. How many plots must each mix have, so that a difference that large is likely to be found? A trial that is too small will probably miss it, and its “no difference found” then says little.

Take two groups of n readings each, with a spread σ² known from earlier seasons. Test the claim of no difference with Z = (X̄₁ − X̄₂)/√(2σ²/n), and reject when |Z| ≥ 1.96 (30s1). The power is the chance of rejecting when the claim is false (30s3).

Plots-needed technique: for the bell test of no difference between two groups of n readings each, with spread σ², the power against a true difference d is about 0.8 when n = 2(1.96 + 0.84)²σ²/d² ≈ 15.7σ²/d².

\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1 - \bar X_2) = 2\sigma^2/n @@ the two-sample technique (30s2)
Z \text{ is about a bell of average } \delta = d\big/\sqrt{2\sigma^2/n} \text{ and spread } 1 @@ divide the difference by its width (30s1)
\text{power} \approx \text{the bell’s area above } 1.96 - \delta @@ as in the test-power technique (30s3); the other tail is below −1.96 − δ, and tiny
\text{the bell’s area above } {-0.84} \approx 0.80 @@ the bell’s table
\htmlClass{new}{1.96 - \delta = -0.84 \iff \delta = 2.80 \iff n = 2(2.80)^2\sigma^2/d^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve for n
  
\text{in blocks:}\ \ \sigma^2 = 2.5,\ d = 2:\quad n = 15.68 \times 2.5/4 = 9.8 @@ 10 plots to a mix
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no blocks:}\ \ \sigma^2 = 10.5,\ d = 2:\quad n = 15.68 \times 10.5/4 \approx 41.2} @@ 42 plots to a mix

The count goes as 1/d²: a difference half as large needs four times the plots. It goes as σ² too, which is why the layout of the last footing matters so much. And the count is fixed before the trial starts. The 0.05 of the test is the chance of a false alarm in a trial of a size chosen beforehand.

The accepted names

The squares-split technique is the analysis of variance, here one-way, shortened to ANOVA. Q, B and W are the total, the between-groups (or treatment) and the within-groups (or error, or residual) sums of squares. The counts of directions k − 1 and N − k are degrees of freedom, and a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom is a mean square. The ratio is the F ratio, or F statistic, and the law held in trust is the F distribution with k − 1 and N − k degrees of freedom. When the top has 2 degrees of freedom and the bottom m, the chance of F ≥ f is exactly (1 + 2f/m)−m/2, which gives 3.885 and 0.0062 for m = 12. The claim that the treatment does nothing is the null hypothesis; in the form “changes no reading” it is the sharp null hypothesis. The label-shuffle technique is the randomization test, also called a permutation test, and its share of splits is an exact p-value. A fair draw over all the plots is a completely randomized design; each treatment once in each block is a randomized complete block design, and its three-way split is a two-way analysis of variance without interaction. The plots-needed technique is a sample size or power calculation; 0.8 is a custom, not a result. Testing many pairs is the problem of multiple comparisons, taken up in the infinity series at ∞78.

Who said it first

The split, the layout in blocks and the fair draw as the ground of the test are the work of Ronald Fisher, who came to the Rothamsted experimental station in England in 1919 as its statistician and worked for fourteen years on its field trials. He set the methods out in the book Statistical Methods for Research Workers, first published in 1925. His test of a lady who said she could taste whether the milk or the tea went into the cup first, published in 1935, is commonly called the first published test of the label-shuffle kind. The letter F was given to the ratio by George Snedecor, in his honour.
