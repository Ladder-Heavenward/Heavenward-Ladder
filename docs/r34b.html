34b · proof

Open Sets Alone

The last step ended with a suspicion. Continuity can be said with open sets alone, and two different distances that make the same open sets make the same continuous maps. So this step throws the distance away and keeps only a family of sets called open, obeying the three rules the open sets of a distance always obeyed. Some such families come from a distance and some do not, and the difference shows up in a plain place: whether two different points can be held apart by open sets. With a family of open sets, continuity is pulling back, and the proof that a chain of continuous maps is continuous shrinks to one line. Then come the ways to make new spaces from old: by taking a part, by taking pairs, and by gluing points together. Gluing the two ends of a stretch makes a loop, and gluing the sides of a square makes the surface of a doughnut. None of this needs a distance, and none of it could be said without open sets.

A family of open sets on a set holds the empty set and the whole, every union of its members, and the overlap of any two. A map is continuous when it pulls open sets back to open sets, and continuous maps chain. A base is a family whose unions make every open set, and boxes are a base for pairs. A space keeps points apart when any two points sit in separate open sets, and then a list settles at one point at most. A part takes the open sets that meet it, and a glued space takes the sets whose pull-back is open, so a map out of a glued space is continuous exactly when it is continuous before the gluing.

34b1A Family of Open Sets

A family of open sets on a set X is a family 𝒯 of parts of X with three properties: ∅ and X are in 𝒯; every union of members of 𝒯 is in 𝒯; the overlap of any two members of 𝒯 is in 𝒯. Its members are the open sets, and X with 𝒯 is a space. 34a2’s open-family technique says every distance makes one. Its accepted name is a topology, from the Greek topos, place: the study of place without measure. It is this step’s one new academy word.

Families that come from no distance in sight. On any set, every part: the scattered family. On any set, only ∅ and X: the lumped family. On the line, ∅ together with every set whose leftover is finite: the finite-leftover family. Its three properties hold because a union only shrinks a leftover, and two finite leftovers have a finite union.

Same-opens technique: on the plane, the straight distance and the street distance make the same family of open sets.

d(x, y) = \sqrt{p^2 + q^2},\ \ t(x, y) = |p| + |q| @@ p and q the two gaps between x and y along the axes
d^2 = p^2 + q^2 \le (|p| + |q|)^2 = t^2 \le 2(p^2 + q^2) = 2d^2 @@ (|p| − |q|)² ≥ 0 gives 2|p||q| ≤ p² + q²
d \le t \le \sqrt2\,d @@ square roots keep order (8f)
B_t(x, r) \subseteq B_d(x, r),\ \ B_d(x, r/\sqrt2) \subseteq B_t(x, r) @@ a point nearer by t is nearer by d; a point nearer than r/√2 by d is nearer than r by t
\htmlClass{new}{U \text{ open for } d \iff U \text{ open for } t} \quad \blacksquare @@ every ball of either kind holds a ball of the other kind about the same point

So street and straight distance give the same continuous maps of the plane (34a4), though they never agree on how far it is from the Pearl District to the Rose Garden.

34b2Continuity Is Pulling Back

From here on, a map f from a space X to a space Y is continuous when the pull-back of every open set of Y is open in X. For spaces with distances, the pull-back technique (34a4) says this is the old continuity. For every other space, it is the definition.

Pull-back-chain technique: a continuous map after a continuous map is continuous.

f : X \to Y,\ \ g : Y \to Z \text{ continuous},\ \ W \subseteq Z \text{ open} @@ the setting
(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = \{x : g(f(x)) \in W\} = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W)) @@ the pull-back, read twice
\htmlClass{new}{(g \circ f)^{-1}(W) \text{ is open}} \quad \blacksquare @@ g⁻¹(W) is open, and f pulls it back to an open set

11c needed a chain of three gaps and a new Greek letter in the middle. Here the whole proof is one line, because the gaps were never the point. Two more facts are as quick. Every map out of a scattered space is continuous, since every pull-back is open there. Every map into a lumped space is continuous, since the only pull-backs to check are of ∅ and Y.

34b3Bases, and Boxes for Pairs

A family ℬ of open sets is a base when every open set is a union of members of ℬ. The balls of a distance are a base: each point of an open set has a ball inside it, and the open set is the union of those balls. On the line, the stretches with fraction ends are a base, and they can be listed.

For spaces X and Y, a box is a set U × V of pairs, with U open in X and V open in Y. Two boxes overlap in a box, (U × V) ∩ (U′ × V′) = (U ∩ U′) × (V ∩ V′), so the unions of boxes are a family of open sets on X × Y (2h’s pairs). These are the open sets of X × Y. The straight distance on the plane makes the same open sets as boxes of the line, since a ball holds a box about its centre and a box holds a ball.

Two-parts technique: a map g into X × Y is continuous exactly when both of its parts, the left entry and the right entry of g, are continuous.

\ell(x, y) = x,\ \ \ell^{-1}(U) = U \times Y,\ \text{a box} @@ the left entry is continuous; so is the right entry, likewise
g \text{ continuous} \Rightarrow \ell \circ g,\ r \circ g \text{ continuous} @@ the pull-back-chain technique (34b2)
g^{-1}(U \times V) = (\ell \circ g)^{-1}(U) \cap (r \circ g)^{-1}(V) @@ g(z) is in the box exactly when its left entry is in U and its right entry in V
g^{-1}(\textstyle\bigcup_i B_i) = \bigcup_i g^{-1}(B_i) @@ a pull-back of a union is the union of the pull-backs
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both parts continuous} \Rightarrow g \text{ continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ overlaps of two open sets, and unions of open sets, are open

34b4Points Kept Apart

In a space, a list x₁, x₂, … settles at x when every open set holding x holds every xₙ from some N on. With a distance, this is 34a3’s settling, since every open set about x holds a ball about x. Without one, strange things happen. In the lumped family every list settles at every point at once, since the only open set holding a point is the whole space.

A space keeps points apart when any two different points x and y sit in open sets U and V that share no point. Every space with a distance does: the balls of reach d(x, y)/2 about each, by 10b’s half-the-distance line.

One-limit-apart technique: in a space that keeps points apart, a list settles at one point at most.

x_n \to x,\ \ x_n \to y,\ \ x \ne y @@ suppose two limits
x \in U,\ y \in V,\ U \cap V = \emptyset,\ U, V \text{ open} @@ the space keeps points apart
x_n \in U \text{ for } n \ge N_1,\ \ x_n \in V \text{ for } n \ge N_2 @@ the list settles at both
x_{\max(N_1, N_2)} \in U \cap V = \emptyset @@ a false line
\htmlClass{new}{x = y} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition falls

The finite-leftover family on the line does not keep points apart. Two open sets that are not empty have finite leftovers, and the line is endless, so they always share a point. So no distance makes it. A family of open sets is more than a distance with its numbers rubbed out.

34b5Parts, and Gluing

A part A of a space X is a space of its own: its open sets are the overlaps U ∩ A with U open in X. So [0, 1/2) is open in [0, 1], as (−1, 1/2) ∩ [0, 1], though it is not open in the line.

Gluing. Let ∼ be a sameness relation on X, sorting it into 2h’s classes, and let X/∼ be the set of classes. The map q sends x to its class. A set of classes is open when its pull-back by q is open in X. That is the largest family that keeps q continuous. Glue 0 to 1 in [0, 1], and nothing else: the result is a loop. Glue the left side of a square to the right, and the top to the bottom: the surface of a doughnut, 34k’s torus.

Glued-map technique: a map g out of a glued space X/∼ is continuous exactly when g ∘ q is continuous.

(g \circ q)^{-1}(W) = q^{-1}(g^{-1}(W)) @@ the pull-back, read twice
g \text{ continuous} \Rightarrow g \circ q \text{ continuous} @@ the pull-back-chain technique
g \circ q \text{ continuous} \Rightarrow q^{-1}(g^{-1}(W)) \text{ open for open } W @@ the line above
\htmlClass{new}{g^{-1}(W) \text{ is open in } X/\!\sim} \quad \blacksquare @@ that is what open means in a glued space

So to make a continuous map out of a glued space, make one out of X that sends glued points to one place. The loop made from [0, 1] maps onto the circle by t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt), since 0 and 1 land on the same point. Is that map a matching of the two spaces, open set for open set? 34e answers.

The accepted names

A family of open sets is a topology, and a space is a topological space. The scattered family is the discrete topology; the lumped family is the indiscrete or trivial topology; the finite-leftover family is the cofinite topology; the family of the converse standing question is the cocountable topology. A space that keeps points apart is a Hausdorff space, or T₂. The boxes make the product topology, the open sets of a part make the subspace topology, and the open sets of a glued space make the quotient topology. The space of the build standing question is the Sierpiński space.

Who said it first

Felix Hausdorff laid down spaces by neighbourhoods in 1914, with the rule that keeps points apart among them. Kazimierz Kuratowski built them in 1922 from a rule that hands each set its closure, and Pavel Alexandrov and others moved to open sets in the 1920s. The family of open sets became the standard start in the 1930s and 1940s, with the books of the group writing as Nicolas Bourbaki. Frigyes Riesz had already proposed nearness of points to sets in 1909.

A proof that is not allowed

“A list settles at x and at y. Take ε = d(x, y)/2. Then the terms are near both, which is impossible. So limits are unique in every space.” Not allowed: the proof used a distance. In the lumped family there is no distance and no pair of separate open sets, and every list settles at every point.
