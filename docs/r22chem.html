Does the Order of Adding Change a Crystal's Energy?

Does the order of adding change a crystal’s energy? Table salt is a crystal of charged atoms. Each sodium carries one charge and each chlorine the opposite one, and they are packed so that every atom sits among neighbours of the other kind. Opposite charges pull together and like charges push apart. Chemists wanted the energy that holds such a crystal together, because it says how hard the crystal is to melt, or to pull apart in water. To find it, they added up the pull and the push on one atom from every other atom in the crystal, near and far. What makes it hard is that there are endlessly many atoms, and their pulls and pushes nearly cancel. The total then depends on the order of adding, and a careless order gives a wrong answer, or none. This page shows, with rung twenty-two’s sums whose signs alternate and rung seventeen’s log as an area, that a single row of such atoms has a total, what the total is, how reordering the very same terms changes it, and why the order that matches a real crystal adds the atoms in shells, nearest first.

Along a row, the neighbours of one atom take turns: the nearest two pull, the next two push, the next two pull, each pair weaker than the last. So the energy is an endless sum whose signs alternate and whose terms shrink, and it settles. Its total is twice the log of two, found by fencing the partial sums between areas under one over x. Added in another order, the same terms settle somewhere else. Gathering two pulling shells for every pushing one gives a bigger total, and one for every two gives a smaller one. Every such order gathers pieces of the crystal whose charge grows without bound. A real crystal is close to neutral, piece by piece, and every order that keeps it so gives the shell by shell total.

22chem1A Row of Alternating Charges

From here on, on this page, a charged row is a straight row of atoms, a apart, whose charges take turns: +e, −e, +e, −e, and so on without end in both directions. Here e means the size of one electron's charge, not 17e's number e.

A measured law gives the energy of two charges q₁ and q₂ at distance r: K q₁ q₂ / r, with Ke² = 1.44 eV·nm. A negative energy is a pull; a positive one, a push. Pick one atom, of charge +e. Its shell n is the two atoms at distance na, one on each side. They carry −e when n is odd and +e when n is even.

\text{shell } n:\ \ 2 \cdot \tfrac{K e \cdot (\mp e)}{na} = -\tfrac{2Ke^2}{a} \cdot \tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} @@ the measured law, two atoms; odd shells pull
U = -\tfrac{2Ke^2}{a}\left(1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \tfrac14 + \cdots\right) @@ the shells, nearest first
\text{signs alternate, sizes } \tfrac1n \text{ fall to } 0 @@ the bracket technique (22g)

Charged-row technique: the energy of one atom in a charged row is −2Ke²/a times 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + ⋯, a sum that settles between any two partial sums in a row.

22chem2Its Total Is Twice the Log of Two

Write Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/n. Take the first 2n terms of the bracket, and turn them into a stretch of Hₙ’s terms that the log can fence.

S_{2n} = H_{2n} - 2\left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots + \tfrac1{2n}\right) @@ add every term, then take the even ones off twice
= H_{2n} - H_n = \tfrac1{n+1} + \tfrac1{n+2} + \cdots + \tfrac1{2n} @@ 2 · 1/(2j) = 1/j
\int_{n+1}^{2n+1}\tfrac{dx}{x} \le \tfrac1{n+1} + \cdots + \tfrac1{2n} \le \int_{n}^{2n}\tfrac{dx}{x} @@ each 1/j is a strip of width 1; 1/x falls (15h)
\ln\tfrac{2n+1}{n+1} \le S_{2n} \le \ln 2 @@ the log as an area (17a); log-of-a-quotient (17c)
\htmlClass{new}{1 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 - \cdots = \ln 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ (2n + 1)/(n + 1) → 2; and S₂ₙ₊₁ − S₂ₙ = 1/(2n + 1) → 0

Total technique: the bracket totals ln 2, so one atom of a charged row has energy −2 ln 2 · Ke²/a. For table salt’s spacing, Ke²/a = 1.44 / 0.282 ≈ 5.11 eV, and the energy is about −1.386 × 5.11 ≈ −7.08 eV.

The same fences, from pm to qm, give 1/(pm + 1) + ⋯ + 1/(qm) between ln((qm + 1)/(pm + 1)) and ln(q/p). So Hqm − Hpm heads to ln(q/p), for any whole p and q. The next step needs this.

22chem3The Same Terms, Another Total

Make every term positive and the bracket becomes 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯, which passes every ceiling (22d’s grouping technique). So the bracket settles only by taking turns, and 22g’s reorder technique says such a sum can be reordered to total anything. For the row, a reorder is an order of gathering the shells.

Gather in blocks: p pulling shells, the nearest not yet taken, then q pushing shells, the nearest not yet taken, and again. After m blocks, the pulling shells taken are 1, 3, …, 2pm − 1 and the pushing shells 2, 4, …, 2qm.

\text{pulls} = 1 + \tfrac13 + \cdots + \tfrac1{2pm - 1} = H_{2pm} - \tfrac12 H_{pm} @@ every term to 2pm, less the even ones
\text{pushes} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots + \tfrac1{2qm} = \tfrac12 H_{qm} @@ take ½ out
\text{pulls} - \text{pushes} = (H_{2pm} - H_{pm}) + \tfrac12 (H_{pm} - H_{qm}) @@ regroup a finite sum
\htmlClass{new}{\to \ln 2 + \tfrac12 \ln\tfrac{p}{q}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 22·chem.2, twice; inside a block the partial sums move by at most p + q terms, each heading to 0

Block-order technique: gathering p pulling shells for every q pushing shells gives the total ln 2 + ½ ln(p/q), not ln 2. One pull for two pushes gives ½ ln 2. Two pulls for one push give (3/2) ln 2. One pull for four pushes gives 0: no energy at all, from the very same atoms.

22chem4Why Shell by Shell

Each order of gathering builds the row piece by piece: the centre atom and the shells taken so far. A pulling shell adds −2e and a pushing shell +2e. So a piece holding u pulling and v pushing shells has charge e(1 − 2u + 2v).

\text{shell by shell}:\ \ u - v \in \{0, 1\},\ \text{charge } +e \text{ or } -e @@ at every stage
p : q \text{ blocks}:\ \ \text{charge} = e\,(1 - 2pm + 2qm) \text{ after } m \text{ blocks} @@ it passes every bound unless p = q
\text{pulls} - \text{pushes} = (H_{2u} - H_u) + \tfrac12 (H_u - H_v) @@ as in 22·chem.3, with u pulls and v pushes taken
|u - v| \le C \Rightarrow |H_u - H_v| \le \tfrac{C}{\min(u, v)} \to 0 @@ at most C terms, each at most 1/min(u, v)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{bounded charge} \Rightarrow \text{total} = \ln 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ H₂ᵤ − Hᵤ → ln 2 (22·chem.2)

Neutral-piece technique: every order of gathering whose pieces keep a bounded charge gives the same total, ln 2. A real crystal is grown, or cut, in pieces that are neutral but for a few charges at the surface. So the shell by shell total is the crystal’s energy. The lopsided orders describe pieces with huge charge, which no crystal on a shelf carries.

The accepted names

The charged row is a one-dimensional ionic crystal, and the measured law is Coulomb’s law, with K the Coulomb constant. The number multiplying Ke²/a, here 2 ln 2, is the Madelung constant; for the salt crystal in three directions it is about 1.7476. The bracket is the alternating harmonic series. A sum that settles only because of its signs converges conditionally, and the block-order technique is a worked case of the Riemann rearrangement theorem. The neutral-piece technique is the idea behind Evjen’s method, and the modern way to sum a crystal is Ewald summation.

Who said it first

Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet showed in 1837 that the alternating harmonic series, reordered two positive terms to one negative, totals (3/2) ln 2; Bernhard Riemann proved (written 1854, published 1868) that such a sum can be reordered to total anything. Erwin Madelung computed the energy of ionic crystals in 1918, and Paul Peter Ewald found a sum that settles quickly in 1921. H. M. Evjen (1932) warned that the sums in use did not settle, and summed in neutral blocks. In 22·chem.6.5, David Borwein, Jonathan Borwein and Keith Taylor proved in 1985 that the ball by ball sum for salt does not settle.
