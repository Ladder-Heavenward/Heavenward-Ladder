10a · proof

The Challenge and the Answer

This step writes down what “gets as close as you like” means, as one statement of every and some, and then wins the game three times. Two Greek letters come in, one for the challenger's gap and one for your answer. A neighbourhood of a point is defined with the point itself taken out. And the line for “a function heads to a value at an input” is laid down, with its marks. Then the first games: the linear function, where the answer undoes the step; squaring, where a first promise is needed to tame what is left over; and one over the input, where the first promise must also keep the inputs away from 0. Along the way, a technique that makes 9a's missing input harmless: the value at the point plays no part.

For every gap they name around the value, you name a reach around the input such that every input within that reach, other than the input itself, lands its output in their gap.

10a1Two Letters, for Two Gaps

The game has two moves, and each move is a positive real. One is a gap named by the challenger: how near the outputs must come. The other is a reach named by you: how near the inputs must be. Any letters would do. Mathematicians have used the same two Greek letters since the nineteenth century, so the ladder takes them, one line each.

From here on, ε (the Greek letter epsilon, in its small form) names a positive real given as a challenge: how near the outputs must come. On the ladder’s limit pages ε always names a positive real, and its capital form is not used. A page that gives ε another job says so.

From here on, δ (the Greek letter delta, in its small form) names a positive real given as an answer: how near the inputs must be. On the ladder’s limit pages δ always names a positive real. A page that gives δ another job says so. Its capital form is a different mark, and is not used here.

Both are names in 0a's sense, like any letter; the habit only tells you their job. With low marks they name several of a kind: δ₁, δ₂ and δ₃ are three answers, as 3f's low marks allow. Each sign will want a condition on its letter, “positive”, so first a shorthand, the one 4j made for numbers, now with reals.

From here on, ∀ε > 0 P(ε) is short for ∀ε (ε ∈ ℝ ∧ 0 < ε ⇒ P(ε)), and ∃δ > 0 P(δ) is short for ∃δ (δ ∈ ℝ ∧ 0 < δ ∧ P(δ)). Read them “for every positive ε” and “for some positive δ”. Likewise with any letter in place of ε or δ.

10a2A Neighbourhood, With the Point Taken Out

8h's stretch says that the reals within d of w, |x − w| < d, are exactly the interval (w − d, w + d). The game needs that interval with one real removed: w itself, because the claim is about w's neighbours, not about w.

From here on, for a real w and a positive real δ, the neighbourhood of w of reach δ is the set {x ∈ ℝ : 0 < |x − w| < δ}. It is the interval (w − δ, w + δ) with w itself removed, which is (w − δ, w) ∪ (w, w + δ). Its accepted name is a punctured neighbourhood. And “f is defined near w” means that some neighbourhood of w is a part of dom f.

0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta @@ the condition of the line, for a real x
|x - a| \lt \delta \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{a - \delta \lt x \lt a + \delta} @@ the stretch (8h, by unpack-the-bars)
0 \lt |x - a| \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{x \ne a} @@ |p| = 0 only at p = 0 (8h); |p| ≥ 0; and x − a = 0 exactly when x = a (adding-keeps-answers)
x \ne a \;\Leftrightarrow\; x \lt a \lor a \lt x @@ three-way-order on ℝ (8b): exactly one of x < a, x = a, a < x
\begin{gathered}\{x : 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta\}\\ = \htmlClass{keep}{(a - \delta, a) \cup (a, a + \delta)}\end{gathered} @@ chase a member through the three lines; merge-membership (2f); same-members

So “x is in the neighbourhood of w of reach δ” and “w − δ < x < w + δ and x ≠ w” are the same statement. For x ↦ x², every real is an input, so it is defined near every w. For x ↦ 1/x, defined on ℝ ∖ {0}, it is defined near every w ≠ 0, with reach |w|, and not near 0. 9a's H(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1), defined on ℝ ∖ {1}, is defined near 1: every neighbourhood of 1 leaves 1 out.

10a3The Definition

Here is the claim in plain words first. f heads to L at w when, however small a gap anyone names around L, there is a neighbourhood of w in which f stays inside the gap. And now the line, which says exactly that and nothing more.

From here on, let f be a function whose inputs and outputs are reals, defined near a real w, and let L be a real. Then lim_{x→w} f(x) = L means: ∀ε > 0 ∃δ > 0 : ∀x ∈ dom f (0 < |x − w| < δ ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε). Read it “f heads to L at w”, or “the limit of f(x), as x heads to w, is L”. It may also be written f(x) → L as x → w. A real L for which it is true is a limit of f at w, and f has a limit at w when some real is one.

From here on, lim, from the Latin limes, a boundary, is a mark read “the limit”. The whole of lim_{x→w} f(x) = L is one statement. Until 10b proves that at most one real L passes, lim_{x→w} f(x) is not used alone as the name of a real.

From here on, x → w, read “x heads to w”, is part of the mark: it names the letter that runs through the inputs and the real it is near. It is not a statement, and nothing moves. This arrow is neither 4d's arrow in f : A → B nor 5·chem's “become” nor 1e's “gives” in a working; what stands on its two sides says which. The letter x is bound by the mark, as 2b's placeholders are bound, so lim_{t→w} f(t) = L says the same thing.

From here on, a colon after a some, as in ∃δ > 0 : P, is read “such that”, as 2e's colon was read inside braces: there is a positive δ such that P. It adds nothing to the statement; it only marks where the some's condition begins.

4d's colon in f : A → B and 5j's colon of a ratio keep their jobs; what stands on each side says which.

Read the line from the right end, which is where the action is. |f(x) − L| < ε: the output is within ε of L, a distance in 8h's sense around a displacement f(x) − L. In 9e's plane, the pair (x, f(x)) lies in one of the rows from L − ε to L + ε: call those rows the target. 0 < |x − w| < δ: the input is in the neighbourhood of w of reach δ. In the plane, the pair lies in one of the columns from w − δ to w + δ, the column at w left out: call those columns the window. The arrow is the promise: every pair of f in the window is in the target. ∀x ∈ dom f: for every input, so no input in the window escapes. ∃δ > 0 : you can make such a promise with some positive reach. ∀ε > 0: whatever positive gap the challenger names. Read in full, the line says this. For every positive ε there is a positive δ such that, for every input x, if x is in the neighbourhood of w of reach δ then f(x) is within ε of L. This is the form the line took in lectures in Berlin in the nineteenth century.

A well-known calculus book calls the limit perhaps the most important idea in calculus, and the hardest. It advises learning this definition word for word, like a poem, rather than carrying a vague version of it that lets false statements through.

Each piece earns its place. Why must δ be positive? A reach of 0 gives the condition 0 < |x − w| < 0, which no x meets. The if would then be so for every x by 1d's free-if, and every L at all would “win”. The window must hold inputs for the promise to say anything. Why must f be defined near w? For the same reason: if no input of f were near w, every promise would be kept with nothing in the window. Why the order ∀ε ∃δ? Because the challenger moves first and your δ may depend on their ε. Swap the signs, ∃δ ∀ε, and you would need one reach that works against every gap at once. Then for each x in that window, |f(x) − L| would be less than every positive real, and so 0. (Were it positive, the challenge ε = |f(x) − L| would give |f(x) − L| < |f(x) − L|.) f would be exactly L on a neighbourhood of w. 2b's licensed-swap says the swapped sentence gives the true one, never back: ∃∀ is the stronger claim.

10a4The Value at the Point Plays No Part

The 0 < in 0 < |x − w| keeps w out of the game. That is on purpose. Agreeing-near technique: if f and g are defined near w and f(x) = g(x) for every x in some neighbourhood of w, of reach r, then lim_{x→w} f(x) = L exactly when lim_{x→w} g(x) = L. First a small tech about 8b's min. Lesser-below-both technique: for reals p and q, min(p, q) ≤ p and min(p, q) ≤ q; and if p and q are both positive, so is min(p, q). By 8b's line, min(p, q) is p if p ≤ q and q if not. So in either case it is one of them, and is at most the other (three-way-order on ℝ turns ¬(p ≤ q) into q < p). ∎

\begin{gathered}\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \text{call } \delta \gt 0 \text{ with}\\ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |g(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon\end{gathered} @@ lim g = L: use the every at ε, then the some
\delta' = \htmlClass{new}{\min(\delta, r)} \gt 0 @@ lesser-below-both: positive, and at most each
\begin{gathered}0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta' \;\Rightarrow\\ 0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \;\land\; 0 \lt |x - a| \lt r\end{gathered} @@ assume the left; at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ chains δ′ ≤ δ and δ′ ≤ r
f(x) = g(x) @@ x is in the neighbourhood of reach r: the hypothesis
|f(x) - L| = |g(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon @@ the swap axiom; the promise for g
\lim_{x \to w} f(x) = L \quad \blacksquare @@ prove an if; speak of everything (x, then ε); ︿ the line

So two functions that agree near w, except perhaps at w, have the same limits there.

From here on, a function has a hole at w when it is defined near w but w is not one of its inputs. 5f's hole, a splitting of the fractions with no fraction at the split, is another use of the word; what it is said of says which.

That is what makes 9a's hole harmless. H(x) = (x² − 1)/(x − 1) on ℝ ∖ {1} agrees with x ↦ x + 1 on every neighbourhood of 1 (difference-of-squares read in ℝ, then cancel x − 1, which is not 0 there). So once the next section proves lim_{x→1} (x + 1) = 2, agreeing-near gives lim_{x→1} H(x) = 2. Give the function a stray value at 1: 3, by a separate case (7e's definition by cases). It still agrees with x + 1 on the neighbourhood, and the limit is still 2. The value at 1 is 3, the limit at 1 is 2, and nothing is wrong.

10a5The First Game: The Linear Function

Line-limit technique: for reals m, b and w, lim_{x→w} (mx + b) = mw + b. The trick is the one this whole rung runs on: rewrite the output's distance until the input's distance appears inside it.

|(mx + b) - (mw + b)| = |\htmlClass{new}{mx - mw}| @@ the rule at x and at w; negative-of-a-sum, collect (8d)
= |\htmlClass{new}{m(x - a)}| = |m|\,|x - a| @@ spread on ℝ backwards; size-of-a-product (8h): the displacement x − a appears
\text{put } \htmlClass{new}{\delta = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{|m|}} \gt 0 @@ |m| positive since m ≠ 0; its reciprocal is positive (positive-reciprocal on ℚ), and so is the product (positive-product on ℝ)
0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; |m|\,|x - a| \lt |m|\,\delta = \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree, multiplying by the positive |m|; reciprocal on ℝ
|(mx + b) - (ma + b)| \lt \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap axiom; prove an if; speak of everything; for m = 0 the left is |b − b| = 0 < ε, and any δ wins

The linear function multiplies distances by |m| (9e's same-ratio, read with bars), so you must come |m| times nearer than the gap you were given. For 3x + 1 at 2, heading to 7, the answer is δ = ε/3. When the challenger names 0.6, you answer 0.2: at x = 2.15, which is within 0.2 of 2, the output is 7.45, within 0.6 of 7. If they name 0.003, you answer 0.001. You never run out of answers, and that is what the line claims. Two instances are used constantly. m = 1, b = 0 gives lim_{x→w} x = w, answered by δ = ε. And m = 0 gives a constant, lim_{x→w} b = b, where every δ wins because the output never moves.

10a6The Second Game: x², and a First Promise

Square-limit technique: for every real w, lim_{x→w} x² = w². Expose the displacement: |x² − w²| = |x − w| · |x + w|, by difference-of-squares read in ℝ and size-of-a-product. But now what is left over, |x + w|, is not a constant. The tempting answer δ = ε/|x + w| is out of sequence. The line says ∃δ ∀x, so δ is named before any x is handed over, and a δ with x in it names nothing. You need a number at least as big as |x + w| for every x you might be handed, and with no limit on x there is none. So make a first promise.

|x^2 - a^2| = |x - a|\,\htmlClass{keep}{|x + a|} @@ difference-of-squares read in ℝ; size-of-a-product
\begin{gathered}\htmlClass{new}{|x - a| \lt 1} \;\Rightarrow\\ |x + a| = |(x - a) + 2a| \le |x - a| + 2|a|\end{gathered} @@ the first promise; x + a = (x − a) + 2a; triangle and size-of-a-product (8h)
|x + a| \lt \htmlClass{new}{1 + 2|a|} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: the leftover has a ceiling on this stretch
\text{put } \htmlClass{new}{\delta = \min\!\big(1, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{1 + 2|a|}\big)} @@ both promises at once (lesser-below-both)
\begin{gathered}0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\\ |x^2 - a^2| \lt (1 + 2|a|)\,|x - a|\end{gathered} @@ δ ≤ 1 gives the ceiling; order-and-arithmetic-agree by the positive |x − a|
|x^2 - a^2| \lt (1 + 2|a|)\,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1 + 2|a|} = \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare @@ δ ≤ ε/(1 + 2|a|); order-and-arithmetic-agree; reciprocal on ℝ

At w = 2, the answer is δ = min(1, ε/5). For small gaps ε/5 is the smaller: ε = 0.1 gives δ = 0.02, and at x = 2.019 the output is 4.076361, within 0.1 of 4. For big gaps 1 is: ε = 10 gives δ = 1. Why both? Drop the first promise and answer ε = 10 with ε/5 = 2. Then x = 3.9 is within 2 of 2, and 3.9² = 15.21, which is 11.21 from 4: the challenger wins. Out at 3.9 the leftover |x + 2| is 5.9, more than the 5 you counted on. The ceiling was only true inside the first promise, so the answer must keep both. The 1 is a convenience: any fixed positive first promise works, with its own ceiling.

10a7The Third Game: 1/x, and Keeping off 0

One-over-x-limit technique: for every real w ≠ 0, lim_{x→w} 1/x = 1/w. Here the leftover has no ceiling at all near 0, so the first promise has a second job: keep the inputs away from 0.

\left|\tfrac1x - \tfrac1a\right| = \tfrac{|a - x|}{|x|\,|a|} = |x - a| \cdot \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac{1}{|x|\,|a|}} @@ one bottom (5h's fractions-of-rationals, read in ℝ); size-of-a-product; size-of-a-negative
\htmlClass{new}{|x - a| \lt \tfrac{|a|}{2}} \;\Rightarrow\; |a| - |x| \le |a - x| \lt \tfrac{|a|}{2} @@ the first promise, half the distance from a to 0; the reverse form of triangle (8h)
|x| \gt \tfrac{|a|}{2} \gt 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \tfrac{1}{|x|} \lt \tfrac{2}{|a|} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree; x ≠ 0, so 1/x names a real; positive-reciprocal on ℚ read in ℝ
\text{put } \htmlClass{new}{\delta = \min\!\big(\tfrac{|a|}{2}, \tfrac{a^2\varepsilon}{2}\big)} @@ both promises (lesser-below-both); |a|² = a² by size-squared
\begin{gathered}0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; \left|\tfrac1x - \tfrac1a\right| \lt |x - a| \cdot \tfrac{2}{a^2}\\ \lt \tfrac{a^2\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\tfrac{2}{a^2} = \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare\end{gathered} @@ the ceiling, then the second promise; order-and-arithmetic-agree twice; reciprocal on ℝ

Near 3 a simpler first promise does the job: |x − 3| < 1 keeps x above 2, so 3|x| > 6 and the leftover 1/(3|x|) is below 1/6. Then δ = min(1, 6ε) wins. Check a round: ε = 0.01 gives δ = 0.06, and at x = 2.95 the output 1/2.95 is about 0.338983, which is about 0.00565 from 1/3: inside the gap. Different first promises give different recipes, and both are honest; the technique's recipe gives min(3/2, 9ε/2) at 3. But near w = ½ the promise of 1 would let x be 0.001, where the leftover 1/(|x||w|) is 2000, or reach past 0 altogether. The technique's promise, half the distance to 0, never does. This is the first place the answer depends on where the inputs are heading as well as on the gap. Near 0 it must shrink, and at 0 there is no answer at all (10e shows why).

10a9Running It Backwards

Handed a recipe and asked what it was for: δ = ε/7 wins every round for a linear function exactly when |m| ≤ 7, because the line needs |m| · ε/7 ≤ ε. Handed a δ that loses, find the x that escapes: it is where the leftover is largest, at the edge of the window furthest from the trouble. Handed a function with no value at w and asked for its limit, find a function that agrees with it near w and whose limit you have, then cite agreeing-near. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The line’s form is the one Karl Weierstrass gave in his Berlin lectures. The calculus book is Michael Spivak’s. The 1821 course in 10a.4.4 is Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s Cours d’analyse, and the footnote is Niels Henrik Abel’s (1826).

Spivak calls the limit perhaps the most important idea in calculus, and the hardest, and advises learning this definition word for word, like a poem, rather than carrying a vague version of it that lets false statements through.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 5

Accepted names: the line is the epsilon-delta definition of the limit; the neighbourhood with its point left out is a punctured neighbourhood (or deleted neighbourhood).
