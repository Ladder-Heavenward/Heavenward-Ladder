37f · proof

The Spectral View

Speech does not hold still. A word lasts a fraction of a second and its recipe changes many times inside it. This step looks at a sound the way a recording studio screen does: cut it into short pieces, break each piece into frequencies, and lay the pieces side by side, so that time runs across and frequency runs up. Rung 32 already set the price of doing that: the shorter the piece, the blurrier its frequencies. Then the step takes a voice apart into a buzz and a tube. The buzz sets the pitch and the tube sets the vowel, and the tube’s favourite frequencies, which can be worked out from its length, are the marks by which one vowel is told from another. The hiss of an s has no pitch at all, only a hill of high frequencies. And every instrument is known as much by how its notes begin as by their recipe.

Cut a sound into short pieces and break each into frequencies: time across, frequency up. Short pieces blur frequency. A voice is a buzz shaped by a tube: the buzz gives the pitch, the tube gives the vowel. A hiss has no pitch but a band. Quality is the recipe and the way it moves.

37f1A Spectrum That Moves

Take a stretch of sound of length T, from a time t₀ to t₀ + T, and break it into frequencies by 32e1’s transform. Slide the stretch along and do it again. From here on, the sound picture of a sound is the amount at each frequency in the stretch starting at each time: a function of two variables, time across and frequency up, drawn as darkness on a page.

The price is 32f2’s. A stretch of length T cannot be sharp in frequency: a single turning cut to length T spreads over a band about 1/T wide. So the sound picture trades one sharpness for the other.

Window-trade technique: with stretches of length T, two turnings closer than about 1/T in frequency blur into one, and two clicks closer than T in time blur into one. Proof: the two widths of 32f2, read for a stretch cut out of a sound.

T = 0.02 \text{ s}:\ 1/T = 50 \text{ Hz} @@ every syllable seen; harmonics of a 100 Hz voice barely apart
T = 0.1 \text{ s}:\ 1/T = 10 \text{ Hz} @@ harmonics clear; a quick d or t smeared
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sharp in time} \Rightarrow \text{blurred in frequency}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the window-trade technique

37f2Source and Filter

A voice is made in two parts. The folds in the throat open and shut many times a second and let out puffs of air: a buzz that repeats at the pitch of the voice and so, by the repeat-rate technique (37b1), holds every whole multiple. Then the buzz passes up the throat and out of the mouth, a tube that favours some frequencies and damps others: a filter in 32i2’s sense, which multiplies each turning by its own number.

Source-filter technique: a buzz passed through a filter has, at each of its harmonics, the buzz’s amount times the filter’s gain there; the buzz sets which frequencies are present and the filter sets how much of each. Proof: 32i2’s gain-per-turning technique, applied turning by turning.

\text{buzz at } 100 \text{ Hz: harmonics } 100, 200, 300, \ldots @@ a low voice
\text{buzz at } 250 \text{ Hz: harmonics } 250, 500, 750, \ldots @@ a high voice
\text{each amount} \times \text{the tube’s gain } H(f) @@ the same tube shape: the same H
\htmlClass{new}{\text{different pitch, same hills in the recipe}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the source-filter technique

So pitch and vowel are separate knobs. Sing “ah” up a scale and the harmonics move while the hills of the tube stay; say “ee, ah, oo” on one note and the harmonics stay while the hills move. The listener hears the hills as the vowel and the spacing of the harmonics as the pitch.

37f3Formants

The hills of the tube’s gain are its formants, counted from the lowest: F1, F2, F3. A straight tube closed at one end and open at the other, about as long as a throat and mouth, 17.5 cm, has them at frequencies set by its length alone.

Like 33c3’s string held at both ends, a tube holds only certain shapes. At the closed end the air cannot move, so the pressure has its crest there; at the open end the pressure is the room’s, so the sound pressure is 0. The shapes cos((2k − 1)πx/(2L)) do both, for k = 1, 2, 3, …

Tube technique: a tube closed at one end and open at the other favours the frequencies (2k − 1)c/(4L), the odd multiples of c/(4L). Proof: the shape cos((2k − 1)πx/(2L)) has wavelength 4L/(2k − 1), and the wavelength technique (37a4) turns it into the frequency.

\lambda_k = 4L/(2k - 1) @@ a crest at the closed end, 0 at the open end
f_k = c/\lambda_k = (2k - 1)\,c/(4L) @@ the wavelength technique
\htmlClass{new}{L = 0.175 \text{ m}:\ 490,\ 1470,\ 2450 \text{ Hz}} \quad \blacksquare @@ c = 343 m/s; close to the neutral vowel in “about”

Other vowels squeeze the tube in places and move the hills. Laid down, measured from adult men: “ee” has F1 ≈ 270 and F2 ≈ 2290; “ah” has F1 ≈ 730 and F2 ≈ 1090; “oo” has F1 ≈ 300 and F2 ≈ 870. So a vowel is a region of the plane of (F1, F2), and the plane is a map of every vowel a mouth can make.

37f4Sibilance

Hiss “sss” and the folds are still. The sound comes from air forced through a narrow gap against the teeth: a rush with no period, so no pitch (37b1). Its sound picture is not a ladder of harmonics but a smear: a hill spread over a band of frequencies.

From here on, a hiss is a sound with no period whose amounts, averaged over a stretch, fill a band. Laid down, measured: “s” puts most of its power above about 4000 Hz; “sh”, made with the tongue further back and a longer space in front of the gap, starts lower, about 2000 Hz; “f” is weak and spread thin across the top.

Hiss-band technique: a hiss is told from another hiss by where its band lies, not by any pitch. Proof: neither has a period, so pitch is not defined (37b1); what is left to tell them apart is the amount at each frequency, and the bands differ.

\text{an old phone line passes about } 300 \text{ to } 3400 \text{ Hz} @@ laid down, by the line
\text{“s”: most of its band above } 4000 \text{ Hz} @@ cut away
\htmlClass{new}{\text{“s” and “f” are hard to tell apart on the phone}} \quad \blacksquare @@ hence “s as in Sam”

37f5Attack and Decay

A recipe is a steady thing: the amounts of one repeating note (37d1). Real notes begin and end. A piano hammer strikes and the string’s high turnings flare up and die within a fraction of a second; the low ones ring on. A violin’s bow scrapes before the string settles into its note.

Laid down, measured: cut the first tenth of a second off recordings of a piano, a trumpet and a violin playing one note, and listeners often cannot name the instrument; play a piano note backwards, and it sounds like a reed organ. Quality is the recipe and the way it moves: how a note begins and ends tells as much as what it holds while it lasts.

So the recipe of 37d1 is a column of the sound picture taken in the steady middle of a note, and the instrument is the whole picture. Two notes with equal recipes can still sound like different instruments, if their pictures differ at the start.

The accepted names

The sound picture is a spectrogram, and the transform of short stretches is the short-time Fourier transform. The source-filter technique is the source–filter model of speech; the folds are the vocal folds, or vocal cords, and the tube is the vocal tract. The hiss sounds are fricatives, and s and sh are sibilants. How a note begins and ends is its envelope: attack, decay, sustain and release.

Who said it first

The sound picture was made a machine at Bell Laboratories in the 1940s, the sound spectrograph. Gunnar Fant set out the source–filter account of speech in Acoustic Theory of Speech Production (1960). The vowel numbers above are averages from Gordon Peterson and Harold Barney’s study of 1952.

A proof that is not allowed

“A voice at 100 Hz has a formant at 490 Hz. So it has a turning at 490 Hz.” Not allowed: the source-filter technique multiplies amounts that are there; the buzz has turnings only at 100, 200, …, 500, …, and the hill near 490 lifts the ones nearest it, at 500 and 400, not a new one at 490.
