Collecting and Sorting

Rung 1 gave you the techs of reasoning, and every proof on the ladder from here on is written in them. But reasoning needs something to reason about. The ground floor had a cat and a town and a person, borrowed from life; mathematics wants objects of its own, names it can build and take apart and be sure of. This rung supplies the only kind it ever needs. The name is the collection, which mathematics calls a set: a thing whose whole nature is to have members. A set is fixed entirely by what is in it, and nothing else about it counts. A short list of tools, each saying exactly what the members of its result are, builds every set you will ever meet from any at all.

You already do this too. Nobody lists every cat by name, Serena and Murphy and Smudge and and and. You say “the cats”, and the group stands for all its members at once. A set is just the way mathematics writes that habit down, so that one name can carry a whole crowd.

The rung has two goals, and they run in opposite directions. The first turns claims into collections that can be handled as single things: gather what passes a claim into a set. The second gets the claim back out of a collection: being a member is passing the claim. Go around once and you land where you started, the way unpack undoes pack, one level up. One direction has an edge. A claim can be gathered only inside a set you already have, and 2e shows what happens when there is none.

To know a set, know what it takes to be a member. To show sets are the same, show that each member of either is a member of the other. To build a set, name the tool and say what its members are.

The survey · what this rung claims, and what you can do once you know it

Still in midair

Rung 1 ran in foreclosure: its unpacks leaned on membership and the sift before either was built. This rung builds them, and so completes the unpacks’ foreclosure. The proof itself is still held in trust. It is a collection of statements in order, and this rung makes collections and pairs in order, but counting the lines and saying something about every proof wait for rung 4. So the plane is still being assembled in midair (1a7), and some questions ask for an argument before every piece of it is built.

What it means to say the claim

A gap that many things fit, taken as one thing, is a set: 2d1 says it in full. Every way rung 1 changed and joined statements still works when the gap holds a set; the marks just get rounder.

The people in a room, as a collection, are the same collection standing or sitting, in whatever order they came in, and however many times you list one of them by mistake. That is the whole content of the claim, and 2d1 walks through it: a collection has no ingredients other than its members. Not order, not arrangement, not repetition, not the words you used to describe it.

It sounds too plain to need saying. It needs saying because it is a decision. “The colours of a traffic light” and “red, amber and green” are the same object, because the same things are in each, however differently they were described. This is the ground floor's idea of sameness, narrowed to collections. Things are the same when nothing you can ask tells them apart, and the only thing you can ask of a collection is who is in it. One of the first to turn collections into mathematical objects, late in the century before last, put it this way:

He said that by a set he meant any gathering into a whole of definite, distinguishable objects of our perception or thought. Those objects are the set’s elements.

“Into a whole” is the point: the members, gathered, become a thing in its own right. It can itself be named, reasoned about, and made a member of further collections. This ladder uses the later word, and calls a collection a set; its things are its members. Both words are given their meanings on 2d, with a mark for “is a member of”, and 2d6 gives both founders’ words. The principle that a set is nothing but its members is laid down there as an axiom, under its accepted name, extensionality.

Where the tools come from

If a set is fixed by its members, then to build a set is to say exactly who its members are. That is what each of the rung's tools does. Every tool takes sets you already have and hands back a set. It comes with a line of the shape “a thing is a member of the result exactly when…”. The line finishes the phrase with a statement made from rung 1's joining words about the sets you started from. The tools are:

  • Sift. From a set and a condition, the set of those members that pass the condition. The members of the result are exactly the members of the set you started from of which the condition is true. “The fliers among the animals in the zoo.”
  • Gather. From any things, the set whose members are exactly those things. “Zoe and Randy, as a set.”
  • Merge. From a set whose members are themselves sets, the set of everything that is in any of them. Tip the boxes out together. This is where the mark everyone has seen, the one read “union”, is brought in. Its line says to consider the set before the sign and the set after it as one set.
  • All the subsets. From a set, the set whose members are exactly its parts: every set you could make by keeping some of its members and dropping the rest.

Every tool needs a set to start from, and the tools alone, with nothing in hand, build nothing. So the rung lays down an axiom of a different kind, first of all, which does not build but simply asserts: there is a set with nothing in it. Sift any other set with a condition nothing can pass, and you land on it again. By the same-members axiom, there is only one. That set is the only brick the ladder ever needs. Rung 3 gathers it, gathers the result, and keeps going, and everything built above is made of what comes out.

The restriction that sifting needs a set to sift from is the whole safety of the subject. Early in the century before this one, a young logician wrote a short argument to an older one, who had just completed a foundation for arithmetic. The older man's system allowed “the set of all things such that…” for any condition at all, with no set to sift from. The letter asked for the set of all sets that are not members of themselves, and asked whether it is a member of itself. Either answer leads to the other, and something totally false follows by rung 1's techs alone. The foundation fell. 2e shows the argument in full, and exactly which line the sifting tool refuses to write: here the argument proves a technique instead of destroying the subject.

What you can do because you know this

You can test sets for sameness without caring how they were described. Chase a member through both descriptions; if everything in either is in the other, they are the same set. The proofs of this rung are nearly all of that shape. It is the same shape rung 1 taught, an every around an exactly-when, so you already know how to write them.

You can build. Every object the ladder meets later is made by these tools from the set with no members. When a later rung says “let s be the set of all…”, you will be able to ask which tool's axiom the line cites, and which set it sifts from.

You can make order out of orderless things. A set does not care which member you name before which. Yet the ladder needs things in an order: a sender and a recipient, an input and an output, a left entry and a right entry. “t then b” can be encoded as a set built by gather alone, the pair. 2h proves that the trick does what order should do: if two such pairs are the same set, their left entries are the same and their right entries are the same. From pairs come products, and from products relations. A relation is a set of pairs, all that “is a parent of” or “is the same colour as” amounts to. From relations comes the idea that will make new objects on rung 5: deciding what should count as the same, and working with the classes that decision makes.

You can rewrite by techniques. Each tool's membership line is a statement built with rung 1's joining words. So each technique of 1f becomes a technique about sets, name for name. The direction 1f withheld is withheld here too, in exactly the same place.

And you can begin to show what cannot exist. Run against a set of everything, 2e’s outsider set takes one case to ⊥ in rows, and 3a17’s boxes finish the argument: there is no set of all sets. Membership never loops either. No set is a member of itself, no set is a member of a member of itself, and no chain of membership runs down forever. That is the axiom of foundation, laid down in words and in marks in 3a’s cellar (3a·f), once boxes are in hand: a condition that climbs from the members of a set to the set itself is so of every set. Rung 4 leans on it when it needs to know that a step taken again and again never lands where it has already been.

These are first tools. People who use them every day still find new things to build with them.

And 2·stat asks who is in either group and who is in both, sifting each group from a roster and reading the picture of two overlapping groups as rows. It carries a box on loose, then locked. And 2·bio asks whether a whale is a fish: sifted by gills and fins it is not, but any group made of an ancestor and all its descendants that holds a salmon and a lungfish holds the whale. It carries a box on two sizes of truth, again.

Which step does what

The proof steps establish the claim. 2d gives “set” and “member” their meanings, and brings in the marks for membership and its denial and the list between braces. It states the same-members axiom in words and then in marks, and defines “subset”. It proves that every set is a subset of itself, and that sets are the same exactly when each is a subset of the other. And it keeps “member” and “subset” apart. 2e brings in conditions and the Greek letter for “any condition”, and defines sifting. It lays down the axiom that a set exists, derives the set with no members and shows it unique, and runs the outsider-set argument against the sift without a bowl. 2f brings in gather and merge, and defines the set of common members by sifting. It proves the techniques, each matching one from 1f's list. 2g brings in the set of all the subsets and proves that the empty set and the set itself are always on the list.

Then support. 2h builds the pair, and proves that equal pairs have equal parts. It defines products, relations, their properties, and the classes of a sameness relation. 3a·f lays down foundation, and proves that no set is a member of itself and that there is no set of all sets. 2i is the technique page, pictures and practice, and it ends with the rung's benchmark line.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, two steps carry a green box. 2d has two sizes of truth, again: a set is nothing but its members, and no member is the set. 2f has loose, then locked: what was gathered, under what condition, and what locked it.

2k comes last, just before the conclusion, with a green box of its own: the set that speaks for its members. A set carries its condition to every member; it looks at none of them, and it is right about each.

2j carries one too, in its last footing: the ledger, out the window. A ledger is a list of claims, each with what pays for it. A forecast kept that way can have every line paid for and still fail the moment you look out the window: each tech did what it promises, true in, true out, and the trouble was in what went in. Books that balance on paper are not yet the weather.

Rung 3 asks what happens when a tool is applied again and again without end, and whether the endless result can be a set in its own right. Every word of that question is given its meaning here.

Who said it first

The test of sameness from the ground floor is Leibniz’s. The founder who turned collections into mathematical objects is Georg Cantor; his words were these:

Cantor, opening his account of the subject, said that by a set he meant any gathering into a whole of definite, distinguishable objects of our perception or thought, and that those objects are the set's elements.

Georg Cantor, Contributions to the Founding of the Theory of Transfinite Numbers (1895), §1, trans. P. E. B. Jourdain, 1915, p. 85

The founder writing a little earlier is Richard Dedekind, in Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen? (1888). The young logician who wrote the letter, in 1902, is Bertrand Russell; the older man whose foundation fell is Gottlob Frege. The pair built by gather alone is Kazimierz Kuratowski’s (1921).

Accepted names

The outsider-set argument is Russell’s paradox. The pair of 2h is the Kuratowski ordered pair. The techniques of 2f that match 1f’s list of rewrites are called, in the books, the laws of the algebra of sets.
