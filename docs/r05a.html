5a · proof

Equations Revisited

Each rung has opened with a way of building logic: 0a the name and the statement, 1a the line, 2a rows, 3a boxes, 4a the proof. This step goes back to the line with what the ladder now holds. A line about numbers is an equation, and working it is 1a’s line technique, with rung 4’s techniques as the moves. Each of those was proved for every number, so the line holds down the whole series at once. Then the step meets the operators 1a4 promised: not equal, the inequalities, and results in, each with its own condition and its own moves, and names two more, therefore and about equal. It ends with the question that is the rest of this rung: an equation whose gap no counting number fills.

Read the operator first. Make only the moves that keep its condition. When no number fits a gap, the line is asking for more numbers.

5a1A Line About Numbers

1a wrote one long line: statements joined by operators, the whole of it true or false. Its gaps took statements about Ann, Ben and the last piece of pie. Since rung 4 the gaps can take numbers, and names for numbers can be built from other names: 2 + 3, 2 · (3 + 4), 3².

An equation is a line whose operator is =, with a number named on each side. As 1a4 said, = tells you what work you are doing: its two sides name one number, so either may stand in for the other wherever it appears.

Rung 4’s techniques are the moves. Each was proved once and is cited by name. In 2 · (3 + 4) = 2 · 3 + 2 · 4, the spread technique for times over plus is the whole licence. Working on, 2 · 3 + 2 · 4 = 6 + 8 = 14, each change worked out by 4g’s times and 4f’s plus. Every change keeps the line true, and every change is paid for by its technique.

5a2Down the Whole Series

1a6 said that working a line is induction over the series: every change keeps the line true, so what falls out cannot fail if the givens hold. On rung 4 the series became an object: the counting numbers, 0, 1, 2 and on, each the next one after the one before.

Put a letter in a gap: (n + 1) · 2 = n · 2 + 2. The letter n names any number at all, so the line is a claim about every number on the series. Working it is still 1a’s line technique. (n + 1) · 2 = 2 · (n + 1), by the turn-about technique for times; = 2 · n + 2 · 1, by the spread technique; = 2 · n + 2, since multiplying by 1 changes nothing; = n · 2 + 2, by the turn-about technique for times again.

Every technique of rung 4 was proved by induction, once, for every number. So a line worked with them holds at every number on the series at once, and no number has to be checked by itself. That is the difference between working the line and trying cases. Trying n = 0, 1, 2 and 3 checks four numbers. Working the line covers them all.

5a3The Operator’s Condition

A line can tell you one thing about its statement: whether it conforms to its operator’s condition. Each operator has its own condition. = conforms when its two sides name one number, and meeting that condition is what makes a line an equation.

1a4 said that equal is a type of operator, and that later rungs bring new operators that do its job in their own ways. They come now: not equal, ≠; the inequalities, < and ≤; and results in, →. Two more are named here and worked later: therefore, ∴, the operator of a proof’s last line, and about equal, ≈. Each has a condition, and each has its own moves that keep the condition. A move that keeps one operator’s condition can break another’s.

So before you work a line, read its operator. It tells you what the line claims and which techniques you may reach for.

5a4Not Equal

≠ is 1b7’s: x ≠ b unpacks to ¬(x = b). So ≠ conforms exactly when the = inside it does not. 3 ≠ 5 conforms, because 3 = 5 does not. 2 + 3 ≠ 5 does not, because 2 + 3 = 5 does. Every move of ≠ is a move of ¬ over =: unpack, work inside the bracket, pack.

Some moves of = carry over, inside the bracket. ≠ turns about by 1b8: from 3 ≠ 5, write 5 ≠ 3. The swap axiom still works inside the bracket, so 2 + 3 ≠ 7 gives 5 ≠ 7. Adding the same number to both sides keeps ≠: the cancel technique for plus says that if p + c = b + c, then p = b, and turn and deny (3a15) makes that: if ¬(p = b), then ¬(p + c = b + c). Multiplying both sides by a number other than 0 keeps ≠ in the same way, by the cancel technique for times.

Other moves do not carry over, because ¬ over = has no chain. ≠ does not chain: ¬(3 = 5) and ¬(5 = 3), yet 3 = 3. And multiplying both sides by 0 breaks it: 3 ≠ 5, but 3 · 0 = 5 · 0.

5a5Less Than, and at Most

4h built order: p < b exactly when b is p plus some number other than 0, and p ≤ b exactly when b is p plus some number. An inequality is a line whose operator is <, ≤, > or ≥. It conforms when its sides compare that way. 3 < 5 conforms; 5 < 3 does not; 3 ≤ 3 conforms.

Its moves are 4h’s techniques. < chains, by the chain technique for less. Adding the same number to both sides keeps it, by the keep-order technique for plus. Multiplying both sides by the same number other than 0 keeps it, by the keep-order technique for times.

When < turns about, the sign turns about too. From 3 < 5, write 5 > 3, never 5 < 3; ≤ turns about into ≥ the same way. And multiplying both sides by 0 turns < into =: 3 · 0 = 5 · 0. Where = lets either side stand in for the other, an inequality keeps a direction, and every move must keep it.

5a6Results In

A chemical change is written as a line too. A car engine burns octane, the main part of petrol, in the oxygen of the air: 2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O, “two molecules of octane together with twenty-five of oxygen result in sixteen of carbon dioxide together with eighteen of water”. That burning pushes the pistons, and the pistons turn the shaft. Its operator is →, read “results in”, and its + is read “together with”. The two sides do not name one thing: exhaust is not fuel and air. So → is not =, and neither side may stand in for the other.

Results in is 1d’s if, made particular to chemistry. Read the burning as “if two molecules of octane together with twenty-five of oxygen, then sixteen of carbon dioxide together with eighteen of water”. What a chemist writes over the arrow, the heat or the solvent, is more of the if: “if these reagents, dissolved at 200 degrees in this solvent, then this resultant”.

What → claims is a ledger, the one 1a6’s candle kept: no atom is made or lost. → conforms when each kind of atom is counted the same on both sides. Each count is a product, the number in front of a molecule times the small low number after an atom, or a sum of such products. Carbon: 2 · 8 = 16 going in, and 16 · 1 = 16 coming out. Hydrogen: 2 · 18 = 36 going in, and 18 · 2 = 36 coming out. Oxygen: 25 · 2 = 50 going in, and 16 · 2 + 18 · 1 = 50 coming out. So results in conforms exactly when a list of equations holds, one for each kind of atom.

Its moves are the moves of those equations. Multiplying every number in front of a molecule by the same number other than 0 keeps every count balanced: 4 C₈H₁₈ + 50 O₂ → 32 CO₂ + 36 H₂O conforms too. Changing a small low number is not a move: it names a different molecule. 5·chem finds the numbers in front of the molecules by solving the equations.

5a7Therefore, and About Equal

A number is an object, packed from a statement about the relationships of the sets that contain it (3c), so a line about numbers is a run of statements, and so is an arrangement of rows. One operator joins such runs: every proof since 4a has ended on it. Its mark is ∴, read “therefore”, from 4e’s list. It says that the lines above it, taken together, give the line after it. ∴ conforms when the line after it is reached from the lines above by licensed techniques. It is 1d’s if laid across a whole argument: the givens stand on the if side, the last line on the then side, and 3a’s prove-an-if is how a ∴ is earned. A short argument of this shape, given lines and a therefore, has an old name, a syllogism; 2j met the best of them.

∴ chains: a line reached by one proof may stand as a given of the next, and that is how a proved technique gets cited. ∴ does not turn about: you cannot get the premises back from the conclusion. Every M is P, every S is M, therefore every S is P; but “every S is P” gives back neither premise.

One more operator joins the table: ≈, read “about equal”. It is worked at 5h9. v ≈ b says that b is v rounded to as many places as b shows. It is not a sameness, so nothing swaps across it, and ≈ does not chain: 0.445 ≈ 0.45 and 0.45 ≈ 0.5, yet 0.445 rounded to one place is 0.4. Each rounding drops a little, and the drops pile up. Later rungs keep ≈ honest by saying how much was dropped.

Anexact, yet rigorous · loose, then locked

≈ lets a line talk about something closer to a guess than a technique. 1/3 ≈ 0.33 does not claim that 1/3 is 0.33. It claims that 0.33 is 1/3 rounded to two places, and that claim conforms or it does not, like any line.

A guess that says how far it may miss is a statement you can work. Each later tool locks the guess tighter, a place at a time, until at limits the miss is made smaller than any size you name.

1/3 ≈ 0.33. Add three of each side. Does 1 ≈ 0.99 conform?

5a8Which One Goes off First

Rung 5 is the first place operators stack in one line: 2 + 3 · 4² holds three of them. 4i2 laid down the order they go off in, as a decision: inner brackets first, the innermost box before the box around it; then powers; then multiplying and dividing; then adding and taking away. There is a reason the decision runs this way.

Each operator on 4g’s ladder of operations packs a series of the one below it. 3 · 4 packs 4 + 4 + 4. 4² packs 4 · 4. So a power is a packed run of products, and a product is a packed run of sums. Working the highest first unpacks each packed series before anything outside it touches it. 2 + 3 · 4² is 2 + 3 · 16 = 2 + 48 = 50. Start with 2 + 3 instead, and the line breaks into the middle of the run that 3 · 4² packs: (2 + 3) · 16 = 80, a different number. Dividing and taking away are multiplying and adding run backwards, so each goes off with its own.

Brackets come first because a bracket is a box (3a): what is inside is worked to one name before the line outside uses it, inner box before outer. A root is a power run backwards, so it goes off with the powers. A function such as sin or log goes with the brackets: its input is its box, worked to one number before the function goes off on it, and its output is one name the rest of the line may use.

A series is an arrangement with a restricted order, and the restriction makes all the difference in the induction. A proof is the same in that it has a sequence, a kind of series restricted only by contingent order. But anything might be placed in a proof: when nothing is true, everything is permitted, and secret messages can ride along in it. So a proof is about an arrangement. If a proof is satisfied, the arrangement may set into a technique, and its use enters the series. That is why ∴ sits on the table with the operators of a line.

A move is allowed in a line only if it keeps the condition of that line’s operator. Adding the same thing to both sides keeps every exact condition in the table, and so does multiplying both sides by the same number other than 0. ≈ is the exception: a move can push a rounding across its halfway line. The series needs its condition to stay true, so, easy as that, you just can’t violate those rules. If it’s equals, you have to make it so the balance doesn’t change across the sign. Not equal and about equal are both rigorous too: if you know where your limit is, then you have to keep to it. x ≠ 4, x + 4 ≠ 8; √2 ≈ 1.414, 1/7 ≈ 0.142857. The inequal sign has to turn about if you change the inequality. The resultant sign and the therefore sign have their own rules, rigorous in their own way.

The operator says what the line claims, and the claim says which moves to look for.

5a10Asking Backwards

An equation can have a gap that no technique fills: 3 + x = 5. Working it now asks a question: which number, put in the gap, makes the line conform? 2 does, since 3 + 2 = 5. Adding run forwards always has an answer, by the sums-are-numbers technique. This runs adding backwards.

Ask it the other way: 5 + x = 3. Whatever number x is, 5 ≤ 5 + x by the at-most-is-adding technique, and 3 is less than 5. So no counting number put in the gap makes the line conform. The line can be written, and no number makes it an equation. 5b1 proves the plainest case, 3 + x = 0, as a technique. Multiplying run backwards gets stuck the same way: no counting number x has 2 · x = 1, and 5c1 proves that even the integers have none.

The counting numbers run forwards without getting stuck, and get stuck running backwards. This rung makes room. 5b builds numbers that answer every p + x = b, and 5c builds numbers that answer every p · x = b with p other than 0. Each new kind of number on the ladder is built the same way, to give some line an answer it lacked: rung 8 for x · x = 2, and later rungs for x · x + 1 = 0.

5a11Moves to Check

Each of these looks like a move and is not:

  • Turning < about without turning the sign: from 3 < 5, writing 5 < 3.
  • Chaining ≠: from x ≠ b and b ≠ c, writing x ≠ c.
  • Multiplying both sides of ≠ or < by a number that might be 0.
  • Swapping across →: writing 16 CO₂ + 18 H₂O where 2 C₈H₁₈ + 25 O₂ stood, as if → were =.
  • Changing a small low number to balance a change: H₂O₂ is not water.

Each one keeps the look of a line and breaks its operator’s condition.

Accepted names

An operator’s condition is a relation. = is an equivalence relation: every number is the same as itself, and it turns about and chains. < is a strict order: it chains, and no number is less than itself. ≠ turns about and does not chain. A line with a gap is an equation in an unknown, and a number that makes it conform is a solution; an equation with no solution among some numbers is unsolvable there. A balanced chemical change obeys the conservation of mass; the numbers in front of the molecules are stoichiometric coefficients, and stoichiometry is the reckoning of them.

Who said it first

Robert Recorde brought in the sign = in 1557, “bicause noe .2. thynges, can be moare equalle”. The signs < and > are Thomas Harriot’s, printed in 1631. Antoine Lavoisier laid down in 1789 that in every operation nothing is created, and Jeremias Benjamin Richter named stoichiometry in 1792.
