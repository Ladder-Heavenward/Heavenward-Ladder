23k · technique

Expansions that diverge yet help

Some endless sums wander for every input but one, and are still the best way to compute. The trick is to stop early. Their pieces shrink for a while and then grow, and the error of stopping is no bigger than the first piece left out. So you stop at the smallest piece, and the answer is good to that size. Such a sum makes a different promise from a sum that settles: not that more pieces always help, but that a fixed number of pieces gets better and better as the input shrinks. The same habit of mind sizes an area by its peak: a tall narrow hill has an area of about its height times its width. Put together with a fence made of trapezoids and an old product for half of pi, it proves at last how big n factorial is, which the last rung could only state.

A sum that wanders can still compute, if you stop at its smallest piece. An area is about its peak times its width. With trapezoids under the log curve and areas under powers of the sine, the size of n factorial is proved.

23k1A sum that wanders, and computes

Stands on 15i (the finite geometric sum, exact), 16e (parts), 16i (improper integrals), 22k1 (O) and 23c3 (Σ k!xᵏ has reach 0).

Let f(x) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−t)/(1 + xt) dt for x ≥ 0. Its endless power sum is 1 − x + 2!x² − 3!x³ + ⋯, which 23c3 found settles only at x = 0. Yet the sum, stopped early, gives f.

\int_0^\infty t^k e^{-t}\,dt = k! @@ parts (16e): the area for k is k times the area for k − 1; the area for 0 is 1 (16i)
\tfrac{1}{1+xt} = \textstyle\sum_{k \lt n} (-xt)^k + \tfrac{(-xt)^n}{1+xt} @@ the finite geometric sum, exact (15i)
f(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \lt n} (-1)^k k!\,x^k + R_n(x),\ \ R_n(x) = (-x)^n \int_0^\infty \tfrac{t^n e^{-t}}{1+xt}\,dt @@ areas add (15e), one piece at a time
|R_n(x)| \le n!\,x^n @@ 1 + xt ≥ 1 for x, t ≥ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\Big|f(x) - \textstyle\sum_{k \lt n} (-1)^k k!\,x^k\Big| \le n!\,x^n \text{ for every } n} \quad \blacksquare @@ yet the sum wanders for every x > 0 (23c3)

From here on, Σ uₖxᵏ is an expansion of f as x shrinks when, for every n, stopping before uₙxⁿ leaves an error that is O(xⁿ) as x heads to 0: some C keeps it at most C·xⁿ for every small enough x > 0. The sum need not settle anywhere but 0. Accepted name: an asymptotic expansion.

The two promises point opposite ways. A sum that settles promises: fix x, take more pieces, and the error heads to 0. An expansion as x shrinks promises: fix the number of pieces, shrink x, and the error shrinks like the next power. The sum for f keeps the second promise and breaks the first.

Cut-at-the-smallest technique: when each error is at most the next piece in size, and the pieces shrink and then grow, stop just before the smallest piece; the error is at most that piece. At x = 0.1 the pieces in size are 1, 0.1, 0.02, 0.006, 0.0024, …, smallest at k = 9 and 10, both 0.00036288. Nine pieces give 0.915819, and f(0.1) = 0.915633. Twenty give 0.9076, and thirty give −65.

23k2An area dominated by its peak

Stands on 23k1, 23b1 (the match-error technique), 14a (a top has slope 0), 17e (exp climbs) and 15e (a bigger curve has a bigger area).

Peak technique: if ln g has its top at m, and its second slope is at least −K on a stretch of width w beside m, then g stays above e^(−Kw²/2)·g(m) there, and the area under g is at least w·e^(−Kw²/2)·g(m). Near a smooth top a curve falls away like the square of the distance, and the second slope says how fast.

\ln g(t) = \ln g(m) + 0\cdot(t-m) + \tfrac12(\ln g)''(c)\,(t-m)^2 @@ the match-error technique with n = 1 (23b1); the slope is 0 at the top (14a)
\ln g(t) \ge \ln g(m) - \tfrac12 K w^2 \text{ on the stretch} @@ (ln g)″ ≥ −K there, and |t − m| ≤ w
g(t) \ge e^{-Kw^2/2}\,g(m) @@ exp climbs (17e)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int g \ge w\,e^{-Kw^2/2}\,g(m)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a bigger curve has a bigger area (15e)
  
n! = \int_0^\infty g,\ \ g(t) = t^n e^{-t},\ \ \ln g = n\ln t - t @@ 23k1
(\ln g)' = \tfrac{n}{t} - 1 = 0 \text{ at } t = n @@ the top is at n (17b)
(\ln g)'' = -\tfrac{n}{t^2} \ge -\tfrac1n \text{ for } t \ge n @@ t² ≥ n²
\htmlClass{new}{n! \ge \sqrt n\,e^{-1/2}\big(\tfrac{n}{e}\big)^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the peak technique with K = 1/n and w = √n on [n, n + √n]; g(n) = (n/e)ⁿ

The top of the hill is (n/e)ⁿ and its width is about √n. 22k3's fence missed that width: it was loose by a factor of n. A sum can be sized by its peak the same way: nⁿ/n! is one piece of eⁿ = Σ nᵏ/k!, so nⁿ/n! < eⁿ, the lower half of 22k3's fence in one line. Two things remain: an upper side, and the constant in front of √n.

23k3A trapezoid fence for ln n!

Stands on 22k3 (the factorial fence), 14g1 (a curve that bends down lies above its chords and below its tangent lines), 17a (ln(1 + u) ≤ u), 17i (the area under ln), 15g2 (the collapse) and 22a2 (the climb technique).

Write dₙ = ln n! − (n + ½) ln n + n, so that n! = e^(dₙ)·√n·(n/e)ⁿ. Trapezoid-fence technique: dₙ falls and settles at some c, with c < dₙ < c + 1/(8n). Lay trapezoids under ln, one per strip, and measure the slivers between.

\tfrac12(\ln k + \ln(k+1)) \le \int_k^{k+1} \ln x\,dx \le \ln(k + \tfrac12) @@ ln bends down: its chord lies under it, its tangent line at k + ½ over it (14g1); the area under that tangent line is its height at the middle
0 < s_k = \int_k^{k+1} \ln - \tfrac12(\ln k + \ln(k+1)) \le \tfrac12\ln\Big(1 + \tfrac{1}{4k(k+1)}\Big) \le \tfrac{1}{8k(k+1)} @@ (k + ½)² = k(k + 1) + ¼; ln(1 + u) ≤ u (17a)
\int_1^n \ln x\,dx - \big(\ln 2 + \cdots + \ln(n-1) + \tfrac12\ln n\big) = s_1 + \cdots + s_{n-1} @@ add the strips: the trapezoids from 1 to n
n\ln n - n + 1 - \ln n! + \tfrac12\ln n = 1 - d_n @@ the area under ln is x ln x − x (17i)
d_n = 1 - (s_1 + \cdots + s_{n-1}) \text{ falls, and } s_1 + s_2 + \cdots \le \textstyle\sum \tfrac18\big(\tfrac1k - \tfrac{1}{k+1}\big) = \tfrac18 @@ each sliver is positive; the sum collapses (15g2)
\htmlClass{new}{d_n \to c,\ \ 0 < d_n - c = s_n + s_{n+1} + \cdots < \tfrac{1}{8n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ running totals that climb under ⅛ settle (22a2); the tail collapses the same way

d₁ = 1, d₁₀ = 0.92727, d₁₀₀₀ = 0.91902. So n! is e^c·√n·(n/e)ⁿ to within a factor e^(1/(8n)), with c somewhere near 0.919. The fence holds c in a window. It does not name c. A different area names it.

23k4The size of n!, proved

Stands on 23k3, 16e (parts), 16h4 (one integral through a smaller one), 21g (the slopes of sin and cos), 21c (sin² + cos² = 1), 10f4 (the squeeze) and 10c (limits multiply).

Size-of-factorials technique: n! ~ √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ, and more: √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ < n! < √(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ·e^(1/(8n)). The constant comes from areas under powers of sin, which know π.

I_m = \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^m x\,dx,\ \ I_0 = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \ I_1 = 1 @@ −cos is an antiderivative of sin (21g)
I_m = (m-1)(I_{m-2} - I_m) \text{ for } m \ge 2 @@ parts (16e) on sinᵐ⁻¹x · sin x; cos² = 1 − sin² (21c)
I_m = \tfrac{m-1}{m}\,I_{m-2} @@ as 16h4 pulled Jₙ out of both sides
I_{2k} = \tfrac{(2k)!}{(2^k k!)^2}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \ I_{2k+1} = \tfrac{(2^k k!)^2}{(2k+1)!} @@ induction (4c): each step multiplies by (m − 1)/m
I_{2k+1} \le I_{2k} \le I_{2k-1} = \tfrac{2k+1}{2k}\,I_{2k+1} @@ 0 ≤ sin x ≤ 1, so a higher power is lower (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{I_{2k}}{I_{2k+1}} \to 1,\ \text{that is,}\ \tfrac{(2^k k!)^4}{((2k)!)^2\,(2k+1)} \to \tfrac{\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ratio is squeezed between 1 and (2k + 1)/(2k) (10f4); write it out
  
k! = e^{d_k}\sqrt k\,\big(\tfrac{k}{e}\big)^k,\ \ (2k)! = e^{d_{2k}}\sqrt{2k}\,\big(\tfrac{2k}{e}\big)^{2k} @@ the meaning of dₙ (23k3)
\tfrac{(2^k k!)^4}{((2k)!)^2\,(2k+1)} = e^{4d_k - 2d_{2k}}\cdot\tfrac{k^2}{2k(2k+1)} @@ the powers 2⁴ᵏ(k/e)⁴ᵏ cancel
\to e^{4c - 2c}\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac{e^{2c}}{4} @@ d_k and d₂ₖ head to c (23k3); limits multiply (10c)
\tfrac{e^{2c}}{4} = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \text{so } e^c = \sqrt{2\pi} @@ a list settles at one place only (22a1)
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt{2\pi n}\,\big(\tfrac{n}{e}\big)^n < n! < \sqrt{2\pi n}\,\big(\tfrac{n}{e}\big)^n e^{1/(8n)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ n! = e^(dₙ)√n(n/e)ⁿ and c < dₙ < c + 1/(8n) (23k3)

At n = 10 this reads 3 598 696 < 3 628 800 < 3 643 962. The ratio n!/(√(2πn)(n/e)ⁿ) is 1.0084 at n = 10 and 1.00083 at n = 100, close to e^(1/(12n)). The corrections go on: ln n! = (n + ½) ln n − n + ln √(2π) + 1/(12n) − 1/(360n³) + 1/(1260n⁵) − ⋯. That sum wanders for every n, like 23k1's. It is an expansion as n grows, stated here and held in trust. Stopped after 1/(12n), it gives 10! as 3 628 810, off by ten.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

An expansion as x shrinks is an asymptotic expansion; the sum 1 − x + 2!x² − ⋯ is Euler's divergent series, or Stieltjes' series, and cutting at the smallest piece is optimal truncation. The peak technique is the lower half of Laplace's method. The size-of-factorials technique is Stirling's formula, and the sum that continues it is the Stirling series. The limit for π/2 is the Wallis product, π/2 = (2·2)/(1·3) · (4·4)/(3·5) · (6·6)/(5·7) ⋯.

Who said it first

John Wallis found his product in 1656, in Arithmetica infinitorum. Abraham de Moivre found in 1730 that n! grows like a constant times √n(n/e)ⁿ, and James Stirling found the constant √(2π) the same year. Thomas Bayes noticed, in a letter printed in 1763, that Stirling's sum wanders. Leonhard Euler gave 1 − 1 + 2 − 6 + ⋯ the value 0.5963… in 1760. Pierre-Simon Laplace sized integrals by their peaks in 1774. Niels Henrik Abel banned divergent series in a letter of 1826. Henri Poincaré and Thomas Stieltjes, both in 1886, defined asymptotic expansions and made them rigorous.
