17·bio · biology

Can a Population Double Forever?

Can a population double forever? A population with food and room to spare grows at a rate proportional to its size: each animal or cell adds young at its own pace, whatever the others do. That is 17j's equation, and its answer doubles on a fixed clock forever. No real population does that for long. This page adds the simplest brake: a rate that shrinks as the population nears what its place can hold. It solves the new equation with 17k's split, and reads off the moment of fastest growth.

Unchecked, a population grows at a rate fixed by its size, and grows by a fixed factor in equal times. With a ceiling, growth slows by the share of room already taken. The logarithm of the ratio between those living and the room left then climbs in a straight line, and the population climbs an S toward the ceiling. Growth is fastest when the population is half the ceiling.

Unchecked

Suppose each member of a population adds, on average, r new members per unit time. Then P′ = rP, and P = P₀ert by proportional-slope. r has the accepted name the intrinsic rate of increase. The population doubles every ln 2 / r (doubling-time).

\text{a bacterium dividing every } 20 \text{ min}:\ \ P = P_0 \cdot 2^{3t} @@ t in hours; 17j
P(24) = P_0 \cdot 2^{72} \approx 4.7 \times 10^{21}\,P_0 @@ from one cell of about 10⁻¹² g, some 4700 tonnes in a day, if nothing stopped it

A ceiling

Let the rate per member fall in a straight line from r, when P is small, to 0 when P reaches K: P′ = rP(1 − P/K). K has the accepted name carrying capacity; the equation is the logistic equation.

Logistic-solution technique: if P′ = rP(1 − P/K) and 0 < P₀ < K, then, while 0 < P < K, P(t) = K/(1 + Ce−rt) with C = (K − P₀)/P₀.

G(t) = \ln P - \ln(K - P) @@ both have values: 0 < P < K
G' = \Big(\tfrac1P + \tfrac{1}{K - P}\Big)P' = \tfrac{K}{P(K - P)}\,P' @@ slope-of-log and chain for slopes; 17k's split read backwards
= \tfrac{K}{P(K - P)} \cdot \tfrac{rP(K - P)}{K} = r @@ the equation
G = rt + \ln\tfrac{P_0}{K - P_0} @@ same-slopes (14e); t = 0 fixes the constant
\tfrac{P}{K - P} = \tfrac{e^{rt}}{C} \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{P = \tfrac{K}{1 + Ce^{-rt}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ exp of both sides; solve for P

Fastest-growth technique: along the logistic curve, P′ is greatest when P = K/2, which happens at t = ln C / r.

P' = \tfrac{r}{K}P(K - P) = \tfrac{r}{K}\Big(\tfrac{K^2}{4} - \big(P - \tfrac{K}{2}\big)^2\Big) @@ completing the square (8i)
\text{greatest at } P = \tfrac{K}{2},\ \ P' = \tfrac{rK}{4} @@ a square is never negative (square-never-negative, 8f)
Ce^{-rt} = 1 \iff t = \tfrac{\ln C}{r} \quad \blacksquare @@ P = K/2 in logistic-solution
  
K = 5000,\ P_0 = 500,\ r = 0.3 \text{ per year} @@ a herd on the Olympic Peninsula, a model
C = 9,\ \ P(10) = \tfrac{5000}{1 + 9e^{-3}} \approx 3453 @@ logistic-solution
\text{fastest growth at } t = \tfrac{\ln 9}{0.3} \approx 7.3 \text{ years, } 375 \text{ elk a year} @@ fastest-growth: rK/4

Many things set K: food, nesting sites, predators, disease. A K that falls, when a forest is cut or a river dammed, drags the population down with it. A population above K shrinks, since then P′ < 0. Two species that share a food each lower the other's ceiling.

In 1845 a Belgian mathematician proposed that the obstacles to a population's growth increase in proportion to the excess of the population over a certain size. He named the curve that results the logistic, and fitted it to the censuses of France, Belgium and Essex.
