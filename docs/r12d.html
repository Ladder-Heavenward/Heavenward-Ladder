12d · proof

Why Having No Holes Matters

12a, 12b and 12c each cited rung 8's no-holes at one line. Was it needed, or would the fractions have done? This step runs the three techniques in a world with only the fractions, and watches them fail. Take the square of the input less 2, on the fractions from 1 to 2. It is continuous in the same sense, with fractions everywhere. It is negative one at 1 and 2 at 2, and it is never 0. Replay 12a's proof there, and every line goes through but the one that took a least upper bound. Then comes a step that is continuous on the fractions because it steps across the hole. Last comes a bounded continuous function on the fractions with no greatest value, whose failure leads back to the same hole.

Parse every line again with fractions in place of reals. The techs of arithmetic, order and the challenge game all survive. The least upper bound does not. And every failure of the three techniques falls through the hole where the square root of 2 would be.

12d1A World With Only the Fractions

8d proved that ℚ is a regular number system: it obeys the twelve techniques. Everything rung 8 built for the reals from the twelve techniques and comparison alone can be carried out for fractions by the same columns. 8h's |x|, with size-of-a-product, unpack-the-bars and triangle, is among it. The columns go through because ℚ obeys the techniques (5d) and any two fractions compare (any-two-compare on ℚ). What ℚ lacks is having no holes (8d's no-holes-system, by 8c's no-least-fraction-bound). So read the challenge game with fractions everywhere.

From here on, in this step, take a set D of fractions and a function f from D to ℚ. f is continuous over ℚ at r ∈ D when this is true. For every positive fraction ε there is a positive fraction δ such that every x ∈ D with |x − r| < δ has |f(x) − f(r)| < ε. And f is continuous over ℚ on D when it is so at every member of D. It is 11a's continuity-unpacked with every real replaced by a fraction. 12b's “bounded” and 12c's “greatest value” are read over ℚ the same way, with fractions for bounds and values.

Reuses, said out loud, for this step only. 10a's ε and δ always named positive reals; here they name positive fractions. Since 8d's naming line lets a fraction name a real, 10a's lines are still so of them. 8h's |p| was said of a real. For a fraction r, |r| here names r if 0 ≤ r and −r if r < 0, a fraction, which names the same real as 8h's |r| through the naming line. H names 8a's hole's heap, as 8c's no-least-fraction-bound did (not 9a's function H). s names a step function (not 4d's next-one function s, nor 12a's candidate s). And h names a reciprocal (not 10f's or 12e's h).

The stretch of this step is D = {x ∈ ℚ : 1 ≤ x ≤ 2}, the fractions from 1 to 2, ends kept. It is the closed interval [1, 2] with its irrationals taken out.

Continuous-over-ℚ technique: f(x) = x² − 2 is continuous over ℚ on D.

\text{let } r \in D,\ \text{a fraction } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ \delta = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4}} @@ a fraction, positive (5f's keep-order for times on ℚ)
|f(x) - f(r)| = |x^2 - r^2| = |x - r|\,|x + r| @@ difference-of-squares (5i); size-of-a-product, for fractions by 8h's column
|x + r| = x + r \le 4 @@ 1 ≤ x, r ≤ 2 in D: x + r positive, at most 2 + 2 (keep-order for plus on ℚ)
|x - r| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; |f(x) - f(r)| \lt 4\delta = \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ keep-order for times on ℚ: multiply by the positive |x − r| (or 0 < ε when x = r)

No real number was mentioned: the input the game is played at, x, ε, δ and every output are fractions. The game is played and won entirely inside ℚ.

12d2The Intermediate Value Technique Fails Over ℚ

No-in-between-over-ℚ technique: f(x) = x² − 2 is continuous over ℚ on D, f(1) = −1 < 0 < 2 = f(2), and f has no zero in D. Continuous-over-ℚ, arithmetic, and 5f's no-fraction-squares-to-2: no fraction squares to 2. ∎

Over ℚ, then, a continuous function can go from below 0 to above it and skip 0. Which line of 12a's proof broke? Replay it with fractions in place of reals, line by line.

That is 8c's no-least-fraction-bound once more, now with a recipe: each step is the average of u and 2/u. Call it the averaging step. Over the reals the creeping stops at a number, √2, which is sup A by 8f's hole's-cut-is-√2, and there f is 0. Over the fractions it stops nowhere. No-least-fraction-bound-on-D's column needs no either-way principle at all: every comparison in it is between fractions, and settled-order on ℚ settles those.

12d3A Step That Is Continuous Over ℚ

It gets stranger. Over ℚ, even a step is continuous, if it steps across the hole. Let H = {x ∈ ℚ : x < 0 ∨ x² < 2}, 8a's hole's heap. Let s(x) = −1 for x ∈ H, and s(x) = 1 for every other fraction x (7e's definition by cases; for fractions the case is decidable).

Hole-makes-a-step technique: s is continuous over ℚ at every fraction. It takes the values −1 and 1, and no value between.

\text{let } r \in \mathbb Q;\ \ r \in H \lor r \notin H @@ decidable for fractions: settled-order on ℚ and settled-join (7c), no either-way principle
\text{case } r \in H:\ \ \text{call } t \in H,\ r \lt t;\ \ \delta = t - r @@ H is a cut (8a's hole's-heap-is-a-cut) with no greatest member: cut (iv). For r ≥ 0, 8a's hole's-step step gives t; for r < 0, t = 0 will do
\qquad |x - r| \lt \delta \Rightarrow x \lt t \Rightarrow x \in H \Rightarrow s(x) = -1 = s(r) @@ unpack-the-bars; cut (iii): closed downward
\text{case } r \notin H:\ \ r \gt 0,\ r^2 \gt 2;\ \ \text{call } t,\ 0 \lt t \lt r,\ t^2 \gt 2;\ \ \delta = r - t @@ no-fraction-squares-to-2; 8a's no-least-above (or no-least-fraction-bound-on-D's u′)
\qquad |x - r| \lt \delta \Rightarrow x \gt t \gt 0 \Rightarrow x^2 \gt t^2 \gt 2 \Rightarrow s(x) = 1 = s(r) @@ unpack-the-bars; squares-keep-order on ℚ
|s(x) - s(r)| = 0 \lt \varepsilon \text{ for every } \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare @@ one δ wins every round: s is constant on a window around every fraction

Over the reals, the same step, −1 below √2 and 1 from √2 on, is not continuous at √2: it is 11a's step u moved to √2. Over the fractions √2 is not an input. Every fraction sits on one side of the hole, with a whole window of fractions around it on the same side. Nothing between −1 and 1 is taken, not even 0, and the step is continuous.

The same column works for any set S of fractions with a member and a fractional upper bound but no least one. Let the step be 1 at the fractional upper bounds of S and −1 elsewhere. It is continuous over ℚ and skips everything between. So take a regular number system, and ask every continuous function on a closed interval to take every value between its end values. That forces every set with a member and an upper bound to have a least one. The intermediate value technique and no-holes are two faces of one property.

12d4No Greatest Value Over ℚ

No-highest-over-ℚ technique: g(x) = −(x² − 2)² is continuous over ℚ on D and bounded there, and it has no greatest value on D.

g(r) - g(x) = (x^2 - r^2)(x^2 + r^2 - 4) @@ difference-of-squares (5i) on (x² − 2)² − (r² − 2)²
|x^2 + r^2 - 4| \le 4,\ \ |x^2 - r^2| \le 4|x - r| @@ 1 ≤ x², r² ≤ 4 on D; continuous-over-ℚ's line
|x - r| \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{16} \Rightarrow |g(x) - g(r)| \lt \varepsilon @@ size-of-a-product: continuous over ℚ on D, with δ = ε/16
-4 \le g(x) \le 0 @@ −1 ≤ x² − 2 ≤ 2, so (x² − 2)² ≤ 4; square-never-negative for fractions
\text{let } c \in D:\ \ g(c) \lt 0 @@ c² ≠ 2 (no-fraction-squares-to-2), so (c² − 2)² is positive
c' = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{c^2 + 2}{2c}} \in D @@ c′² > 2 > 1 and c′ > 0, so c′ > 1; c′ = c/2 + 1/c ≤ 1 + 1 = 2
|c'^2 - 2| = |c^2 - 2| \cdot \tfrac{|c^2 - 2|}{4c^2} \le \tfrac12 |c^2 - 2| @@ no-least-fraction-bound-on-D's line; |c² − 2| ≤ 2 and 4c² ≥ 4 on D
g(c') \ge \tfrac14 g(c) \gt g(c) \quad \blacksquare @@ square the line above (squares-keep-order on ℚ); g(c) < 0: no input of D reaches a greatest value

So the fraction c′ always beats c, and the values creep up on 0 without reaching it: g(3/2) = −1/16, g(17/12) = −1/20736, g(577/408) = −1/27 710 263 296. Here the sup over ℚ exists. 0 is a fraction, and it is the least fractional upper bound of the values, since each step at least quarters the gap and 4ⁿ passes every bound. So 12c's first block survives. Which line breaks? The supposition “never reached” is true. The trick's function h(x) = 1/(0 − g(x)) = 1/(x² − 2)² is continuous over ℚ on D, by the same columns. The next line, “bounded-on-a-closed-interval for h”, fails. h(3/2) = 16, h(17/12) = 20736, h(577/408) = 27 710 263 296. Each averaging step multiplies h by at least 4, so h passes every bound on D.

And inside bounded-on-a-closed-interval's own proof, the line that fails is again the least upper bound. The inputs up to which h is bounded are exactly the fractions of D with x² < 2. Below the hole, the gap 2 − t² stays at least 2 − x². Past it, 8a's hole's-step brings fractions from below as near the hole as you like, each step cutting the gap |t² − 2| to at most 2/9 of itself. That set is A once more, with no least fractional upper bound. Three techniques, one line, one hole.

12d6Running It Backwards

Handed a technique of this rung and asked whether it needs no-holes, read it over ℚ and look for a counterexample at a hole. It may be a polynomial with fraction coefficients whose sign changes across an irrational zero. Or it may be a function whose top sits at an irrational input. Handed a proof, find the line that took a least upper bound: that is the line the counterexample breaks. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Dedekind says that the fractions form a domain that is not continuous: they have gaps, and every gap is a splitting of the fractions into a lower and an upper class with no fraction producing it; and he takes the line's having no such gaps as the essence of continuity.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), §III–IV, after Beman (1901)

The averaging step, u to the average of u and 2/u, is Heron’s rule for square roots (13m). The mathematician of question 12d.4.4 is Leopold Kronecker.
