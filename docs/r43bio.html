What Do Sulfur and Phosphorus Do That Carbon Cannot?

What do sulfur and phosphorus do that carbon cannot? Sarah reads the label on a bag of garden fertiliser: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and in small print sulfur. A cell cannot be built without either. This page finds the work each does: phosphorus as a charged handle and a coin, sulfur as a looser oxygen that ties, carries and holds iron.

Phosphorus gives a cell a charged group that is far downhill to break and slow to break; sulfur gives it an atom that holds what it carries loosely. One keeps and pays, the other hands over.

43bio1Two Elements Off the Main List

Sarah reads the label on a bag of garden fertiliser: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and in small print sulfur. A living thing is mostly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Sulfur and phosphorus come next, and a cell cannot be built without either. In the table (5chem11) sulfur stands under oxygen and phosphorus under nitrogen. So the first guess is two gaps for sulfur and three for phosphorus, and the guess is half right.

Each is a larger atom than the one above it, holds the shared part less closely, and can take more partners (2chem12). In a cell phosphorus is almost always found with four oxygens round it, and sulfur with two partners, three, or four.

43bio2A Handle With a Charge

The first thing a cell does to a sugar it has taken in is join a phosphate to it. At the acidity of a cell (17·chem) a phosphate group has given up its hydrogens and carries negative charge. A charged molecule cannot slip back out through the oily wall of the cell (27chem9). So the phosphate is a handle that keeps the sugar in. The step runs downhill by 20 kJ a mole.

The enzyme that does it passes the phosphate to the sugar’s OH forty thousand times more readily than to the OH of water, although water is all round it.

Handle technique: joining a charged group to a molecule keeps it inside the cell and marks it for the enzymes that grip that group.

43bio3Ready to Break, and Slow to Break

19·bio’s coin, ATP, is a chain of three phosphates. Breaking the chain by water gives out a large amount of energy. Yet ATP dissolved in water sits for a long time unbroken. The two facts answer two different questions, and 28·chem kept them apart for coal lying in air: how far downhill, and how high the hill on the way (21chem2). The hill is high because the phosphates are thick with negative charge and push away the attacker, which brings a pair of electrons.

In a cell ATP is almost always held with a magnesium ion, whose two positive charges cover the negative ones so that an attacker can get in, and an enzyme decides when.

Coin technique: a cell’s coin must be far downhill to break and slow to break by itself, so that it is spent only where an enzyme lowers the hill.

43bio4Paying for an Uphill Step

Halfway down the path that takes a sugar apart, one step runs uphill by 6.7 kJ a mole and the next runs downhill by 18.8. Free energies add (11chem8), so the pair runs downhill by 12.1 and both go. That is how a cell does anything that will not go by itself: it joins the step to one that runs further downhill, most often the breaking of ATP, through a shared molecule that the first step makes and the second uses.

Pairing technique: an uphill step goes when it shares a molecule with a downhill step and the two free energies sum to less than zero.

43bio5The Backbone of the Message, and the Wall

A phosphate with four oxygens can hold two partners and still keep one negative charge. That is the link of DNA and RNA: sugar, phosphate, sugar, phosphate (4·bio, 34·bio), each link charged. The charge keeps the message in the cell, and pushes off the water that would otherwise cut the chain, so the message lasts. The same group with two oily tails makes the wall of the cell: charged heads to the water on both sides, tails to each other (27chem9).

43bio6Sulfur, the Looser Oxygen

An alcohol is R-OH and a thiol is R-SH (9chem21). Sulfur holds its hydrogen less closely than oxygen, so a thiol gives up H⁺ far more easily, and the charged sulfur left behind is one of the readiest givers of an electron pair in a protein. One of the twenty amino acids of 4bio4 carries a thiol, and enzymes put it where an attack is needed. Two thiols can also join, sulfur to sulfur, each giving up its hydrogen. In 6·chem’s count that is an oxidation, and it can be undone.

A hair is held in its shape by such links between its chains: a permanent wave breaks them, resets the hair, and makes them again.

Link technique: two thiols joined sulfur to sulfur tie two parts of a protein together, and the tie is made and broken by handing electrons over.

43bio7Why a Sulfur Ester

A cell carries two-carbon pieces about on a sulfur, joined as R-CO-S-R, where a chemist’s ester would use oxygen (9chem21). The courses ask the question in so many words: why sulfur and not oxygen? In an oxygen ester the oxygen shares an unshared pair with the C=O beside it, and the group needs two gap statements, like the ring of 31chem5. That sharing steadies it.

Sulfur is a size larger and shares poorly, so the sulfur ester behaves like a bare C=O: an attacker gets in more easily, and the hydrogen on the next carbon comes off more easily, with a pKa of 18 against 22. Four units of pKa is ten thousand times (17·chem). So the piece is easy to pass on and easy to build with.

Carrier technique: a group that is carried to be given away is joined through the atom that holds it least firmly.

43bio8Sulfur Holds the Iron

The electrons of 19·bio’s chain travel through small cages of iron and sulfur: two or four irons, held to the protein by the thiols of 43bio6, with bare sulfur atoms between. Each iron steps between +2 and +3 (38chem32) as an electron arrives and leaves. Sulfur also carries one-carbon pieces: a sulfur given a third partner carries a positive charge and hands a methyl group to whatever attacks it.

Accepted names

The handle is phosphorylation, and the enzyme a kinase; the first one here is hexokinase. The links of ATP are phosphoanhydride bonds; “far downhill and slow” is thermodynamically labile and kinetically stable. Pairing is coupling. The link of DNA is a phosphodiester, and the wall is made of phospholipids. The amino acid with a thiol is cysteine, the sulfur-to-sulfur link a disulfide bond. The sulfur ester is a thioester, the carrier coenzyme A, the loaded carrier acetyl-CoA. The cages are iron-sulfur clusters, and the methyl carrier S-adenosylmethionine.
