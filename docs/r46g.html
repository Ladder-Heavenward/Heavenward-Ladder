46g · proof

No Formula

A formula by roots builds a tower of fields: each floor adds one root of something on the floor below. This step reads the tower in the group of the equation. Adding the roots of one is a turn-about step, and once they are there, adding one root of a number is a clock step. So an equation with a formula has a group that comes apart into turn-about steps, and the other way round, a group that comes apart gives a formula, built step by step from sums that turn by a fixed root of one. Then a single equation of degree five: it cannot be factored, it has exactly three roots on the line, and so its group holds a swap and a five-loop, which together make every shuffle of five. That group does not come apart, and the equation has no formula by roots. Not a formula nobody has found yet. None. The step ends by paying what 46a held in trust: the cubic of rung 21 has no formula by real roots, and the plane is needed.

A formula by roots is a tower of root steps. With the roots of one present, each step has a clock group, so the group of an equation with a formula is solvable; and a solvable group gives a formula by turning sums. An equation of degree five with three real roots that cannot be factored has all the shuffles of five as its group, so it has none. An irreducible cubic with three real roots has no formula by real roots.

46g1Root Steps

A tower of roots over F is a chain F = F₀ ⊆ F₁ ⊆ ⋯ ⊆ Fₖ inside ℂ with Fᵢ₊₁ = Fᵢ(aᵢ) and aᵢ^(nᵢ) in Fᵢ for some whole nᵢ ≥ 2. A polynomial f over F has a formula by roots when its splitting field lies inside the top of some tower of roots. This is 46a1’s definition read as fields: each root in a formula adds one floor.

Root-step technique: adjoining the n-th roots of 1 to F gives a turn-about group; and if F holds the n-th roots of 1 and aⁿ = c is in F, then F(a) is the splitting field of xⁿ − c and its group over F is a clock.

\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{s},\ \tau(\zeta) = \zeta^{t} \Rightarrow \sigma\tau(\zeta) = \zeta^{st} = \tau\sigma(\zeta) @@ roots to roots, and powers of ζ; F(ζ) is fixed by where ζ goes
\text{the roots of } x^n - c \text{ are } a, \zeta a, \ldots, \zeta^{n-1}a \text{, all in } F(a) @@ (ζᵏa)ⁿ = c, and ζ is in F

46g3A Solvable Group Gives a Formula

Turning-sum technique: if F holds the p-th roots of 1 and K over F is a splitting field whose group is a clock of prime size p, then K = F(t) with tᵖ in F.

G = \{e, \sigma, \ldots, \sigma^{p-1}\},\ \ \zeta \in F,\ \zeta^p = 1,\ \zeta \ne 1 @@ given
t = \alpha + \zeta^{-1}\sigma(\alpha) + \zeta^{-2}\sigma^2(\alpha) + \cdots + \zeta^{-(p-1)}\sigma^{p-1}(\alpha) @@ for α in K, the turning sum
\sigma(t) = \sigma(\alpha) + \zeta^{-1}\sigma^2(\alpha) + \cdots + \zeta^{-(p-1)}\alpha = \zeta t @@ σ fixes ζ; σᵖ = e
\sigma(t^p) = \zeta^p t^p = t^p,\ \ \text{so } t^p \in K^G = F @@ the fixed-size technique (46d2)
\text{some } \alpha \text{ has } t \ne 0 @@ e, σ, …, σ^(p−1) are not tied over K: the fewest-terms argument of 46d2 shows no K-sum of different symmetries is 0 at every α
t \notin F \text{ since } \sigma(t) = \zeta t \ne t @@ t ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{F \subsetneq F(t) \subseteq K,\ [K : F] = p \text{ prime}:\ \ K = F(t)} \quad \blacksquare @@ degrees up the tower

Solvable-means-formula technique: if G_f is solvable, f has a formula by roots. Adjoin ζ, the n-th roots of 1 for n = |G_f|: a root step. Over F(ζ) the group of f is a part-group of G_f (each symmetry is fixed by where it sends the roots of f), so it is solvable (46e2), and its chain of pieces has prime clocks (46e3). The matching of 46d turns the chain into a tower of fields, each floor of prime degree p over the last with a clock group and ζ^(n/p) present. The turning-sum technique makes each floor a root step.

46g4A Quintic With No Formula

Prime-member technique: if a prime p divides the size of a finite group G, some member of G has order p.

The accepted names

A tower of roots is a radical tower, and a formula by roots is a solution by radicals. The formula-means-solvable and solvable-means-formula techniques together are Galois’s theorem: a polynomial is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable. The turning sum is a Lagrange resolvent; that the symmetries are not tied over K is Dedekind’s lemma on the independence of characters. The prime-member technique is Cauchy’s theorem, and the proof by p-tuples is James McKay’s (1959). The no-real-route technique is the theorem on the casus irreducibilis. The quintic result is the Abel–Ruffini theorem.

Who said it first

Paolo Ruffini published a proof that the general quintic has no formula by radicals in 1799, with gaps; the mathematicians he asked would not judge it, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy wrote to him in 1821 that he thought it sound. Niels Henrik Abel gave a complete proof in 1824 and 1826. Évariste Galois found the criterion by groups; his memoir was returned by Siméon Denis Poisson in 1831 as not clear enough to judge, and Galois died in a duel in 1832. Joseph Liouville published the memoir in 1846. Charles Hermite solved the quintic in 1858 with elliptic modular functions, which are not radicals. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the prime-member technique in 1845. The casus irreducibilis was settled by Pierre Wantzel in 1843 and Otto Hölder in 1891.

A proof that is not allowed

“x⁵ − 4x + 2 has no rational root and cannot be factored, so its roots cannot be written with radicals.” Not allowed: x⁵ − 2 cannot be factored either, and its roots are ⁵√2 times the fifth roots of 1. Being irreducible is not the obstruction. The group is: S₅, with A₅ as a piece.
