How Old Is the Seafloor?

How old is the seafloor? Down the middle of every ocean runs a ridge where hot rock wells up, cools into new floor, and is pushed aside both ways by the rock that comes after it. So the floor gets older the further it lies from the ridge, on either side. The rock also keeps a record of which way the earth’s magnetic field pointed when it cooled, and the field has flipped many times. This page writes the floor’s age as a distance over a rate with 8h’s absolute value, reads the mirrored magnetic stripes, compares fast and slow ridges with 8g’s inequalities, and finds a bound on how old any seafloor can be.

Put the ridge at 0 on a line across the ocean, and let the floor move away at speed v on each side. A place at x is |x|/v years old, the same at x and at −x. Where a magnetic flip of known age shows up at distance d, the speed is d over that age.

8phys21A Ridge That Spreads Both Ways

Lay a line across an ocean, at right angles to its ridge, with the ridge at 0, east positive and west negative (8a). New floor forms at 0 and moves away on both sides at the same speed v, called the half rate: the two sides part at 2v.

From here on, on this page, the age of the floor at a place is the time since it formed at the ridge. Floor at x has travelled the distance |x| from the ridge (8h), at speed v.

\text{distance from the ridge} = |x| @@ distance on the line (8h)
\text{distance} = v \cdot \text{age} @@ steady speed (5j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{age}(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{v}} @@ the same at x and −x

The age is 0 at the ridge and grows on both sides: a V shape, the graph of |x| scaled by 1/v.

8phys22Stripes That Mirror

Cooling rock locks in the direction of the earth’s magnetic field, like a compass needle frozen in place. The field has flipped, north to south and back, many times. A ship towing a magnet sensor across the ridge reads stripes: floor magnetised the present way, then the flipped way, then the present way again.

The stripes on the two sides are mirror images of each other, because floor at x and floor at −x formed at the same time, under the same field. That match is what convinced geologists in the 1960s that the floor spreads.

Mirror-age technique: where the floor spreads both ways from a ridge, the floor at the same distance on either side is the same age, so any record it keeps repeats in mirror image across the ridge.

8phys23Age From Distance and Rate

The latest flip of the field happened about 780,000 years ago, and its edge can be found on the floor. Units first (5j): 1 cm a year for a million years is 1,000,000 cm, which is 10 km.

\text{edge of the last flip: } |x| \approx 9.75 \text{ km} @@ from the ship’s record
v = \dfrac{|x|}{\text{age}} = \dfrac{9.75 \text{ km}}{0.78 \text{ million years}} @@ distance over age
= 12.5 \text{ km per million years} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{= 1.25 \text{ cm a year}} @@ the half rate

With v known, every other place on the line gets its age: floor 100 km from this ridge is 100 ÷ 12.5 = 8 million years old. A geologist with one dated stripe edge and a ship’s record dates the whole crossing.

8phys24Fast and Slow Ridges Compared

Ridges spread at very different speeds. The ridge in the eastern Pacific has a half rate near 7.5 cm a year, six times the Atlantic’s 1.25. Compare the same age on both.

\text{Atlantic: } |x| = 12.5 \times 0.78 \approx 9.75 \text{ km} @@ the last flip
\text{Pacific: } |x| = 75 \times 0.78 \approx 58.5 \text{ km} @@ the same flip
v_1 < v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{|x|}{v_1} > \dfrac{|x|}{v_2} \text{ for } x \ne 0 @@ dividing by the bigger positive gives less (8g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at any distance, the slow ridge’s floor is older}} @@

A fast ridge spreads its stripes wide and a slow one packs them close, but the stripes come in the same order with the same ages: the field’s history is one record, printed at two scales.

8phys25Why the Oldest Seafloor Is Young

The earth is about 4,500 million years old. If floor only ever formed and moved away, the oldest floor could be nearly that old. It isn’t: where ocean floor meets a continent or another plate, it can bend down and sink back into the earth at a deep trench.

\text{distance from ridge to trench} \le L @@ an ocean has a width
\text{age} = \dfrac{|x|}{v} \le \dfrac{L}{v} @@ the greatest age is where |x| is greatest (8g)
L \approx 9\,000 \text{ km},\ v \approx 5 \text{ cm a year} = 50 \text{ km per million years} @@ a wide ocean, a middling rate
\htmlClass{new}{\text{age} \le 180 \text{ million years}} @@ the oldest floor found is about that age

Floor that is carried to a trench and sinks can be no older than the time it takes to cross the ocean. The oldest seafloor found, in the western Pacific, is about 180 million years old, a twenty-fifth of the earth’s age. The oldest continental rock, which floats and does not sink, is twenty times older.

Accepted names

The ridge is a mid-ocean ridge, and new floor forming there is seafloor spreading. A flip of the field is a geomagnetic reversal, and the latest is the Brunhes-Matuyama reversal. The mirrored stripes are magnetic anomalies, and the trench where floor sinks is a subduction zone.

Who said it first

Harry Hess proposed seafloor spreading in 1962. Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews, and separately Lawrence Morley, explained the mirrored stripes as a record of reversals in 1963.
