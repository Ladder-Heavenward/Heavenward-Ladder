37i · proof

Rhythm, and Falling Into Step

A drummer plays three even beats in the time a bass player plays two, and the two parts meet only at the start of each bar. That is a polyrhythm, and when the two parts meet and where they miss is whole-number arithmetic from rung 4 and rung 5. Speed the same pattern up until each bar lasts a hundredth of a second and it stops being a rhythm: the ear hears two notes a pure fifth apart, rung 6’s interval, because rhythm and pitch are one thing at two speeds. Last, a crowd claps out of time and within seconds claps together. Two things that each keep their own time, and nudge each other a little, fall into one time when their natural speeds are close enough. Rung 24’s rate statements say exactly how close.

A rhythm is a pattern that repeats every bar, so it has a rate, and a fast enough rhythm is a pitch. Three against two meet only at the bar line, because three and two share no divisor. Two timekeepers that nudge each other lock together when the gap between their speeds is no more than the strength of the nudge.

37i1A Beat Is a Frequency Too

From here on, a rhythm with bar length B is a set of times in [0, B), the onsets, played again in every bar. Its tempo, counted in beats per minute, is 60 divided by the time between beats in seconds: 120 beats per minute is a beat every half second, 2 Hz.

So a rhythm is a sound that repeats every B, and by the repeat-rate technique (37b1) it is a sum of turnings at the whole multiples of 1/B. A bar of 2 s is 0.5 Hz. What the ear makes of a rate depends on where it lies: far below the hearing window (37b2) it is heard as a beat to tap to; inside it, as a pitch.

Bar-rate technique: a rhythm repeats once a bar, so its rate is one over the bar length, and its beats are a whole multiple of that rate. Proof: the rhythm’s onsets fall again every B, and a beat that divides the bar into n equal parts has rate n/B.

37i2Three Against Two

A p against q rhythm plays p even beats and q even beats in one bar: onsets iB/p for i = 0, …, p − 1 and jB/q for j = 0, …, q − 1. Where do they meet?

Meet-once technique: when p and q share no divisor but 1, the two parts of p against q meet only at the start of the bar, and the bar holds p + q − 1 onsets.

iB/p = jB/q \Rightarrow iq = jp @@ a meeting, multiplied through by pq/B
p \mid iq,\ \text{and } p, q \text{ share no divisor but } 1 @@ the line above
p \mid i @@ 4j13, running the gcd backwards: 1 = sp + tq, so i = s(ip) + t(iq), and p divides both
0 \le i < p \Rightarrow i = 0,\ \text{so } j = 0 @@ 4j4, size and divisors

37i3A Fast Rhythm Is a Pitch

Play the two parts of three against two as clicks, and shrink the bar. At a bar of 1 s the clicks are a rhythm at 3 and 2 a second. At a bar of 1/100 s, the parts are click trains at 300 and 200 a second. Each repeats inside the hearing window, so each has a pitch (37b1): 300 Hz and 200 Hz.

Rhythm-is-pitch technique: p against q with bar B is, once q/B and p/B are inside the hearing window, two pitches whose ratio is p/q, so the rhythm becomes the interval p/q. Proof: each part repeats every B/p or B/q, so their pitches are p/B and q/B by the repeat-rate technique, and their ratio is p/q, whatever B is.

B = 1/100 \text{ s}:\ 3/B = 300,\ 2/B = 200 \text{ Hz} @@ two click trains
300/200 = 3/2 @@ the ratio is the rhythm’s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a pure fifth}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 6·phys: four against three becomes a fourth, five against four a major third

So the pitch line of 37b3 runs on below the window as a line of tempos, and one ratio is one distance on both: three against two, slowed to a rhythm or sped to a chord, is the same 0.585 of an octave.

37i4Falling Into Step

Two timekeepers, each with its own speed: two players, two pendulum clocks on one wall, or a person and a beat. Let θ₁ and θ₂ be their turns, counted in radians, with natural rates ω₁ and ω₂. Each nudges the other towards itself, by an amount that grows with how far apart in the turn they are and fades as they line up: θ₁′ = ω₁ + K₁ sin(θ₂ − θ₁) and θ₂′ = ω₂ + K₂ sin(θ₁ − θ₂), with K₁, K₂ > 0. That is a pair of 24a1’s statements on rates, laid down as the plainest that nudge.

Let φ = θ₂ − θ₁, the gap in the turn, Δω = ω₂ − ω₁ and K = K₁ + K₂. Subtracting the statements gives one statement for the gap: φ′ = Δω − K sin φ.

Lock-in technique: the gap settles to a resting place that holds exactly when |Δω| ≤ K, and then the two keep one time.

\varphi' = \Delta\omega - K\sin\varphi @@ subtract the two statements; sin(−x) = −sin x
\varphi' = 0 \iff \sin\varphi = \Delta\omega / K @@ a resting place
\text{has an answer} \iff |\Delta\omega| \le K @@ sin takes every value in [−1, 1] and no other (21c1)
\tfrac{d}{d\varphi}(\Delta\omega - K\sin\varphi) = -K\cos\varphi < 0 \text{ at the answer with } \cos\varphi > 0 @@ 24k1: a resting place holds when the rate falls through 0
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi \to \varphi^*,\ \ \theta_1' = \theta_2'} \quad \blacksquare @@ at rest, the gap is fixed: one speed for both

With K = 0.5 a second and Δω = 0.3 a second, the gap settles at the φ* with sin φ* = 0.6, about 0.64 radians: the two lock with one a little ahead. With Δω = 0.7 there is no resting place, the gap keeps growing, and the two slip past each other again and again. A clapping audience sorts itself by this: claps close enough in speed lock, and the crowd claps as one.

The accepted names

Falling into step is entrainment, or synchronisation; locked is phase-locked, and the plainest nudging statement is the Adler equation, the two-member case of the Kuramoto model. The range |Δω| ≤ K is the locking range, or Arnold tongue. Three against two is a hemiola; a rhythm sped into pitch is a click train, and the borderline near 20 Hz is the lower limit of pitch.

Who said it first

Christiaan Huygens saw two pendulum clocks on one beam fall into step in 1665. Robert Adler wrote the statement for the gap in 1946, for radio oscillators; Yoshiki Kuramoto solved it for a whole crowd in 1975. Clapping audiences falling into step were measured by Zoltán Néda and others in 2000.

A proof that is not allowed

“Three against two meets at 0 and at B. So it meets twice a bar.” Not allowed: B is the start of the next bar, not a time in [0, B); the onsets of one bar are counted once, and the meeting at B is the next bar’s meeting at 0.
