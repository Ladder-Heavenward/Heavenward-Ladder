22i · technique

Testing sums by hand

By now there are many ways to decide whether an endless sum settles, and the work is choosing the one that fits. The look of the pieces usually tells you which: a power of a fixed number points one way, a power of n another, a sign that flips another. And deciding that a sum settles is only half the job. To use its total you need a number, and a number is only as good as the size of its possible error. Each test that says a sum settles also says how much is left in the part you did not add.

The shape of the pieces says which test to reach for. Each test that shows a sum settles also fences the part left over. A total is used with its error beside it.

22i1Which test, when

Choosing technique: ask, in this order: do the pieces head to 0? Is it a geometric sum or a p-sum? Do the pieces look like one of those, far out? Are there factorials or nth powers? Do the pieces come from a falling curve? Do the signs take turns? The first question that answers settles it.

  • Pieces not heading to 0: the sum wanders (22d2). Check this first; it is the cheapest.
  • Pieces like rⁿ or 1/nᵖ, far out: compare (22e1), or compare by the ratio of pieces (22e1).
  • Factorials, or a fixed number to the nth power: the ratio test (22e2). Something to the nth power whole: the root test (22e3).
  • Pieces f(n) with f falling, often with a log in them: the area technique (22f1).
  • Signs that take turns: the bracket (22g1); then ask separately whether it settles in size (22e4).
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{n}{2n+1} \text{ wanders} @@ the pieces head to ½, not 0 (22d2)
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{2n+1}{n^3 + 1} \text{ settles} @@ the pieces over 1/n² head to 2; the p-sum with p = 2 settles (22e1, 22f2)
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{n^3}{3^n} \text{ settles} @@ the ratio of neighbours heads to ⅓ (22e2)
\textstyle\sum_{n \ge 2} \tfrac{1}{n \ln n} \text{ wanders} @@ the area ∫₂^b dx/(x ln x) = ln ln b − ln ln 2 passes every ceiling (22f1)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum \tfrac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n} \text{ settles only by taking turns}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bracket settles it; in size it is the p-sum with p = ½, which wanders (22g1, 22f2)

22i2A total and its error

Leftover technique: stop after n pieces, and the error is the tail. If each piece is at most k times the one before, with k < 1, the tail is at most uₙ₊₁/(1 − k). If the pieces come from a falling curve, the tail lies between ∫ₙ₊₁^∞ f and ∫ₙ^∞ f. If the signs take turns and the sizes fall to 0, the tail is less than uₙ₊₁ in size. The first is a geometric sum laid over the tail. The other two are 22f3 and 22g1.

\tfrac{u_{k+1}}{u_k} = \tfrac{1}{k+1} \le \tfrac{1}{9} \text{ for } k \ge 8 @@ the pieces are 1/k!, counting from k = 0
\text{tail after } \tfrac{1}{7!} \le \tfrac{1/8!}{1 - 1/9} = \tfrac{9}{8 \cdot 8!} < 0.0000280 @@ the leftover technique
1 + 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac16 + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{5040} = 2.7182540\ldots @@ the first eight pieces
\htmlClass{new}{2.718254 < \textstyle\sum \tfrac{1}{k!} < 2.718282} \quad \blacksquare @@ the total, fenced

Eight pieces give the total to four places. The same work on 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + ⋯ needs about ten thousand pieces for four places, because its bracket bound is only the next piece, 1/(n + 1). Both settle. One settles fast. How fast a sum settles is a fact about it as real as whether it does.

22i3Illegal moves

\tfrac1n \to 0, \text{ so } \textstyle\sum \tfrac1n \text{ settles}\,? @@ heading to 0 is needed, not enough (22d3)
\textstyle\sum u_n \text{ settles, so any order of it settles at the same total}\,? @@ only if it settles in size (22g3)
\tfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} < 1 \text{ for every } n, \text{ so it settles}\,? @@ Σ1/n has every ratio below 1; the ratios must stay below one k < 1
\text{ratio test gives } 1, \text{ so the sum wanders}\,? @@ a ratio of 1 decides nothing: Σ1/n wanders, Σ1/n² settles
\text{the next piece is } 0.001, \text{ so the total is right to } 0.001\,? @@ only for the bracket; Σ1/n has next pieces as small as you like and no total

The accepted names

Choosing among the tests is usually taught as a strategy for testing series. The leftover technique gives remainder estimates: for the ratio test, for the integral test, and for the alternating series test. The total of 1 + 1 + 1/2! + ⋯ is e, shown on the next step.

Who said it first

Error bounds for sums are as old as the sums. Isaac Newton estimated the leftover of his series by eye; Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1797) and Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1821) gave the first bounds that were proved.
