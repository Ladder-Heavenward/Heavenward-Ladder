21c · proof

Sin² + cos² = 1

Every point you reach by walking around the circle is one unit from the centre. So its two coordinates, squared and added, always make one. That single fact lets you find one coordinate from the other, up to a sign, and the sign is settled by which quarter of the circle you are in. Divide the up by the across and you get the steepness of the line from the centre to your point. That steepness is called the tangent, because it can be read off the line that just touches the circle where the walk begins.

Across squared plus up squared is always one, so either coordinate tells you the other once you know which quarter you are in. The up divided by the across is the steepness of the angle, called its tangent.

21c1On the circle

Circle technique for cos and sin: for every θ, cos² θ + sin² θ = 1. On the upper half, sin θ = √(1 − cos² θ) by definition. The lower half flips the sign of sin, which squaring forgets. Whole turns change nothing.

0 \le \theta \le \pi:\ \sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta @@ ﹀ sin, cos (21b1), squared
\pi < \theta < 2\pi:\ \sin^2\theta = (-\sin(2\pi - \theta))^2 = 1 - \cos^2\theta @@ 21b2, and the first line at 2π − θ
\htmlClass{new}{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ every θ, by whole turns (21b3)
-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1,\ \ -1 \le \sin\theta \le 1 @@ each square is at most the sum, 1

Here sin² θ means (sin θ)², the square of the number sin θ. It is not sin(sin θ).

The mark ⊥ comes back here. The point at θ is cos θ across and sin θ up: an arrow along the floor and an arrow up the wall, and (1, 0) · (0, 1) = 0, so the two are perpendicular at every θ. They are the side next to θ and the side facing it in 21b5’s right triangle, and neither has any share of the other: no amount of across makes any up. The circle technique is the right-triangle technique of 18a, laid on those two sides. Each also runs out to nothing a quarter turn on. cos 0 = 1 and cos(π/2) = 0; sin 0 = 0 and sin(π/2) = 1. The opening of a quarter turn is cos(π/2) = 0, and an opening of 0 is what 18a calls perpendicular: the arrow at π/2 has no share at all along the arrow at 0. The right angle is where one direction stops saying anything about the other. 27i2 shows why that earns it the same mark as the totally false.

21c2Signs by quarter

Quarters technique: the signs of cos θ and sin θ are fixed by the quarter of the circle the walk ends in. Cos falls from 1 to −1 on [0, π], passing 0 only at π/2. Sin is positive strictly between 0 and π. The lower half flips the sign of sin and keeps cos.

0 < \theta < \tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ \cos > 0,\ \sin > 0 @@ the first quarter
\tfrac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \pi:\ \cos < 0,\ \sin > 0 @@ the second
\pi < \theta < \tfrac{3\pi}{2}:\ \cos < 0,\ \sin < 0 @@ the third: the flip of the second over the floor
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{3\pi}{2} < \theta < 2\pi:\ \cos > 0,\ \sin < 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fourth: the flip of the first

21c3The tangent and its family

From here on, when cos θ ≠ 0, tan θ = sin θ / cos θ and sec θ = 1 / cos θ. When sin θ ≠ 0, cot θ = cos θ / sin θ and csc θ = 1 / sin θ. They are read tangent, secant, cotangent and cosecant.

Tangent-is-steepness technique: tan θ is the slope of the line from the centre to the point at θ. The line through (0, 0) and (cos θ, sin θ) rises sin θ over a run of cos θ. Where that line meets the upright line x = 1, which touches the circle at (1, 0), its height is tan θ, and its distance from the centre is |sec θ|.

\text{slope} = \frac{\sin\theta - 0}{\cos\theta - 0} = \tan\theta @@ the slope of a line through two points (18c)
\text{on } x = 1:\ y = \tan\theta \cdot 1 @@ the line is y = (tan θ) x
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the point } (1, \tan\theta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ on the line that touches the circle at (1, 0): the tangent line

21c4The circle, divided through

Tangent-secant technique: wherever cos θ ≠ 0, 1 + tan² θ = sec² θ. Divide the circle technique through by cos² θ. Dividing by sin² θ instead gives 1 + cot² θ = csc² θ.

\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1 @@ 21c1
\frac{\cos^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \frac{1}{\cos^2\theta} @@ divide by cos² θ ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ ﹀ tan, sec

In the picture this is the right triangle (0, 0), (1, 0), (1, tan θ): its sides are 1 and |tan θ|, and its longest side is |sec θ|.

The accepted names

cos² θ + sin² θ = 1 is the Pythagorean identity; 1 + tan² θ = sec² θ and 1 + cot² θ = csc² θ are its relatives. The quarters are the four quadrants, numbered anticlockwise. The names tangent and secant come from the touching line and the cutting line: Latin tangere, “to touch”, and secare, “to cut” (Thomas Fincke, 1583).

Who said it first

In 21c.4.5, the surveyor is Carl Friedrich Gauss, and the triangle Hoher Hagen, Brocken and Inselsberg (about 1821–1825). Whether he meant it as a test of space is disputed: Arthur Miller (1972) says no, Ernst Breitenberger (1984) says perhaps.
