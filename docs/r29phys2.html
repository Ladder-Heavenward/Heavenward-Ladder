How Do Continents Turn on a Sphere?

How do continents turn on a sphere? The plates of the earth’s crust drift a few centimetres a year, opening oceans and pushing up mountains. On a flat map they seem to slide; but they ride on a ball, and on a ball nothing simply slides. This page takes plates as rigid caps on a sphere (29b), finds that every move of a cap is a turn about one pole with 27o’s turns of space, finds speed growing away from that pole, joins two turns into one with 19’s groups, and finds that sliding faults lie on circles about the pole.

A rigid move of a sphere that keeps its centre is a turn of space, and every turn of space has an axis (27o). Where the axis meets the surface is the pole of the plate’s motion. A point at angle Δ from the pole moves at ωR sin Δ, along the circle about the pole through it. Faults where plates slide past each other lie along those circles, which is how the pole is found.

29phys21Plates as Rigid Caps on a Ball

Jean is a geophysicist. The earth’s outer shell is broken into a dozen large plates, each stiff enough to move as one piece over millions of years. A plate is a cap: a patch of the surface of a ball of radius R = 6,371 km.

From here on, on this page, a move of a plate is a motion that carries every point of it across the sphere while keeping all distances between its points: a distance-keeping motion of the sphere (29b).

29phys22Every Move of a Cap Is a Turn About One Pole

A move of the sphere keeps its centre and keeps distances, so it is a turn of space (27o). A turn of space keeps one line through the centre fixed: an arrow it only stretches by 1 (27f).

\text{turn } M:\ M\,a = a \text{ for the axis } a @@ an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1 (27f, 27o)
\text{the axis meets the surface at two points} @@ one of them is called the pole
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every move of a plate is a turn through some angle about its pole}} @@

Pole-of-turning technique: every motion of a rigid plate on a sphere is a turn about an axis through the centre; find the axis’s pole, and the motion of every point of the plate follows from one rate of turning. On a flat map a plate could slide in a straight line; on a ball, there is no such thing.

29phys23Speed Grows Away From the Pole

Turning at rate ω about the pole, a point at angle Δ from the pole goes round a circle of radius R sin Δ (29b). Its speed is ω times that.

v = \omega R\sin\Delta @@ R sin Δ, the circle’s radius
\omega = 1° \text{ per million years} \approx 1.75 \times 10^{-8} \text{ radians a year} @@ 
\Delta = 90°:\ v \approx 1.75 \times 10^{-8} \times 6.371 \times 10^8 \text{ cm} \approx 11 \text{ cm a year} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta = 30°:\ v \approx 5.6 \text{ cm a year};\ \ \Delta = 0:\ v = 0} @@ still at the pole

Jean fits the speeds of satellite-tracked stations across a plate: stations near one point barely move, and the speeds grow as the sine of their angle from it, as one pole and one rate predict, to within a millimetre a year.

29phys24Two Turns Make One

Plate A turns relative to plate B, and B turns relative to plate C. Then A turns relative to C, and that is a turn too: turns of space form a group (19a), and the composite of two turns is a turn about some new axis.

(A \text{ to } C) = (B \text{ to } C) \circ (A \text{ to } B) @@ composing moves (19)
\text{for small rates: } \vec\omega_{AC} \approx \vec\omega_{AB} + \vec\omega_{BC} @@ turning arrows along their axes add
\htmlClass{new}{\text{three plates meeting at a point: } \vec\omega_{AB} + \vec\omega_{BC} + \vec\omega_{CA} \approx 0} @@ a check on the measurements

Plates turn so slowly that their turning arrows add like arrows. Where three plates meet, the three relative turning arrows must close into a triangle, and geophysicists use this to test a set of measured poles.

29phys25Sliding Faults Lie on Circles About the Pole

Where two plates slide past each other without opening or crushing, the fault must run along the direction of motion. Every point moves along its circle about the pole, so the fault lies along such a circle.

\text{sliding faults: along circles about the pole} @@ 
\text{the great circles square to the faults all meet at the pole} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spreading is fastest } 90° \text{ from the pole, slowest near it}} @@ v = ωR sin Δ

The sliding faults between two plates lie on circles about their pole, so drawing great circles square to them finds the pole; the spreading rate along a ridge then grows as the sine of the angle from it. Ocean ridges are broken into steps by such faults, and their pattern on the sea floor fixes the pole of every pair of plates.

Accepted names

The pole of turning is the Euler pole, and that every rigid motion of a sphere is a turn is Euler’s rotation theorem. The turning arrow is the angular velocity vector. Sliding faults are transform faults, and the meeting of three plates a triple junction.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler proved the rotation theorem in 1775; Dan McKenzie and Robert Parker, and Jason Morgan, applied it to the plates in 1967 and 1968 (not yet revisited for the dates).
