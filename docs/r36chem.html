What a Molecule Does With Light

What a molecule does with light: why does a highlighter glow, and a glow-in-the-dark star keep glowing after the lamp is off? A sunny window fades a poster in a summer, and a beach in July burns skin in an afternoon, though the light through the window looks the same as the light on the sand. A yellow highlighter glows brighter than the page it marks, a toy star on a bedroom ceiling glows for an hour after the light goes out, and a coating of tiny tubes looks like a hole in the world. What makes it hard is that each is a molecule doing something with a photon, and the photon’s energy, the molecule’s gaps and the time it takes are all too small or too fast to watch. This page answers with the rung’s gap-to-wavelength technique, the shares of 36·phys, and 17·chem’s decay, and says which parts are laid down and which are guesses with a range.

A mole of photons at a wavelength carries a definite energy, and only ultraviolet carries enough to break a carbon bond. A dye’s colour is the white it leaves after taking a band, so it sits across the circle from the band. A molecule that gives light back gives it back with less energy, so redder. If each excited molecule lets go with the same chance each second, the afterglow falls by halves; real glowing paints fall more slowly than that. And a forest of tubes that makes light bounce many times sends back almost none.

36chem1A Mole of Photons

Chemists count molecules by the mole of 5·chem, N_A ≈ 6.022 × 10²³ of them, and give a bond’s energy per mole: 14·chem’s table has 348 kJ for a mole of carbon–carbon single bonds. A photon at λ carries hc/λ (36b2). So a mole of photons carries N_A·hc/λ, and N_A·hc ≈ 6.022 × 10²³ × 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ ≈ 0.1196 J·m per mole.

Bond-breaking technique: a photon can break a bond whose energy per mole is H only if λ ≤ N_A·hc/H; for a carbon–carbon single bond, that is λ at most about 344 nm. Proof: the photon’s energy per mole N_A·hc/λ must reach H, and dividing both sides (5b) turns the inequality for energy into one for λ, reversed, since a longer wavelength is a smaller energy (5j3). Then 0.1196/348 000 ≈ 3.44 × 10⁻⁷ m.

\lambda = 600\ \text{nm}:\ \ 0.1196/(6.00 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 199\ \text{kJ per mole} @@ orange light
\lambda = 500\ \text{nm}:\ \ 0.1196/(5.00 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 239\ \text{kJ per mole} @@ green light
\lambda = 300\ \text{nm}:\ \ 0.1196/(3.00 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 399\ \text{kJ per mole} @@ ultraviolet, past the window
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only the last reaches } 348:\ \text{it can break a C–C bond}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bond-breaking technique

So a photon in the window can lift a molecule up a gap and leave its bonds whole, and a photon of ultraviolet can cut one. That is why the summer window fades the poster slowly, since glass takes away most ultraviolet, and why the sun on open sand burns: the skin is taking photons that can break its bonds. Being able to is not the same as doing: whether a photon breaks a bond depends on whether the molecule takes it and where the energy goes, and the technique only says when it cannot.

36chem2The Colour Is What Is Left

A dye in white light takes a band and passes the rest, at the shares of 36·phys2. So the light that comes through is the white less the band, and by three-numbers col of what comes through is col(white) − col(band). On the rung’s circle (36d), white sits on the grey axis at the centre, and the band has a hue θ.

Left-colour technique: a dye in white light that takes away a band of hue θ looks a colour of hue opp(θ), across the circle from the band. Proof: mixing is adding vectors (the mix technique, 36c2), so in the plane of hues col(white) − col(band) is 0 − r·h(θ), with r the band’s radius of 36e, since white sits at the centre; and −r·h(θ) = r·h(opp(θ)) by the opposite technique (36d3).

So a dye that takes the orange near 600 nm looks blue, one that takes the greens looks magenta, and one that takes the violets and blues looks orange, as a carrot does. A chemist reading a solution’s colour reads its band from the opposite side of the circle. The technique is the rung’s model, the circle of 36d, and the colours it predicts are near, not exact: the band has a width, the white is not perfect, and the eye’s circle is bent by choice.

36chem3Given Back, a Little Redder

Some molecules that take a photon give one back. The molecule climbs a gap, loses a little of its energy as warmth while it settles, and falls back down a smaller gap. From here on, the molecule’s yield φ is the share of the photons it takes that it gives back, a number from 0 to 1 (here φ is a share, not the hue φ of 36d).

Redder-return technique: a molecule that takes light at λ₁ and gives light back at λ₂ has λ₂ ≥ λ₁, and the share of the energy it gives back is φ·λ₁/λ₂, which is at most 1. Proof: each photon given back carries at most the energy of the one taken, hc/λ₂ ≤ hc/λ₁, so λ₂ ≥ λ₁ (5j3); φ photons back per photon taken, each with hc/λ₂ against hc/λ₁, is the share φ·λ₁/λ₂; and φ ≤ 1, λ₁/λ₂ ≤ 1.

\lambda_1 = 490\ \text{nm},\ \ \lambda_2 = 515\ \text{nm} @@ a highlighter’s green dye takes blue, gives green
1239.84/490 \approx 2.53\ \text{eV},\ \ 1239.84/515 \approx 2.41\ \text{eV} @@ 36·phys7’s hc in eV·nm
2.53 - 2.41 = 0.12\ \text{eV per photon} @@ left as warmth
\htmlClass{new}{\text{with } \varphi = 0.93:\ \ 0.93 \times 490/515 \approx 0.885 \text{ of the energy given back}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the redder-return technique

The highlighter glows brighter than the page because it adds light the page cannot. It takes blue that the eye counts little and gives back green that the eye counts much, and in daylight it also takes ultraviolet the eye counts not at all and gives back visible light. That is 36·phys4’s unseen light turned the other way: now the light outside the window does change the colour. Washing powders do it on purpose, taking ultraviolet near 350 nm and giving back blue near 430 nm, so a yellowed shirt is pushed back toward the grey axis and looks whiter.

36chem4The Glow After the Light Stops

Most molecules give back their photon within a few billionths of a second, so the glow stops when the light does. Some are held in a state they leave slowly, and they glow on. Suppose, as a guess with its range, that each held molecule has the same chance k per second of letting go, whatever has happened before. Then the number still held, N, falls at the rate kN, and the glow, one photon for each letting go, is kN.

Afterglow technique: if each held molecule lets go with the same chance k per second, the afterglow is I₀e^(−kt) and halves every ln 2/k seconds, at every stage. Proof: N′ = −kN is 17·chem’s first-order statement, so N = N₀e^(−kt) by the first-order technique, and the glow kN is k·N₀e^(−kt).

The guess fails for the toy stars on a ceiling, and the way it fails is worth knowing. Their glow halves in a few minutes at first and then takes longer and longer to halve again. The paint holds its energy in traps of many depths, and each depth has its own k. A sum of decays with different rates is not a decay with one rate.

Many-traps technique: if I(t) = u·e^(−k₁t) + v·e^(−k₂t) with u, v > 0 (here two starting sizes, not 36f’s amounts) and k₁ > k₂, the rate −I′/I falls from (uk₁ + vk₂)/(u + v) toward k₂ as t grows, so the halving time grows toward ln 2/k₂. Proof: −I′/I = (uk₁e^(−k₁t) + vk₂e^(−k₂t))/I is an average of k₁ and k₂ with weights in proportion to u·e^(−k₁t) and v·e^(−k₂t); the first weight over the second is (u/v)e^(−(k₁ − k₂)t), which heads to 0 (17’s exponentials), so the average heads to k₂.

36chem5Blacker Than Black

A black paint takes most of the light and still sends back a few hundredths of it from its top face. A coating grown as a forest of carbon tubes, each thousands of times thinner than a hair, sends back far less: its makers report as little as 0.035% of the window’s light. Two of the rung’s tools say how such a number can come about. The forest is nearly all empty space, so its top face is between air and a stuff of index very near 1, and 36·phys5’s face share ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² is nearly 0. And light that goes in between the tubes meets one tube after another before it can come out.

Many-bounce technique: light that must be sent back n times, keeping a share r each time, comes out with the share rⁿ; to come out with less than a share s it needs n > log s / log r bounces, for 0 < r < 1. Proof: a share of a share (36·phys2) n times is rⁿ; rⁿ < s exactly when n·log r < log s, and dividing by log r, which is below 0, turns the inequality round.

r = 0.3,\ \ s = 0.00035 @@ suppose each tube sends back 30%; the target is 0.035%
\log 0.00035 / \log 0.3 \approx (-3.456)/(-0.523) \approx 6.61 @@ logs in tens (18·chem)
0.3^6 \approx 0.00073 > s,\ \ 0.3^7 \approx 0.00022 < s @@ check either side
\htmlClass{new}{\text{seven bounces are enough}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the many-bounce technique

The r here is made up to show the shape of the argument, not measured. What the technique shows for any r below 1 is that blackness grows like a power: each extra bounce multiplies what is left by r. That is why the forest is grown tall and sparse, and why touching it, which crushes the tubes flat, makes a shiny dark grey.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The study of what light does to molecules is photochemistry. The left-colour technique is the rule of complementary colours, and a dye’s band is its absorption band. Giving light back after taking it is luminescence: fluorescence when it is quick, phosphorescence when it is slow, and the shift to longer wavelengths is the Stokes shift. The yield φ is the quantum yield. The green dye of a highlighter is fluorescein, and the washing-powder molecules are optical brighteners. The toy stars are often painted with strontium aluminate, whose many traps give a long afterglow. The forest of tubes is a vertically aligned carbon nanotube array, sold as Vantablack.

Who said it first

George Gabriel Stokes named fluorescence and found the shift to longer wavelengths in 1852. Albert Einstein’s photon of 1905, and his law of 1912 that one photon acts on one molecule, settled the camps of 36·chem.6.4; Johannes Stark had proposed the same law in 1908. Edmond Becquerel measured the slow glow of phosphors with a spinning-disc phosphoroscope in 1859. Surrey NanoSystems announced the forest coating in 2014.
