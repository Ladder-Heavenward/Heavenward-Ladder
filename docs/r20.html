Moving the Plane

Rung 18 built the plane, with distances. Rung 19 built the algebra of moves. This rung puts them together. Some moves of the plane keep every distance: you can slide a drawing, turn it, or flip it over, and nothing in it changes size. A Greek geometer proved his triangles equal by laying one on top of another, but he never said what moving a figure is. Here the moves come first. Every move that keeps distances turns out to be made of flips, at most three of them, and whether the count is even or odd decides whether a figure still reads the same way round. Then the rung turns the idea around: a geometry is a choice of moves, and the facts of that geometry are exactly what those moves leave unchanged.

You can slide a drawing and turn it in your head. Proving what those moves keep is another matter, and by now you know that the other matter is where the fun is.

A move that keeps every distance can always be made by flipping the plane over a line, at most three times. Two flips make a turn or a slide, so turns and slides keep a figure the same way round, and an odd number of flips leaves it backwards. Each family of moves keeps some facts and loses others, and the bigger the family, the fewer facts it keeps.

What it means to say it

Until now a geometry has been a place: points, lines, circles, distances. This rung says a geometry is also a way of moving around in that place. Choose the moves you allow, and you have chosen which facts matter. If you may slide, turn and flip, then size matters and position does not: two triangles with the same sides are the same triangle in two places. If you may also stretch, then only shape matters: every circle is the same as every other.

Notice what this changes. The old geometry held “lay one figure on the other” in trust. Here the move is a function, built from formulas, and its keeping of distance is proved. The old tests for equal triangles stop being axioms and become things you can check.

Where it comes from

The first proof of equal triangles in the old geometry, around 300 BC, moved one onto another, and readers argued for two thousand years about whether that was allowed. A French geometer objected in 1557. A German mathematician, in 1899, took side-angle-side as an axiom instead, as 18f5 told. A German philosopher, in 1768, pointed out that a left and a right hand match in every length and still cannot be swapped. Another German mathematician, in 1872, proposed that each geometry is the study of what a group of moves keeps, and that sorted all the geometries of his time into one family tree.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, the moves that keep every distance form a group (20b3), and so do the moves that keep shape (20b3). Two such moves in a row make another, and each can be undone.

Second, slides, turns and flips all keep distances (20d1, 20d2, 20d3), and two figures are called congruent when one of these moves carries one onto the other (20d4).

Third, two flips make a turn or a slide (20e2, 20e3), and every move that keeps distances is made of at most three flips (20f3). A move is decided by where it sends three corners of a triangle (20f2).

Fourth, a figure’s corners go round one way or the other. Slides and turns keep that way round (20g2); each flip reverses it (20g3). So an even number of flips can never equal an odd number (20g4).

Fifth, a geometry is a group of moves, and its facts are what those moves keep. A bigger group keeps fewer facts (20h3).

What follows

A graph can be moved by changing its equation, and the change always runs backwards: to slide a graph right, take away from x. A turn about the centre is just a pair of numbers, and two turns combine by a rule for pairs. That rule completes an old foreclosure: the quarter turn done twice is the half turn, so in the plane there is a number whose square is minus one.

What you can do because you know it

You can write any slide, turn or flip as a formula and prove it keeps distances. You can find the flips that carry one triangle onto a congruent copy, and prove the old tests for congruent and similar triangles. You can tell whether a move keeps a figure the same way round. You can move a graph and find its new equation, combine turns as pairs, and solve equations that have no answer on the line.

That is the geometry of moves. The man who proposed it spent a lifetime on it and called his own work a beginning, and you have only just begun.

And 20·chem asks whether a molecule can be left-handed: it reads a carbon from one of its neighbours, where turns keep the way round and a mirror reverses it, and shows why a living body can tell the two hands of a drug apart. And 20·nav asks which way a ship faces after its turns: headings add, starting again past 360, the ship's place and facing are one move of the plane, and a voyage is its orders composed, in an order that matters.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends 18’s plane and distances, 19’s groups, rung 9’s functions and their undoing, and 8i’s quadratics. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that a geometry is what its moves keep, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

The old geometry is Euclid’s. His first proof of equal triangles, around 300 BC, moved one onto another. Jacques Peletier objected in 1557, and David Hilbert, in 1899, took side-angle-side as an axiom instead.

Immanuel Kant, in 1768, pointed out that a left and a right hand match in every length and still cannot be swapped. Felix Klein, in 1872, proposed that each geometry is the study of what a group of moves keeps. He spent a lifetime on it and called his own work a beginning.

Accepted names

The congruence tests are often called Euclid’s tests.
