How do tiny magnets agree?

How do tiny magnets agree? Each atom of iron is a small magnet, and each pulls on only its nearest neighbours. Yet below 1043 kelvin, about 770 °C, a bar of iron can be a magnet as a whole, with nearly every atom pointing one way, and above that temperature it cannot, however strong the neighbours’ pull. Nothing tells the atoms far apart to agree. The agreement appears all at once, at one temperature, out of nothing but each atom’s wish to match the ones beside it and the heat that shakes them. This page builds the simplest model of that: arrows that point up or down, one at each dot of a grid, with a little energy saved for every link whose two ends agree. On a line of dots it solves the model exactly with a two-by-two grid, and finds no agreement at any temperature above zero. On a flat grid an exact answer exists, found in 1944, and is hard; the page instead finds an approximate one, in which each arrow feels only the average of its neighbours, and that one has a temperature where agreement begins. It reads the strength of iron’s pull between neighbours off its measured temperature. And it ends with the same model turned into a memory, where stored patterns are the low points of the energy.

Arrows up or down on a grid save energy J for each agreeing link. A pair agrees on average by tanh(J/kT). On a ring, the shares come from a two-by-two grid’s stretches, and the agreement dies out along the ring at every temperature above zero. If each arrow feels its neighbours’ average, agreement appears below kT = qJ, growing as the square root of the distance below. Iron’s 1043 K gives J of about 0.01 eV. Stored patterns are low points of the energy, and arrows that follow their local field never climb.

40·phys.1Arrows on a grid

Put an arrow sᵢ = +1 (up) or −1 (down) at each dot i of a graph: the dots of a square grid, or of a ring. In a field h, the energy of a whole arrangement s is E(s) = −J Σ sᵢsⱼ − h Σ sᵢ, the first sum over the links, the second over the dots. J > 0 is the energy saved by a link whose two arrows agree. By 30·phys, at temperature T the share of the arrangement s is e^(−E(s)/kT) divided by Z, the total of e^(−E/kT) over every arrangement.

Pair-agreement technique: two linked arrows with no field agree on average by tanh(J/kT) = (e^(J/kT) − e^(−J/kT))/(e^(J/kT) + e^(−J/kT)).

K = J/kT;\ \ \text{arrangements } {+}{+}, {-}{-} \text{ weigh } e^{K};\ \ {+}{-}, {-}{+} \text{ weigh } e^{-K} @@ E = −J for agreeing, +J for not
Z = 2e^{K} + 2e^{-K} @@ the four arrangements
\text{average of } s_1 s_2 = \frac{2e^{K} - 2e^{-K}}{2e^{K} + 2e^{-K}} @@ +1 for agreeing, −1 for not, each with its share
\htmlClass{new}{= \tanh K} \quad \blacksquare @@ near 1 when cold, near K when hot

At room temperature kT ≈ 0.0257 eV. A pull of J = 0.01 eV gives tanh(0.39) ≈ 0.37: linked neighbours agree a little more than half the time, (1 + 0.37)/2 ≈ 69%. The question is whether that local lean adds up to agreement across a whole bar.

40·phys.2A ring, exactly

Put N arrows on a ring, each linked to the next, with field h; write K = J/kT and b = h/kT. The weight of an arrangement is a product of one factor for each link: T(sᵢ, sᵢ₊₁) = e^(K sᵢsᵢ₊₁ + b(sᵢ + sᵢ₊₁)/2), so that each arrow gets half its field from each side. Read T as a 2 × 2 grid, rows and columns marked +1 and −1.

Ring-grid technique: Z for the ring is λ₊ᴺ + λ₋ᴺ, where λ± = e^K cosh b ± √(e^(2K) sinh² b + e^(−2K)) are the stretches of T. The total of the weights over all arrangements is the sum down the middle of Tᴺ, since the ring closes; by the straighten-out technique of 27f, Tᴺ = P Dᴺ P⁻¹, whose sum down the middle is that of Dᴺ. The stretches come from det(T − λI) = 0.

N \text{ large: } Z \approx \lambda_+^N @@ λ₊ > |λ₋|
\text{average arrow } m = \frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial b} = \frac{\sinh b}{\sqrt{\sinh^2 b + e^{-4K}}} @@ the slope of ln Z in b gives the average of Σ sᵢ (30·phys)
h \to 0 \text{ at any } T > 0:\ \ m \to 0 @@ e^(−4K) > 0 keeps the bottom away from 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on a ring, no agreement across the whole at any temperature above zero}} \quad \blacksquare @@

The reason is cheap walls. Break the ring into a long run of ups and a long run of downs: it costs 2J at each of the two walls and gains, in the count of where to put them, about k ln(N²) of entropy. For large N the gain wins at any T above zero. On a flat grid a wall between two regions is a whole line of disagreeing links, and its cost grows with its length. That is why two dimensions differ.

40·phys.3Each arrow feels the average

On a flat grid, each dot has q neighbours: 4 on a square grid. Guess that each arrow feels its neighbours only through their average m, and the field. Then one arrow sits in the field h + qJm, and its two states weigh e^(±(h + qJm)/kT).

Average-field technique: if each arrow feels only the average of its q neighbours, then m = tanh((qJm + h)/kT); with no field, a solution m ≠ 0 exists exactly when kT < qJ, and just below that temperature m ≈ √3 (1 − T/T_c)^(1/2), with kT_c = qJ.

m = \tanh(x),\ \ x = \tau m,\ \ \tau = qJ/kT = T_c/T @@ one arrow’s average, h = 0
\tanh \text{ has slope 1 at 0 and bends down for } x > 0 @@ so m = tanh(τm) meets m only at 0 when τ ≤ 1, and also at ±m₀ when τ > 1
\tanh x \approx x - x^3/3 \text{ near } 0 @@ its series (23h)
m = \tau m - \tau^3 m^3/3 \Rightarrow m^2 = 3(\tau - 1)/\tau^3 @@ divide by m ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{m \approx \sqrt{3}\,(1 - T/T_c)^{1/2} \text{ just below } T_c} \quad \blacksquare @@ τ − 1 ≈ 1 − T/T_c, τ³ ≈ 1

So the guess has a temperature where agreement begins. For the square grid it says kT_c = 4J. The exact answer, of 1944, is kT_c = 2J/ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269J: the guess is 76% too high, since it ignores that neighbours lean together, not each alone. Near T_c the exact m grows as (1 − T/T_c)^(1/8), not the square root. Measured magnets near their temperature grow with a power near 1/3, which the three-dimensional grid gives, and which no average-field guess can.

40·phys.4Iron

Iron’s atoms sit on a cubic grid with one atom at each corner and one at the centre of each cube; each atom has q = 8 nearest neighbours. Its measured temperature of agreement is T_c = 1043 K.

J \approx kT_c/q = 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \cdot 1043 / 8 \text{ J} @@ the average-field technique, read backwards
\approx 1.80 \times 10^{-21} \text{ J} \approx 0.011 \text{ eV} @@ 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
\text{one atom’s magnet in a field of 1 T: } \mu B \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J} @@ one electron’s magnet; iron has about two to an atom
\htmlClass{new}{J / \mu B \approx 190:\ \text{the neighbours’ pull far outweighs a strong magnet’s}} \quad \blacksquare @@

Above T_c the bar has no agreement, and a field leans it a little. With m small, the average-field technique gives m ≈ (qJm + h)/kT, so m ≈ h/(k(T − T_c)): the lean grows without bound as T falls to T_c. Above-the-point technique: above its temperature of agreement, in the average-field guess, a magnet’s lean in a small field is h/(k(T − T_c)). Measured on nickel and iron, the lean does grow as one over T minus a fixed temperature, close above it, with a fitted temperature a little above the true one.

The pull J does not come from the atoms’ magnets at all: two such magnets an atom apart pull with about a hundred-thousandth of an eV. It comes from 37·phys’s swap sign, through the energy of two electrons whose spins agree or disagree. Agreement in iron is magnetic in its effects and electric, through exclusion, in its cause.

40·phys.5A memory made of arrows

Let the links have their own strengths wᵢⱼ = wⱼᵢ, and the energy E(s) = −½ Σᵢ Σⱼ wᵢⱼ sᵢ sⱼ, with wᵢᵢ = 0. To store a pattern ξ of ups and downs, set wᵢⱼ = ξᵢξⱼ/N. Then E(ξ) = −(N − 1)/2, the least the energy can be, and −ξ is equally low.

Downhill-update technique: setting one arrow sᵢ to the sign of its field Σⱼ wᵢⱼ sⱼ never raises the energy.

\text{the terms of } E \text{ holding } s_i:\ -s_i\,f_i,\ \ f_i = \textstyle\sum_{j} w_{ij} s_j @@ the halves of the two terms wᵢⱼ and wⱼᵢ add
s_i \to \text{sign}(f_i):\ \ -s_i f_i \to -|f_i| @@ the least of −f_i and +f_i
\htmlClass{new}{E \text{ falls or stays}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other terms do not hold sᵢ
  
\xi = (+1, -1, +1, -1),\ \ w_{ij} = \xi_i\xi_j/4 @@ one pattern stored
\text{start } (+1, +1, +1, -1) @@ one arrow wrong
f_2 = \tfrac{\xi_2}{4}(\xi_1 s_1 + \xi_3 s_3 + \xi_4 s_4) = -\tfrac14(1 + 1 + 1) = -\tfrac34 @@ arrow 2’s field
\htmlClass{new}{s_2 \to -1:\ \text{the pattern is back}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy fell from 0 to −3/2

Each update is 38·bio’s threshold unit; the whole is a ring of them with links both ways, which settles where a one-way ring of 38g ticks for ever. Many patterns can be stored by adding their w’s. They interfere, and past about 0.14N patterns for N arrows the stored ones stop being low points; the count was made with the methods of this page’s first footings, for random patterns, in 1985.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The model is the Ising model, with J the exchange coupling. The temperature of agreement is the Curie temperature, and agreement across the whole is spontaneous magnetization, a phase transition. The ring-grid technique is the transfer matrix method. The average-field technique is mean-field theory (Weiss's molecular field, 1907), and the exponent of m near T_c is the critical exponent β: ½ in mean field, ⅛ exactly in two dimensions, about 0.326 in three. The above-the-point technique is the Curie–Weiss law. The memory is the Hopfield network, its w's are Hebbian weights, and its limit of 0.138N is from Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky (1985). The swap-sign pull is the exchange interaction (Heisenberg, 1928).

Who said it first

Wilhelm Lenz proposed the model in 1920 and his student Ernst Ising solved the line in 1925, finding no transition, and wrongly guessed none in any dimension. Pierre Weiss gave the average field in 1907; Pierre Curie had measured the temperatures in 1895. Rudolf Peierls argued in 1936, by the cost of walls, that two dimensions do have a transition. Lars Onsager solved the square grid exactly in 1944. John Hopfield proposed the memory in 1982, and shared the physics prize of 2024 for it with Geoffrey Hinton.

A proof that is not allowed

“The average-field equation has a solution m ≠ 0 below T_c on every grid, including the line, where q = 2. So a line of arrows agrees below kT = 2J.” Not allowed: the average-field guess was not proved; it was guessed, and the line is where its guess can be checked exactly, by the ring-grid technique, which finds no agreement at any T above zero. The guess is wrong there, and the exact answer is the judge.
