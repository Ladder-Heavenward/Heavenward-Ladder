Which Way Is “Up” for a Plane Over Paris?

Which way is “up” for a plane over Paris? A satellite gives a place as three numbers measured from the earth’s centre. The pilot wants east, north and up from where the plane is. The plane’s own sensors measure along its nose, its wing and its floor, which tilt and turn. Every navigation system juggles these frames all the time. This page builds each frame from cos and sin, passes between them with rung 27’s turn grids, undoes a turn by turning its grid on its side, and shows why the order of a plane’s turns cannot be swapped.

Write a place on the earth from its latitude and longitude with cos and sin. The local frame’s east, north and up are three unit arrows at right angles; a displacement’s local parts are its dots with them. Stack the three as the rows of a grid T: T is a turn of space, and its undo is its side-turn Tᵀ. Turns in space do not swap, so a craft’s heading, pitch and roll must be done in an agreed order, and a chain of frames is the product of their grids.

28nav2From Latitude and Longitude to the Earth Frame

Take the earth as a ball of radius R. A place at latitude φ, longitude λ, at height h above the ground, is found by turning up from the equator by φ and round from longitude 0 by λ.

x = (R + h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda @@ cos and sin as parts (21)
y = (R + h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda @@ turned round by λ
z = (R + h)\sin\varphi @@ the height above the equator’s plane
\htmlClass{new}{\text{up} = (\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\ \cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\ \sin\varphi)} @@ the unit arrow from the centre

Slopes of the place in λ and in φ point east and north; scaled to length 1 they are the local frame’s other two arrows.

28nav3Into the Local Frame and Back

Stack east, north and up as the rows of a grid T. Then T times an arrow’s earth parts gives its local parts, by the read-off technique.

T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -c & 0 & c \\ c & 0 & c \end{pmatrix},\ \ c = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} @@ east, north, up as rows
d = (-c,\ 0,\ c) \text{ km} @@ a move in earth parts
T d = (0,\ c^2 + c^2,\ -c^2 + c^2) = (0, 1, 0) @@ one km due north
T^{\mathsf T} T = I @@ rows of length 1, at right angles
\htmlClass{new}{T^{-1} = T^{\mathsf T}} @@ back to earth parts: the side-turn

Frame technique: the grid whose rows are a frame’s three arrows turns earth parts into that frame’s parts, and its side-turn turns them back. No elimination is needed: undoing a turn of space is free.

28nav4Turns That Will Not Swap

The craft frame is the local frame turned three times: by the heading about up, then the pitch about the new wing, then the roll about the new nose. Try two turns of 90° in both orders, on the nose arrow, starting north.

\text{turn about up by } 90°, \text{ then about east by } 90° @@ nose: north → west → west
\text{turn about east by } 90°, \text{ then about up by } 90° @@ nose: north → up → up
\text{west} \ne \text{up} @@ the two orders differ
\htmlClass{new}{R_1 R_2 \ne R_2 R_1 \text{ for turns of space}} @@ products of grids need not swap (27d)

Turns of space do not swap, so a craft’s attitude is three angles together with an agreed order. Aircraft use heading, then pitch, then roll. With the nose straight up the first and last turn are about the same line, and the three angles stop being fixed by the attitude.

28nav5A Chain of Frames

A camera under the wing sees a runway light. Its reading passes from camera to craft to local to earth, each step a turn grid and a slide.

v_{\text{earth}} = T_{\text{loc}}^{\mathsf T}\, T_{\text{craft}}^{\mathsf T}\, T_{\text{cam}}^{\mathsf T}\, v_{\text{cam}} @@ arrows: the turns only
\text{points: add each frame’s point as you pass it} @@ the slides
(ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1} @@ undress in reverse (19c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each grid kept as a turn, renewed as the craft moves}} @@ one product per reading

Chain technique: passing an arrow through a chain of frames is multiplying by their grids in turn, and coming back is multiplying by their side-turns in reverse order. Rounding slowly spoils a grid’s right angles, so systems make it a true turn again from time to time, as 21·comp did for spinning models.

Accepted names

The earth frame is earth-centred, earth-fixed (ECEF); the local frame is east-north-up (ENU), a local tangent plane; the craft frame is the body frame. A grid whose rows are a frame’s arrows is a direction cosine matrix, a rotation matrix, and TᵀT = I makes it orthogonal. Heading, pitch and roll are Euler angles (here yaw, pitch, roll), and losing an angle with the nose straight up is gimbal lock; systems avoid it with quaternions. Real systems use a flattened earth, the WGS 84 ellipsoid.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler showed in 1776 that every turn of space about a point is a turn about one line, and described turns by three angles.
