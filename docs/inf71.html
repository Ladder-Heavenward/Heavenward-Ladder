∞71 · research front

Why Does One Controller Work on Everything?

Why does one controller work on everything? The rule that holds a room’s warmth, a car’s speed on a hill, a drone level in a gust, a chemical plant’s temperature and the head of a disc drive over its track is, in most of them, the same rule: push in proportion to the error, plus a part for the error’s running total, plus a part for its slope. It was written down for steering ships in 1922, it needs no model of what it controls, and three dials are tuned on site. 24·eng found that pushing in proportion to the gap leaves a gap, and that adding the running total removes it, but held in trust whether the room would then settle or swing. That question has a clean answer. Write the loop as a transfer function, as 32j did, and draw the curve its values trace as the frequency runs: the loop settles exactly when that curve does not wind round the point −1, which is 34l’s winding number at work, and an engineer reads off from the drawing how far the loop is from swinging. A 1945 result shows that no controller, however clever, can make a loop insensitive at every frequency: push the error down at some and it comes up at others. Since about 2000, a different controller has spread from refineries to cars and robots: at every tick it plans the best moves ahead by solving a small problem of finding a lowest point, applies the first move and plans again, and proofs of when this is stable came around 2000. Whether a controller can be tuned from data alone, with a guarantee, is open.

A loop with plant P and controller C gives y = PC/(1 + PC) r. The running total removes droop; the slope adds a brake. The loop settles exactly when the roots of D + N lie left of the line Re s = 0, and, when D has none to the right, that count is the clockwise winding of L(iω) = N/D round −1: for k/(s + 1)³, settled exactly when k < 8. For k/(s + 1)² the sensitivity S = 1/(1 + L) has ∫₀^∞ ln|S(iω)| dω = 0. A plan with the right last cost makes the planned cost fall by at least x² each tick.

inf71.1The Thermostat as a Loop

24·eng.3 heated a house in proportion to the gap and found it settled 2.9 degrees short. Read the same house with 32j3’s transfer technique. Write s for 2πiξ, so a slope becomes a multiplication by s. The house, c T′ = u − λ(T − T_o), measured from the outside temperature, turns an input of heat u into a warmth y = P(s)u with P(s) = 1/(cs + λ). The heater by the gap turns an error e into a push u = C(s)e, here C = G. The loop closes with e = r − y, the set point less the warmth.

Closed-loop technique: a plant P driven by a controller C from the error e = r − y gives y = L/(1 + L)·r and e = S·r, with L = PC the loop and S = 1/(1 + L) its sensitivity.

y = P\,u = P\,C\,e = L\,e @@ round the loop: plant after controller (27d’s doing one after another)
e = r - y = r - L\,e @@ the error is what is left of the set point
(1 + L)\,e = r @@ gather
\htmlClass{new}{e = \tfrac{1}{1 + L}\,r = S\,r,\ \ y = \tfrac{L}{1 + L}\,r} \quad \blacksquare @@ when 1 + L is not 0
  
L(0) = P(0)\,G = G/\lambda = 2000/250 = 8 @@ steady: s = 0, no slopes
S(0) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 8} = \tfrac19 @@ the share of the gap that stays
\tfrac19 \times (20 - (-6)) = 2.89 @@ the set gap of 26 degrees
\htmlClass{new}{\text{settles } 2.89 \text{ degrees short}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 24·eng.3’s droop, now as S(0)

The sensitivity S is the loop’s report card: at each frequency, the share of a disturbance or a change of set point that still gets through as error. A large loop gain L makes S small. The house needs L(0) to be endless for no droop, and a controller with a part that divides by s does exactly that.

Drawn as ∞12.1’s boxes and wires, the house and the heater are two boxes in a row, and the loop is a wire from the warmth back to the error. The closed-loop technique says what the whole drawing does as one box, L/(1 + L), whatever the boxes hold inside. The plan is the loop; the furniture of the house, and the order of each tick’s heating, are let go.

inf71.2Three Terms, and What Each Buys

The three-term controller pushes u = Ge + κ∫e + Dé: in transfer form C(s) = G + κ/s + Ds. 24·eng.4 found that the running total can rest only where the gap is 0, and held in trust whether it gets there. The slope part brakes a plant that would otherwise swing.

Total-and-slope technique: (i) the house c T′ = u − λ(T − T_o) under u = Ge + κ∫e settles to the set point, with no droop, for every G ≥ 0 and κ > 0; (ii) a mass m x″ = u under u = Ge alone swings for ever, and under u = Ge + Dé with D > 0 it settles.

c\,s\,y + \lambda y = G(r - y) + \tfrac{\kappa}{s}(r - y) \Rightarrow c s^2 + (\lambda + G)s + \kappa = 0 @@ the house’s trial equation, after multiplying by s (24h3)
\text{roots: sum } -\tfrac{\lambda + G}{c} < 0,\ \text{product } \tfrac\kappa c > 0 @@ real roots are then both below 0; complex ones have real part −(λ + G)/(2c) < 0
\text{every deviation dies};\ \ S(0) = \tfrac{1}{1 + \infty} = 0 @@ 26·cb24’s decaying-turn technique; C(0) is endless
m s^2 + G = 0:\ s = \pm i\sqrt{G/m};\ \ m s^2 + Ds + G = 0:\ \text{real parts } -\tfrac{D}{2m} \text{ or both roots} < 0 @@ the same test for the mass
\htmlClass{new}{\text{total: no droop; slope: a brake}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each term buys one thing
  
c = 1.8\times10^7,\ \ \lambda + G = 2250 @@ 24·eng’s Truckee house, G = 2000
(\lambda + G)^2 = 4c\kappa \Rightarrow \kappa = 0.0703 \text{ W per degree second} @@ the edge of swinging: one double root
s = -\tfrac{2250}{3.6\times10^7} = -6.25\times10^{-5} \text{ per s} @@ a time scale of 16 000 s, 4.4 hours
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa = 0.5:\ \ s = -6.25\times10^{-5} \pm 1.55\times10^{-4}\,i} \quad \blacksquare @@ seven times the total: faster, but it swings past 20 degrees first

The mass with G = 4 and m = 1 swings at 2 radians a second for ever; with D = 4 the trial equation is (s + 2)² = 0 and it creeps in without swinging. So each term buys one thing and costs another: more total means less patience and more swing, more slope means more brake and more amplified noise, since a slope taken of a noisy reading is noisier still.

inf71.3The Curve That Must Not Wind Round −1

Trial equations of higher degree have no formula to read off. Write the loop as L = N/D, polynomials with fewer roots on top than below. Then 1 + L = (D + N)/D, and the loop settles exactly when every root of D + N has real part below 0. The roots need not be found. Count them with a loop, as 26h3 did: run round the right half of a disc of radius ρ, up the semicircle from −iρ to iρ and then down the line of the imaginary numbers. The count holds round this half-disc as round 26h3’s circle; that step is held in trust.

Encirclement technique: if D has no roots with real part 0 or above, and L(iω) never equals −1, then the number of roots of D + N with real part above 0 is the number of times the curve L(iω), with ω running from −∞ to ∞, goes clockwise round −1. The loop settles exactly when that number is 0.

\#\text{roots of } D + N \text{ inside} = w\big((D + N)\circ\gamma,\ 0\big),\ \ \#\text{roots of } D = w(D\circ\gamma, 0) = 0 @@ the image-winding technique (34l3) round the half-disc γ
\tfrac{(fg)'}{fg} = \tfrac{f'}{f} + \tfrac{g'}{g} \Rightarrow w(fg, 0) = w(f, 0) + w(g, 0) @@ the winding-total technique (34l2): totals add
w\big((D + N)\circ\gamma, 0\big) = w(D\circ\gamma, 0) + w\big((1 + L)\circ\gamma, 0\big) = w(L\circ\gamma, -1) @@ D + N = D(1 + L); 1 + L winds round 0 as L winds round −1
\text{on the arc, } |L| < 1 \text{ for large } \rho @@ fewer roots on top: the leash technique (34l3) holds 1 + L near 1, winding 0
\htmlClass{new}{\#\text{roots to the right} = \text{clockwise turns of } L(i\omega) \text{ round } -1} \quad \blacksquare @@ down the line is ω from ∞ to −∞; reversed, anticlockwise becomes clockwise

The curve L(iω) is measured, not computed: drive the open loop with a turning of each frequency and record the gain and the shift, as 32j’s transfer function promised. So the test runs on a plant nobody has modelled, and it says more than yes or no. The distance from the curve to −1 is how much error in the plant the loop can bear before it swings. That is why it became the engineer’s working tool, and why one controller, tuned by its curve, works on plants of every kind.

inf71.4Reading the Curve

Take three identical lags in a row, a plant like three tanks that each smooth what flows in, under a push in proportion to the error: L(s) = k/(s + 1)³. Each lag turns a frequency ω back by arctan ω, and shrinks it by 1/√(1 + ω²).

Three-lag technique: the loop L = k/(s + 1)³ with k > 0 settles exactly when k < 8; at k > 8 two roots lie to the right.

L(i\omega) = \tfrac{k}{(1 + i\omega)^3},\ \ \text{turned back by } 3\arctan\omega @@ the size of a product (25c), three times
3\arctan\omega = \pi \iff \omega = \tan\tfrac\pi3 = \sqrt3 @@ where the curve crosses the line of negative numbers
|L(i\sqrt3)| = \tfrac{k}{(1 + 3)^{3/2}} = \tfrac k8 @@ so the crossing is at −k/8
k < 8:\ \text{the crossing is right of } -1,\ \text{no turn round it};\ \ k > 8:\ \text{left, two clockwise turns} @@ ω and −ω give mirror halves
\htmlClass{new}{\text{settles} \iff k < 8} \quad \blacksquare @@ the encirclement technique
  
(s + 1)^3 = -k \Rightarrow s = -1 + k^{1/3}e^{\pm i\pi/3},\ \ -1 - k^{1/3} @@ the three cube roots of −k (25)
\text{real part } -1 + \tfrac12 k^{1/3} @@ cos(π/3) = ½
k = 8:\ 0;\ \ k = 27:\ +0.5;\ \ k = 4:\ -0.21 @@ at the edge, past it, inside it
\htmlClass{new}{k = 4:\ \text{a gain margin of } 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plant’s gain could double before the loop swings

Here the roots could be found by hand, and agree. In practice they cannot: the plant has dead times and lags nobody wrote down. The curve still can be drawn, and the margin read off it. A delay of T seconds turns each frequency back by ωT more without shrinking it, which moves the crossing in towards −1; that is why loops with long delays must be tuned gently.

inf71.5What No Controller Can Do

A good loop has S small, so that disturbances barely get through. Can S be small at every frequency? A result of 1945, held in trust here in general, says no: if L has no roots of D to the right, has at least two more roots below than on top, and the loop settles, then ∫₀^∞ ln|S(iω)| dω = 0. Where ln|S| is below 0, error is pushed down; somewhere it must come up by the same area. Engineers call it the waterbed.

Waterbed technique: for L = k/(s + 1)² with k > 0, the loop settles and ∫₀^∞ ln|S(iω)| dω = 0.

S = \tfrac{(s + 1)^2}{(s + 1)^2 + k},\ \ (s + 1)^2 + k = (s + 1 - i\sqrt k)(s + 1 + i\sqrt k) @@ roots −1 ± i√k: the loop settles
2\ln|S(i\omega)| = 2g(\omega) - g(\omega - \sqrt k) - g(\omega + \sqrt k),\ \ g(u) = \ln(1 + u^2) @@ |1 + i(ω ∓ √k)|² = 1 + (ω ∓ √k)²
\int_{-R}^{R}\big(g(\omega) - g(\omega - b)\big)\,d\omega = \int_{R - b}^{R} g - \int_{-R - b}^{-R} g @@ a shifted area: the pieces in common cancel (15)
\Big|\int_{R - b}^{R} g - \int_{-R - b}^{-R} g\Big| \le b\,\ln\tfrac{1 + (R + b)^2}{1 + (R - b)^2} \to 0 @@ g rises with |u|; each piece is between b g(R − b) and b g(R + b)
\htmlClass{new}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} 2\ln|S| = 0,\ \ \int_0^\infty \ln|S(i\omega)|\,d\omega = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ b = ±√k; |S| is the same at ω and −ω
  
|S(0)| = \tfrac{1}{1 + 3} = 0.25,\ \ \ln 0.25 = -1.386 @@ slow disturbances cut to a quarter
|S(i\omega)| = 1 \text{ at } \omega = \sqrt{5/2} = 1.58 @@ below this, error is pushed down
\int_0^{1.58}\ln|S| = -1.245,\ \ \int_{1.58}^{\infty}\ln|S| = +1.245 @@ checked numerically
\htmlClass{new}{|S| \text{ peaks at } \sqrt7/2 = 1.32 \text{ at } \omega = \sqrt6} \quad \blacksquare @@ disturbances near 2.45 rad/s are made 32% worse

Turn k up and the dent at low frequencies deepens, and the bulge grows to match. No choice of three terms, or of any controller, escapes it. The engineer chooses where to put the bulge: at frequencies where there is little disturbance to amplify. A plant with roots of D to the right is worse: then the integral is not 0 but a positive amount set by those roots, a price paid for holding up something that would fall.

inf71.6Planning at Every Tick

The three-term controller looks only at the error now. A plant with limits, a valve that cannot open past full, a car that cannot brake harder than its tyres grip, needs a controller that looks ahead. Model-predictive control does this. At each tick it plans the next moves to make a cost least, by a search for the lowest point such as 28o’s descent, applies only the first move, and plans again at the next tick from where the plant has actually got to. Take the unstable plant x ↦ 2x + u, doubling each tick if left alone, and the cost x² + ρu² per tick, with moves costing ρ = 2.

\min_u\ (2x + u)^2 + \rho u^2 \Rightarrow u = -\tfrac{2x}{1 + \rho} @@ slope 0 (13)
x_{\text{next}} = 2x - \tfrac{2x}{1 + \rho} = \tfrac{2\rho}{1 + \rho}x @@ the plant under the plan
\rho = 2:\ \ x_{\text{next}} = \tfrac43 x @@ moves are dear, so the plan does too little
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a short plan can let an unstable plant run away}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the plan is best for one tick, and wrong for ever

Last-cost technique: if the plan’s cost ends with a last cost V(x) = p x² equal to the cost of the best endless plan, so that p = 1 + 4pρ/(p + ρ), then under the plan V falls by at least x² each tick, and x heads to 0.

p x^2 = \min_u\big(x^2 + \rho u^2 + p(2x + u)^2\big) @@ the best endless plan: one tick, then the best from there
V(x_{\text{next}}) = V(x) - (x^2 + \rho u^2) \le V(x) - x^2 @@ the plan’s move achieves the min
\textstyle\sum_k x_k^2 \le V(x_0) @@ V never below 0: add the falls
\htmlClass{new}{x_k \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a sum of squares with a finite total has terms heading to 0 (22)
  
p = 1 + \tfrac{8p}{p + 2} \Rightarrow p^2 - 7p - 2 = 0,\ \ p = \tfrac{7 + \sqrt{57}}{2} = 7.275 @@ the quadratic formula
u = -\tfrac{2p}{\rho + p}x = -1.569x,\ \ x_{\text{next}} = 0.431x @@ now the plan is stable
\text{descent on } J(u) = 7.275(2 + u)^2 + 2u^2,\ \eta = 0.05:\ \ u = -1.455,\ -1.560,\ -1.568 @@ 28o’s downhill steps from u = 0, for x = 1
\htmlClass{new}{x^2 + J_{\min} = 1 + 6.275 = 7.275 = V(1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ and V(1) − V(0.431) = 7.275 − 1.353 = 5.922 ≥ 1

With limits on u the plan has no formula, and a descent or one of its cousins runs at every tick: slowly enough in a refinery, where a tick can be a minute, and against the clock in any machine whose tick is short. The last cost, or a last condition that the plan must end at rest, is what proofs of around 2000 used to show such controllers stable, by exactly this falling V.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞71.oq.1Can a three-term controller be tuned from data alone, with a guarantee? Tuning by hand needs an engineer, a working plant and time, and the result is only as good as the tests run. Methods that tune from a recorded run exist, and some come with guarantees, but only under assumptions about the plant, that it is straight and does not change, which the data alone cannot confirm. A method that tunes from a short record of an unknown plant and proves the loop it gives will settle, with a stated margin, is sought.
∞71.oq.2Can a system that learns as it runs be controlled with a guarantee? Learned controllers, trained by trial and reward, balance robots and fly drones well in tests, but the encirclement and last-cost proofs need a model, and the learned controller is not one anyone wrote. Bounds that hold while the controller is still learning, without being so cautious that it learns nothing, are an active question with no general answer.
∞71.oq.3How wrong may the model be inside a planning controller before the falling-cost proof fails? The last-cost technique assumes the model is exact. Versions that allow a bounded error in the model exist, but they plan for the worst case and are slow or timid. How to plan with a model known only roughly, fast enough for a car or a drone, and still prove the loop safe, is open.
∞71.oq.4How fast can the plan be solved, with a proof that it was solved in time? A planning controller is only as good as its solution at each tick, and on a small chip the descent must stop after a fixed budget. Bounds on how many steps suffice for a given accuracy exist for straight plants with simple limits; for plants that are not straight, where the cost can have many valleys, there is no such bound, and the controller may act on a plan that is not the best.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Feedback is older than its theory. Governors held steam engines to speed in the nineteenth century, and a paper of 1868 on governors began the mathematics of when such a loop settles, by the roots of its trial equation, a test that works when the equation can be written. In 1922 a study of steering ships set down the rule with three terms: steer by the heading error, by how long it has lasted, and by how fast it is changing. The rule spread through industry in the following decades, with its three dials tuned on site.

The telephone network forced the next step. Amplifiers for long lines distorted the signal, and feeding back a share of the output, an idea of 1927 at the telephone laboratories, cured the distortion but made some amplifiers howl. Nobody could write the trial equation of an amplifier with dozens of parts and delays. The 1932 paper on regeneration gave the test of inf71.3, read from measured curves, and a 1945 book from the same laboratories turned it into the engineer’s methods of margins, and proved the integral limit of inf71.5.

From the 1960s, with the filter of ∞70 and computers aboard, control theory turned to state models and plans that minimise a cost, and the three-term controller was called old fashioned in papers while it went on running nearly every loop in plants. Engineers in refineries found in the late 1970s that planning ahead with a model, and planning again at every tick, handled limits on valves and flows that the three-term rule could not, and model-predictive control spread through the process industries well before theory could say when it was stable.

Around 2000 survey papers set out when a planning controller is stable: with a last cost or a condition that the plan must end at rest, the planned cost falls each tick, as the last-cost technique shows. As chips grew quicker, the same planning moved from slow plants towards fast machines, and the solvers themselves became a field. Critics point out that the guarantees assume a good model, and that tuning a planning controller, with its costs and horizons and limits, is no easier than tuning three dials.

What is argued now is where learning fits. Learned controllers can do things no hand-tuned loop does, and they come without the margins of inf71.4 or the falling account of inf71.6. Some groups build learning on top of the classical loop and keep its proofs; others train end to end and test heavily. The waterbed says a controller cannot be good everywhere, whoever designs it; whether a learned controller knows where it has put its bulge is one way of stating what the argument is about.

The accepted names

The three-term controller is the PID controller (proportional, integral, derivative), with gains K_p, K_i, K_d. The closed-loop technique gives the complementary sensitivity T = L/(1 + L) and the sensitivity function S = 1/(1 + L). The encirclement technique is the Nyquist stability criterion, from the argument principle; the curve L(iω) is the Nyquist plot, and the three-lag margin is the gain margin. The 1868 test was Maxwell’s, made general by the Routh–Hurwitz criterion. The waterbed technique is a case of Bode’s sensitivity integral, the waterbed effect, and the version for unstable plants is due to Freudenberg and Looze (1985). Planning at every tick is model predictive control (MPC), or receding-horizon control; the last cost is the terminal cost, here the solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation, and the falling account is a Lyapunov function.

Who said it first

James Clerk Maxwell wrote on governors in 1868. Nicolas Minorsky set out three-term ship steering in 1922. Harold Black conceived the negative feedback amplifier in 1927 at Bell Labs; Harry Nyquist published the criterion in 1932, and Hendrik Bode the book of 1945. John Ziegler and Nathaniel Nichols gave tuning rules in 1942. Model predictive control in industry was developed by Jacques Richalet and colleagues and by Charles Cutler and Brian Ramaker at Shell in the late 1970s; David Mayne, James Rawlings, Christopher Rao and Pierre Scokaert published the survey of stability and optimality in 2000.
