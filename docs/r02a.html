Arrange Your Series

Rung 1 worked one line at a time. A line held statements side by side, and every move rewrote it. This step lets you write the work out as a series of rows, one under another, and each row is a line. The first rows lay out the givens, a part at a time. Each row after that is the result of a technique on the rows above, and the truth is kept from row to row, so the last row says something the givens already guaranteed. When a row offers options, each option gets rows of its own, set in from the margin, and that is a case. Following a case down its rows shows how that one way things could be affects what you started with. The step goes through rung 1’s joining words and shows what rows do to each. It ends where the rest of the rung begins: cases do most of their work once there are tools to move from one case to another.

Write the givens as rows. Under them, write only what a technique gives from the rows above. When a row offers options, set each option in and follow it down. Come back to the margin only with what every case reached.

2a1A Row Is a Line

Rung 1 kept everything in one line. A long line gets hard to read: its statements run together, and a move made at one end is hard to see from the other. So write the line out as rows, one under another. A row is a line: it holds statements, it meets or fails its operator’s condition, and it is worked by rung 1’s techs. A series is rows read from the top down.

The givens come first, each on a row of its own. Ann is at the bakery or at the library; Ann is not at the library; the bakery is on Elm Street. On rung 1 that was one line holding three statements. Here it is three rows, and each can be pointed to.

A row, once written, stays where it is. It is never moved and never rubbed out, so anyone reading the series later sees the work in the order it was done.

2a2Aspects of the Givens

A given can say several things at once. “Gomez is a puppy” says that Gomez is a dog and that Gomez is young. Unpack it, and each aspect may have a row of its own: Gomez is a dog; Gomez is young. The two rows say together what the one given said. Spreading a given over rows writes out its aspects; it adds nothing the given did not say.

Which aspects to write out is a choice. Write out the ones the work will lean on. The rest stay packed in the given’s row, where they can still be unpacked later.

2a3Results Keep the Truth

Under the givens, a row may be written only as the result of a technique on rows above it. Keep a part of an and, join two rows, add an option, carry an if through, unpack, swap equals: every tech of rung 1 works across rows as it worked inside a line. A line keeps the truth; the technique changes the arrangement and stays true. If the rows it used are true, so is the row it writes, and so the truth runs down the series from the givens, row by row.

The last row is the series’ output: a new arrangement of what the givens said, laid out to answer a question. Gomez is a puppy; if Gomez is young, Gomez needs his shots. Unpack the first row: Gomez is young. Carry the if through: Gomez needs his shots. That output was in the givens all along. The series brought it out where it can be seen.

2a4An and Splits; Two Rows Join

An and in a row may be split into two rows, one for each part: keep left, keep right. Two rows may be joined into one and. A stack of rows says all of its rows, as one row of ands would. So rows and ∧ are two layouts of one claim, and that is why the givens may be spread out without changing what they say.

2a5Cases Are Set In

An or does not split like an and. Its options are not all so. One of them is, and the row does not say which. So each option gets rows of its own, set in from the margin under the or. A case is one option of an or, written as a set-in row, with the rows that work it set in beneath it.

Inside a case, the rows may use the case’s own rows and any row above it at the margin: the givens stand in every case. They may not use the rows of another case. What one case writes belongs to that one way things could be, and to no other. On rung 1 the options were worked side by side inside one line. Here each has room of its own.

2a6What a Case Does to the Givens

Inside a case, the case row is in hand like any other row, so the givens can answer it. An if whose condition is the case can be carried through, and a given that denies the case clashes with it.

Gomez is a puppy or a kitten. If Gomez is a puppy, Gomez chews shoes. If Gomez is a kitten, Gomez climbs curtains. In the puppy case, the first if fires: Gomez chews shoes. In the kitten case, the second fires: Gomez climbs curtains. Each case shows what the givens come to, that one way. Neither row comes back to the margin. The shoes belong to the puppy case and the curtains to the kitten case.

Following a case down its rows shows how one way things could be affects what you started with. That is the reason to write cases at all.

2a7A Case That Cannot Stand

A case row can clash with a row above it. Then the case’s rows equal ⊥: that way things could be cannot stand with the givens. A case that reaches ⊥ is closed. Ann is at the bakery or the library, and Ann is not at the library. In the library case, the case row clashes with the given denial, and the case is closed.

A closed case shows that the givens leave that way no room. It does not write a denial at the margin. Here the denial was given, but a closed case never writes one on its own. Rows can show that a way cannot stand; saying so as an output of its own comes later. Whether the givens themselves can stand together is 2a11’s question.

2a8Back to the Margin

When every case of an or reaches the same row, that row may be written again at the margin. One of the cases is so, because the or-row says so, and each case reached the row. So the row stands whichever way things are. A closed case reaches any row at all, by everything permitted. This is 1c’s reasoning by cases, laid out so that each case can be seen.

Ann is at the bakery or the library. Ann is not at the library. If Ann is at the bakery, Ann is on Elm Street. The bakery case reaches “Ann is on Elm Street” by the if. The library case is closed, so it reaches the same row too. Back at the margin: Ann is on Elm Street.

2a9What Rows Do to the Joining Words

Rows give each joining word of rung 1 a shape on the page.

  • ∧ is a stack: its parts are rows, one under another (2a4).
  • ∨ is a fork: its options are cases, set in side by side (2a5).
  • ¬ is a wall: a row that a case can run into, and close on (2a7).
  • ⇒ is a trigger: a row that fires in any row or case below it that meets its condition (2a6).
  • = is a key: a row that lets a swap be made in any row below it, at the margin or in a case.

Each mark still makes a statement, and each row still meets or fails its operator’s condition, as 1a4 said. What rows change is where the work can be seen. A long line’s work is spread out so that every move has a row, and every way things could be has its case. The marks mean what they meant on rung 1; rows give each of them a place to work.

2a10Moving Between Cases

With two cases, writing each out is easy. A shelter card that reads “a puppy, a kitten, a parrot, a rabbit, or any of forty breeds of dog” makes it slow. With every member of a collection, or every counting number, the cases never end, and no series can write them all.

Cases do most of their work once there are tools to move from one case to another: a way to say something of any case at once, and a way to pick out the cases that pass a test. The rest of this rung builds those tools. 2b speaks of every case at once. 2d to 2g gather the cases into sets, and sort them.

2a11Cases That Can Stand Together

Snow or rain tonight, and not snow. Can both rows be so at once? Yes: in the row where it rains. Givens that have a row where every one of them is so can stand together. Givens with no such row equal ⊥ in every row, and any argument from them is closed before it starts, in 2a7’s sense.

Showing “can stand together” takes one row: name it, and check each given in it. Showing “cannot” takes every row. 1e’s table lists them all, four for two letters, eight for three, so the check ends. When rung 2 lets things in, a row becomes a world, and one world does the same work: 2b20.

Why it matters here. A closed case shows that the givens leave that way no room. Before any case is set in there is the opposite worry: givens that cannot stand together close every case, and rows under them reach anything at all. So a set of givens worth arguing from is one that can stand together, and one row is the cheapest proof that it can.

2a12Running It Backwards

Wanting a row at the margin, look first for a technique that writes it from the rows you have. If none does, look for an or-row. Set in each of its options, and ask of each case whether it can reach the row wanted. A case that clashes with a row above reaches it for free. If every case reaches it, the row comes back to the margin. If one case sticks, that or-row was not enough, and another or-row, or another given, is needed.

Having an or-row and wanting anything at all, ask: can each case reach it?

Accepted names

A series of rows, each the result of rows above it, has the accepted name derivation; its last row is the derivation’s conclusion. A set-in case has the accepted name subderivation. Reasoning by cases is also called proof by cases, or or-elimination.

Who said it first

Laying an argument out in rows, with its cases set in, is the layout that Stanisław Jaśkowski and Gerhard Gentzen each published in 1934. Frederic Fitch’s textbook of 1952 drew the set-in rows with a bar down their left side, and that drawing is still the commonest one in classrooms.
