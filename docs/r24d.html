24d · proof

Tame rules fix the future

Here is the main result of the rung. Take a rule that gives the slope from the place, and suppose it is tame: moving the place a little changes the slope by at most a fixed multiple of the move. Then from any start there is a solution, and only one, at least for a while. The proof is the repeated integration of twenty-four b, seen as a map that shrinks: over a short enough stretch, feeding two guesses through the rule brings them closer by a fixed fraction, so the last step's technique hands over exactly one solution. A second result says how far apart two solutions can drift when their starts differ: no faster than a growth function. Together they say a tame rate fixes the future from the present.

If the slope changes no faster than in proportion to a change of place, a rule with a start has exactly one solution near the start. Repeated integration is a map that shrinks, and its fixed point is the solution. Two solutions with nearby starts drift apart no faster than a growth function.

24d1Tame rules

From here on, F(x, y) is tame on a rectangle R = [x₀ − α, x₀ + α] × [y₀ − β, y₀ + β] when F is continuous on R and there is one number L with |F(x, y) − F(x, z)| ≤ L|y − z| for all (x, y) and (x, z) in R. The number L is its constant.

F(x, y) - F(x, z) = \partial_y F(x, c)\,(y - z) @@ the mean value technique (14c), in y with x held still
|\partial_y F| \le L \text{ on } R \Rightarrow |F(x, y) - F(x, z)| \le L|y - z| @@ the slope in y has a ceiling
\htmlClass{new}{y' = x^2 + y^2 \text{ is tame on every rectangle}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope of x² + y² in y is 2y, at most 2(|y₀| + β) on R

Here ∂_y F(x, c) is the slope of F in y alone, with x held still. Rung 28 builds such slopes in full. Here they are only the slope of one-input functions, y ↦ F(x, y).

Not every rule is tame. √|y| has slopes that pass every ceiling near y = 0, and 24e shows the price.

24d2One solution, for a while

Existence technique: let F be tame on R with constant L, and |F| ≤ M on R. Choose h > 0 with h ≤ α, hM ≤ β and hL < 1. Then y′ = F(x, y), y(x₀) = y₀ has exactly one solution on [x₀ − h, x₀ + h].

D = \{\text{continuous } y \text{ on } I = [x_0 - h, x_0 + h] \text{ with } |y - y_0| \le b\} @@ guesses that stay in the rectangle
(Ty)(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x} F(t, y(t))\,dt @@ repeated integration, as a map (24b2)
|(Ty)(x) - y_0| \le |x - x_0|\,M \le hM \le b @@ the area of something at most M in size: T sends D into D
|(Ty)(x) - (Tz)(x)| \le \Big|\int_{x_0}^{x} |F(t, y) - F(t, z)|\,dt\Big| \le hL\,\|y - z\| @@ tame: each gap at most L‖y − z‖
\|Ty - Tz\| \le hL\,\|y - z\|,\ hL < 1 @@ T shrinks, with k = hL
\text{exactly one } y \in D \text{ with } Ty = y @@ the shrinking-map technique (24c3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{exactly one solution on } I} \quad \blacksquare @@ fixed points of T are the solutions (area form, 24b1); a solution on I stays in D, since |y − y₀| ≤ hM ≤ β

The proof is a recipe as well as a guarantee. Repeated integration from the flat line at y₀ settles everywhere at once on I, and after n rounds it is within (hL)ⁿ/(1 − hL) times the first step of the solution.

24d3Two starts drift apart slowly

Drift technique: if y and z solve the same tame rule on an interval from x₀, with constant L, then |y(x) − z(x)| ≤ |y(x₀) − z(x₀)|·e^(L|x − x₀|). In particular, the same start gives the same solution, on the whole interval where both exist.

u(x) = |y(x) - z(x)| \le u(x_0) + L\int_{x_0}^{x} u(t)\,dt @@ subtract the area forms (24b1); tame inside the area
U(x) = \int_{x_0}^{x} u,\ \ U' = u \le u(x_0) + LU @@ the slope of an area is the height (16b)
\big(U e^{-L(x-x_0)}\big)' = (U' - LU)e^{-L(x-x_0)} \le u(x_0)e^{-L(x-x_0)} @@ the product rule, as in 24a3
U e^{-L(x-x_0)} \le \tfrac{u(x_0)}{L}\big(1 - e^{-L(x-x_0)}\big) @@ compare areas from x₀, where both sides start at 0 (14e)
u \le u(x_0) + LU \le u(x_0)e^{L(x-x_0)} @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{|y(x) - z(x)| \le |y(x_0) - z(x_0)|\,e^{L(x - x_0)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and for x ≤ x₀ the same with x₀ − x

The bound is honest and grim. Two starts a millionth apart may drift apart like e^(Lx), so after a stretch of 14/L they can be a million times further apart. A tame rate fixes the future, but small errors in the present grow, and a rule with a large L forgets its start's digits fast. 24k and the weather annex return to this.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A tame rule is Lipschitz continuous in y. The existence technique is the Picard–Lindelöf theorem (or the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem); the drift technique is Grönwall's inequality, and with it the uniqueness half and continuous dependence on initial conditions.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved existence for rules with bounded slopes in the 1820s, by stepping. Rudolf Lipschitz weakened the condition in 1876. Émile Picard (1890) and Ernst Lindelöf (1894) gave the proof by repeated integration, and Thomas Grönwall proved his inequality in 1919.
