Can a Quantum Search Beat Looking Everywhere?

Can a quantum search beat looking everywhere? Hunting for one marked box among a million, with no clue where it is, you must open boxes one by one: half a million on average. No cleverness helps, because nothing about one box tells you about another. Yet a quantum computer can find it in under a thousand steps. This page counts the ordinary search, writes the quantum state as an arrow in one plane with 41·phys’s qubits, makes a turn from two flips as 20e did, counts the turns needed with 21’s radians, and finds why turning too far overshoots.

Put every box into the state at once, an arrow tilted a tiny angle θ ≈ 1/√n from the unmarked direction toward the marked one. Two flips, one across the unmarked direction and one across the start, make a turn by 2θ toward the marked box. After about (π/4)√n turns the arrow points at the marked box, and reading it gives that box. Turn further and it swings past and away.

41comp21n Boxes, One Marked: Checking Takes About n/2 Tries

Mike works on search problems. A black box answers one question: given a number from 1 to n, is it the marked one? With no clue where the mark is, open boxes in any order.

\text{average tries} = \dfrac{1 + 2 + \cdots + n}{n} = \dfrac{n + 1}{2} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{n = 10^6:\ \text{about } 500\,000 \text{ tries}} @@ and no method does better on average

41comp22The State as an Arrow in One Plane

A quantum computer holds a state that is an arrow of size one, with a part for each of the n boxes (41·phys). Start with all parts equal, 1/√n each. Only two directions matter: m, the marked box, and u, the even spread over all the unmarked ones. The state stays in the plane of m and u throughout.

s = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\,m + \sqrt{\tfrac{n - 1}{n}}\,u @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\sin\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt n}:\ \ \theta \approx \dfrac{1}{\sqrt n} \text{ for large } n} @@ the tilt from u toward m

Reading the state picks a box with chance equal to its part squared. At the start, the marked box is picked with chance 1/n: no better than guessing.

41comp23Two Flips Make a Turn

One try of the black box, done on the whole state at once, flips the sign of the marked part: a flip across u. A second step, built from the start state, flips across s. Two flips across lines at angle θ make a turn by 2θ (20e).

\text{flip across } u, \text{ then across } s:\ \text{turn by } 2\theta @@ two flips (20e)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after } k \text{ rounds: tilt} = (2k + 1)\,\theta} @@ each round costs one try

Two-flip search technique: flip the state across the unmarked direction with one try, then across the start; each round turns it 2θ toward the marked box, so the marked part grows round by round.

41comp24About √n Turns Reach the Marked Box

(2k + 1)\theta \approx \dfrac{\pi}{2} @@ pointing at m: a quarter turn in radians (21j)
k \approx \dfrac{\pi}{4\theta} \approx \dfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt n @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{n = 10^6:\ k \approx 785 \text{ tries, against } 500\,000} @@ a square root of the work

A quantum search finds one marked box among n in about (π/4)√n tries, where looking needs about n/2; for a million boxes, under a thousand against half a million. For four boxes, θ = 30°, and one round turns the state from 30° to 90°: exactly onto the marked box. Small quantum computers run this four-box search and pick the marked box in nearly every run, as the turn predicts, the misses coming from noise in the machine.

41comp25Turning Too Far Overshoots

The rounds do not stop by themselves. Each turns the state by the same 2θ, past the marked direction and on.

P(k) = \sin^2\!\big((2k + 1)\theta\big) @@ 
n = 10^6:\ k = 785:\ P \approx 1;\ \ k = 1\,570:\ P \approx 0 @@ twice as many rounds: back to nothing
\htmlClass{new}{\text{stop at the right round, or the gain swings away}} @@

Quantum search must be stopped at the right count; more rounds swing the state past the marked box and lose what was gained. And the square root is the best any quantum search can do: no method finds an unmarked box in fewer than about √n tries, so quantum computers speed up blind search, but do not make it free.

Accepted names

This is Grover’s algorithm; the black box is an oracle, the second flip the diffusion operator, and growing the marked part amplitude amplification. The square-root gain is a quadratic speedup.

Who said it first

Lov Grover found the search in 1996; Charles Bennett, Ethan Bernstein, Gilles Brassard and Umesh Vazirani proved in 1997 that no quantum search beats √n (not yet revisited for the dates).
