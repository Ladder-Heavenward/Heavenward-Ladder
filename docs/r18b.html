18b · proof

The Triangle Inequality in the Plane

Rung 8’s triangle technique took its name from geometry and promised the geometry later: on the line, the size of a sum is never more than the sum of the sizes. This step proves the plane's version. Going from one point to another by way of a third is never shorter than going straight, and it is exactly as short only when the third lies on the way. The proof needs one inequality about the dot product, found by asking when a square of rung 8 can be zero. With it, “straight” stops being a word about pictures: the segment between two points is the set of points that cost nothing extra.

The size of the dot of two arrows is never more than their lengths multiplied, with equality only when one is a multiple of the other. So a detour is never shorter than the straight way, and it is equal exactly when the stop lies on the segment.

18b1How Large a Dot Product Can Be

Dot-bound technique: for all displacements u and v, |u · v| ≤ |u| |v|. The two sides are equal exactly when one of u, v is a multiple of the other.

v = 0:\ \ |u \cdot 0| = 0 = |u|\,|0| @@ and 0 = 0 · u, a multiple: the claim is true
v \ne 0:\ \ q(t) = |u - tv|^2 \ge 0 \text{ for every real } t @@ a length squared (square-never-negative)
q(t) = |u|^2 - 2t\,(u \cdot v) + t^2|v|^2 @@ right-triangle with −tv; the dot technique
t_0 = \tfrac{u \cdot v}{|v|^2}:\ \ q(t_0) = |u|^2 - \tfrac{(u \cdot v)^2}{|v|^2} \ge 0 @@ completing the square in t (8i); |v|² > 0 by distance-laws
(u \cdot v)^2 \le |u|^2|v|^2 \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{|u \cdot v| \le |u|\,|v|} @@ multiply by |v|²; roots keep order (powers-keep-order)
\text{equality} \iff q(t_0) = 0 \iff u = t_0 v @@ distance-laws: a length is 0 only for 0; and if u = sv, both sides are |s| |v|² \quad \blacksquare

18b2The Triangle Inequality

Triangle technique in the plane: for all displacements u and v, |u + v| ≤ |u| + |v|. So for all points P, Q, R, d(P, R) ≤ d(P, Q) + d(Q, R).

|u + v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 + 2\,u \cdot v @@ right-triangle
\le |u|^2 + |v|^2 + 2|u|\,|v| = (|u| + |v|)^2 @@ dot-bound: u · v ≤ |u · v| ≤ |u| |v|; square-of-a-sum
|u + v| \le |u| + |v| @@ both are not negative, so the roots keep the order (powers-keep-order)
u = Q - P,\ v = R - Q:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{d(P, R) \le d(P, Q) + d(Q, R)} \quad \blacksquare @@ u + v = R − P (18a)

With v on one axis and u on the same axis, this is rung 8's triangle, so triangle is the special case where the three points lie on a row. Now the name is earned, and the flag 8h opened for it is closed.

18b3When the Detour Costs Nothing

From here on, for points P and R, the set {P + t(R − P) : 0 ≤ t ≤ 1} is the segment from P to R, written PR. PR is a name, for one set of points. Its accepted name is a line segment, and P and R are its endpoints. Three points are collinear when one lies on the segment between the other two; three points that are not collinear make a triangle.

Equality-on-the-segment technique: d(P, R) = d(P, Q) + d(Q, R) exactly when Q is on the segment PR.

Q \in PR \Rightarrow \text{equality} @@ part-of-the-way: t · d + (1 − t) · d = d
\text{equality} \Rightarrow u \cdot v = |u|\,|v| @@ triangle in the plane's second line, read as an equality
u = 0:\ Q = P,\ \ t = 0 @@ on the segment
u \ne 0:\ v = su,\ s \ge 0 @@ dot-bound's equality case; s ≥ 0 since u · v = s|u|² ≥ 0
R - P = u + v = (1 + s)u @@ so Q − P = u = (R − P)/(1 + s)
\htmlClass{new}{Q = P + t(R - P),\ \ t = \tfrac{1}{1 + s} \in (0, 1]} \quad \blacksquare @@ on the segment

So the segment PR is exactly the set of points through which the trip from P to R costs no more than going straight. A broken path P, Q₁, …, Qₙ, R is at least d(P, R) long, by triangle in the plane and induction on n. Rung 8 said the line ℝ was a straight line in name only; this is what the name means.

(1, 2), (4, 6), (10, 14):\ \ 5 + 10 = 15 @@ collinear: (4, 6) is ⅓ of the way
(0, 0), (3, 2), (6, 8):\ \ \sqrt{13} + \sqrt{45} \approx 10.31 \gt 10 @@ a triangle; the detour costs 0.31
\text{sides } 4 \text{ and } 9:\ \ 5 \lt \text{third side} \lt 13 @@ triangle in the plane three ways round, strict for a triangle

18b4What Even a Donkey Knows

A commentator on the Elements, in the 5th century, reports that the Epicureans mocked the Elements' proof that two sides of a triangle together are longer than the third, saying the thing is plain even to a donkey, which goes straight to its hay and not round two sides. He answers that what is plain to the senses still needs a reason to be known.

Who said it first

The commentator of 18b4 and 18b.4.5 is Proclus, whose Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements, in the 5th century, discusses the twentieth proposition of Book I, Euclid's proof that two sides of a triangle together are longer than the third.

Proclus reports that the Epicureans mocked Euclid's proof that two sides of a triangle together are longer than the third, saying the thing is plain even to a donkey, which goes straight to its hay and not round two sides. He answers that what is plain to the senses still needs a reason to be known.

Proclus, Commentary on the First Book of Euclid's Elements, on I.20 (5th century); paraphrase
