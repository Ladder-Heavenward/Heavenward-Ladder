Where Does the Carbon Go?

Where does the carbon go? Of the carbon dioxide put into the air by burning, some is gone within a few years, taken up by the sea and by plants. Yet part of it lingers for centuries. How can one gas leave the air at two such different paces? This page treats the air, the surface sea and the deep sea as boxes that trade carbon, as 27’s systems of rate statements; finds the steady state where every box balances, as 34h promises; splits a pulse into parts that fade at different rates with 27f; finds the slow part; and asks whether the steady state holds.

Each box sends carbon to its neighbours at a fixed share of what it holds each year. That makes a linear system x′ = Mx. Its stretching numbers are the rates: one is 0, for the carbon that is kept in total, and the others are negative, each a pace of fading. A pulse into the air is a sum of these fading shapes; the slowest sets how long part of it stays.

34chem1Air, Ocean and Land as Boxes That Trade

Claire models carbon for a climate lab. In a simple model she uses three boxes: the air, A; the surface sea, S; and the deep sea, D. Each sends carbon to its neighbours at a fixed share per year of what it holds.

From here on, on this page, the boxes are measured by how much extra carbon each can take up relative to the air: the surface water’s chemistry lets it hold about as much extra as the air above it, and the deep sea about ten times that. The shares are a teaching model, set to give the real paces roughly.

A\prime = -0.1A + 0.1S @@ air to sea, sea to air
S\prime = 0.1A - 0.12S + 0.002D @@ the surface also sinks 0.02 of its carbon a year
D\prime = 0.02S - 0.002D @@ the deep sea returns slowly
\htmlClass{new}{x\prime = Mx,\ \ x = (A, S, D)} @@ a system of rate statements (27·cb24)

34chem2A Steady State Where Every Box Balances

Each column of M adds to 0: what leaves one box arrives in another, so the total is kept. Shares of a fixed total stay in a closed triangle, and the trading carries the triangle into itself; so some share stays put (34h). Set every rate to 0:

0.1A = 0.1S,\ \ 0.02S = 0.002D @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{A : S : D = 1 : 1 : 10} @@ the air holds 1/12 of any extra, at rest

Box-balance technique: write each box’s carbon rate as what flows in minus what flows out, as a share of what each holds; set them all to 0 for the steady state, and read the paces of change from the system’s stretching numbers.

34chem3A Pulse Spreads and Fades at Several Rates

The stretching numbers of M (27f) are the rates at which each shape of the system fades. One is 0, for the kept total. The other two come from M’s polynomial.

\lambda\,(\lambda^2 + 0.222\lambda + 0.0024) = 0 @@ its trace and its two-by-two corners
\lambda = 0,\ \ -0.211,\ \ -0.0114 \text{ per year} @@ 
\text{a pulse into the air: } A(t) = 0.083 + 0.45\,e^{-0.211t} + 0.47\,e^{-0.0114t} @@ fitted to the start: A = 1, A′ = −0.1, A″ = 0.02

Nearly half of a pulse leaves the air in about five years, into the surface sea; nearly half leaves only as the surface sea passes it to the deep, over about ninety years; and a twelfth stays until slower processes, over thousands of years, take it. Claire runs her model against the measured rise of carbon dioxide in the air since measurements began, feeding in each year’s burning, and it follows the measured curve, as the boxes say.

34chem5Is the Steady State Stable?

Every stretching number except the 0 is negative. So any upset fades back to the steady shares, and the only lasting change is to the total.

\text{all rates but one} < 0 @@ every shape of an upset fades
\htmlClass{new}{\text{added carbon spreads to the shares } 1:1:10 \text{ and stays}} @@ the total is kept

The boxes return to their steady shares after any upset, but the added total is never removed by trading alone; only burial in rock, far slower, does that. That is why a ton of carbon burned today raises the air’s share for longer than any one human life.

Accepted names

This is a box model of the carbon cycle; the share of a pulse left in the air is its airborne fraction, and fits of several fading rates are like the Bern carbon cycle model. That the surface sea holds less extra than its size suggests is set by the Revelle factor.

Who said it first

Roger Revelle and Hans Suess showed in 1957 that the sea takes up added carbon dioxide more slowly than had been thought (not yet revisited for the date).
