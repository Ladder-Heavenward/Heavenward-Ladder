30g · proof

Averages as integrals

A number that depends on chance, like the total of two dice or tomorrow’s rainfall, is a function on the chance space: for each outcome, it gives a number. Its average is its total over the chance space, with chance as the size. Rung five’s statistics annex found averages by weighting each value by its chance. That is the step-total of this rung, and now it works for every sized function, not only those with a few values. From the average come the spread, which measures how far the values stray from it, and the co-spread of two such numbers. Averages always add. Spreads add when the parts are independent, and then they add the way the squares of the two short sides of a right triangle add.

A chance variable is a sized function on a chance space, and its average is its total. Averages add; independent averages multiply; and the spreads of independent parts add.

30g1A number that depends on chance

Stands on 30f1, 30c, 30d1.

From here on, a chance variable on a chance space is a function X from Ω to the reals whose height sets {X > t} are events. Its accepted name is random variable. P(X ∈ A) is the chance of the event that X lands in A.

From here on, its average is its total over Ω with chance as the size:

E X = ∫ X dP,

when ∫|X| dP is finite. Its accepted names are expected value, expectation and mean, often written μ.

X = x_i \text{ on } \{X = x_i\},\ \ i = 1, \ldots, n @@ a step function on Ω (30c1), with signs split as in 30c3
\htmlClass{new}{E\,X = x_1 P(X = x_1) + \cdots + x_n P(X = x_n)} @@ step-total: value times size
X = \text{one fair die}:\ \ E\,X = (1 + 2 + \cdots + 6)/6 = 3.5 @@ 5·stat’s weighted average

Average-adds technique: E(aX + bY) = a E X + b E Y, and X ≤ Y gives E X ≤ E Y. This is the total adding and scaling (30d1), with signs split as in 30c3. So the total of two dice averages 7, with no need to list the 36 outcomes.

30g2The spread

Stands on 30g1.

From here on, for X with average μ, its spread is Var X = E[(X − μ)²], and its spread width is σ = √Var X. For two chance variables X and Y with averages μ and ν, their co-spread is Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ)(Y − ν)]. The accepted names are variance, standard deviation and covariance.

Spread-of-a-sum technique: Var X = E X² − μ², Var(aX + b) = a² Var X, and Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y).

(X - \mu)^2 = X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2 @@ two-term square (5i)
\text{Var}\,X = E\,X^2 - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 = E\,X^2 - \mu^2 @@ average-adds (30g1); E of a constant is that constant
aX + b - (a\mu + b) = a(X - \mu) \Rightarrow \text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\,\text{Var}\,X @@ a shift does not spread
\big((X + Y) - (\mu + \nu)\big)^2 = (X - \mu)^2 + (Y - \nu)^2 + 2(X - \mu)(Y - \nu) @@ two-term square
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}\,X + \text{Var}\,Y + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ average-adds

One fair die: E X² = 91/6, so Var X = 91/6 − 49/4 = 35/12, and σ ≈ 1.71.

30g3Independent parts

Stands on 30g1, 30g2, 30f2, 30d1.

From here on, chance variables X and Y are independent when the events {X ∈ A} and {Y ∈ B} are independent for every pair of intervals A and B. Many chance variables are independent when every finite selection of such events multiplies.

Independent-averages-multiply technique: if X and Y are independent with finite averages, E(XY) = E X · E Y.

X = x_i \text{ on } \{X = x_i\},\ \ Y = y_j \text{ on } \{Y = y_j\} @@ first, finitely many values
E(XY) = \sum_{i, j} x_i y_j\,P(X = x_i,\ Y = y_j) = \sum_{i, j} x_i y_j\,P(X = x_i)P(Y = y_j) @@ step-total on the pieces; independent
= \big(\textstyle\sum_i x_i P(X = x_i)\big)\big(\sum_j y_j P(Y = y_j)\big) = E\,X \cdot E\,Y @@ the double sum splits
X, Y \ge 0:\ \ s_n(X) \uparrow X,\ \ s_n(Y) \uparrow Y @@ total-from-below (30c3): each sₙ(X) uses only sets {X ∈ interval}
E(s_n(X)\,s_n(Y)) = E\,s_n(X) \cdot E\,s_n(Y) \to E\,X \cdot E\,Y @@ rising-limit (30d1) on each side
\htmlClass{new}{E(XY) = E\,X \cdot E\,Y} \quad \blacksquare @@ for signs, split X = X⁺ − X⁻ and Y likewise

Independent-spreads-add technique: if X and Y are independent, Cov(X, Y) = 0, so Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y. Cov(X, Y) = E(XY) − μν = 0. And n independent copies of X with spread σ² have Var(X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ) = nσ²: their average has spread σ²/n and width σ/√n.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting, and the triangle

4j counted, and 21h split a motion into parts along directions you choose. Spreads add the same way. The co-spread is a measuring stick for chance variables, as 27h’s were for arrows: ⟨X, Y⟩ = Cov(X, Y), and Var X is the square of X’s length.

The spreads of independent parts add, so their widths combine like the two short sides of a right triangle.

Independent parts are perpendicular: their co-spread is 0. So their widths add like the sides of a right triangle. Two independent errors of widths 3 g and 4 g make an error of width 5 g, not 7 g.

A bag of flour is weighed on a scale with width 3 g, and the scale’s own zero is set with width 4 g, independently. How wide is the error of the reading? Of ten bags weighed one after another and added?

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A chance variable is a random variable, its average the expected value or mean E[X] = μ, its spread the variance Var(X) = σ², its width the standard deviation σ, and the co-spread the covariance Cov(X, Y). Average-adds is linearity of expectation. Co-spread over the product of the widths is the correlation coefficient, which lies between −1 and 1 by 27h’s measuring-stick inequality, accepted name Cauchy–Schwarz. Uncorrelated means co-spread 0, which is weaker than independent.

Who said it first

Christiaan Huygens wrote the first book on expected values in 1657. Abraham de Moivre and Pierre-Simon Laplace used spreads in the eighteenth century, and Carl Friedrich Gauss built his method of least squares on them in 1809. Ronald Fisher named the variance in 1918. Treating the expected value as a Lebesgue integral is Kolmogorov’s, in 1933.

A proof that is not allowed

“A die averages 3.5, so a roll of 3.5 is the most likely.” Not allowed: the average is a total over the chance space, and it need not be a value X ever takes. No face shows 3.5.
