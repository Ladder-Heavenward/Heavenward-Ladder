26i · technique

e^(iθ) by Hand

Rung twenty one proved the sum formulas for the cosine and the sine by turning the plane, and proved the powers of a turn by induction, one power at a time. Rung twenty six showed that a turn is the growth function fed an imaginary number, and that the growth function still turns adding into multiplying. With those two facts in hand, the old formulas stop being things to remember. Multiply out one complex number, and read off its floor part and its upright part: two formulas fall out at once. Raise a turn to a power, and the formulas for every multiple of an angle fall out the same way. Run it backwards, writing the cosine as half of a turn plus its flip, and every power of the cosine or the sine turns into a plain sum of cosines and sines, ready to be integrated.

Find one complex number two ways and compare the parts: the floor parts agree, and the upright parts agree. A turn raised to a power gives the formulas for multiple angles. The cosine is half a turn plus its flip, so its powers become sums.

26i1The Sum Formulas in One Line

21d proved the sum technique by carrying arcs round the circle with a turn. Here is the same result again, with nothing to carry and nothing to remember.

Part-reading technique: to prove a fact about cos and sin, find one complex number two ways, each written as floor part plus i times upright part; then the floor parts agree, and the upright parts agree. Two numbers of the plane are equal exactly when their floor parts and their upright parts are (25a).

e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = e^{ia}\,e^{ib} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b1)
= (\cos \alpha + i\sin \alpha)(\cos \beta + i\sin \beta) @@ the turn-growth technique (26a3), twice
= (\cos \alpha\cos \beta - \sin \alpha\sin \beta) + i(\sin \alpha\cos \beta + \cos \alpha\sin \beta) @@ share out; i² = −1
e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = \cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta) @@ the turn-growth technique at α + β
\htmlClass{new}{\cos(\alpha+\beta) = \cos \alpha\cos \beta - \sin \alpha\sin \beta,\ \ \sin(\alpha+\beta) = \sin \alpha\cos \beta + \cos \alpha\sin \beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the part-reading technique: floor parts, then upright parts
  
\tfrac{5\pi}{12} = \tfrac{\pi}{4} + \tfrac{\pi}{6} @@ 75° as a sum of angles whose cos and sin are known (21b)
\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{12} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac12 @@ the floor part of the line above
\htmlClass{new}{\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{12} = \tfrac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.2588} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: cos 75° = 0.258819 to six places

21d needed one argument for the cosine and another for the sine. Here one multiplication gives both, because one complex number holds both. The difference formulas of 21e come the same way from e^(i(α − β)) = e^(iα) e^(−iβ), with e^(−iβ) = cos β − i sin β by reversing an angle (21e1).

26i2Powers of a Turn in One Line

21·cb20 proved that the n-th power of cos θ + i sin θ is cos nθ + i sin nθ, by the multiply-and-add technique and induction, one power at a time. The plane-adding technique does it in one line, and for negative powers too.

Many-angle technique: for every whole n, (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos nθ + i sin nθ; so cos nθ and sin nθ are the floor and upright parts of (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ multiplied out by the two-term power.

(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \big(e^{i\theta}\big)^n @@ the turn-growth technique (26a3)
\big(e^{i\theta}\big)^n = e^{in\theta} \text{ for } n \ge 1 @@ the plane-adding technique (26b1), n − 1 times; induction (4c)
\big(e^{i\theta}\big)^{-n} = 1/e^{in\theta} = e^{-in\theta} @@ 1/e^z = e^(−z) (26b2)
\htmlClass{new}{(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta \text{ for every whole } n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turn-growth technique at nθ
  
(c + is)^3 = c^3 + 3c^2(is) + 3c(is)^2 + (is)^3 @@ c = cos θ, s = sin θ; the two-term power (4k)
= (c^3 - 3cs^2) + i(3c^2 s - s^3) @@ i² = −1, i³ = −i
\cos 3\theta = c^3 - 3c(1 - c^2),\ \ \sin 3\theta = 3(1 - s^2)s - s^3 @@ the part-reading technique (26i1); s² = 1 − c² (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta,\ \ \sin 3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta} \quad \blacksquare

The same line finds any power of any number of the plane. 1 + i is √2 e^(iπ/4), so (1 + i)¹⁰ = 2⁵ e^(i10π/4) = 32 e^(iπ/2) e^(2πi) = 32i. Ten multiplications become one.

26i3Powers of cos and sin as Sums

Run the turn-growth technique at θ and at −θ, and add or subtract. The cosine and the sine come out as halves of a turn and its flip.

Power-to-sum technique: cos θ = (e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ))/2 and sin θ = (e^(iθ) − e^(−iθ))/(2j); so every power of cos θ or sin θ, and every product of such powers, is a sum of terms cos kθ and sin kθ, found by multiplying out.

e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta @@ the turn-growth technique at −θ (26a3); cos(−θ) = cos θ, sin(−θ) = −sin θ (21e1)
e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta,\ \ e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta @@ add; subtract
\htmlClass{new}{\cos\theta = \tfrac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2},\ \ \sin\theta = \tfrac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}} \quad \blacksquare
  
\cos^3\theta = \tfrac18\big(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}\big)^3 @@ the power-to-sum technique
= \tfrac18\big(e^{3i\theta} + 3e^{i\theta} + 3e^{-i\theta} + e^{-3i\theta}\big) @@ the two-term power (4k); e^(iθ)e^(−iθ) = 1 (26b1)
= \tfrac18\big(2\cos 3\theta + 6\cos\theta\big) @@ pair each term with its flip: e^(ikθ) + e^(−ikθ) = 2 cos kθ
\htmlClass{new}{\cos^3\theta = \tfrac34\cos\theta + \tfrac14\cos 3\theta} \quad \blacksquare

The terms always come in flipped pairs, so a power of the cosine is a sum of cosines: cos⁴θ = (3 + 4 cos 2θ + cos 4θ)/8. Over a whole turn every cos kθ with k ≠ 0 has area 0, so ∫ from 0 to 2π of cos⁴θ dθ is 2π · 3/8 = 3π/4. 21n halved the arc twice to get there; here it is one multiplication.

For a complex z, cos z and sin z mean their endless sums (23b2), which settle everywhere by the plane-size technique (26a1). Splitting e^(iz) and e^(−iz) into even and odd pieces, as 26a3 did, gives cos z = (e^(iz) + e^(−iz))/2 and sin z = (e^(iz) − e^(−iz))/(2j) for every complex z, not only real θ.

26i4Illegal Moves

(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos^n\theta + i\sin^n\theta\,? @@ the power of a sum is not the sum of the powers (4k); the middle terms carry the turn

The accepted names

The many-angle technique is de Moivre’s formula. The power-to-sum technique gives Euler’s formulas for cosine and sine, cos θ = (e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ))/2 and sin θ = (e^(iθ) − e^(−iθ))/(2j); writing powers of cos and sin as sums of multiple angles is called linearization, or power reduction. cos 3θ = 4cos³θ − 3cos θ is the triple-angle formula, and cos nθ written as a polynomial in cos θ is the Chebyshev polynomial Tₙ.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre used the many-angle pattern in 1722 and in his Miscellanea analytica of 1730, without writing e to an imaginary power. Leonhard Euler wrote cos θ = (e^(iθ) + e^(−iθ))/2 in his Introductio of 1748, and from it read off the sum formulas and the multiple-angle formulas as this step does. Pafnuty Chebyshev studied the polynomials Tₙ from 1854.
