31k · technique

Projections and Least Squares by Hand

A surveyor in Bellingham has a point and a sloping plane of ground, and needs the foot of the drop. A student rolls a ball down a ramp and times it, and needs the curve the timings lie near. A laboratory in Corvallis reads the colour of four known solutions, and needs the line that turns a colour into a strength. Each is a shadow, and the rung has already proved how to find one. This step does them by hand, with the shortcuts that make the hand work short. A plane is best handled through the one vector at right angles to it. A curve with powers in it is a fit whose columns are the powers. A line forced through zero needs only one ratio. And readings that are not all equally good get a product that trusts each one as much as it deserves.

To drop a vector onto a plane, take away its shadow on the vector at right angles to the plane. To fit a polynomial, use the powers as the columns. A line through zero has slope the sum of products over the sum of squares. Readings of different widths are fitted under a product that weights each by one over its spread.

31k1A Shadow on a Plane

A plane through 0 in space is the set of vectors at right angles to one vector n, its normal. Its shadow is easiest found from n alone, with no basis of the plane at all.

Through-the-normal technique: the shadow of f on the plane at right angles to n is f − ((f · n)/|n|²) n, and the distance from f to the plane is |f · n|/|n|. The plane is W, its W⊥ is the line through n (27i2), and f splits into its shadow on that line and the rest (31b1). The rest is the shadow on W.

f = (1, 2, 3),\ \ n = (1, 1, 1),\ \ \text{plane } x + y + z = 0 @@ the plane at right angles to n
f\cdot n = 6,\ \ |n|^2 = 3 @@ the dot
Pf = (1, 2, 3) - 2(1, 1, 1) = (-1, 0, 1) @@ the through-the-normal technique
Pf\cdot n = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0 @@ check: Pf lies in the plane
\htmlClass{new}{\text{distance } = \tfrac{|f\cdot n|}{|n|} = \tfrac{6}{\sqrt3} = 2\sqrt3 \approx 3.46} \quad \blacksquare @@ the length of 2(1, 1, 1)

31k2A Curve Through Timings

Power-columns technique: to fit y ≈ c₀ + c₁x + ⋯ + c_d x^d to n points, use the columns 1, x, …, x^d; then XᵀX has Σᵢ xᵢ^(j+k) in row j and column k, and the best c solves XᵀXc = Xᵀy. It is the miss-at-right-angles technique of 31b3 with powers for columns, and its grid needs only the sums of powers.

t: 0, 1, 2, 3 \text{ s};\ \ s: 0.0,\ 1.1,\ 3.9,\ 9.1 \text{ m} @@ distances rolled, read off a tape; the numbers are a model
\textstyle\sum t^0, \ldots, \sum t^4 = 4,\ 6,\ 14,\ 36,\ 98 @@ the sums of powers
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 & 14 \\ 6 & 14 & 36 \\ 14 & 36 & 98\end{pmatrix}c = \begin{pmatrix} 14.1 \\ 36.2 \\ 98.6\end{pmatrix} @@ Σs, Σts, Σt²s
c = (0.035,\ -0.065,\ 1.025) @@ elimination (27j)
\text{misses: } -0.035,\ 0.105,\ -0.105,\ 0.035 @@ they add to 0, and to 0 against t and t²: right angles (31b4)
\htmlClass{new}{s \approx 1.025\,t^2,\ \ \text{an acceleration of about } 2.05 \text{ m/s}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ s = ½ g′ t² with g′ = 2c₂

The fit put tiny numbers in front of 1 and t. That is a finding, not a demand: the ball started at rest at the zero of the tape. Had the timings been fitted with columns 1 and t only, the shadow would have been the best line, and its misses would have shown a curve, low at the ends and high in the middle.

31k3Lines Through Zero, and Readings That Are Not All as Good

Through-zero technique: the best line y ≈ bx through 0 has b = Σxᵢyᵢ / Σxᵢ². It is the closest-point technique of 31b1 on the line through x, with g = x.

x: 0.5,\ 1,\ 2,\ 4 \text{ mg/L};\ \ y: 0.11,\ 0.20,\ 0.41,\ 0.79 @@ four known strengths and the colour each reads; the numbers are a model
\textstyle\sum x_iy_i = 4.235,\ \ \sum x_i^2 = 21.25 @@ products and squares
b = 4.235/21.25 \approx 0.1993 @@ the through-zero technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a reading of } 0.30 \text{ means about } 0.30/0.1993 \approx 1.51 \text{ mg/L}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line, run backwards

Weighted-shadow technique: with weights wᵢ = 1/σᵢ², the best fit is the shadow under the weighted product, and solves XᵀWXc = XᵀWy, with W the grid of the wᵢ down its middle. Fitting one number to the readings gives Σwᵢyᵢ / Σwᵢ, with spread 1/Σwᵢ.

y: 10.2,\ 9.8,\ 10.9 \text{ cm};\ \ \sigma: 0.1,\ 0.2,\ 0.5 @@ three tools of different quality
w: 100,\ 25,\ 4 @@ one over each spread
\textstyle\sum w_iy_i = 1308.6,\ \ \sum w_i = 129 @@ X is the column of 1s
\htmlClass{new}{\hat y = 1308.6/129 \approx 10.14 \text{ cm},\ \ \text{width } 1/\sqrt{129} \approx 0.088} \quad \blacksquare @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3), on Σwᵢyᵢ/Σwᵢ

The plain average, 10.30, leans towards the worst tool. The weighted one stays near the best, and its width is smaller than the best tool’s alone.

\text{fit } y \approx c_0 + c_1x + c_2x^2 \text{ by three fits of one column each}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> the columns are not at right angles; fit them together, or straighten them first
\text{a line through 0 fits better than a line with a start}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> it is the shadow on a smaller piece: its misses can only be larger

The accepted names

The normal of a plane is its normal vector, and I − nnᵀ/|n|² is the orthogonal projector onto the plane. Fitting with the powers as columns is polynomial regression, and X with columns 1, x, …, x^d is a Vandermonde matrix. A line through 0 is regression through the origin. The weighted-shadow technique is weighted least squares, and Σwᵢyᵢ/Σwᵢ with wᵢ = 1/σᵢ² is the inverse-variance weighted mean. A colour reading against strength is a calibration curve, and in a spectrometer its straightness is the Beer–Lambert law.

Who said it first

Galileo Galilei timed balls rolling down ramps about 1604 and found distances in proportion to the squares of the times. Carl Friedrich Gauss weighted readings by their precision in 1809 and 1823. August Beer stated the law of colour and strength in 1852.
