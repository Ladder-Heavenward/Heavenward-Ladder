Why Do Peas Come Three to One?

Why do peas come three to one? In 1865 a friar in Brno read two papers to the local natural history society about crossing garden peas. He had counted his peas: nearly twenty thousand seeds and plants in the second generation of his seven main experiments alone. The counts kept landing near one small ratio: three to one. Nobody listening saw what it meant. The friar had found that inheritance comes in units that are handed on by chance, and that chance, when the cases are equally likely, is a fraction. This page is his arithmetic. It uses products of sets for the crosses, 4k's product rule to count them, fractions for the chances, and ratios for the counts. Then comes the gap between what chance promises and what a real field of peas delivers.

Each parent hands on one of its two copies of a gene, either equally likely. The offspring's possible pairs are the product of the two parents' sets of copies; count the pairs that show a trait and divide by the number of pairs. That fraction is the chance, and the ratio of counts in a large field comes out near it.

5bio1The Friar's Count

The friar worked in the garden of St Thomas's Abbey in Brno, then Brünn, from 1856 to 1863. He chose peas because their traits come in sharp either-or forms. And a pea flower normally fertilises itself, so a strain can be kept pure. He picked seven traits, each with two forms: smooth or wrinkled seed, yellow or green seed, and so on. First he crossed a pure smooth strain with a pure wrinkled one. Every seed of the first generation was smooth; the wrinkled form had vanished. Then he let those smooth plants fertilise themselves, and in the second generation the wrinkled form came back:

Each ratio in the last column is 5j's ratio scaled to have 1 as its second part, with the first part rounded by 5h's line. So 5474 : 1850 = (5474/1850) : 1. And 100 · 5474 = 1850 · 295 + 1650, with 2 · 1650 ≥ 1850, so 5474/1850 rounds up to 2.96. Seven different traits, seven different sizes of sample, and the ratios crowd near one number.

He put all the experiments together and found, over all of them, a ratio of 2.98 to 1 between the numbers of plants with the first form and with the second. He read it as 3 to 1.

Together: 14 949 plants of the first form and 5010 of the second, and 14 949/5010 ≈ 2.98. The paper was printed in 1866 and then ignored for thirty-four years. In 1900 three botanists found it again while chasing the same ratios. A fourth became its champion in England and saw the English translation into print.

5bio2The Friar's Model, in Plain Words

Here is the explanation, in today's words.

  • A gene is a unit of inheritance for one trait, handed from parents to offspring. Each plant carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent.
  • An allele is one of the forms a gene comes in. For seed surface, write R for the smooth allele and r for the wrinkled one (the letters geneticists use).
  • A plant's genotype for a gene is the pair of alleles it carries: RR, Rr or rr. A pure smooth plant is RR, a pure wrinkled one rr, and a cross of the two, a hybrid, is Rr.
  • Smooth is dominant: a plant with at least one R has smooth seeds. Wrinkled is recessive: it shows only in rr. What a plant shows is its look. Its accepted name is its phenotype.
  • A gamete is a pollen grain or an egg cell. Each gamete carries just one allele of each gene, one of the plant's two copies, either one equally likely. A seed starts when one egg and one pollen grain join, so it gets one copy from each.

That is the whole model. Everything else is counting, and the counting is mathematics already built.

5bio3Chance as a Fraction of Equally Likely Cases

A happening can turn out in exactly n ways, n a number other than 0, and there is reason to take the ways as equally likely: nothing favours one over another. 5·stat gave the chance of an event, a set of k of those ways, as the fraction k/n. This page uses that line.

The chance of an event is also called its probability.

Six cards numbered 1 to 6, shuffled, one drawn: six equally likely ways. The event “an even number” is the set {2, 4, 6}, so its probability is 3/6 = 1/2 (a fraction is a class: any member names it). All the weight rests on “equally likely”, and that is where the slips happen. “It will rain or it won't, so the probability is 1/2” uses two ways that are not equally likely. Choosing ways that really are is the science; the counting is easy. A probability is never below 0 or above 1, since 0 ≤ k ≤ n. An event with no ways has probability 0, and the event of all n ways has probability 1.

A great writer on chance made this the definition. Reduce the happening to cases equally possible, and count those favourable to the event. Its probability is the number favourable divided by the number of all the cases.

5bio4The Cross Grid Is a Product of Sets

Cross two hybrids, Rr with Rr. The egg comes with one of the mother's two copies, the pollen with one of the father's. A possible seed is a pair (egg's copy, pollen's copy). So the set of possible seeds is 2h's product of the two sets of copies. Its number of members is the product of theirs, by 4k's stages-multiply technique. A geneticist drew such products as a grid in the early 1900s, and the grid kept his name. A cross is written with 2h’s product mark: Rr × Rr names the cross of an Rr plant with an Rr plant, since its seeds come from the product of their sets of copies. An arrow after a cross, as in Rr × rr → 41 smooth, reads “gave”. It is 2i's grid of a product: a row for each of the mother's copies, a column for each of the father's, a cell for each pair.

\{R_1, r_1\} \times \{R_2, r_2\} @@ the mother's copies times the father's (low marks keep the copies apart)
(R_1, R_2),\ (R_1, r_2),\ (r_1, R_2),\ (r_1, r_2) @@ the product: 2 · 2 = 4 pairs (stages-multiply), each equally likely
RR,\ Rr,\ Rr,\ rr @@ read as genotypes: (R₁, r₂) and (r₁, R₂) are two different cases with the same alleles
\htmlClass{new}{3} \text{ of the } 4 \text{ have an } R @@ smooth wherever an R is present (dominance)
\text{probability of smooth} = \tfrac34,\quad \text{of wrinkled} = \tfrac14 @@ cases in the event over all cases
\text{smooth} : \text{wrinkled} = \htmlClass{new}{3 : 1} @@ part to part (5j); smooth is 3/4 of the whole, part to whole

The genotypes come out 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. The 2 is the subtle bit. (R₁, r₂) and (r₁, R₂) are different cases: in one the R came from the mother, in the other from the father. Collapse them and you have three genotypes, RR, Rr and rr, but they are not equally likely, and treating them as three equal cases gives the wrong probability 1/3 for RR. The equally likely cases are the pairs of copies, not the letters on them. That is why the low marks are there: an RR parent has two copies, both R, and they are still two objects. The cross grid has the accepted name Punnett square.

The friar tested the 1 : 2 : 1 directly. Among the smooth plants of the second generation, the model says 1 case in 3 is RR, which should breed true, and 2 in 3 are Rr, which should split again. He grew 565 plants from smooth seeds: 193 gave only smooth seeds, and 372 gave both. 372 : 193 ≈ 1.93 : 1, close to the 2 : 1 the model predicts. This is a probability taken inside a smaller set: sift the smooth cases out of the four (2e), and count again among those three.

5bio5Two Genes: A Product of Products

Now follow two genes at once: seed surface (R smooth, r wrinkled) and seed colour (Y yellow, y green; yellow dominant). A plant that is Rr and Yy makes four kinds of gamete, one allele of each gene: the product {R, r} × {Y, y}, written RY, Ry, rY, ry. They are equally likely if the two genes are handed on separately (the friar's second finding). Cross two such plants and the grid has 4 · 4 = 16 equally likely cells, by stages-multiply.

4 \cdot 4 = 16 \text{ cells} @@ four kinds of gamete from each parent (stages-multiply)
\text{smooth: } 3 \cdot 4 = 12;\ \ \text{smooth and yellow: } 3 \cdot 3 = \htmlClass{new}{9} @@ surface and colour are separate stages: stages-multiply again
\begin{aligned}&\text{smooth green } 3,\ \text{wrinkled yellow } 3,\\ &\quad \ \text{wrinkled green } 1\end{aligned} @@ the rest, by the same counting
9 : 3 : 3 : 1 @@ the friar's ratio for two genes
\tfrac{9}{16} = \tfrac{3\cdot 3}{4\cdot 4} = \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac34 @@ 5h's fractions-of-rationals ii: the probability of both is the product of the probabilities

That last line is general. Suppose a happening is two separate stages, with b equally likely ways for the first and d for the second. Stages-multiply gives b · d equally likely ways for the pair. If m of the first stage's ways are in one event and c of the second's in another, the pair is in both in m · c ways. And (mc)/(bd) = (m/b)(c/d). The friar's own count, from 556 seeds: 315 smooth and yellow, 108 smooth and green, 101 wrinkled and yellow, 32 wrinkled and green. The model's expected count for each phenotype is its probability times the total: 556 · 9/16 = 312.75, then 104.25, 104.25 and 34.75. No count can be 312.75, of course, and none of his is exactly on its expectation, but all four are within a few seeds.

5bio6Counts Wobble; the Ratio Is What Chance Promises

Notice what 3 : 1 claims and what it doesn't. It does not say that out of every four peas three are smooth. It says each pea, separately, has probability 3/4 of being smooth, so in a large field the fraction of smooth peas should come out near 3/4. The friar's 5474 : 1850 is 2.96 : 1, not 3 : 1, and that is exactly what chance looks like. Sow 16 peas in the right picture a few times: sometimes 11 smooth, sometimes 14. Sow 7324, the friar's number, and the ratio sits near 3. Yet the count of wrinkled peas usually misses its expected 1831 by more seeds than in a field of 16. Both are true at once: as fields grow, the counts wander further from their expectations, but the fractions wander less. Saying exactly how much less needs tools far up the ladder; the picture lets you watch it.

How close is suspiciously close? A founder of modern statistics argued in 1936 that the friar's numbers, taken together, fit the expectations rather better than chance usually allows. Whether that points to unconscious rounding, an over-helpful assistant, or the critic's own assumptions is still argued over. The test he used belongs to statistics, much higher up; what is here is enough to see why the question makes sense.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth, again

2b named two sizes of truth, 5·stat's mean was paid by no household, and 5·chem's mole showed the crowd made countable in a jug of gas. 3 : 1 lives at the size of the crowd. One pea is smooth or wrinkled; it has no ratio. What one pea has is a chance, 3/4, and a chance is only seen in a crowd.

Don't carry a crowd-truth down to the one, or a one-truth up to the crowd, without naming the bridge.

The bridge between the two sizes is the wobble. 2.96 in one field, 3.15 in another, and the fractions settle as the fields grow. Read a crowd-truth onto one pea and you expect three smooth in every four. Read one pea onto the crowd and one wrinkled seed overturns the friar.

A gardener plants four seeds from the friar's hybrid cross, and all four come up wrinkled. Was the friar wrong?

5bio7Running It Backwards: What Were the Parents?

The friar also ran his model in reverse. Given a smooth plant, is it RR or Rr? Cross it with a wrinkled plant, rr, whose every gamete carries r. If the smooth parent is RR, every seed gets an R and is smooth. If it is Rr, half the cases give an r from it too, and those seeds are wrinkled. So wrinkled seeds near half the time mean Rr; all smooth suggests RR. This is the test cross, and it is division run backwards: from the ratio in the product, recover a factor.

\text{smooth} \times rr \;\to\; 41 \text{ smooth},\ 39 \text{ wrinkled} @@ the counted seeds
\{R, \htmlClass{new}{r}\} \times \{r, r\}:\ \ Rr,\ Rr,\ rr,\ rr @@ an Rr parent: 2 of 4 cases wrinkled, probability 1/2
\{R, R\} \times \{r, r\}:\ \ Rr,\ Rr,\ Rr,\ Rr @@ an RR parent: no wrinkled seed at all
\text{the smooth parent was } \htmlClass{new}{Rr} @@ wrinkled seeds appeared, so it had an r to give; 41 : 39 is near 1 : 1

A test cross can never quite prove RR. If six seeds all come up smooth, an Rr parent may have been unlucky. Six seeds from Rr × rr are six separate stages of 2 equally likely ways each. So there are 2⁶ = 64 equally likely lists of results (4k's counted-functions counts them), and just one of them is all smooth: probability 1/64. Unlikely, not impossible. That is the honest shape of every conclusion drawn from chance.

Who said it first

The friar is Gregor Mendel. The three botanists who found his paper again in 1900 were Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak; William Bateson was its champion in England. The writer on chance is Pierre-Simon Laplace. The grid is Reginald Punnett’s. The critic of 1936 is R. A. Fisher.

Mendel put all the experiments together and found, between the numbers of plants with the first form and with the second, taken over all of them, a ratio of 2.98 to 1, which he read as 3 to 1.

Mendel, Versuche über Pflanzen-Hybriden (1866), after Bateson's revision of the Royal Horticultural Society translation (1901)

Laplace made this the definition: reduce the happening to cases equally possible, count those favourable to the event, and its probability is the number favourable divided by the number of all the cases.

Laplace, Essai philosophique sur les probabilités (1814), the first principle

Accepted names

The cross grid is the Punnett square. The ways a happening can turn out are its outcomes, and the set of all of them is the sample space. The friar’s first finding, that each parent hands on one of its two copies, either equally likely, is the law of segregation; his second, that two genes are handed on separately, is the law of independent assortment. Together they are Mendel’s laws.
