Why Does a Pipe Split Along Its Length?

Why does a pipe split along its length? A frozen water pipe bursts in a long slit, never in a ring, and a sausage on the grill splits the same way. Air tanks for divers, the hull of a deep submarine, a space station module and a space suit are all the same problem: a thin wall holding back a difference in pressure. 16·cb12 found pressure is force per area, and 15·eng added up a pressure over a wall by strips. This page cuts a pipe in half on paper and balances the halves, finds that the wall is pulled twice as hard round the pipe as along it, finds why deep hulls are spheres, and says why tanks are tested with water, not air.

Cut the pipe lengthwise. The pressure pushing one half away is the pressure times the flat cut, 2r per unit length; two strips of wall hold it. So the pull round the wall is pr/t. Along the pipe, the end cap’s push pr² π is held by a ring of wall 2πr t: pr/2t, half as much. A sphere is pulled pr/2t every way, the best a wall can do. A gas stores far more energy than water at the same pressure, so tanks are tested full of water.

16eng21Cutting the Pipe in Half

Terry’s pipe has radius r and a thin wall of thickness t, with pressure p inside more than outside. Imagine it cut lengthwise through its middle, a length L of it.

\text{fill the cut with a flat lid: the water in the half pipe is still} @@ so its pushes balance (16·cb12)
\text{push on the curved half} = \text{push on the lid} = p \cdot 2r \cdot L @@ pressure times the flat area
\text{held by two strips of wall, each } t \cdot L @@ one at each edge of the cut
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma_{\text{round}} \cdot 2tL = p \cdot 2rL \Rightarrow \sigma_{\text{round}} = \dfrac{pr}{t}} @@ σ, the pull per area of wall

From here on, on this page, the stress σ in a wall is the pull across it per area of wall, as pressure is push per area.

16eng22Along the Pipe

\text{push on the end cap} = p \cdot \pi r^2 @@ pressure times its area
\text{held by a ring of wall, area } 2\pi r \cdot t @@ thin wall: circumference times thickness
\sigma_{\text{along}} = \dfrac{p \pi r^2}{2 \pi r t} = \dfrac{pr}{2t} @@ cancel π r
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma_{\text{round}} = 2\,\sigma_{\text{along}}} @@ the wall is pulled twice as hard round

Thin-wall technique: in a pipe of radius r and wall t under pressure p, the wall is pulled pr/t round the pipe and pr/2t along it. So a pipe tears along a line running its length, which is a slit: the frozen pipe and the sausage agree.

16eng23A Ball Is Best

Cut a sphere through its middle: the push p πr² on the flat cut is held by a ring of wall 2πr t, in every direction.

\sigma_{\text{sphere}} = \dfrac{pr}{2t} @@ half the pipe’s worst
1000 \text{ m down: } p = \rho g d \approx 1025 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 1000 \approx 10^7 \text{ Pa} @@ about 100 times the air’s
r = 1 \text{ m},\ \sigma \le 1.75 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa}:\ t \ge \dfrac{pr}{2\sigma} \approx 2.9 \text{ cm} @@ steel, with a margin of two
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the deepest trench: about } 1.1 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa}} @@ over a thousand times the air’s

For a given wall, a sphere holds twice the pressure of a pipe of the same radius, which is why deep diving craft carry their crew in a ball. A hull squeezed from outside can also fold inward before the steel gives, and its walls are made thicker for that.

16eng24Holding Air in Space

\text{station cabin: } p \approx 1.0 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa} @@ sea-level air, vacuum outside
r = 2.1 \text{ m},\ \sigma \le 10^8 \text{ Pa}:\ t \ge \dfrac{pr}{\sigma} \approx 2 \text{ mm} @@ aluminium, round the pipe
\text{suit: } p \approx 3 \times 10^4 \text{ Pa, pure oxygen} @@ a third of the air’s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lower pressure, softer suit: the hands can bend it}} @@ σ falls with p

A space station needs only millimetres of metal to hold its air; its walls are thicker to stop flying debris. A suit at a third of an atmosphere stays soft enough to work in, but the wearer must first breathe pure oxygen for hours to clear nitrogen from the blood, the same danger a diver meets coming up.

16eng25Test With Water, Not Air

A new tank is filled and pumped to one and a half times its working pressure. If it bursts, what is let loose is the energy stored in what fills it: 15·chem’s area under pressure against volume.

\text{gas: } p \text{ falls as } \tfrac1V \text{ while it expands} @@ 15·chem
\text{area under } \tfrac1V \text{ from } V \text{ to } 200V @@ a 200-fold drop to the air, large
\text{water: shrinks under } 2 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa by about } 1\% @@ it barely stores anything
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a burst under water test: a split and a splash}} @@ under gas: a blast

A wall holding a gas holds back far more energy than one holding a liquid at the same pressure, so pressure vessels are proved full of water. Divers’ tanks are tested this way every few years.

16eng27The Wall a Pressure Needs

Pressure inside pulls a pipe’s wall apart round its circumference, pr/t (16eng22). Turned round, it gives the thickness a pressure needs. Designers raise the pressure for surges, use a stress the metal can take with margin, and add metal that rust may eat over the years.

\text{design pressure } 1.1 \times 2 = 2.2 \text{ MPa} @@ room for surges
\text{allowed stress } 138 \text{ MPa} @@ the steel’s strength over a margin
t = \dfrac{p\,r}{\sigma} = \dfrac{2.2 \times 300}{138} \approx 4.8 \text{ mm} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{+ 3 \text{ mm for rust}:\ \text{order } 8 \text{ mm pipe}} @@

Wall-thickness technique: the thickness is the design pressure times the radius over the allowed stress, plus the metal rust will take. Shown on a test rig: a thin steel tube, filled with water and pumped slowly, bulges and then splits along its length at the pressure where pr/t reaches the steel’s breaking stress.

Accepted names

The round stress is the hoop stress, the along stress the longitudinal or axial stress; pr/t and pr/2t are the thin-walled pressure vessel results. Folding inward under outside pressure is buckling. Proving with water is a hydrostatic test; the deep sphere a pressure hull; the oxygen breathing before a spacewalk a prebreathe.

Who said it first

The thin-wall rule goes back to Edme Mariotte in the 1680s, who tested pipes for the fountains of Versailles. The bathyscaphe Trieste carried Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh in a steel sphere to the bottom of the Challenger Deep, about 10.9 km down, in 1960.
