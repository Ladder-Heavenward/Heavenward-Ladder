1f · technique

Rewriting by the Rulebook

In English you can turn “nobody came” into “it is not so that anybody came” without checking who came, because the two sentences always say the same thing. Logic has a precise version of that: rewriting by the rulebook. 1e gave every statement a column. Each entry in the rulebook is a technique: a rewrite between two statements whose columns match. Once a technique is proved, you can use it to rewrite, each move on its own line, without drawing another table. Each rewrite is a transform in 1a's sense: it changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. This step is the rulebook, with a name for each technique. It covers the jobs you will do with the rulebook most: pushing a not inward, and handling the if family. It shows the tempting moves that are not in the rulebook. And the rulebook's last column says which directions this rung's techs license for any statement whatever, and which wait for rung 7.

Find a piece of the statement that matches a side of a technique; replace it with the technique's other side; write the technique's name beside the line. The column never changes, so the final line says what the opening line said.

1f1Why You May Rewrite a Piece

The power of a rephrasing is not that “nobody came” means “not anybody came”. The power is that you may make the swap inside a sentence that holds it, and nothing else changes. Example: “if nobody came, the party was a flop” becomes “if not anybody came, the party was a flop”. Logic gets the same power for the same reason. Each machine in 1e reads only the T or F of its inputs. So replace a piece of a statement with another piece that has the same column. Every machine above the piece receives, in every row, exactly what it received before. So the column of the whole is untouched. That is the licence for every rewrite in this step: substitute for the piece, where the sameness is “same column”. It rests on 0b's swap axiom, with 1e's = between statements. By that axiom, the statement with the piece replaced has the old statement's column.

A chain of such rewrites shows that the statement it starts from and the statement it ends at are equivalent, with no table. Tables check each technique once; chains then use the technique for ever after. Check once, use for ever: that is the trade rewriting always makes, and rung 5 will make it again with a rulebook of its own.

1f2A Mark for “always”

The rulebook wants a partner for ⊥: a statement that is true in every row. The denial of the totally false will do. ⊥ is F in every row (1e2), so its denial is T in every row, and a line may hold it beside anything without changing what the line says. 3a earns it as a technique from nothing, with a box: suppose ⊥, and refute. Here is the mark:

Example: P ∧ ⊤ has the column of P. In the row where P is T, the and gets T and T, which gives T. In the row where P is F, the and gets F and T, which gives F. So adding “and always” says nothing new. ⊤ is the statement that never fails, and ⊥ is the statement that never holds.

1f3The Rulebook

Here are the techniques, with names to say beside each rewrite. Each is a matching of columns, which you can check in 1e's machine. Each is written as a sameness, and the technique is the move: where a piece matches one side, you may write the other side in its place. Read each left to right and right to left. A technique used backwards is still the technique, as far as columns go. The last column of the table is about something else, and footing 1f4 explains that column.

Turn-about is a name you have met. 0b6's turns a sameness about; 1c3's and 1c10's turn an and or an or about. Every entry in the book is a technique: proved, and cited by its name.

Say-it-again: saying a thing twice adds nothing. P ∧ P is P, and P ∨ P is P. The = in the table is 1e's = between statements: the two sides have the same column in every row.

1f4Settled Statements, and the Last Column

Every technique in the book has matching columns. So for any statement you can settle, every technique may be used both ways. To settle a statement is to find out whether it is true or false. For example, you settle “the school library is open” by walking past the door. Once you can find your row, the table does the rest. Unless a question says a statement is unsettled, its statements are settled, so on that question use the whole book freely.

The last column is for the other kind: statements nobody can settle. That column is where the rung's gap shows up. From here on, beside a technique, 1e's arrow also has this job. → stands for the technique read left to right, its forward direction. ← stands for the technique read right to left, its backward direction. For example, not-through for and read → turns ¬(P ∧ Q) into ¬P ∨ ¬Q.

An arrow marked any is a direction the rung's techs prove, for any statements at all. Where a technique on an earlier step does it, that technique is named. A direction marked rung 7 is one the table supports and the techs do not. It needs the principle rung 7 lays down: that every statement has a row.

1f5Why a Direction Waits for Rung 7

Take not-through for and. If you know “not both P and Q”, does that tell you “P fails, or Q fails”? Only if you can find out which one fails, and a line cannot: “Ann and Ben cannot both be guilty” never points at an innocent suspect.

Read not-through for and the other way, and it is fine. If P fails, then certainly not both P and Q. So the backward direction is marked any, and the forward direction is marked rung 7.

Likewise ¬¬P says “P is not totally false”. On this rung that is weaker than P. So not-not is fine backward and waits forward. Each technique marked with a rung-7 direction has this shape: fine one way, blocked the other. The either-way principle, P ∨ ¬P, is the gap itself. When you make a chain, it is worth knowing which steps leaned on a rung-7 direction. The machine in footing 1f8 marks them for you.

1f6Turned-About Places

A further subtlety, which the machine also tracks. A technique licensed both ways may be used on a piece anywhere inside a statement, and the whole says the same by techs. (The proof is a routine on the joining word above the piece.) A technique licensed only forward, or only backward, may be used on a piece standing on its own. It may also be used on a piece inside an and, an or, or the conclusion of an if.

Inside a not, or inside the hypothesis of an if, the direction turns about. Those places are turned about: making the hypothesis stronger makes the if weaker. Example: “if you have a dog, you get a sticker” promises more than “if you have a big dog, you get a sticker”. Strengthen the hypothesis from “a dog” to “a big dog”, and the if says less. The machine marks a one-way technique used in a turned-about place as a rung-7 step. That is why the if-as-or step in footing 1f7 is licensed there: it sits inside a not.

1f7Pushing a Not Inward: The Negation of an If

The commonest job in all of mathematics is to say exactly what it would take for a statement to fail. The method: put ¬ in front of the whole thing, then push it inward, one technique at a time, until it sits only on letters. Every joining word the not passes through changes. Through ∧ it becomes ∨, by not-through, and through ∨ it becomes ∧. Through ⇒ it becomes ∧ with the ¬ moved onto the conclusion. And ¬ on ¬ cancels.

Here is the negation of an if, the one people get wrong most. Take “if it rains, the game is cancelled”. What would it take for that promise to fail? It fails only if it rains and the game is played anyway. Here is the same answer reached in three rewrites, with P for “it rains” and Q for “the game is cancelled”.

Start from : what it would take for “if P then Q” to fail. First rewrite the if inside as an or, by if-as-or read forward: . On its own that direction waits for rung 7. But here the if sits inside a not, a turned-about place, so here the forward direction is licensed.

Next push the not through the or, by not-through for or. The or turns into an and: . That technique works both ways, for any statement.

Last, cancel the not on a not, by not-not read forward: . That is a rung-7 direction, fine for a settled P.

So the negation of an if is not another if. The negation of an if is an and: the hypothesis happened and the conclusion did not. “It is not so that if it rains the game is cancelled” means “it rains and the game is played”. The negated-if technique in the book is that whole chain packed into one rewrite. Read right to left, negated-if may be used on any statement. If P happened and Q did not, then “if P then Q” certainly failed: suppose the if, carry it through, clash, refute.

1f8A Chain That Simplifies, and the Rewriting Machine

A chain can also make a statement smaller. Take (P ∧ Q) ∨ (P ∧ ¬Q): “P and Q, or P and not Q”. It feels as if it should just be P. Here is the plan: pull the shared P out, then show that what is left adds nothing.

Read spread and over or backward: both sides of the or are “P and something”, so the P comes out in front, giving P ∧ (Q ∨ ¬Q). Then Q ∨ ¬Q is ⊤ by the either-way principle. That step is rung 7's very principle, so it needs Q settled until then. Last, units: P ∧ ⊤ is P. The chain is honest for any Q you can settle, and the either-way step must be marked.

1f9The If Family

Every if has relatives, and here are their names. Start from P ⇒ Q:

  • the converse, Q ⇒ P: the arrow turned about;
  • the inverse, ¬P ⇒ ¬Q: both sides denied, arrow left alone;
  • the contrapositive, ¬Q ⇒ ¬P: both at once.

The contrapositive is equivalent to the original: that is the turn-and-deny technique, whose forward direction is 1d's. The converse and the inverse are not equivalent to the original. But they are equivalent to each other, since the inverse is the contrapositive of the converse. So they split into partners that stand or fall together: the original with its contrapositive, the converse with the inverse. 1e's table showed the rows TF and FT, where the partnerships disagree.

Example: “if it is a cat, it is an animal” and “if it is not an animal, it is not a cat” stand or fall together. “If it is an animal, it is a cat” and “if it is not a cat, it is not an animal” are the other partnership. Rex, an animal that is not a cat, sinks both.

1f11A Chain for Turn-and-Deny

1f12Where a Licence Comes From

Here is a lesson about where licences come from. Spread or over and spreads ∨ over ∧. Now read “or” the Latin way, as aut, “this or that but not both”. The same-looking spreading move becomes illegal. The row TTF convicts it. Q ∧ R is T ∧ F, which is F, so P aut (Q ∧ R) is T aut F, which is T. But P aut Q is T aut T, which is F, so (P aut Q) ∧ (P aut R) is F.

So the move is legal for vel and illegal for aut. A move's licence comes from the rulebook it belongs to, never from its looks. Rung 5 comes back to this with a rulebook of its own.

1f13Growing a Statement on Purpose

Simplifying is only half the craft. The other half reads the techniques right to left, and makes a statement bigger. Why make it bigger? Because a bigger statement can show how to argue for it. The outermost mark of a statement says what the first move of the argument must be. An if wants a supposition. An and wants each part proved. A small statement can hide the shape a proof needs, and a technique read the other way can bring that shape out.

To grow a statement, choose the shape you want to prove, find a technique whose other side has that shape, and read the technique toward it.

Here is the method on a school rule. Let W be “it is raining”. Let S be “the field is muddy”. Let C be “recess is indoors”. The rule: if it is raining or the field is muddy, recess is indoors. In marks, (W ∨ S) ⇒ C.

The Rule's outermost mark is ⇒, and its hypothesis is an or. Proving it straight means supposing an or. An or hands you nothing until you take cases. So grow the rule first.

Read cases backward. Cases, read backward, is fine for any statement. It turns the rule into : if it is raining, recess is indoors; and if the field is muddy, recess is indoors.

Now the outermost mark is ∧. An and wants each part proved, so the argument is two ifs. One supposes rain. One supposes mud. Neither supposes an or.

The slides take the cases technique itself apart, from the other techniques, one rewrite at a time.

1f16A Letter From Nowhere, and a Growth That Waits

A growth can even bring in a letter the statement never had. P becomes P ∨ ⊥, by units read backward. ⊥ becomes Q ∧ ¬Q, by clash read backward. Both directions are any. So P = P ∨ (Q ∧ ¬Q), and Q has walked in from nowhere. Writing a thing in a clever form is a real move, not a trick, and sometimes the clever form is exactly the shape you need.

One tempting growth does not work on this rung. Grow P into P ∧ ⊤, and then grow ⊤ into Q ∨ ¬Q. That would split P into two cases, Q and not Q. The second growth reads either-way right to left, and either-way waits for rung 7. Any statement can be grown into its cases on Q only once every statement has a row.

A growth needs a target. Grown at random, a statement only gets longer. So decide first which outermost mark you want, then look in the book for the technique that puts that mark there. The slides grow P into a statement that spells out cases on Q, with no rung-7 step. 4e makes arguing by cases a shape of its own.

Who said it first

The opposite of “either A or B” is “neither A nor B”, and the opposite of “both A and B” is “either not A or not B”. He observed that the same rules govern classes of things, which is rung 2's business.

De Morgan, Formal Logic (1847)

De Morgan’s name stuck to the not-through techniques, but medieval logicians, William of Ockham among them, had stated both rules in words. What De Morgan and Boole added was the rulebook: rules you can apply to letters without thinking about rain.

Accepted names. In the books these techniques are called laws: turn-about is commutativity, regroup associativity, spread distributivity, say-it-again idempotence (from the Latin for “the same power”: done twice, it does no more than done once; Boole’s law), absorb absorption, not-through De Morgan’s laws, not-not double negation, turn-and-deny the contrapositive, hypotheses-in-turn exportation, and the either-way principle the law of excluded middle. Hearing “and not if you don’t” in an everyday “if” is what linguists call conditional perfection.
