What Does a Summary Forget?

What does a summary forget? Five statistics annexes, from rung 2 to rung 30, each turned a roster, a stand of trees, a state’s counties or a row of coin tosses into a few numbers. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. A summary is an arrow out of the data, and a question can be answered from the summary exactly when the question’s arrow passes through it. An or-question forgets who is in both groups. A test’s arrow runs from the state to the result, and turning it round needs the start as well. A fair draw is an arrow that no shuffle of the members changes. And the likelihood of a row of tosses passes through one count, so the order of the row is forgotten and nothing is lost. Last, the splits a fair draw chooses among are one piece of a map that soon outgrows a climber, and then a machine.

b = g ∘ a for some g exactly when a(x) = a(y) forces b(x) = b(y). ∨(T, F) = ∨(T, T) and ∧(T, F) ≠ ∧(T, T): ∧ does not pass through ∨. The turned arrow of f at π: g(θ | x) = f(x | θ)π(θ)/Σ f(x | t)π(t); another π, another g. D(σS) = D(S) for every shuffle σ gives each member the same chance, k/N. P(list | p) = pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ passes through (n, k), and records in a row add their counts. Splits of N plots into k named groups of n: M = N!/(n!)ᵏ, each with chance 1/M.

infstat.1What an Or Forgets

2·stat4 asked a hiking club one question, “did you climb or paddle?”, and found that the answers cannot say who did both. Its proof was a witness: two clubs with the same yes group and different both regions. Read as arrows, the witness is the whole of a technique.

Let a be the arrow that sends each member x to the pair (in h?, in k?). The or-question is ∨ ∘ a, and the both-question is ∧ ∘ a. To answer one question from another’s answers is to find an arrow g from answers to answers.

Passes-through technique: for arrows a : X → A and b : X → B, there is an arrow g with b = g ∘ a on the values a takes exactly when a(x) = a(y) forces b(x) = b(y). Then b passes through a.

b = g \circ a,\ a(x) = a(y) \Rightarrow b(x) = g(a(x)) = g(a(y)) = b(y) @@ rightward: g gives one output for one input
a(x) = a(y) \Rightarrow b(x) = b(y):\quad \text{put } g(a(x)) = b(x) @@ leftward: the rule gives one output for each value of a, whichever x is used
\htmlClass{new}{b \text{ passes through } a \iff \big(a(x) = a(y) \Rightarrow b(x) = b(y)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ when a is one-to-one (9c), everything passes through it
  
\lor(T, F) = T = \lor(T, T),\qquad \land(T, F) = F \ne T = \land(T, T) @@ 1c’s tables
\htmlClass{new}{\land \text{ does not pass through } \lor} @@ the forcing fails: no g turns or-answers into both-answers
(h, k) \mapsto h \cup k:\quad (\{Zoe\}, \{Randy\}) \text{ and } (\{Zoe, Randy\}, \{Zoe, Randy\}) \text{ agree};\quad h \cap k \text{ differs} @@ 2·stat4’s two clubs: the same failure, one level up
\text{each region } = c \circ a \text{ for a condition } c \text{ on pairs} @@ so every region passes through a: the split-the-question technique (2·stat4)

What hides is the arrangement: the members. The lines use only which answers are equal. A summary is worth exactly the questions that pass through it.

infstat.2An Arrow Turned Round Needs Its Start

5·stat4 tested 1000 tanoaks. The test flags 9 infected trees in 10 and 1 healthy tree in 20, and still a flagged tree is infected with chance only 18/67. In ∞13’s boxes the test is a chance arrow f from the tree’s state to the result. The question asked runs the other way, from the result to the state.

Start-matters technique: the turned arrow of f is fixed by f and the start π together: g(θ | x) = f(x | θ)π(θ) / Σt f(x | t)π(t). The same f with another start has another turned arrow.

g(\theta \mid x) = \tfrac{f(x \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\sum_t f(x \mid t)\,\pi(t)} @@ the turn-around technique (∞13); the turned-chance technique (5·stat4)
\pi = (\tfrac{20}{1000}, \tfrac{980}{1000}):\quad g(\text{infected} \mid \text{flagged}) = \tfrac{0.9 \cdot 0.02}{0.9 \cdot 0.02 + 0.05 \cdot 0.98} = \tfrac{18}{67} @@ the stand of 5·stat4
\pi' = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12):\quad g'(\text{infected} \mid \text{flagged}) = \tfrac{0.9 \cdot 0.5}{0.9 \cdot 0.5 + 0.05 \cdot 0.5} = \tfrac{18}{19} @@ the same test, in a stand half infected
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one } f,\ \text{two starts, two turned arrows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so g is not made from f alone

What hides is the arrangement: the thousand trees and the tree of cases. The mistake of guessing nine in ten is now easy to name. It reads f, which runs from state to result, as if it ran from result to state. The row “result, then f” does not compose.

infstat.3A Fair Draw Sees Nothing

6·stat2 proved that a fair sample of k from N gives each member the chance k/N, by counting one set of pairs in two ways. 6·stat5 said in words what fair asks: the draw must look at nothing about the members. That demand is a statement about arrows, and the count follows from it.

A draw is a list of chances D(S), one for each part S with k members. A shuffle σ of the population carries each part S to a part σS. The draw sees nothing when D(σS) = D(S) for every shuffle σ and every part S.

Sees-nothing technique: if D(σS) = D(S) for every shuffle σ, every member has the same chance of being in the sample, and that chance is k/N. For a set A of m members, the sample’s share of A is m/N on average.

P(x \text{ in}) = \textstyle\sum_{S \ni x} D(S) = \sum_{S \ni x} D(\sigma S) = \sum_{S' \ni \sigma x} D(S') = P(\sigma x \text{ in}) @@ D sees nothing; S′ = σS runs over the parts that hold σx
\text{some shuffle sends } x \text{ to any } y,\ \text{so every member has one chance } c @@ swap x and y
\textstyle\sum_x P(x \text{ in}) = \sum_S D(S) \cdot k = k @@ each part holds k members; the chances D total 1
\htmlClass{new}{Nc = k,\quad c = \tfrac kN} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fair-share technique (6·stat2), with no counting of parts
E\,\tfrac{|S \cap A|}{k} = \tfrac1k \textstyle\sum_{x \in A} P(x \text{ in}) = \tfrac1k \cdot m \cdot \tfrac kN = \tfrac mN \quad \blacksquare @@ expected-values-add (5·stat3): the fair-sample technique (6·stat3)
  
N = 36,\ k = 4:\quad c = \tfrac{4}{36} = \tfrac19 @@ Lane’s chance, with no 58 905 in sight
\text{first four in the alphabet}:\quad D(\{Baker, Benton, Clackamas, Clatsop\}) = 1 @@ swap Baker and Lane, and D of the new part is 0: this draw sees the names
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{Baker in}) = 1,\quad P(\text{Lane in}) = 0} @@ the technique’s demand fails, and so does its result

What hides is the arrangement: who the members are. The proof never names a county or counts the parts. It also shows where a draw leans: in December 1969 the capsules that went in last came out early (6·stat5), so a shuffle of the birthdays changed the chances.

infstat.4The Order of the Tosses Is Forgotten

14·stat1 found the chance of a given list of tosses with k heads in n: pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. 14·stat6 tossed a coin 10 times and then 30 more and found that two records of one coin are one record. The first footing’s technique says both at once.

Count-carries technique: at every guess p, the chance of a list of tosses passes through its count (n, k). So two lists with one count have one likelihood at every guess, and whatever is worked out from the likelihood is worked out from the count. Lists in a row add their counts.

P(\text{list} \mid p) = p^k(1 - p)^{n-k} @@ the chance-of-a-record technique (14·stat1): the order of the heads is not in it
\text{count}(x) = \text{count}(y) \Rightarrow P(x \mid p) = P(y \mid p) \text{ for every } p @@ so x ↦ P(x | ·) passes through the count (first footing)
P(x \text{ then } y \mid p) = p^{k_1}(1 - p)^{n_1 - k_1} \cdot p^{k_2}(1 - p)^{n_2 - k_2} = p^{k_1 + k_2}(1 - p)^{(n_1 + n_2) - (k_1 + k_2)} @@ fresh spins: the chances multiply (14·stat6)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{count}(x \text{ then } y) = \text{count}(x) + \text{count}(y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ lists in a row to counts under plus: composing is kept
  
(10, 7) + (30, 9) = (40, 16) @@ the counts add
\text{best guess } \tfrac{16}{40} = 0.4 @@ the share seen in both together (14·stat6)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac12(0.7 + 0.3) = 0.5 \text{ is not it}} @@ the two best guesses do not carry enough: the share forgets n

What hides is the sequence: the order of the tosses, and the place where one record ends and the next begins. Nothing about the guess is lost with it. The share k/n alone is a poorer summary. The pooled best guess does not pass through the two shares, since 7 of 10 with 9 of 30 and 7 of 10 with 3 of 10 have the same shares and different pooled guesses, 0.4 and 0.5.

infstat.5A Count Left to the Machine

30·stat3 began from one line: each split of the fifteen plots has the chance 1/M, with M = 756 756. Draw the splits as things and the shuffles of the plots as arrows. A split says which plots get which mix.

Split-count technique: the shuffles of N plots carry any split into k named groups of n onto every other, and (n!)ᵏ of them keep a split. So the splits number M = N!/(n!)ᵏ, and a draw that sees nothing gives each the chance 1/M.

\text{for splits } s, t:\ \text{some shuffle } \sigma \text{ has } \sigma s = t @@ send the plots of each group of s to the plots of the same group of t: one piece
\sigma s = s \iff \sigma \text{ shuffles each group within itself}:\quad (n!)^k \text{ keepers} @@ the groups are named by their mixes, so none is exchanged
M = \tfrac{N!}{(n!)^k} @@ the arrows-by-ends technique (∞·chem)
\htmlClass{new}{D(\sigma s) = D(s) \text{ and one piece}:\quad D(s) = \tfrac1M \text{ for every } s} \quad \blacksquare @@ as in the third footing: equal along every arrow, and the chances total 1
  
\text{a climber reads}:\quad 6 \text{ plots in three pairs},\ \ \tfrac{720}{8} = 90 \text{ splits} @@ a page of them, each with its ratio
\text{a machine reads}:\quad 15 \text{ plots in three fives},\ \ 756\,756 \text{ splits},\ 5754 \text{ with } B \ge 40 @@ counted by machine (30·stat3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither reads}:\quad 60 \text{ plots in three twenties},\ \ \approx 5.8 \times 10^{26} \text{ splits}} @@ at a billion a second, longer than the age of the universe

Where neither reads, the third footing is used once more, with the splits as the population. A fair sample of the splits has, on average, the population’s own share of splits with a ratio as large as the one seen. The machine draws ten thousand shuffles; the count is the climber’s to check, since no count can pass M.

What stays with the climber is what no count supplies: that the draw was fair, what claim is tested, and the level α, chosen before looking. What hides is the arrangement: which plot is which.

The accepted names

When b = g ∘ a, b factors through a, and b is determined by a; the pairs with a(x) = a(y) are the kernel of a. A summary of data is a statistic, and one through which the likelihood passes at every guess is a sufficient statistic; the count (n, k) is sufficient for the binomial model, and that every list with one count has one likelihood is the factorization criterion. The turned arrow is the posterior, the start is the prior, and the turning is Bayes’ rule; reading f as its own turned arrow is the base-rate fallacy, or the confusion of the inverse. The test’s two chances are its sensitivity and its false positive rate. A draw that no shuffle changes is exchangeable, or invariant under the symmetric group; with parts of one size it is the simple random sample, and “right on average” is unbiased. Putting two records together is pooling. The count M is a multinomial coefficient; the label-shuffle test is a permutation test, or randomization test, and with a fair sample of the splits in place of them all it is a Monte Carlo permutation test.

What the arrows let hide

In the first three footings the arrows hide arrangement: the members of the club, the trees of the stand, the names of the counties. In the fourth they hide sequence: the order of the tosses and the seam between two records. In the fifth the map itself hides, by its size. A climber reads a map of dozens of arrows, and a machine one of thousands or millions. Past that, what every arrow keeps is still read, and it is the climber’s to ask for. The page’s one tool is the first footing’s: a question is answerable from a summary exactly when its arrow passes through the summary’s. The or-answers fail it for the both region, the two shares fail it for the pooled guess, and the count meets it for the likelihood. No dated claim is made on this page.
