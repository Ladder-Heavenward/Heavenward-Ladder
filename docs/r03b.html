3b · proof

Necessary and Possible

3a’s boxes let you suppose. With them come two old words, made exact. A statement is necessary when it holds whatever you suppose, and possible when it can be supposed without a clash. They are the bridge from the boxes to what comes next: a process that never stops.

Necessary is an every over the ways things could go; possible is a some. Put the necessity on the whole if, not on its result.

3b1The Words, Then the Marks

“Necessarily P” says that P holds whatever else is so; “possibly P” says that P can hold. The ladder writes them with two marks set in front of a statement: □P for necessarily P, and ◇P for possibly P.

They are operators, like 1b’s not: each takes one statement and makes a new one. 0a’s possibility axiom used the everyday word, for what a logical system can represent. From here on the word has this mark and this job.

3b2Necessary Is an Every

P is necessary when every box you could open on the givens reaches P. Whatever you suppose, P comes out. “If the door is closed, it is not open” is necessary: no supposition about the door gets round it.

So □ is 2b’s every, said over the ways things could go instead of over the members of a set. To show □P, suppose anything at all and reach P, as 2b showed an every by working on anything.

3b3Possible Is a Some

P is possible when at least one way of supposing P never meets a clash. “The door is open” is possible: suppose it, and nothing you have is refuted. “The door is open and closed” is not possible: suppose it, and 3a’s refutation reaches the totally false.

So ◇ is 2b’s some, said over the ways. To show ◇P, give one way in which P holds and nothing clashes, as 2b showed a some by giving an example.

3b4The Two Together

If P is necessary, then not-P is not possible. Every way reaches P, so no way holds not-P without a clash. In marks, □P gives ¬◇¬P, the way an every gives “no counterexample”.

The way back, from “not-P is not possible” to “P is necessary”, needs the step 3a12 could not take, from ¬¬P to P. It waits for rung 7 with that step, as 2b’s “not every” to “some … not” does.

3b5A Process Without End Is Possible

Suppose a process in which each step is followed by a next one, and nothing ever stops it. Open the box and look for a clash. None comes: nothing you have says that a next one must run out. So a process without end is possible.

Necessarily, if the process never ends, then it never runs out. That is necessary, because “never runs out” is what “never ends” says. The next step, 3c, builds such a process, one next one at a time.

3b6Where the Necessity Sits

“Necessarily, if it never ends then it never runs out” puts □ in front of the whole if: □(E ⇒ N). That is safe.

Illegal move: sliding the □ onto the result. “If it never ends, then necessarily it never runs out”, E ⇒ □N, claims more: that the process could not have run out at all. The everyday version is the old slip: “necessarily, a bachelor is unmarried” is safe; “a bachelor is necessarily unmarried” says he could never marry.

Accepted names

Logic with □ and ◇ is modal logic. The difference between □(E ⇒ N) and E ⇒ □N is the medieval distinction between the necessity of the consequence and the necessity of the consequent.
