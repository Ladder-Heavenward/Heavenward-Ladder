32c · proof

When the series returns the function

The step before proved that sums of turnings come close to a repeating function on average: the energy they miss shrinks to nothing. An average can hide a lot. A sum can be close on average and still be wrong at a point, or at many points, by as much as you like. So this step asks a sharper question. Pick one input. Do the partial sums, read at that input, settle at the value of the function there? The answer turns on the shape of the function near that input. Where it has a slope, they do. Where it jumps, they settle, but at the halfway point of the jump, which may not be the function’s value at all. And next to a jump, the sums always overshoot. Add a thousand turnings or a million, the overshoot gets narrower and moves closer to the jump, but it never gets lower. It stays at about a tenth of the jump, for ever.

The partial sum at a point is a weighted average of the function near that point, with a weight built from the turnings. Where the function has a slope, the average settles at its value, because the leftover is a coefficient of a function of finite energy, and those fade. At a jump the average settles halfway. Beside a jump the sums rise past the top by about nine per cent of the jump, at every number of turnings, so the sums never come near the function everywhere at once.

32c1The partial sum is a weighted average

Put the coefficient totals inside the partial sum, and swap the finite sum with the total. What comes out is a total of f near x against one fixed weight.

Turning-kernel technique: the Nth partial sum at x is the average of f(x − s) over one repeat, weighted by D_N(s) = sin((N + ½)s) / sin(s/2), a weight whose own average is 1.

S_Nf(x) = \textstyle\sum_{|n| \le N}\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t)\,e^{in(x - t)}\,dt = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x - s)\,D_N(s)\,ds @@ a finite sum passes through a total; s = x − t, and f repeats every 2π
D_N(s) = \textstyle\sum_{|n| \le N} e^{ins} = e^{-iNs}\,\tfrac{e^{i(2N+1)s} - 1}{e^{is} - 1} @@ the geometric sum (15i), for s ≠ 0
= \tfrac{e^{i(N + \frac12)s} - e^{-i(N + \frac12)s}}{e^{is/2} - e^{-is/2}} = \tfrac{\sin((N + \frac12)s)}{\sin(s/2)} @@ top and bottom times e^(−is/2); the power-to-sum technique (26i)
\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} D_N(s)\,ds = 1 @@ only the turning n = 0 has a total (32a1)
\htmlClass{new}{S_Nf(x) - f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \big(f(x - s) - f(x)\big)D_N(s)\,ds} \quad \blacksquare @@ f(x) is the average of the constant f(x)

D_N is 2N + 1 at s = 0 and swings ever faster away from it. The question of a point is the question of whether that swinging cancels everything but the value at s = 0.

32c2Where there is a slope, the sum returns

Slope-returns technique: if f repeats every 2π, is sized and bounded, and has a slope at x, then S_N f(x) settles at f(x).

g(s) = \tfrac{f(x - s) - f(x)}{\sin(s/2)} \text{ for } 0 \lt |s| \le \pi @@ the leftover’s weight, with sin((N + ½)s) taken out
|f(x - s) - f(x)| \le C|s| \text{ for } |s| \lt r @@ the slope-bounds-change technique (13b)
|\sin(s/2)| \ge |s|/\pi \text{ on } [-\pi, \pi] @@ sin bends down on [0, π/2], so it lies above its chord (14)
|g(s)| \le C\pi \text{ near } 0,\ \text{ and } g \text{ is bounded beyond } r @@ f is bounded, and sin(s/2) is away from 0 there
\sin((N + \tfrac12)s) = \sin Ns\,\cos\tfrac s2 + \cos Ns\,\sin\tfrac s2 @@ the sum formula (21d)
S_Nf(x) - f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int g(s)\cos\tfrac s2\,\sin Ns\,ds + \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int g(s)\sin\tfrac s2\,\cos Ns\,ds @@ the turning-kernel technique (32c1)
\htmlClass{new}{S_Nf(x) - f(x) \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ both are coefficients of functions of finite energy, which fade (32b1)

The proof needs less than a slope: a bound |f(x − s) − f(x)| ≤ C|s| near x is enough. Continuity alone is not. It is proved much higher up the ladder that some continuous function has partial sums that wander at a point without settling.

32c3At a jump, halfway

Let f have a limit from each side at x, f(x⁻) from the left and f(x⁺) from the right (10e), and a slope from each side: (f(x + h) − f(x⁺))/h and (f(x − h) − f(x⁻))/(−h) settle as h heads to 0 from above.

Halfway technique: at a jump with a slope on each side, the partial sums settle at the halfway point of the jump, whatever value f has at the jump itself.

D_N(-s) = D_N(s) @@ sin is odd, so the quotient is even (9e)
S_Nf(x) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\big(f(x - s) + f(x + s)\big)D_N(s)\,ds @@ fold the total onto [0, π]
\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} D_N(s)\,ds = \tfrac12 @@ half of an even function’s total (32c1)
S_Nf(x) - \tfrac{f(x^-) + f(x^+)}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\big(f(x - s) - f(x^-) + f(x + s) - f(x^+)\big)D_N(s)\,ds @@ the halfway value is the average of its constant
\htmlClass{new}{S_Nf(x) \to \tfrac{f(x^-) + f(x^+)}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope-returns proof, with one-sided slopes bounding the bracket

The sawtooth of 32b4 is x on (−π, π). At π it jumps from π down to −π. Its sum there is Σ i(−1)ⁿe^(inπ)/n = Σ i/n over n ≠ 0, which is 0, since the terms for n and −n cancel: the halfway point, whatever value the sawtooth is given at π.

32c4The overshoot that never goes away

The square wave q is 1 on (0, π) and −1 on (−π, 0), repeated. It is odd, so it has sines alone (32a3), and its coefficients are bₙ = 4/(πn) for odd n and 0 for even n. Take N = 2M − 1.

Lasting-overshoot technique: beside each jump of the square wave, the partial sums rise past 1 to a peak that heads to (2/π) times the total of sin t / t from 0 to π, about 1.179, however many turnings are used.

S_Nq(x) = \tfrac4\pi\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\tfrac{\sin((2j - 1)x)}{2j - 1} @@ the cosine-and-sine technique (32a3)
\tfrac{d}{dx}S_Nq(x) = \tfrac4\pi\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\cos((2j - 1)x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\,\tfrac{\sin 2Mx}{\sin x} @@ a geometric sum of turnings, as in 32c1
\text{first peak at } x = \tfrac{\pi}{2M} @@ the slope is first 0 there (14)
S_Nq\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2M}\big) = \tfrac2\pi\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\tfrac{\pi}{M}\cdot\tfrac{\sin t_j}{t_j},\ \ t_j = \tfrac{(2j - 1)\pi}{2M} @@ rewrite each term
\htmlClass{new}{S_Nq\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2M}\big) \to \tfrac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\tfrac{\sin t}{t}\,dt \approx 1.1790} \quad \blacksquare @@ a sum of strips of width π/M at their middles closes on the area (15)

The jump is 2, from −1 to 1, and the peak passes 1 by 0.179: about nine per cent of the jump. As M grows the peak moves toward the jump and gets narrower, so at every fixed x it does go away, as the slope-returns technique says. But it is always there somewhere. So the sums never come within 0.17 of q everywhere at once, and they could not: a limit everywhere at once of continuous sums would be continuous (the kept-continuity technique, 23d), and q jumps.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

D_N is the Dirichlet kernel. The slope-returns technique is Dirichlet’s test, or the Dini criterion in its stronger form with the bound C|s|. The halfway technique is the Dirichlet–Jordan result for functions with jumps. The lasting overshoot is the Gibbs phenomenon, and (2/π)∫₀^π sin t / t dt the Wilbraham–Gibbs constant, about 1.17898; the total of sin t / t is the sine integral Si(π) ≈ 1.8519. The bound C|s| is a Lipschitz condition.

Who said it first

Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved in 1829 that the series returns the function at points of continuity and settles at the middle of each jump, for functions with finitely many jumps and turns. Ulisse Dini sharpened the test in 1880. Paul du Bois-Reymond built a continuous function in 1873 whose series fails to settle at a point. Henry Wilbraham found the overshoot in 1848. Albert Michelson’s machine of 1898, which added eighty turnings with springs, drew it; J. Willard Gibbs explained it in letters to Nature in 1898 and 1899. Lennart Carleson proved in 1966 that the series of every function of finite energy returns it at all points but a set of size zero.

A proof that is not allowed

“D_N heads to a spike of total 1 at 0, so S_N f(x) heads to f(x) for every f.” Not allowed: D_N does not head to a spike. Its total size, (1/2π)∫|D_N|, grows like (4/π²) ln N without bound, and that growth is what lets a continuous function’s series fail at a point. 32d finds a weight that stays at or above 0 and does what this proof wanted.
