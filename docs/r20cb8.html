Numbers that turn

9·cb8 answered 8i’s question, a number whose square is negative one, with pairs of reals and a rule for multiplying them, and did nothing more with them. Here they move. The plane has room for it. A number on the line can be read as a move: multiplying by 3 stretches, and multiplying by −1 is the half turn. The quarter turn, done twice, is the half turn. So in the plane there is a number whose square is −1, and it is a move you have already made.

The number a is the pair (a, 0), the stretch by a. Pairs multiply by 20j’s rule. The quarter turn, done twice, is the half turn. So a square equal to negative one has answers in the plane.

20·cb8.1The line inside the plane

Read the number a as the pair (a, 0), and the pair as the move (x, y) ↦ (ax, ay) that 20j4 gives it. On the floor, the pair rule is the old multiplying, and adding pairs is adding. For a > 0 the move is a stretch by a. For a < 0 it is the half turn and a stretch by −a. So −1 is the half turn.

20·cb8.2The quarter turn, squared

18f2’s quarter turn i = (0, 1), done twice, is the half turn, and the half turn is the number −1. 8i found no answer to x² + 1 = 0 on the line. There is none on the floor, but there are two in the plane.

(0, 1) \cdot (0, 1) = (0 - 1,\ 0 + 0) = (-1, 0) @@ the pair product
(0, -1) \cdot (0, -1) = (-1, 0) @@ likewise: the three-quarter turn, twice
(a, b)^2 = (a^2 - b^2,\ 2ab) = (-1, 0) \Rightarrow b \ne 0,\ a = 0,\ b = \pm 1 @@ 2ab = 0 and a² − b² = −1: no others
\htmlClass{new}{x^2 + 1 = 0:\ x = (0, 1) \text{ or } (0, -1)} \quad \blacksquare

As on 9·cb8, a + bi names the pair (a, b). Its i is the quarter turn (0, 1) of 18f2, and i² = −1.

The new number was not assumed. It is the quarter turn of 18f2, a move you made again on 20c, and the rule that squares it was proved for every turn on 20j.

20·cb8.3Arithmetic by hand

9·cb8 found it first: pairs written a + bi follow every old rule of arithmetic, with one new fact: i² = −1. Share out (a + bi)(c + di) as on 5i and replace i² by −1. The answer is 20j’s pair rule.

(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 @@ share out (5i)
= (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i @@ i² = −1
\text{solve } x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0:\ (x + 1)^2 = -4 @@ complete the square (8i)
x + 1 = 2i \text{ or } -2i \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{x = -1 \pm 2i} @@ 20·cb8.2's answers

Check: (−1 + 2i)² + 2(−1 + 2i) + 5 = (1 − 4i − 4) + (−2 + 4i) + 5 = 0.

20·cb8.4Flipping, sizes and dividing

The flip of z = a + bi over the floor is 9·cb8’s conjugate, a − bi, written z̄ from here on. 9·cb8’s absolute value, |z| = √(a² + b²), is the distance of z from 0.

A pair times its flip is its size squared, a number on the floor, and that is how to divide.

z\,\bar z = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 @@ the bi terms cancel; −b²i² = b²
\frac{w}{z} = \frac{w\,\bar z}{z\,\bar z} = \frac{w\,\bar z}{|z|^2} @@ times z̄ over z̄; z ≠ 0
|z - w| = d(z, w),\ \ \tfrac{z + w}{2} = \text{the midpoint} @@ 18a's distance and midpoint, read as pairs

The accepted names

Pairs a + bi are complex numbers; the floor is the real axis and the upright axis the imaginary axis; |z| is its absolute value. The name “complex number” was given on 9·cb8, where the pairs were proved to be a field.

Names for the new numbers

In La Géométrie (1637), Descartes called such roots “imaginary”, and the word stuck. In 1831 Gauss proposed calling 1, −1 and i the direct, inverse and lateral units, and said that if they had been named so, the obscurity would never have arisen. The ladder keeps “quarter turn” instead of “lateral”, because lateral suggests a shift sideways, and i is not a slide: it is a turn.

Who said it first

Caspar Wessel (1797) and Jean-Robert Argand (1806) first drew such numbers as points and their products as turns. The two parts of 10 with product 40 in 8i’s last standing question are Cardano's (Ars Magna, 1545); Rafael Bombelli (L'Algebra, 1572) was the first to calculate with such numbers to reach real answers.
