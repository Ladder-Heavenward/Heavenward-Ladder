13n · technique

Units and Standards

Rung 13 made the slope at a point. When the input and the output are measured, the slope is a rate, and it carries units: metres per second, grams per mole, joules per kelvin. This step works the units of a rate, checks lines by their units, and changes units by multiplying by one. Then it asks what fixes a unit at all. Since 2019 every base unit is fixed by giving an exact number to a constant of nature, and the mole, the kelvin and the kilogram each have a history of how they got there.

Carry the units through every line. A rate has the units of the output over the units of the input; only like adds to like; a change of units is a multiplication by one.

13n1The Units of a Rate

Let s(t) be the distance a cart has gone, in metres, at time t, in seconds. The chord slope (s(t + h) − s(t))/h is a distance over a time: metres over seconds. The limit as h goes to 0 keeps the units, since every chord slope on the way carries them.

Rate-units technique: if f gives outputs in one unit at inputs in another, f′ has the units of the output over the units of the input, and f″ has the units of the output over the input’s unit squared.

s \text{ in m},\ t \text{ in s} @@ the measured function
\tfrac{s(t + h) - s(t)}{h} \text{ in } \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} @@ a change in metres over a change in seconds
s'(t) \text{ in m/s} @@ the limit keeps the units of every chord slope (13a)
\htmlClass{new}{s''(t) \text{ in m/s}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rate-units technique, applied to s′

So a speed is in metres per second, and the slope of the speed, the acceleration, is in metres per second per second.

13n2Only Like Adds to Like

A unit rides along with its number as a factor. Products multiply units, and quotients divide them. A sum needs one unit for every term: 3 m + 2 s is not a length or a time. Both sides of an equality name one quantity, so they carry one unit.

Unit-check technique: in a correct line every term of a sum has the same units, and both sides of = have the same units. A line that fails the check is wrong; a line that passes may still be wrong.

u \text{ in m/s},\ a \text{ in m/s}^2,\ t \text{ in s} @@ starting speed, acceleration, time
ut \text{ in } \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \cdot \text{s} = \text{m} @@ the seconds cancel
\tfrac12 a t^2 \text{ in } \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} \cdot \text{s}^2 = \text{m} @@ ½ is a bare number and carries no unit
\htmlClass{new}{s = ut + \tfrac12 at^2 \text{ passes: m} = \text{m} + \text{m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the unit-check technique

The check cannot see the ½. s = ut + at² passes it too, and is wrong. The check catches a missing t, not a wrong number.

13n3Changing Units Is Multiplying by One

1 km = 1000 m, so (1000 m)/(1 km) is 1. Multiplying by 1 changes no quantity, and it changes the units in which the quantity is written.

Conversion technique: to change units, multiply by a fraction equal to 1 whose bottom cancels the old unit; for a rate, convert the top and the bottom.

90\,\tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \cdot \tfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \cdot \tfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} @@ two fractions, each equal to 1
= \tfrac{90 \cdot 1000}{3600}\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} @@ km and h cancel
\htmlClass{new}{90\ \text{km/h} = 25\ \text{m/s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the conversion technique

The chain rule says the same of a rate. With t in seconds and τ = t/60 in minutes, t = 60τ, so ds/dτ = (ds/dt)(dt/dτ) = 60 ds/dt (13e). A speed per minute is 60 times the same speed per second, and so it should be: in a minute the cart goes 60 times as far.

13n4What Fixes a Unit

A conversion moves between units. Something must fix a unit in the first place: a standard. A standard can be a thing or a number.

From 1889 to 2019 the kilogram was a thing: a cylinder of platinum and iridium kept near Paris. A thing can change. Its official copies drifted from it by some tens of micrograms over a century, and nothing could say which had moved, since the cylinder was the kilogram by definition.

Since 20 May 2019 every base unit is fixed by a number instead. Seven constants of nature were given exact values, and the units are whatever makes those values true. The second is fixed by a frequency of the caesium atom, 9 192 631 770 per second. The metre is fixed by the speed of light, 299 792 458 m/s. The kilogram is fixed by the Planck constant, h = 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ kg m²/s.

A number cannot drift. Anyone with the right apparatus can realise the unit from it, anywhere.

13n5The Kelvin

Temperature has an absolute zero, and the kelvin counts up from it. From 1954 to 2019 the kelvin was fixed by the triple point of water, where ice, water and vapour stand together: that temperature was 273.16 K exactly.

Since 2019 the kelvin is fixed by the Boltzmann constant, k = 1.380 649 × 10⁻²³ J/K, which links a temperature to an energy. The triple point of water is now a measured value, 273.16 K to within a few parts in ten million.

A Celsius temperature is the kelvin temperature less 273.15: T in K and T − 273.15 in °C. That is a lift, and lifting changes no slope (13d). So a rate per kelvin and the same rate per degree Celsius have the same number: a steel bar that grows 12 millionths of its length per kelvin grows that much per °C too.

13n6The Mole: Counting by Weighing

Atoms are too small to count one by one. The mole counts them by weighing: a mole of anything is a fixed number of its pieces, so the mass of a mole tells you how many pieces a mass holds.

Since 2019 that number is fixed: the Avogadro constant is N_A = 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ per mole, exactly. A mole is exactly that many atoms, molecules or ions.

From 1971 to 2019 the mole was fixed the other way round: as many pieces as there are atoms in 12 g of carbon-12. Then 12 g per mole for carbon-12 was exact, and N_A was measured. Now N_A is exact, and the mass of a mole of carbon-12 is measured: still 12 g, to about a part in a billion.

12\ \text{g of } {}^{12}\text{C} \approx 1\ \text{mol} @@ to about a part in a billion
1\ \text{g} \cdot \tfrac{1\ \text{mol}}{12\ \text{g}} \cdot \tfrac{6.022 \times 10^{23}}{1\ \text{mol}} @@ two conversions, each equal to 1
\htmlClass{new}{1\ \text{g of } {}^{12}\text{C} \approx 5.02 \times 10^{22} \text{ atoms}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the conversion technique

13n7Why Carbon, and Why 12

Atomic masses are written on a scale against one chosen atom. For a long time there were two scales. Chemists set natural oxygen at 16. But natural oxygen is a mix: mostly oxygen-16, with a little oxygen-17 and oxygen-18, found in 1929. Physicists, weighing single isotopes, set oxygen-16 at 16. The two scales differed by about 3 parts in 10 000, enough to matter.

In 1959 to 1961 both sides agreed on a third choice: carbon-12 at exactly 12. It moved the chemists’ numbers by only about 4 parts in 100 000, small enough to leave their tables nearly as they were. And carbon suited the physicists, whose instruments already used carbon compounds as markers of mass.

On that scale natural hydrogen is 1.008. The lone hydrogen-1 atom is 1.007 83; natural hydrogen carries a little heavy hydrogen, and its mix varies with where it was taken, from about 1.007 84 to 1.008 11. That is why tables print 1.008 and not more places.

13n9Running It Backwards

Handed a rate, read its units off: output over input. Handed a line, check its units before its numbers. Handed a quantity in one unit and wanting another, multiply by ones until the old units cancel. Handed a unit, ask what fixes it: since 2019, an exact constant. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The kilogram cylinder, the International Prototype of the Kilogram, was adopted by the first General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1889. The isotopes oxygen-17 and oxygen-18 were found by William Giauque and Herrick Johnston in 1929. The carbon-12 scale was proposed by Josef Mattauch and by Alfred Nier and Alfred Olander in the late 1950s, and adopted by the physicists’ union (IUPAP) in 1960 and the chemists’ union (IUPAC) in 1961. The 26th General Conference voted the redefinition in November 2018, in force from 20 May 2019.

Accepted names: the rate-units and unit-check techniques are dimensional analysis; the conversion technique is the factor-label method, or unit conversion by unit fractions. The seven base units are the second, metre, kilogram, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela, of the International System of Units (SI). A mass on the carbon-12 scale is in daltons, or unified atomic mass units (u); a standard atomic weight is the average for the natural mix.
