7e · technique

Proof by contradiction

“Suppose not.” Two words that open more proofs than any others. You assume the opposite of what you want, follow the moves, and arrive at something totally false. The moves were not wrong, so the assumption was. This step makes the move fast and safe. It shows which of its two forms you are using, and so whether rung 7 is needed at all. It shows how to write the supposition, which is where most mistakes are made, and how to recognise the collision. It shows the descent, which refutes by finding a smaller case. And it shows how to tell when a proof by contradiction is a direct proof wearing a costume, so that you can take the costume off and get more.

To prove a thing, suppose its denial and drive the supposition into something totally false; refuting the denial gives not-not, and not-not out gives the thing.

7e1Two techs with one name

People say “proof by contradiction” for two different techs, and rung 7 is exactly what separates them.

refutationcontradiction proper
you want¬P: “there is no …”, “… is not …”P, a statement that is not a denial
you supposeP¬P
you reach⊥⊥
you write¬P, at once¬¬P, then P
licensed by1b's refute1b's refute, then 7d's not-not-out

From here on, proof by contradiction is the shape: to prove P, assume ¬P, marked as a supposition for contradiction; reach ⊥; refute, which gives ¬¬P; and write P by not-not-out. Its old name is reductio ad absurdum, “leading back to the totally false”. When the statement wanted is a denial ¬Q, supposing Q and refuting is 1b's refutation, 4e's shape, and needs no rung 7. The flag 4e left on proof by contradiction proper is closed here.

4j's no-double-square and 5f's no-fraction-squares-to-2, “no fraction squares to 2”, are refutations: the statement wanted is a denial. So is 2d's outsider-set. Now take “every non-empty set of numbers has a least member”. Proving it by supposing a set has none is contradiction proper. The collision only shows the denial totally false, and the last step is not-not-out. 7·cb4 does exactly that. Knowing which you are doing tells you whether you have used the either-way principle, and so whether your result has an address.

7e2The layout, four parts

Write the four parts so that anyone can see them. The opening line names the supposition and says what kind it is. The middle is ordinary moves. One line names the collision: two lines that cannot both be true. The last line draws the conclusion and names its tech.

\text{suppose, for contradiction, } \htmlClass{new}{\neg P} @@ say it is a supposition, so nobody takes it for a fact
\quad \text{(ordinary moves)} @@ each with its reason
\quad Q,\ \ \neg Q @@ name the two lines that collide
\quad \bot @@ clash
\neg\neg P,\ \ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{P} @@ refute; not-not-out (7d). For a refutation, stop at the refute

7e3Writing the supposition

The commonest error is supposing the wrong thing. The supposition must be the exact denial of the statement, and with 7d every denial can be pushed inward in both directions. Here are the rewrites you will use most, each as one line.

\neg\forall x\,Q(x) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{\exists x\,\neg Q(x)} @@ not-every (7d) and counterexample (2b): “not every” is “some is not”
\neg\exists x\,Q(x) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{\forall x\,\neg Q(x)} @@ not-some (2b): “there is none” is “every one is not”
\neg(A \Rightarrow B) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{A \land \neg B} @@ negated-if forward (7d) and 1f's negated-if: an if is false only when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is not
\neg(A \land B) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{\neg A \lor \neg B} @@ not-both forward (7d) and 1c
\neg(a \lt b) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{b \le a} @@ any-two-compare (4h) for numbers, any-two-compare on ℚ (5d) for rationals
\neg(X \text{ finite}) \;\Leftrightarrow\; X \text{ infinite} @@ 6b's line for infinite

So the denial of “if 3 ∣ n · n then 3 ∣ n”, for every number n, is this: some number n has 3 ∣ n · n and 3 ∤ n. The denial of “there is no greatest even number” is: some even number m has every even number at most m. The first rewrite is the one that needed this rung. Going from “it is totally false that every x has Q” to “here is an x without Q” is a claim that something exists. When it comes from not-every, the some has no address.

7e4Refutations you already own, and the descent

4j refuted p² = 2q² by taking the least q that works and finding a smaller one. Here is the same refutation in a third costume, which shows where the smaller one comes from. It rests on one sameness of integers. Endless-descent technique: the descent step keeps the size of p² − 2q² and changes its sign. In marks: for integers p and q, (2q − p)² − 2(p − q)² = −(p² − 2q²). A refutation of this shape, a smaller one from any one, is called a proof by descent.

(2q - p)^2 - 2(p - q)^2 @@ the left side
= (4q^2 - 4pq + p^2) - 2(p^2 - 2pq + q^2) @@ square-of-a-difference (5i), twice; the displacement pattern in each
= 4q^2 - 4pq + p^2 - 2p^2 + 4pq - 2q^2 @@ spread on ℚ and negative-out on ℚ: the minus belongs to the whole bracket
= \htmlClass{new}{2q^2 - p^2} @@ collect like terms: the pq terms cancel
= -(p^2 - 2q^2) \quad \blacksquare @@ negative-of-a-sum and negative-of-a-negative (5e, 5f)

Read it as counting, and you can see it. Lay out a grid of p rows and p columns, p · p cells (4k's stages-multiply). Shade the cells in the first q rows and the first q columns, q · q of them. Shade again the cells in the last q rows and the last q columns. When q < p < 2q, the two shadings overlap in a grid of 2q − p rows and columns. They miss two grids of p − q rows and columns, one off each end. Counting by 4k's separate-piles, p · p = 2(q · q) − (2q − p)² + 2(p − q)². So suppose p · p = 2(q · q). Then the cells shaded twice exactly match the cells missed: (2q − p)² = 2(p − q)², a smaller pair with the same property. The picture draws it. A refutation of this shape has the accepted name proof by infinite descent: the smaller cases would go down without end.

\text{suppose } q \ne 0 \land p^2 = 2q^2,\ \ q \text{ least such} @@ least-member (4h) on 4j's decidable set of such q, as in no-double-square
q \lt p \lt 2q @@ any-two-compare and keep-order for times (4h): p ≤ q gives p² ≤ q² < 2q²; 2q ≤ p gives 4q² ≤ p²
(2q - p)^2 = 2(p - q)^2 @@ endless-descent, with p² − 2q² = 0
0 \lt p - q \lt q @@ from the line before last, by less-is-adding and keep-order for plus
\htmlClass{new}{p - q} \text{ is a smaller such } q,\ \ \bot @@ a smaller bottom with the property; clash with “least”
\neg\exists p\,\exists q\,(q \ne 0 \land p^2 = 2q^2) \quad \blacksquare @@ refute. A denial: no rung 7

The same map run the other way is a machine for getting close. Undo the descent, (p, q) ↦ (p + 2q, p + q), and endless-descent says the gap p² − 2q² keeps its size and changes its sign. From (1, 1), gap −1, you climb through 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, 99/70, with gaps +1, −1, +1, −1, +1. These are 5f's near misses, each as near as a fraction with its bottom can get, since a gap of 0 is impossible. A proof that something cannot be done, run backwards, became a recipe for doing it as nearly as you like.

7e5Taking the costume off: the endless primes

An ancient book of proofs, which has come down whole, states it as “primes are more than any multitude of primes set out”. That is not “there are endlessly many primes” but “given any list, there is another”. The usual modern proof is by contradiction.

\begin{aligned}&\text{suppose, for contradiction, the}\\ &\quad\text{primes are exactly } p_1, \cdots, p_k\end{aligned} @@ the denial of “the set of primes is infinite”: it has k members, listed by the matching
N = p_1 \cdot \ \cdots\ \cdot p_k + 1;\ \ \text{call } p \text{ a prime divisor of } N @@ 4j's product of a list; prime-divisor
p = p_i \text{ for some } i @@ the supposition: every prime is on the list
p \mid N,\ \ p \mid p_1 \cdot \ \cdots\ \cdot p_k,\ \ p \mid 1,\ \ \bot @@ entry-divides, divides-the-rest; divisor-not-past: no prime divides 1
\neg(\text{the set of primes is finite}) @@ refute: that is 6b's infinite

Look at where the supposition was used: once, in the third line, to say p is on the list. Everything else works for any list of primes, complete or not. So drop the supposition and keep the rest. That is 4j's endless-primes exactly. For any list p₁, ⋯, pₖ there is a prime that is none of them, and the proof hands it to you, a prime divisor of N. The direct proof proves more, needs nothing from rung 7, and its result has an address: the picture computes it. The test, every time: if the supposition is used once, at the end, to produce the collision, the proof is direct underneath. Rewrite it as “given any …, here is …”.

7e6The contrapositive in disguise

“If p · p is even, then p is even. Suppose p · p is even and p is odd. Then p · p is odd, by 4j's odd-times-odd, and not both even and odd, by even-or-odd. Contradiction.” The collision is between the supposed “p · p even” and the derived “p · p odd”, and the derivation used only “p is odd”. Strip the supposition and what is left is a direct proof of the contrapositive: if p is odd, p · p is odd. That is 4j's even-square. When the part of the supposition you used is only the denied conclusion, and the collision is with the hypothesis, you have written a proof by contrapositive. Write it as one. Since the conclusion here is decidable (even-or-odd), even the turn back needs no either-way.

7e7Positive statements that really need rung 7

Some proofs by contradiction cannot have their costume removed. The plainest is not-every itself: from ¬∀x P(x), a thing x with ¬P(x), when nobody can search for one. Here is one about numbers. Member-above-k technique: if a set X of numbers is not a part of S(k), then X has a member greater than k.

\neg(X \subseteq S(k)) @@ given
\text{suppose, for contradiction, } \neg\exists x \in X\,(k \lt x) @@ the denial of what is wanted
\quad \forall x \in X\,\neg(k \lt x) @@ not-some
\quad \text{let } x \in X:\ x \le k,\ \ x \in S(k) @@ any-two-compare (4h); ﹀ ≤ (x < k or x = k, and < is ∈); next-membership (3b)
\quad X \subseteq S(k);\ \ \bot @@ ︿ ⊆; clash with the given
\neg\neg\exists x \in X\,(k \lt x),\ \ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{\exists x \in X\,(k \lt x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; not-not-out

Notice what the proof does not do: it does not tell you where to look. If X were given by a condition you could check, you could search k + 1, k + 2, and so on. But nothing in the column says the search will stop. The proof shows only that the member's absence is totally false. The other shape that needs the rung is “take a smallest counterexample” for a condition nobody can check. 4h's least-member took the least member of a decidable set by searching up from 0. 7·cb4 proves, by contradiction proper, that every set of numbers with a member has one.

7e8Illegal moves

\begin{aligned}&\text{to prove “every prime above 2}\\ &\quad\text{is odd”, suppose every one is even}\,?\end{aligned} @@ not the denial: the denial is “some prime above 2 is even”. Refuting a stronger claim proves nothing
x \cdot x = x,\ x \ne 1:\ \text{cancel } x,\ \text{so } x = 1,\ \bot\,? @@ a collision made by a mistake: cancelling x needs x ≠ 0 (5e's cancel for times on ℚ). The lost answer is 0
\text{the collision never used the supposition}\,? @@ then the givens clash on their own, or a line is wrong: stop and look
\text{no contradiction found, so it is true}\,? @@ failing to break a supposition proves nothing about it
P \text{ used in the middle, while proving } P\,? @@ circling: with ¬P supposed, P collides at once and proves nothing

7e9Running it backwards

Wanting P, try a direct proof first. If it resists, suppose ¬P, push the not all the way in with the rewrites above, and look for something to collide with. Having found a proof by contradiction, look at where the supposition was used. Once, at the end: rewrite it directly and get more. Only its denied conclusion: rewrite it as a contrapositive. Everywhere, with a some coming out of not-every: it is contradiction proper, and its result has no address. Wanting “there is no …”, it is a refutation: stop at the refute. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The gambit is G. H. Hardy’s comparison. The outsider-set is Bertrand Russell’s. Endless-descent, as a sameness, is usually credited to Stanley Tennenbaum; Pierre de Fermat made the method of descent famous. The ancient book of proofs is Euclid’s Elements, and endless-primes is its Book IX, Proposition 20.

Hardy called reductio ad absurdum one of the finest weapons a mathematician has, and compared it to a chess gambit, only bolder: the chess player risks a pawn or a piece, the mathematician offers the whole game.

Hardy, A Mathematician's Apology (1940), §12

Euclid's proposition says that the prime numbers are more than any assigned multitude of prime numbers: given some primes, one finds another, by multiplying them, adding a unit, and taking a prime that measures the result.

Euclid, Elements IX.20, after Heath's translation (1908)

Accepted names

A proof by descent is a proof by infinite descent. The outsider-set is Russell’s paradox. Endless-primes is Euclid’s theorem.
