7e · proof

The Payoff: Double Negation and the Withheld Licences

Since 1b the ladder has held a list of foreclosures. There were directions of techniques that the built techs would not license, and case splits allowed only for decidable statements. There were counts that covered only some of the parts of a set, and a partition proved only in a form that ends in a denial. With either-way a technique, every one of them can be paid, and this step pays them, each in a few lines. First the step from a double not back to the plain statement. Then the five techniques of 1f's rulebook that worked one way only, and 2b's withheld direction for every and some. Then proof by cases on any statement, with a proof that finds a thing it cannot point to. Then 7c's marks, definitions by cases and comparisons. Last, the sets: 2f's and 2i's withheld samenesses, 4k's count of all the parts, and 5·cb2's classes, the same or sharing nothing.

Wherever a column stopped at a double negative or waited for “so or not so”, write the missing line, citing either-way or not-not out. Then notice that the line has no address.

7e1Not-Not Out

Not-not-out technique: two nots in front of a statement may be taken off. In marks: from ¬¬P, P, for any statement P. It is 7b's either-way-gives-not-not-out with 7d's choice-gives-either-way supplying the hypothesis. You already hear this. “It is not that I don’t like you” means I like you, once every statement is so or not so.

\neg\neg P @@ given
P \lor \neg P @@ either-way (the choice-gives-either-way technique, 7d)
\htmlClass{new}{P} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remaining-option technique (1c): the side ¬P is refuted by the given

That is 1b's gap, closed. From here on not-not-out may be cited like a built tech: from ¬¬P, write P. With 1b's not-not-in, which went the other way, P and ¬¬P are interchangeable, and 1f's not-not technique may be used both ways. 6b's flag on non-empty closes here too: from A ≠ ∅, 6b's non-empty technique writes ¬¬∃x (x ∈ A), and not-not-out takes it to ∃x (x ∈ A). Not-not-out has the accepted name double negation elimination: two nots taken off at once.

7e2Not-Both, and If as Or

1f's licence column marked four more techniques as licensed in one direction only: not-both, if as or, the contrapositive, and negating an if. Here is each missing direction, one move per line. Every column cites either-way or not-not-out at least once, and you can see exactly where.

Not-both forward technique: if two statements are not both true, one of them is false. In marks: ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇒ ¬P ∨ ¬Q. You already know this one. “You can’t have cake and ice cream both” means one of them you can’t have.

\text{assume } \neg(P \land Q) @@ to prove an if
P \lor \neg P @@ either-way
\text{case } P:\ \text{assume } Q,\ \ P \land Q,\ \ \bot @@ join; clash with the assumption
\quad \neg Q,\ \ \neg P \lor \neg Q @@ refute; add an option
\text{case } \neg P:\ \ \neg P \lor \neg Q @@ add an option
\htmlClass{new}{\neg(P \land Q) \Rightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases; prove an if

If-as-or forward technique: when an if is true, its hypothesis is false or its conclusion is true. In marks: (P ⇒ Q) ⇒ ¬P ∨ Q. Assume P ⇒ Q. Either-way at P. In the case P, carry the if through to Q and add an option; in the case ¬P, add an option. Reason by cases; prove an if. ∎ You know this too. “If you’re late, you miss the bus” comes to “you are on time, or you miss the bus”.

7e3Turn-and-Deny Turned Back, and the Denied If

Turn-and-deny turned back technique: if denying Q gives the denial of P, then P gives Q. In marks: (¬Q ⇒ ¬P) ⇒ (P ⇒ Q).

\text{assume } \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P;\ \ \text{assume } P @@ to prove an if, twice
\quad \text{assume } \neg Q;\ \ \neg P;\ \ \bot @@ aiming to refute; carry through; clash with P
\quad \neg\neg Q @@ refute
\quad \htmlClass{new}{Q} @@ not-not-out
\htmlClass{new}{(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P) \Rightarrow (P \Rightarrow Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ prove an if, twice

Negated-if forward technique: when an if is false, its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is false. In marks: ¬(P ⇒ Q) ⇒ P ∧ ¬Q. You already reason this way. If “if it rains, the match is off” turned out false, then it rained and the match went ahead.

\text{assume } \neg(P \Rightarrow Q) @@ to prove an if
\quad \text{assume } Q;\ \ P \Rightarrow Q;\ \ \bot;\ \ \neg Q @@ free-if (1d), from the conclusion; clash; refute
\quad \text{assume } \neg P;\ \ P \Rightarrow Q;\ \ \bot;\ \ \neg\neg P @@ free-if, from the denied hypothesis; clash; refute
\quad P @@ not-not-out
\htmlClass{new}{P \land \neg Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ join; prove an if

7e4The Licence Column, Cleared

That clears the column. The flags that 1c, 1d and 1e opened on these four directions, and 1f recorded in its licence column, are closed.

From here on, every technique of 1f's rulebook may be used in both directions, for any statements, in any place. The licence column reads “both ways, any” on every row, and 1f's place rule is no longer needed. Likewise 1e's = between statements licenses rewriting both ways for any statements, and 2f's techniques for sets may be used as samenesses wherever 1f's may.

Notice what did not change. The converse and the inverse of an if are still not techniques: Gomez the dog still sinks “if it is an animal, it is a cat”. A rulebook technique is a matching of columns, and either-way licenses every one; it licenses nothing that was not one.

7e5Every and Some, Both Ways

2b withheld one direction of pushing a not through an every, and 2j needed it to read the diagram of opposition in full. Not-every technique: if not everything has a property, something lacks it. In marks: ¬∀x P(x) ⇒ ∃x ¬P(x).

\text{assume } \neg\forall x\,P(x) @@ to prove an if
\quad \text{assume } \neg\exists x\,\neg P(x) @@ aiming to refute
\quad \forall x\,\neg\neg P(x) @@ the not-some technique (2b)
\quad \text{let } a \text{ be anything: } \neg\neg P(a),\ \ P(a) @@ use the every; not-not-out
\quad \forall x\,P(x);\ \ \bot @@ speak of everything; clash
\neg\neg\exists x\,\neg P(x);\ \ \htmlClass{new}{\exists x\,\neg P(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; not-not-out; prove an if

With 2b's counterexample for the other direction, ¬∀ and ∃¬ are interchangeable; with not-some, ¬∃ and ∀¬ already were. So a not now passes through every and some in both directions, changing each, as 2b's method said it would. The flag that 2b opened on this direction, and 2j met, is closed. The bounded forms come the same way: ¬∀x ∈ d P(x) gives ∃x ∈ d ¬P(x), with negated-if forward unpacking the denied if. In the diagram of opposition, the A and O corners now stand to each other as the E and I corners did. Each is the exact denial of the other, by techs.

A not can now pass through a whole run of signs. It takes one move for each sign, and each sign turns into its partner as the not passes. Take “not everyone has a mother”:

The first move takes the not past the every, by not-every. 2b could not make it. The second takes it past the some, by 2b's not-some technique. Someone has no mother, as far as this column can say: no one at all is that person's mother.

Look at the some that not-every hands you. It came from a refutation and not-not-out. Nothing in the column names a thing x with ¬P(x). A some reached this way is a some with no address.

7e6Every Statement Has a Row

1e promised that rung 7 would grant every statement a row. Either-way does exactly that: for any statement P, P or ¬P, so P sits in the row T or the row F. That flag of 1e is closed. And then 1e's tables and rung 1's techs stop parting company. Always-true-lines technique: a statement built from letters with the joining words, whose column is all T, can be proved. The recipe is from 1935. Split on each letter by either-way; that makes one case for each row. Inside a case, work the row from the inside out, as 1e did, but with a tech at each machine instead of a table entry:

A tautology comes out T in every row, so every case ends in the tautology itself, and reasoning by cases puts it outside. For a statement you write down, the column is finite. Here is one that 7b's world of stages refused. One-way-or-the-other technique: of any two statements, one gives the other. In marks: (P ⇒ Q) ∨ (Q ⇒ P). Either-way at Q. In the case Q, P ⇒ Q by free-if, and add an option. In the case ¬Q, Q ⇒ P by free-if, and add an option. Reason by cases. ∎ Two lines of cases, where rung 1 alone had none at all.

7e7Proof by Cases on Any Statement

From here on, a proof may split on any statement P: case P, reach R; case ¬P, reach R; so R, by either-way and reasoning by cases. The split is 4e's proof by cases with the or supplied by either-way. 4e allowed it only for settled statements; that flag is closed.

Here is the classic, and it should make you uneasy. Room-of-laughers technique: in any room with somebody in it, there is a person such that, if that person is laughing, everybody in the room is laughing. In marks, for a non-empty set X and any condition L:

\forall x \in X\,L(x) \;\lor\; \neg\forall x \in X\,L(x) @@ either-way: split on whether everybody laughs
\text{case everybody:}\ \text{call } y \in X @@ X is non-empty; use the some
\quad L(y) \Rightarrow \forall x \in X\,L(x) @@ free-if (1d): we have the conclusion
\text{case not everybody:}\ \exists x \in X\,\neg L(x),\ \text{call it } y @@ not-every, bounded form; use the some
\quad L(y) \Rightarrow \forall x \in X\,L(x) @@ free-if: the hypothesis is denied
\htmlClass{new}{\exists y \in X\,\big(L(y) \Rightarrow \forall x \in X\,L(x)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ show an example, in each case; reason by cases

Which person? If everyone is laughing, anyone. If not, someone who is not. In a room you can see, you look, and finite-search of 7c settles which case you are in. Now take for the room the even numbers above 2, and for L(n), “n is a sum of two primes”. Room-of-laughers gives a number n such that, if n is a sum of two primes, every even number above 2 is. The even-sum guess hangs on a single number, and nobody can name it. That is what 7d promised: from the even-sum statement, a number with a strange property and no address. The proof is correct. It is also worth exactly as much as it says: a thing exists, and nothing more.

7e8Marks, Definitions by Cases, and Comparisons

7c's mark ⟦P⟧ = {x ∈ 1 : P} had 1 member when P was true and none when ¬P was true, and for an undecided P it was only “a part of 1”. Every-mark-is-a-bit technique: the mark of any statement is 0 or 1. In marks: for any statement P, ⟦P⟧ = 0 ∨ ⟦P⟧ = 1. Either-way at P. In the case P, every member of 1 passes, so ⟦P⟧ = 1 by same-members. In the case ¬P, nothing passes, so ⟦P⟧ = 0 by one-empty-set. ∎ So every mark is a number, 0 or 1. (The annex 4·comp computes with marks of settled statements; its techniques for marks may now be used for every statement, and 4·comp's flag on unsettled marks is closed.)

Take G for the even-sum guess itself, “every even number above 2 is a sum of two primes”, and let m = ⟦G⟧. Then m is a number. m = 0 or m = 1, and m ≤ 1, and m < 1 exactly when G fails. Every one of these is true, and nobody can tell you m. It is a perfectly good number with an unknown name.

The same goes for definitions. 4i licensed a step given by cases on a decidable statement, so that exactly one case is so at each input. Exactly one case of P ∨ ¬P is so, for any P, by either-way and clash.

From here on, a function or a name may be defined by cases on any statement: “t is u if P, and v if ¬P” names exactly one thing.

And comparisons. For numbers and fractions written in marks, “one number is less than another, or not” was already earned, by settled-order and settled-order on ℚ. What changes is that no comparison needs earning any more. Any two numbers the ladder can name compare, however they are named. ⟦G⟧ compares with 1. The least member of a set that nobody can search, which 7·cb4 shows exists, compares with 5. The split is unrestricted, and the case you are in may be unknown.

7e9Sets: The Withheld Samenesses

2f's techniques for sets matched 1f's, with the same withheld direction. Not-both in sets technique: taking away what is in both is taking away what is outside one or the other. In marks: c ∖ (d ∩ b) = (c ∖ d) ∪ (c ∖ b). Chase a member. x ∈ c ∖ (d ∩ b) unpacks to x ∈ c ∧ ¬(x ∈ d ∧ x ∈ b). Not-both forward turns the second part into x ∉ d ∨ x ∉ b. Spread the and over the or (1f's spread and over or) and pack each side: x ∈ (c ∖ d) ∪ (c ∖ b). Every line is now an exactly-when, so same-members closes it. ∎ Splits-by-any-set technique: any set b splits a set d into the part outside b and the part inside b. In marks: (d ∖ b) ∪ (d ∩ b) = d, and d ∖ (d ∖ b) = d ∩ b. For the first, x ∈ d gives x ∉ b ∨ x ∈ b by either-way, and each case lands in one piece; the other inclusion 2i already had. For the second, take x ∉ d ∖ b with x ∈ d. That gives ¬¬(x ∈ b): assume x ∉ b, join with x ∈ d, clash, refute. Then not-not-out gives x ∈ b. ∎ These were 2i's decidable-only steps; they may now be used for any sets. The flags 2f and 2i left on them are closed.

4k proved that a set with n members has 2ⁿ decidable parts, a decidable part s of X being one with ∀x ∈ X (x ∈ s ∨ x ∉ s). That line is a statement about s, and either-way proves it for every part s, member by member. All-parts-counted technique: if X has n members, then 𝒫(X) has 2ⁿ members. Every member of 𝒫(X) is a decidable part, by either-way. So 𝒫(X) is the set of decidable parts, by same-members, and settled-parts counts them. ∎ That closes 4k's flag on the parts it could not count. 2g's keep-or-drop listing is complete for every set, and 1e's tables of n letters have 2ⁿ rows for the same reason. Take a part given by an undecided condition, such as {x ∈ X : x = cat ∧ G}. It is one of the listed parts, {cat} or ∅; either-way says so, and does not say which. (7c's word “decidable”, the or earned without either-way, keeps its job: it marks the parts you can list by looking.)

5·cb2 proved that classes sharing a member are the same, and that different classes share nothing. Both were honest before this rung, because each ends in a denial or uses a shared member in hand. It did not prove the or. Same-or-sharing-nothing technique: two classes of a sameness relation are the same or share nothing. In marks: for a sameness relation on a set d, and x, y ∈ d, [x] = [y] ∨ [x] ∩ [y] = ∅. Either-way at [x] = [y]. In the case of sameness, add an option. In the other, 5·cb2's classes-partition gives [x] ∩ [y] = ∅; add an option. ∎ The same move works for any two pieces of a partition. And 6c's split “B = A or B ≠ A”, earned there by finite-is-settled for finite collections, may now be made for the members of any collection. The flags 5·cb2 and 6c left on these ors are closed.

7e10What Changed, and What Did Not

Nothing proved before this rung is lost. Every constructive column still stands, and is still worth more than a classical one with the same last line, because it hands you what it claims exists. From here on the ladder's columns cite either-way or not-not-out wherever they use them, so that you can always see which lines have no address. What has changed is that the double negatives are gone, the licence column is full, and every case split is allowed.

Now your logic is classical.

7e12Running It Backwards

Wanting P and having only ¬¬P, cite not-not-out. Wanting to push a not inward, do it freely now, and note each line that used a withheld direction, since its result may have no address. Wanting a case split, split on anything. If the conclusion is a some, ask whether the proof says which, and if not, whether a look or a search could. Wanting a count of all the parts, all-parts-counted. Handed a classical proof, ask the constructive question: does it tell me which? Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The recipe for always-true-lines is László Kalmár’s (1935). One-way-or-the-other is named for Michael Dummett. The diagram of opposition is Aristotle’s. The question “does it tell me which?” is L. E. J. Brouwer’s, and the answer about the telescope is David Hilbert’s.

Hilbert answered Brouwer in 1927. To take the law of excluded middle away from the mathematician, he said, would be like taking the telescope from the astronomer, or forbidding the boxer the use of his fists.

Hilbert, “The Foundations of Mathematics” (1927 lecture), trans. in van Heijenoort, From Frege to Gödel (1967); paraphrase

Accepted names

Not-not-out is double negation elimination. Not-both and its partner are De Morgan’s laws. Always-true-lines is the completeness of the truth tables for the statement logic. 4·comp’s techniques for marks are the laws of Boolean algebra. The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
