How Long Must a Heat Exchanger Be?

How long must a heat exchanger be? In a factory, hot oil leaving a machine must be cooled, and cold water coming in needs warming. Run the two in neighbouring pipes and heat passes through the wall from one to the other, which saves both a cooler and a heater. The heat flows faster where the gap in temperature is big, and the gap shrinks as the streams travel. This page finds that the gap shrinks by the same fraction each metre, a 17j decay; finds the average gap that sets the length, a log of a ratio; shows why running the streams in opposite directions beats running them side by side; and measures how well an exchanger does against the best it could.

Heat passes at a rate proportional to the local gap Δ between the streams. The gap shrinks at a rate proportional to itself, so it decays: Δ(x) = Δ₀e^(−kx). The total heat is U·A times the log-mean gap (Δ₁ − Δ₂)/ln(Δ₁/Δ₂). Opposite flows keep the gap bigger along the whole length.

19eng21Two Streams, Heat Flowing by the Gap

Evita’s plant has hot oil entering at 120 °C that must leave at 60 °C, and cold water entering at 20 °C. Oil flows through an inner pipe and water through a jacket around it. Each metre of pipe passes heat at a rate U·P·Δ, where Δ is the local gap in temperature, P the pipe’s wetted width around, and U a number for how easily the wall and fluids pass heat.

From here on, on this page, C is a stream’s heat flow capacity: the heat it carries per degree each second (mass per second times heat per kg per degree). Losing heat Q drops a stream’s temperature by Q/C. The oil’s C is half the water’s, so the water warms half as much as the oil cools.

\text{oil: } 120 \to 60,\ \text{a drop of } 60 @@ 
C_w = 2\,C_o \;\Rightarrow\; \text{water rises by } 30:\ 20 \to 50 @@ the same heat Q
\htmlClass{new}{Q = C_o \cdot 60} @@ per second

19eng22The Gap Shrinks by the Same Fraction per Metre

Run both streams the same way (side by side). Over a short length dx, heat U·P·Δ·dx passes: the oil cools by that over C_o and the water warms by that over C_w. Both changes close the gap.

d\Delta = -\,U P\,\Delta\,dx\left(\tfrac{1}{C_o} + \tfrac{1}{C_w}\right) @@ oil down, water up
\dfrac{d\Delta}{dx} = -k\,\Delta,\quad k = U P\left(\tfrac{1}{C_o} + \tfrac{1}{C_w}\right) @@ a rate proportional to itself
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta(x) = \Delta_0\, e^{-kx}} @@ growth and decay (17j)

The gap falls by the same fraction each metre. At the start it is 100 °C (120 against 20); every metre takes away the same share of what is left. Like any decay, it never quite reaches 0.

19eng23The Log-Mean Gap

The heat over the whole pipe is the sum of U·P·Δ·dx: U times the pipe’s area A times the average gap. The decay makes that average a log.

\Delta_1 - \Delta_2 = Q\left(\tfrac{1}{C_o} + \tfrac{1}{C_w}\right) @@ the gap closes by the total heat over both capacities
\ln \tfrac{\Delta_1}{\Delta_2} = k L = U A\left(\tfrac{1}{C_o} + \tfrac{1}{C_w}\right) @@ the decay over length L, with A = PL
\htmlClass{new}{Q = U A\,\dfrac{\Delta_1 - \Delta_2}{\ln(\Delta_1 / \Delta_2)}} @@ divide the first by the second

Log-mean technique: the heat an exchanger passes is U times its area times the log-mean gap, the difference of the end gaps over the log of their ratio. To size an exchanger, a designer works out Q from what each stream must do, the end gaps from the temperatures, and then the area: A = Q ÷ (U × log-mean gap).

19eng24Counterflow Beats Parallel Flow

Now run the water the other way, entering where the oil leaves. Same temperatures in and out; different end gaps.

\text{side by side: ends } 120 - 20 = 100,\ \ 60 - 50 = 10 @@ 
\text{log-mean} = \dfrac{100 - 10}{\ln 10} \approx 39.1 @@ 
\text{opposite: ends } 120 - 50 = 70,\ \ 60 - 20 = 40 @@ hot in meets warm out
\text{log-mean} = \dfrac{70 - 40}{\ln 1.75} \approx 53.6 @@ the same formula holds
\htmlClass{new}{\text{opposite flow needs } \tfrac{39.1}{53.6} \approx 73\% \text{ of the area}} @@ for the same job

Running the streams in opposite directions keeps the gap more even along the pipe, so the same job needs less area. Side by side, the water can never leave hotter than the oil leaves; opposite, it can leave hotter than the oil’s outlet, because it last meets the hottest oil. Almost every industrial exchanger runs its streams opposite for this reason.

19eng25Effectiveness: the Share of the Most That Could Pass

What is the most heat any exchanger, however long, could pass between these streams? The stream with the smaller C changes temperature most per unit of heat; at best it could swing all the way from its inlet to the other stream’s inlet.

Q_{\max} = C_{\min}\,(T_{\text{hot in}} - T_{\text{cold in}}) = C_o \cdot 100 @@ oil cooled all the way to 20
Q = C_o \cdot 60 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon = \tfrac{Q}{Q_{\max}} = 0.6} @@ the exchanger does 60% of the most it could

An exchanger’s effectiveness is the heat it passes over the most any exchanger could pass between the same two inlets; longer and opposite-flow exchangers push it toward 1, with less and less gain per metre. A plant engineer reading the four temperatures on a running exchanger works out its effectiveness each month; when it falls, the pipe walls are fouling and need cleaning.

Accepted names

The log-mean gap is the log mean temperature difference, LMTD; U is the overall heat transfer coefficient; C is the heat capacity rate. Opposite flow is counterflow, side by side parallel flow. ε is the effectiveness, and UA/C_min is the number of transfer units, NTU.

Who said it first

The log mean temperature difference was in engineering use by the early twentieth century; the effectiveness and NTU method was set out by Kays and London in 1955 (not yet revisited).
