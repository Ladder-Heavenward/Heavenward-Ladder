How far north am I?

How far north am I? Out of sight of land, a ship’s crew could see only water and sky. Yet for centuries they found how far north they were, every clear night, with a stick, a mirror or two, and one star. What makes it hard is that a ship rolls, the horizon is the only steady line, and a degree of error is sixty miles. This page shows, with 21’s angles, 20’s flips and 18·nav’s scale, that the pole star’s height is your latitude, that two mirrors measure an angle twice over, and that heading and speed carried forward as arrows say where you are between sightings.

The pole star sits almost on the line of the earth’s axis, so its height above the horizon is your latitude. Two mirrors turned apart turn a ray by twice the angle between them. A run on a steady heading moves the ship east by the distance run times the sine of the heading, and north by the distance times its cosine, and runs add part by part.

21nav1The pole star’s height is your latitude

Your latitude φ is the angle at the earth’s centre between your place and the equator (21·cb18). The pole star is so far off that its rays reach every place on earth parallel to the earth’s axis. Your horizon is square to the line from the centre to you, as a tangent is square to its radius (21q).

\angle(\text{up},\ \text{equator}) = \varphi @@ up is along the radius to you; latitude, measured at the centre
\angle(\text{up},\ \text{axis}) = 90^\circ - \varphi @@ the axis is square to the equator: the complement (21e)
\angle(\text{horizon},\ \text{axis}) = 90^\circ - (90^\circ - \varphi) @@ the horizon is square to up (21q)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{height of the pole star} = \varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ its rays run along the axis

Pole-star technique: the height of the pole star above the horizon is your latitude. The star sits about 0.7° from the true pole, so every reading carries a cloud of ±0.7° until it is corrected by the time of night (21a). A degree of latitude is 60 nautical miles, so the cloud is about 40 miles each way.

21nav2Two mirrors measure twice

On a rolling deck you cannot hold a ruler steady against the sky. Instead, look at the horizon straight through a clear glass, and at the star in a mirror beside it, bounced off a second mirror that you turn. When the star sits on the horizon in the glass, read the turn.

\text{each bounce flips the ray across its mirror} @@ 20·phys.1
\text{two flips across lines meeting at } \beta = \text{a turn by } 2\beta @@ 20e
\htmlClass{new}{\text{mirrors } \beta \text{ apart bend the ray by } 2\beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the star’s height is twice the mirrors’ angle

Two-mirror technique: turning one mirror by β turns the seen star by 2β, so an arc of 60° reads angles up to 120°. The star and horizon move together as the ship rolls, so the reading holds. The instrument’s arc is a sixth of a circle, and its scale is marked double.

21nav3By day: the noon sun

By day there is no pole star, but 21·then found the noon sun φ − δ from overhead, where δ is the latitude where the sun stands overhead that day. Tables give δ for every day of the year. The sun’s height above the horizon at noon is h = 90° − (φ − δ).

h = 90^\circ - (\varphi - \delta) @@ height is the complement of the angle from overhead
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 90^\circ - h + \delta} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve for φ

Noon technique: your latitude is 90° less the noon sun’s height, plus the day’s δ. Noon is found without a clock: it is the moment the sun stops climbing.

21nav4Arrows carried forward

Between sightings, a ship keeps its place by its heading and its speed. A heading θ is measured from north, turning toward east. A run at speed v on heading θ for time t moves the ship vt sin θ east and vt cos θ north. Runs add part by part. Its accepted name is dead reckoning.

10 \text{ knots},\ 060^\circ,\ 3 \text{ h}:\ \ (30\sin 60^\circ,\ 30\cos 60^\circ) \approx (25.98,\ 15.00) @@ east, north, in nautical miles
8 \text{ knots},\ 150^\circ,\ 2 \text{ h}:\ \ (16\sin 150^\circ,\ 16\cos 150^\circ) \approx (8.00,\ -13.86) @@ the second run
\text{sum} \approx (33.98,\ 1.14) @@ part by part
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 34.0 \text{ nm away, on a heading of about } 088^\circ} @@ size by distance; 1.14 nm north is 1.14′ of latitude

Reckoning technique: heading and speed carried forward as arrows give your place between sightings, and its error grows with every run. A nautical mile is defined as one minute of latitude, a sixtieth of a degree of arc along a line through both poles. So the north part of the reckoning reads straight into latitude, and the pole star checks it.

The accepted names

The pole star is Polaris; its height is its altitude. The two-mirror instrument is the sextant (an arc of a sixth of a circle) or the octant; the turning mirror is the index mirror and the clear glass the horizon glass. The noon sighting is a meridian altitude, δ is the sun’s declination, and the tables are the nautical almanac. Dead reckoning carries course and speed forward; a heading is a course, measured in degrees true.

Who said it first

Arab and Indian sailors measured the pole star’s height with the kamal, a board on a knotted string, by the ninth century. John Hadley (1731) and Thomas Godfrey built the two-mirror octant independently. In 21·nav.6.5, the longitude problem was settled two ways: by John Harrison’s sea clocks (1730s–1761) and by Nevil Maskelyne’s lunar distance tables (1767), whose rivalry ran for decades. The nautical mile as a minute of latitude dates from the seventeenth century.
