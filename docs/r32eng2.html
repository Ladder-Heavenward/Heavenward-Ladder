How Does a Filter Know Which Sounds to Let Through?

How does a filter know which sounds to let through? A recording has a low hum from the mains and a high hiss from the tape, and the voice between them. A box of two parts, a resistor and a capacitor, can quiet the hiss and keep the voice. The box does not know anything; it is a system that is straight and steady in time, and every such system treats each turning on its own. This page shows that one click tells everything about such a system, that turnings go through it changed only in size and timing, works out the resistor and capacitor with rung 24’s rate statements, chains filters by multiplying, and builds the same filter from numbers.

A system that is straight (doubles and sums pass through) and steady (a delay in gives the same delay out) is fixed by its response h to a click: the output is the convolution of the input with h. A turning e^(2πift) comes out as H(f) e^(2πift), with H the transform of h. For the resistor and capacitor, τy′ + y = x gives H = 1/(1 + 2πifτ): turnings below 1/(2πτ) pass, those above fade. Chained filters multiply their H.

32eng21One Click Tells All

From here on, on this page, a system turns an input signal x(t) into an output y(t). It is straight if sums and multiples pass through, and steady if delaying the input only delays the output. Its click response h is its output when the input is a single very short click at time 0.

x \approx \textstyle\sum x(s)\,\Delta s \cdot \text{click at } s @@ the input as many small clicks
\text{each gives } x(s)\,\Delta s \cdot h(t - s) @@ steady: the same response, delayed
y(t) \approx \textstyle\sum x(s)\, h(t - s)\,\Delta s @@ straight: the responses add
\htmlClass{new}{y = x * h = \textstyle\int x(s)\,h(t - s)\,ds} @@ the convolution (32d)

Click technique: a straight, steady system is fixed by its click response: every output is the input convolved with it. Clap in a hall and record the echo, and you can hear any music as that hall would play it.

32eng22Turnings Pass Through

Feed in a single turning, x(t) = e2πift.

y(t) = \textstyle\int e^{2\pi i f s}\, h(t - s)\,ds @@ the convolution
= e^{2\pi i f t} \textstyle\int h(u)\, e^{-2\pi i f u}\,du @@ put u = t − s (16d)
H(f) = \textstyle\int h(u)\, e^{-2\pi i f u}\,du @@ the transform of h (32e)
\htmlClass{new}{y = H(f)\, e^{2\pi i f t}} @@ the same turning, scaled and shifted

Turning technique: a turning goes through a straight, steady system unchanged in frequency, multiplied by the complex number H(f): |H| scales it and the angle of H delays it. Break any signal into turnings, multiply each by H, and add them back: that is what the system does.

32eng23A Resistor and a Capacitor

Evita builds a filter: the signal drives current through a resistor R onto a capacitor C, and the output is the capacitor’s voltage. The current is (x − y)/R, and it fills the capacitor: C y′ = (x − y)/R.

\tau y\prime + y = x,\ \ \tau = RC @@ a straight rate statement (24g)
y = H e^{2\pi i f t}:\ \tau(2\pi i f)H + H = 1 @@ a turning in
H(f) = \dfrac{1}{1 + 2\pi i f \tau},\ \ |H| = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f \tau)^2}} @@ divide; size of a complex number (25c)
\tau = 1 \text{ ms}:\ 50 \text{ Hz} \to 0.95,\ \ 5000 \text{ Hz} \to 0.03 @@ hum kept, hiss cut
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the corner } f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau) \approx 160 \text{ Hz}: |H| = 1/\sqrt2} @@ below passes, above fades

Low-pass technique: a resistor feeding a capacitor passes turnings below 1/(2πRC) and weakens those above in proportion to their frequency. Swap the parts and it passes the highs instead. Here, to keep a voice and cut the hiss, Evita would choose τ smaller, putting the corner near 3000 Hz.

32eng24Chains Multiply, in Decibels They Add

Feed one filter into another: each turning is multiplied by H₁ and then by H₂. Engineers measure |H| in logs.

H_{\text{chain}}(f) = H_1(f)\, H_2(f) @@ one turning, two multiplications
\text{decibels: } 20\log_{10}|H| @@ logs turn products into sums (17c)
5000 \text{ Hz through one filter: } 20\log_{10} 0.032 \approx -30 \text{ dB} @@ 32eng23’s 0.03
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two in a chain: } -60 \text{ dB}} @@ the decibels add

Filters in a chain multiply their H, so their decibels add. Far above its corner, each resistor-capacitor stage loses 20 dB for every tenfold rise in frequency; stacking stages makes the cut as steep as wanted.

32eng25The Same Filter From Numbers

A phone filters sound as numbers, samples taken fs times a second (32g). Replace the slope by a step: yn = a yn−1 + (1 − a) xn, which is 17·logi’s smoothing.

y_n = a\,y_{n-1} + (1 - a)\,x_n @@ nudge toward each new sample
x_n = e^{2\pi i f n / f_s}:\ H = \dfrac{1 - a}{1 - a\,e^{-2\pi i f / f_s}} @@ a turning in
f \text{ small}: H \approx 1;\ \ f = f_s/2: H = \dfrac{1 - a}{1 + a} @@ slowest and fastest
\htmlClass{new}{a = 0.9:\ \text{the fastest turnings are cut to } 0.1/1.9 \approx 0.05} @@ a low-pass filter in one line of code

A filter is a rule, not a box: the same straight, steady rule in numbers does what the resistor and capacitor do. Every sound a phone plays has passed through hundreds of such lines.

Accepted names

A straight, steady system is a linear time-invariant (LTI) system. The click response is the impulse response, and H(f) the frequency response or transfer function. The resistor-capacitor filter is an RC low-pass filter, and its corner the cutoff frequency. 20 dB per tenfold is 20 dB per decade, drawn on a Bode plot. The number filter is a digital filter, here a first-order IIR filter.

Who said it first

Hendrik Bode drew the decibel charts of filters at Bell Labs in the 1930s and 1940s. Oliver Heaviside had treated circuits by their response to sudden inputs in the 1880s and 1890s.
