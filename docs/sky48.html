∞48 · newsflash

Can a Market Find Its Own Price?

Can a market find its own price? Rung 12 proved that a market for one good has a price where buyers and sellers match, and warned that a proof that a price exists is not a proof that anyone finds it. This page follows that warning as far as it has been followed. First the price is widened to a game: any number of players, each with finitely many choices, each free to choose by chance. In 1950 it was shown that every such game has a rest, a choice of chances for everyone from which nobody gains by moving alone. The proof is a kept point of a map that nudges each player toward the choices that would do better, and the kept point comes from the door count of the fifteenth blue sky step, which works in every dimension. The same rest, for two players where one gains what the other loses, is the value of the game that rung 42 found by prices. Then comes the turn. The door count is also a walk, through doors, from the rim to a full cell, and the walk can be very long. In 1994 the problems whose answer is the end of such a walk were gathered into one class. Late in 2005, in three steps and by two groups, finding a rest was shown to be as hard as every problem in that class, first for four players, then three, then two, and the results were presented in 2006. Nobody knows a fast way to find the end of a long walk, and most expect there is none. So a market, or a game, may be proved to have a rest that no method of reasonable speed can find. The page proves the rest, the value, the end of the walk and one piece of the game that hides a walk, and it brings the many-goods price back to rung 12 with the same kept point.

A game gives player i the average gain uᵢ(σ) on a list σ of mixes. The map σᵢₖ ↦ (σᵢₖ + gᵢₖ)/(1 + Σₗ gᵢₗ), with gᵢₖ = max(0, uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ) − uᵢ(σ)), is continuous on a set the same shape as a ball, so it keeps a point (∞15), and a kept point has every gᵢₖ = 0: a rest. With two players and xᵀMy, a rest gives max min = min max. A graph where each dot has one link in and one out at most, given by programs, has a second end; PPAD is every search that fast-reduces to finding it, and since 2006 finding a rest is PPAD-complete. For a market with excess demand z(p) and p · z(p) = 0, the same map gives prices with every zₖ ≤ 0.

sky48.1Many Players, Mixed Moves

42h1 set out a game of two players with one grid of gains, and 42h4 gave each player a grid of their own. Here there are n players. Player i has finitely many choices; a mix σᵢ is a list of chances σᵢₖ ≥ 0 adding to 1, one for each choice k (30f). A list σ = (σ₁, …, σₙ) of mixes, one per player, chosen independently, gives player i an average gain uᵢ(σ), by 30g’s average-adds technique. Write uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ) for that average when player i plays choice k for certain and the others keep their mixes.

σ is a rest when no player can raise their own average by changing their own mix alone: uᵢ(τ, σ₋ᵢ) ≤ uᵢ(σ) for every player i and every mix τ. 42h4 called it an equilibrium. Two facts make a rest checkable.

Below-average technique: uᵢ(σ) is the average, with the chances σᵢₖ, of the gains uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ); so some choice k with σᵢₖ > 0 has uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ) ≤ uᵢ(σ), and no choice beats every other unless the mix uses it alone.

Pure-check technique: σ is a rest exactly when no player gains by switching to a single choice played for certain: uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ) ≤ uᵢ(σ) for every i and k.

u_i(\sigma) = \textstyle\sum_k \sigma_{ik}\,u_i(k, \sigma_{-i}) @@ player i first draws a choice by σᵢ, independently of the others: averages add (30g)
\text{if every used } k \text{ had } u_i(k, \sigma_{-i}) \gt u_i(\sigma) \text{, the average would exceed } u_i(\sigma) @@ the chances add to 1
\text{some used } k:\ u_i(k, \sigma_{-i}) \le u_i(\sigma) @@ the below-average technique
u_i(\tau, \sigma_{-i}) = \textstyle\sum_k \tau_k\,u_i(k, \sigma_{-i}) \le \sum_k \tau_k\,u_i(\sigma) = u_i(\sigma) @@ if every single choice is no better, no mix of them is
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma \text{ is a rest} \iff u_i(k, \sigma_{-i}) \le u_i(\sigma) \text{ for all } i, k} \quad \blacksquare @@ single choices are mixes too: the pure-check technique

Two friends each pick a café, A or B, without talking. Together at A, the first gains 2 and the second 1; together at B, the first gains 1 and the second 2; apart, both gain 0. Both at A is a rest: a lone switch leaves the switcher apart, with 0. Both at B is another. A third mixes. If the first goes to A with chance x, the second’s A earns x and B earns 2(1 − x); these are equal at x = ⅔. If the second goes to A with chance y, the first’s A earns 2y and B earns 1 − y; equal at y = ⅓. At x = ⅔, y = ⅓ every single choice earns ⅔, which is also each player’s average, so by the pure-check technique it is a rest, and both do worse there than at either café together.

sky48.2Every Game Has a Rest

42h4 stated that every game with finitely many players and choices has a rest, and left the proof out. sky15.6’s every-dimension kept-point technique pays for it. The lists of mixes form a set P: one solid triangle of chances for each player, side by side. P is closed and bounded, a straight segment between two of its points stays inside it, and the list of even mixes has room all round it inside P’s own flat. Pushing out from that middle along rays, as sky15.6 did for a solid triangle (34e), makes P the same shape as a solid ball of dimension the number of choices less the number of players. That each ray leaves P at one point, which moves continuously with the ray, is held in trust here; it is a fact about sets with no dents, and the triangle was its first case.

The nudge. For each player i and choice k let the gain gᵢₖ(σ) = max(0, uᵢ(k, σ₋ᵢ) − uᵢ(σ)): how much choice k would beat i’s present average, or 0. Map σ to σ′ with σ′ᵢₖ = (σᵢₖ + gᵢₖ) / (1 + Σₗ gᵢₗ). Each σ′ᵢ is again a mix: its entries are at least 0 and add to (1 + Σₗ gᵢₗ)/(1 + Σₗ gᵢₗ) = 1. Each uᵢ is a sum of products of chances, so it is continuous, and so are max(0, ·), sums and a quotient whose bottom is at least 1 (11b). So the nudge is a continuous map of P to itself.

Every-game-rests technique: every game with finitely many players, each with finitely many choices, has a rest.

\sigma' = \sigma \text{ for some } \sigma \in P @@ the every-dimension kept-point technique (sky15.6), P being the same shape as a ball
\text{player } i:\ \text{some } k \text{ with } \sigma_{ik} \gt 0 \text{ and } g_{ik}(\sigma) = 0 @@ the below-average technique
\sigma_{ik} = \dfrac{\sigma_{ik} + 0}{1 + \sum_l g_{il}} \Rightarrow \textstyle\sum_l g_{il} = 0 @@ the kept point, at that k; σᵢₖ is not 0, so the bottom is 1
g_{il}(\sigma) = 0 \text{ for every } l @@ each gain is at least 0
u_i(l, \sigma_{-i}) \le u_i(\sigma) \text{ for every } l @@ the definition of the gain
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma \text{ is a rest}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pure-check technique, for every player i

In the café game, start at x = y = ½. The first friend’s A earns 1, B earns ½, the average is ¾, so the gains are ¼ and 0, and x′ = (½ + ¼)/(1 + ¼) = 0.6. The second’s A earns ½, B earns 1, so y′ = ½/(1 + ¼) = 0.4. At the mixed rest x = ⅔, y = ⅓ every gain is 0 and the nudge keeps the point. The proof finds the rest as a kept point; it gives no promise that nudging again and again leads there. Here six nudges from ½ give x ≈ 0.661, y ≈ 0.339, creeping toward the mixed rest, and in other games repeated nudges can circle for ever.

sky48.3Two Players, One Grid

42h2’s minimax technique was proved from 42g’s equal prices. The every-game-rests technique gives it a second time, from the kept point, and the two roads explain one difference that matters for the rest of this page.

Rest-gives-value technique: in a game of gain against loss with grid M, a rest (x*, y*) has min over y of max over x of xᵀMy ≤ x*ᵀMy* ≤ max over x of min over y of xᵀMy; with 42h1’s guarantee-order technique, the two sides are equal and x*ᵀMy* is the value.

x^{\mathsf T} M y^* \le x^{*\mathsf T} M y^* \text{ for every } x @@ the row player gains xᵀMy and cannot gain by moving alone
x^{*\mathsf T} M y \ge x^{*\mathsf T} M y^* \text{ for every } y @@ the column player pays xᵀMy and cannot pay less by moving alone
\min_y \max_x x^{\mathsf T}My \le \max_x x^{\mathsf T}My^* = x^{*\mathsf T}My^* @@ the least over y is at most its value at y*
x^{*\mathsf T}My^* = \min_y x^{*\mathsf T}My \le \max_x \min_y x^{\mathsf T}My @@ the largest over x is at least its value at x*
\htmlClass{new}{\max_x \min_y = x^{*\mathsf T}My^* = \min_y \max_x} \quad \blacksquare @@ with the guarantee-order technique (42h1) the chain closes

42h3’s penalty: the taker kicks left with chance about 0.383, the keeper dives left with chance about 0.417, and that pair is a rest: against the keeper’s mix each side of the goal scores about 0.796, and against the taker’s each dive concedes the same. The rest pays the value, 0.796. In stone, paper, scissors the rest is (⅓, ⅓, ⅓) for both and the value is 0.

The difference. For two players and gain against loss the rest is a best corner of a program (42h2’s twin programs), and 42n’s walk from corner to corner finds it. Since 1979 a program can also be solved in a number of steps bounded by a power of its size, a method held in trust here. So these rests are found fast. For two players whose gains do not cancel, or for three players, the kept point gives no program, and the rest of this page is about why no fast method is known.

sky48.4A Walk That Must End

sky15.6 found its full cell by a walk: in by the door on the rim, then through doors, never back. Strip the walk to its bones. The dots are all strings of n bits, 2ⁿ of them. Two programs are given, each fast in the sense of 41h1: N(v) names the dot after v, and B(v) the dot before it. A link runs from v to w when N(v) = w and B(w) = v. So each dot has at most one link out and at most one in, and the dots fall into lines and loops. The string of n zeros, 0ⁿ, has a link out and none in.

Line-end technique: in such a graph, the line that starts at 0ⁿ ends at a different dot with a link in and none out; and the dots with one link and not two come in pairs, the ends of the lines.

v_0 = 0^n,\ v_{j+1} = N(v_j) \text{ while the link is there} @@ follow the links out
v_{j+1} = v_i,\ i \le j \Rightarrow i \ne 0 @@ 0ⁿ has no link in
v_{j+1} = v_i,\ i \ge 1 \Rightarrow v_i \text{ has two links in, from } v_{i-1} \text{ and } v_j @@ unless v_{i−1} = v_j, which repeats earlier
\text{no dot repeats, and there are } 2^n @@ the walk stops within 2ⁿ − 1 steps
\htmlClass{new}{\text{it stops at } v_m \ne 0^n \text{ with a link in and none out}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a line has two ends, and 0ⁿ is only one of them

Three bits: let N take 000 to 011, 011 to 101, 101 to 110, 001 to 010, 010 to 001 and 100 to 111, with B its reverse. The walk from 000 passes 011 and 101 and stops at 110. 001 and 010 make a loop. 100 starts a second line, which ends at 111. So the dots with exactly one link are 000, 110, 100 and 111: four, in two pairs.

The search end of the line: given N and B, with 0ⁿ a start, find a dot other than 0ⁿ with one link and not two. An answer always exists, by the line-end technique, and any answer is checked fast: run N and B on it. It is a search, not a yes-or-no question, and the yes is guaranteed. PPAD is the class of searches that fast-reduce to it, in the sense of 41h3: a fast map from instances to pairs N, B and a fast map from any answer back to an answer. The name, of 1994, reads a parity argument on a graph with directions.

Finding the full cell of sky15.6 is in PPAD when the labelling is given by a program on a cutting into 2ⁿ cells: the doors are the links, and a direction on them, from the order of the labels, is held in trust. Nothing in the line-end technique is fast. Let N run through all 2ⁿ strings in an order where neighbours differ in one bit, with the last having no link out: N and B are short programs, and the walk takes 2ⁿ − 1 steps. Searching all dots does no better. A faster way would need to read the programs, not just run them.

sky48.5A Game That Hides a Walk

Two claims, both stated. Finding a rest is in PPAD: an exact rest can need irrational chances, so the claim is for a rest within a small given ε, and the shape is sky15.6 again, the nudge’s labelling on a fine cutting of P, walked door to door. Finding a rest is PPAD-hard: from programs N and B a fast build makes a game whose rests, read back, give an end of the line. Late in 2005 the hard half was shown for four players, within weeks for three, and in a report posted the same year for two, where gains do not cancel; all three were presented in 2006. The papers are long; the proofs are held in trust here.

The shape of the build. Lay the line out as a path of tiny cubes inside a large cube, and draw a continuous map of the cube to itself that pushes every point along the path, except near its far end, where it stops. A kept point of the map lies at an end. The map is computed by a short circuit of steps: add two numbers, multiply by a constant, compare. Each step becomes a few players with two choices each, whose chance of playing the second choice holds a number between 0 and 1, and whose rests force the step to be computed. Here is one such piece in full.

Four players x, y, w and z each choose 0 or 1; write pₓ, p_y, p_w, p_z for their chances of choosing 1. x and y are inputs, set elsewhere in the game. w gains, for choosing 0, 1 for each of x and y that plays 1, and for choosing 1, 1 if z plays 1. z gains, for choosing 0, 1 if w plays 1, and for choosing 1, 1 if w plays 0.

Adding-player technique: at every rest, p_z = min(1, pₓ + p_y).

w\text{: choice 0 earns } p_x + p_y,\ \text{choice 1 earns } p_z;\ \ z\text{: choice 0 earns } p_w,\ \text{choice 1 earns } 1 - p_w @@ averages of the gains (30g)
p_z \lt \min(1, p_x + p_y) \Rightarrow w \text{ plays } 0 \Rightarrow p_w = 0 @@ choice 0 earns strictly more: the below-average technique
p_w = 0 \Rightarrow z \text{ earns } 1 \text{ against } 0 \Rightarrow p_z = 1 @@ which is not less than min(1, …): impossible
p_z \gt p_x + p_y \Rightarrow p_w = 1 \Rightarrow p_z = 0 @@ the same, the other way; and p_z = 0 is not above pₓ + p_y ≥ 0
\min(1, p_x + p_y) \le p_z \le p_x + p_y,\ \ p_z \le 1 @@ both cases ruled out
\htmlClass{new}{p_z = \min(1,\ p_x + p_y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the adding-player technique

At pₓ = 0.2 and p_y = 0.3: p_z = 0.5, and then z is indifferent, so 1 − p_w = p_w and p_w = 0.5 (checked: it is the only rest of the piece on a grid of steps of 1/200). At pₓ = 0.7 and p_y = 0.6: p_z = 1, w plays 0 for certain, since 1.3 > 1, and z plays 1 because 1 − 0 > 0. The comparing piece is built the same way, with a rest that is exact only away from a tie; that slack is why the 2006 claims speak of rests within ε.

What follows. If a fast method found rests, every search in PPAD would be fast, among them finding sky15.6’s full cell for any labelling a program gives. Nobody has such a method, and since 1964 the known walks for two players, which follow doors through the corners of the mixes, have been shown to take, on some games built for the purpose, a number of steps that doubles with the size (in print 2006). The rest exists for every game, and the best methods known can take about as long as trying every choice.

sky48.6The Dock’s Price, With Many Goods

12·econ’s matching-price technique found a price for one good by the in-between value, which is the kept point in dimension 1. With m goods a price is a list p = (p₁, …, p_m) of numbers at least 0, scaled to add to 1, a point of a solid triangle. At prices p, traders sell what they own and buy what they want with the money; zₖ(p), the excess demand for good k, is what is wanted less what there is. Two properties are assumed: z is continuous, and every trader spends exactly what they earn, so the value of all excess demand is 0, p · z(p) = 0. Prices clear the market when no good is short: zₖ(p) ≤ 0 for every k.

Clearing-price technique: if z is continuous on the solid triangle of prices and p · z(p) = 0 for every p, some prices clear the market.

p^{\prime}_k = \dfrac{p_k + \max(0, z_k(p))}{1 + \sum_l \max(0, z_l(p))} @@ the nudge of sky48.2: raise the price of what is short
p^{\prime} = p \text{ for some } p,\ \ S = \textstyle\sum_l \max(0, z_l(p)) @@ the every-dimension kept-point technique (sky15.6)
p_k\,S = \max(0, z_k(p)) \text{ for every } k @@ multiply out the kept point
S\,\textstyle\sum_k p_k z_k(p) = \sum_k z_k(p) \max(0, z_k(p)) = \sum_k \max(0, z_k(p))^2 @@ multiply by z_k(p) and add
0 = \textstyle\sum_k \max(0, z_k(p))^2 @@ the left side is S times p · z(p) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{z_k(p) \le 0 \text{ for every } k} \quad \blacksquare @@ a sum of squares is 0 only when each is

Two traders, two goods. One owns a unit of the first good and spends half of any money on each good; the other owns a unit of the second and spends a third on the first good and two thirds on the second. At prices (p₁, p₂) the first earns p₁ and the second p₂, and the demand for the first good is ½ + p₂/(3p₁). At (½, ½) it is ⅚ and the demand for the second is 7/6: z = (−⅙, ⅙), and p · z = 0. The nudge gives (3/7, 4/7), toward the second good. Setting ½ + p₂/(3p₁) = 1 gives p₂/p₁ = 3/2, so (2/5, 3/5) clears both markets exactly, and z = (0, 0) there.

In 1954 this was proved for a whole economy with producers, by kept points. The hard half followed it: for markets whose traders value each good by a curve that flattens, finding clearing prices was shown PPAD-complete in 2009. And the road is as doubtful as rung 12 said: in 1960 a small market was given in which prices that rise where demand is short and fall where it is not circle the clearing prices for ever and never reach them.

sky48.7Illegal Moves

\text{a rest is best for everyone}\,? @@ the café game’s mixed rest gives each ⅔, less than either café together
\text{nudging again and again reaches a rest}\,? @@ the kept point exists; 1960’s market circles for ever
\text{a rest exists, so it is found fast}\,? @@ existence is the line-end technique; the walk can take 2ⁿ − 1 steps
\text{finding a rest is NP-complete}\,? @@ it is PPAD-complete; an NP-complete search would put NP inside a class of searches whose answers always exist
\text{every game has a rest in single choices}\,? @@ stone, paper, scissors has none; only mixes rest

Why the fourth is tempting and wrong. The yes of a rest is guaranteed, so the search cannot hide a no. If a search whose answer always exists were hard for every NP question, a guaranteed answer would also certify a no, and NP questions would have short proofs of no as well as of yes, which most expect is false.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞48.oq.1Is every search in PPAD fast? A fast method for NP questions would give one, so a proof that PPAD is not fast would also prove that P is not NP (41·now2). In the other direction no fast method is known for end of the line, for rests, or for clearing prices, and since 2021 the searches in both PPAD and the class of searches for a local best are known to be exactly those of a third class built from continuous descent.
Why it is hard: the programs N and B can be read as well as run, and nobody knows how to turn reading them into a short cut, or how to prove that no short cut exists.
∞48.oq.2For two players, how close to a rest can a fast method come? A rest within ε, where no player can gain more than ε when gains lie between 0 and 1, is found fast for ε = 1/3 (2022), and for any fixed ε in a number of steps like n to the power log n (2003). In 2016 it was shown that for some small fixed ε, faster than that would give a fast method for end of the line beyond what anyone expects. Where between 1/3 and that small ε does the fast part end?
Why it is hard: the methods mix a few choices and prove a bound for the mix; the hardness needs games that defeat every small mix at once.
∞48.oq.3Do real markets reach their clearing prices, and how? The clearing-price technique proves the prices exist; the example of 1960 shows that the simplest rule for moving prices need not reach them; and the PPAD-completeness of 2009 says that no fast rule reaches them in every market of that kind, unless end of the line is fast. Markets nonetheless clear, roughly, every day.
Why it is hard: the hard markets are built, not met, and nobody has a sharp statement of what makes the markets people meet easy.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The story starts with two players. In 1928 the minimax technique was proved for games of gain against loss (42h). Games where both can gain, or both can lose, were left with no general answer.

In 1950 a short note in the proceedings of the national academy in Washington proved that every finite game of any number of players has a rest, by a kept point. The 1951 journal version used the plain kept point of a continuous map, by a nudge like the one on this page. Whether players reason their way to a rest, and which of several rests they would reach, were asked from the start, and this page has not revisited how the early readers divided. The same tool, in 1954, proved that a whole economy with producers has prices that clear every market. In 1994 the economics prize of the Swedish central bank went to the rest and its refinements.

Existence was not finding. In 1960 a market of three goods was given whose prices, moved by the obvious rule, circle for ever. In 1964 a walk for two-player games was published, door to door through the corners of the mixes, which always ends at a rest. How fast it was on the games of the following decades this page has not revisited.

In 1994 the walks were looked at from the side of computing. A paper on proofs of existence that do not show how to find gathered the searches settled by a parity argument on a directed graph into one class, PPAD, and asked where the rest belonged. Two-player games were later built on which the 1964 walk takes a number of steps that doubles with the size (in print 2006). In a few months of late 2005 the rest was shown PPAD-complete for four players, for three, and for two, and the results were presented in 2006. The journal versions appeared in 2009, the same year as the result for markets.

What is argued now is what the hardness means. One side reads it as a limit on the rest as a prediction: if no computer can find it in reasonable time, why should players or markets? The other side answers that the hard games are built to be hard, that the games and markets people meet are small or have a special shape, and that hardness of the worst case says little about the usual one. This page has not revisited where each side stands now. In between sits the work on rests within ε, where a fast method reaches 1/3 and a stated guess forbids reaching every ε, and the question has moved from whether a rest exists to how much of it can be found.

The accepted names

A rest is a Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies, and the every-game-rests technique is Nash’s theorem; the nudge is Nash’s map of 1951. The below-average and pure-check techniques are the standard facts that a mixed strategy is a best response exactly when every pure strategy in its support is. The rest-gives-value technique is the derivation of von Neumann’s minimax theorem from Nash’s theorem. End of the line is the problem END OF THE LINE (also END-OF-LINE); the line-end technique is the parity argument for directed graphs; PPAD is Polynomial Parity Arguments on Directed graphs; the full cell is Sperner’s lemma as a search. The build of sky48.5 uses graphical games and arithmetic gadgets; the adding-player technique is the addition gadget. The clearing-price technique is the existence of a Walrasian equilibrium under Walras’s law; “every trader spends what they earn” is Walras’s law; the two traders have Cobb–Douglas utilities; the price rule of 1960 is tâtonnement, and the market that circles is Scarf’s example. A rest within ε is an ε-Nash equilibrium. The class of 2021 is CLS, shown equal to PPAD ∩ PLS.

Who said it first

John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in 1928. John Nash proved existence in “Equilibrium points in n-person games”, PNAS 36 (1950), by Kakutani’s theorem, and by Brouwer’s in “Non-cooperative games”, Annals of Mathematics 54 (1951). Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu, “Existence of an equilibrium for a competitive economy”, Econometrica 22 (1954). Herbert Scarf, “Some examples of global instability of the competitive equilibrium” (1960). Carlton Lemke and Joseph Howson, “Equilibrium points of bimatrix games” (1964). A proof that is not allowed

“Start anywhere and nudge: each nudge moves toward better choices, so the nudges settle at a rest.” Not allowed: nothing makes the nudges settle, and in games and markets they can circle. The kept point is found by the door count, not by following the nudge, and that is why the proof of existence is not a method.
