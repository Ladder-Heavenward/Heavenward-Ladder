Which Reactor Turns More Into Product?

Which reactor turns more into product? A chemical plant can run a reaction in a big stirred tank, with feed flowing in and product flowing out, or in a long pipe the feed flows through. Same volume, same flow, same reaction. They do not give the same product. This page balances what goes in against what comes out, solves the stirred tank, follows a slug of feed down the pipe with 24’s rate statements and 17’s exp, shows the pipe always wins at equal size, and shows tanks in a row closing in on the pipe as 17e’s limit.

In a stirred tank, the whole tank sits at the outlet’s low concentration, so the reaction runs slowly everywhere: C = C₀/(1 + kτ). In a pipe, each slug decays as e^(−kt) on the way, starting fast: C = C₀e^(−kτ). Since e^(kτ) > 1 + kτ, the pipe leaves less unreacted. N tanks in a row give (1 + kτ/N)^(−N), which closes in on e^(−kτ).

26chem1What Goes in Must Come Out

Sarah runs a reactor where a chemical A turns into a product, at a rate k times its concentration C: the more A there is, the faster it goes.

From here on, on this page, Q is the flow in cubic metres a second, V the reactor’s volume, and τ = V/Q the holding time: how long the feed spends inside on average. Take kτ = 2.

26chem2A Stirred Tank: One Concentration Everywhere

A stirrer mixes the tank so well that every part of it is the same as what flows out. So the reaction runs everywhere at the outlet’s concentration.

Q\,C_0 = Q\,C + k\,C\,V @@ rate kC through the whole volume
C = \dfrac{C_0}{1 + k\tau} @@ divided by Q
\htmlClass{new}{k\tau = 2:\ \text{share turned to product} = \dfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.67} @@

26chem3A Pipe: the Concentration Falls Along It

In a pipe with no mixing along it, follow one slug of feed. It is a little closed reactor travelling down the pipe for time τ, its A falling at rate kC (24h).

\dfrac{dC}{dt} = -k\,C @@ a rate statement (24)
C = C_0\,e^{-kt} @@ growth and decay (17j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at the outlet: } C = C_0 e^{-k\tau},\ \ k\tau = 2:\ \text{share} = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.86} @@

Residence-time technique: for a reaction at rate kC, a stirred tank leaves C₀/(1 + kτ) unreacted and a pipe leaves C₀e^(−kτ), with τ the volume over the flow.

26chem4At Equal Size the Pipe Wins

e^{k\tau} = 1 + k\tau + \tfrac12 (k\tau)^2 + \cdots > 1 + k\tau @@ the power series (23a), every term positive
\text{so } C_0 e^{-k\tau} < \dfrac{C_0}{1 + k\tau} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{90\% \text{ product: tank } k\tau = 9,\ \text{pipe } k\tau = \ln 10 \approx 2.3} @@ the tank must be about four times bigger

At the same size and flow, the pipe turns more into product, because its feed reacts at full strength first, while the tank dilutes the feed at once to the outlet’s weak strength. Tanks are still used where the stirring helps: to hold a steady temperature, or to mix solids.

26chem5Tanks in a Row Close in on the Pipe

Split the volume into N stirred tanks in a row, each with holding time τ/N. Each multiplies the A by 1/(1 + kτ/N), and the factors multiply.

(1 + kτ/N)^N rises toward e^(kτ) as N grows (17e), so a row of many small tanks behaves like a pipe; the pipe is a row of endlessly many thin tanks, each multiplying by its own factor (26b). Sarah checks a real reactor by sending a quick pulse of dye in and timing how it comes out: a single tank spreads it into a long tail, a pipe passes it as a sharp pulse, and a row of tanks lands between, matching the count of tanks that fits.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The stirred tank is a continuous stirred-tank reactor, CSTR; the pipe a plug flow reactor, PFR. τ is the residence time, kτ the Damköhler number, and the row of tanks the tanks-in-series model. The dye test measures the residence time distribution.

Who said it first

Peter Danckwerts set out residence time distributions in 1953 (not yet revisited for the date); the comparison of tank and pipe is in every first course on reactors, as in Octave Levenspiel’s textbook.
