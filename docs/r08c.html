8c · proof

The least upper bound

This is the principle the rung is named for. Take any heap of reals that has something above it all. Then there is a least real above it all. Among the fractions this fails, and 5e's hole is the reason. Among cuts it is true, and the proof is short enough to be surprising. Take the union of the heap: tip all its fractions into one set, with rung 2's merging. Check that the union is a cut, condition by condition. Then notice that it is the least thing above the heap, because being above is being a superset. The same move, run from below, gives a greatest real below any heap that has something below it all.

The least upper bound of a heap of cuts is the union of the heap. An upper bound contains every member, so it contains the union.

8c1Bounds, in words and marks

Stands on 8a7 (order on ℝ) and 6d (upper bounds in a partial order).

From here on, for a set S of reals, a real β is an upper bound of S when ∀α ∈ S (α ≤ β). S is bounded above when it has an upper bound. A least upper bound of S is an upper bound σ of S with σ ≤ β for every upper bound β of S.

Here S is a letter naming a set of reals, as any letter may. 3a's mark S(x), with its bracket, keeps its job: S followed by a bracket is still the next one.

6d's “upper bound” was the same idea for any partial order; here the order is the line's. There is at most one least upper bound. If σ and σ′ are both least, each is at most the other, so σ = σ′ by double-inclusion. So the name below is honest.

From here on, sup S names the least upper bound of S, when there is one. Read it “the least upper bound of S”.

From below: a real β is a lower bound of S when ∀α ∈ S (β ≤ α). S is bounded below when it has one. A greatest lower bound is a lower bound that every lower bound is at most.

From here on, inf S names the greatest lower bound of S, when there is one. Read it “the greatest lower bound of S”. At most one, by double-inclusion, as for sup.

sup S may be a member of S or not. For {1*, 2*, 3*} it is 3*, a member. For the heap of cuts (1 − 1/n)*, n a number other than 0, it is 1*, which no member reaches. You will check it below.

8c2A union of cuts is a cut

Stands on 8c1, 8a4 (the four conditions) and 2c (merge).

Union-of-cuts technique: if S is a set of reals with a member and an upper bound β, then ⋃S is a real. ⋃S is a set of fractions, by 2c's Union, since every member of a member of S is a fraction. Now the four conditions.

\text{call } \alpha \in S;\ \text{call } r \in \alpha @@ S has a member; cut (i) for α
\text{(i)}\ \ r \in \bigcup S @@ 2c's ⋃ line: show an example, α
\text{call } s \in \mathbb Q \text{ with } s \notin \beta @@ cut (ii) for the bound β
t \in \bigcup S \Rightarrow \exists \alpha \in S\,(t \in \alpha) \Rightarrow t \in \beta @@ ﹀ ⋃; α ≤ β is α ⊆ β
\text{(ii)}\ \ s \notin \bigcup S @@ if s were in, s ∈ β: clash. Refute
t \in \bigcup S,\ u \lt t:\ \ t \in \alpha \in S @@ ﹀ ⋃; call α
\text{(iii)}\ \ u \in \alpha,\ \text{so } u \in \bigcup S @@ cut (iii) for α; ︿ ⋃
t \in \alpha \in S:\ \text{call } v \in \alpha,\ t \lt v @@ cut (iv) for α
\text{(iv)}\ \ v \in \bigcup S,\ t \lt v @@ ︿ ⋃; show an example
\htmlClass{new}{\bigcup S \in \mathbb R} \quad \blacksquare @@ all four; the calls close

Each condition is inherited from a member, except (ii), which is inherited from the bound. That is the only job the bound does: without it, the union may swallow every fraction (illegal moves below).

8c3No holes

Stands on 8c2 and 8a7 (order is being a part of).

No-holes technique: every set of reals that has a member and an upper bound has a least upper bound, and it is the union: sup S = ⋃S.

\sigma = \bigcup S \in \mathbb R @@ the union-of-cuts technique; σ names the union (8a's line for σ)
\alpha \in S \Rightarrow \alpha \subseteq \sigma @@ chase: r ∈ α gives r ∈ ⋃S, by show an example
\sigma \text{ is an upper bound of } S @@ ︿ ≤ (8a); α was anything
\beta \text{ an upper bound};\ \ r \in \sigma @@ assume; chase a member
\text{call } \alpha \in S \text{ with } r \in \alpha;\ \ r \in \beta @@ ﹀ ⋃; α ⊆ β
\sigma \subseteq \beta,\ \text{so } \sigma \le \beta @@ ︿ ⊆; ︿ ≤; the call closes
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma = \sup S} \quad \blacksquare @@ an upper bound at most every upper bound

Notice what the column did not cite: no either-way, no not-not-out, no Choice. Every or it had was given. With cuts, no-holes is the cheap principle and the comparison of 8b the expensive one. Books that start from the twelve techniques, with no cuts to build from, lay this one down as an axiom.

One well-known calculus book builds its subject on the twelve properties of numbers that rung 5 proved for the fractions. It points out that the fractions satisfy all twelve, so the twelve alone cannot prove that a number squaring to 2 exists. It adds one more, the least upper bound property, as the thirteenth. With it, the rest of the subject becomes provable.

Greatest-lower-bound technique: every set of reals that has a member and a lower bound has a greatest lower bound. Let L be the set of lower bounds of S, sifted from ℝ. L has a member, since S is bounded below. Every member of S is an upper bound of L: each lower bound is at most each member. So by no-holes, σ = sup L exists. σ is a lower bound of S: for α ∈ S, α is an upper bound of L, so σ ≤ α, since σ is least. And every lower bound β of S is in L, so β ≤ σ, since σ is an upper bound of L. So σ = inf S. ∎ No new set had to be built. The principle from below is the old one, used on the set of lower bounds.

8c4Every real is the least upper bound of its fractions

Stands on 8c3, 8a9 (members are the fractions below) and 2g (the short form of a set).

Real-is-the-merge technique: for every real α, α = sup{r* : r ∈ α}. The set {r* : r ∈ α} is 2g's shorthand, a sift of ℝ. It has a member by (i), and α is an upper bound by members-are-the-fractions-below. Its union is α. A fraction s in some r* with r ∈ α has s < r, so s ∈ α by (iii). And a fraction s ∈ α has some t ∈ α with s < t by (iv), so s ∈ t*. By same-members the union is α, and no-holes says the union is the sup. ∎ So the slogan of 8a has a second reading. A real is the set of the fractions below it, and also the least real above all of them.

8c5The fractions are not complete

Stands on 8c3 and 8a10 (hole’s-step).

Ask the same question inside ℚ. Let H be the hole's heap of 8a, {r ∈ ℚ : r < 0 ∨ r² < 2}. It has fractional upper bounds: 3/2 is above every member, and so is 2. No-least-fraction-bound technique: no fraction is a least upper bound of H among the fractions.

u \text{ a fractional upper bound of } H @@ assume: every member of H is at most u
1 \in H,\ \text{so } 1 \le u,\ u \text{ positive} @@ 1² = 1 < 2; chain for less on ℚ
u^2 \lt 2 \Rightarrow \exists s \in H\,(u \lt s),\ \bot @@ no-greatest-below (5e): a member above an upper bound; clash
2 \lt u^2 @@ refute; no-fraction-squares-to-2: u² ≠ 2; any-two-compare on ℚ
\text{call } s:\ 0 \lt s \lt u,\ 2 \lt s^2 @@ no-least-above (8a)
h \in H \Rightarrow h \lt s @@ h < 0 < s; or h² < 2 < s², and s ≤ h would give s² ≤ h² by squares-keep-order on ℚ
\htmlClass{new}{s \text{ is a smaller fractional upper bound}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so u is not least; u was anything

Every fractional upper bound has a smaller one below it, forever: hole's-step's step from 3/2 runs 10/7, 17/12, 58/41, 99/70, never stopping. On the line the heap of cuts {r* : r ∈ H} does have a least upper bound. By real-is-the-merge it is H itself: 8a's hole's cut, the irrational number sitting at the split. On the line this never fails, and that is what having no holes means.

8c6A sup that is not a member

Stands on 8c3 and 8a8 (fractions-keep-order).

Take S = {(1 − 1/n)* : n ∈ ℕ, n ≠ 0}. Every member is at most 1*, by fractions-keep-order, since 1 − 1/n < 1. The union is 1*. A fraction below some 1 − 1/n is below 1 (chain for less on ℚ). And a fraction r < 1 is below some 1 − 1/n:

r \lt 1:\ \ 1 - r = \tfrac pq,\ \ p, q \text{ positive integers} @@ 5c's <: 1 − r is positive; take a member with positive bottom (scaling with k = −1), whose top is then positive
n = q + 1:\ \ \tfrac1n \lt \tfrac1q \le \tfrac pq @@ compare-by-cross-multiplying (5e): q < q + 1, and 1 ≤ p
r = 1 - \tfrac pq \lt 1 - \tfrac1n @@ keep-order for plus on ℚ: add 1 − p/q − 1/n to both sides of 1/n < p/q
\htmlClass{new}{r \in (1 - \tfrac1n)^{*}},\ \text{so } r \in \bigcup S @@ ︿; show an example

So sup S = 1*. Is 1* a member? Then 1* = (1 − 1/n)* for some n, and fractions-keep-order gives 1 = 1 − 1/n, so 1/n = 0. But n · (1/n) = 1 by reciprocal, while n · 0 = 0 by anything-times-zero, and 1 ≠ 0. So the sup is not reached: the heap climbs toward it forever, and it sits just above them all. Many sups on later rungs are of this kind.

8c8Running it backwards

Stands on 8c1 to 8c6.

Wanting sup S, write the union and check its members are the fractions you expect, by same-members. Wanting to show σ = sup S without the union, show two things. σ is an upper bound. And nothing smaller is: for any β < σ, some member of S is above β. Wanting a greatest lower bound, take the sup of the lower bounds. Handed a set of fractions with no least fractional upper bound, look for the cut it makes on the line: that is its sup there. Found upward; written downward.

The accepted names

sup S is short for the supremum of S, and inf S for its infimum, from the Latin for highest and lowest. Having no holes is called completeness.

Who said it first

The calculus book that adds the least upper bound property as a thirteenth rule is Michael Spivak’s.
