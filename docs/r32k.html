32k · technique

Breaking a signal into frequencies, fast

A machine never sees a whole wave. It sees a list of samples, and the transform it can compute is a list too: one number for each of as many frequencies as there are samples. Done the plain way, each of those numbers is a sum over every sample, so a list of a thousand samples costs a million multiplications, and a second of music costs nearly two billion. For most of the twentieth century that put the frequencies of a sound beyond everyday reach. The way out is the one rung 22 found for sorting: split the work in half. The transform of a list is the transform of its even places and the transform of its odd places, stitched together with one turn each. Split those again, and again, and the cost falls from the square of the length to the length times the number of halvings. The step ends with a number every phone carries: why music is sampled forty-four thousand one hundred times a second, and not forty thousand.

On N equally spaced points, the N stepped turnings are at right angles, so a list of N samples has N coefficients and is rebuilt from them. The transform of a list of even length is the transform of its even places plus a turn times the transform of its odd places, and the second half of the answer uses the same two with a minus sign. Halving again and again makes the cost about N times the number of halvings.

32k1Turnings on N points

Let N be a whole number, and ω = e^(2πi/N), so that ω, ω², …, ω^N = 1 are N equal parts of a turn (21·cb20). For a list x₀, …, x_(N−1), its stepped transform is the list X₀, …, X_(N−1) with

Sampled-turnings technique: on N equally spaced points the N stepped turnings are at right angles, so a list is rebuilt from its stepped transform by x_j = (1/N) Σ X_k ω^(jk).

\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\omega^{j(k - m)} = \tfrac{\omega^{N(k - m)} - 1}{\omega^{k - m} - 1} = 0 @@ the geometric sum (15i), when k − m is not a whole multiple of N; ω^N = 1
\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\omega^{j(k - m)} = N @@ when k − m is a whole multiple of N: every term is 1
\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_k X_k\,\omega^{jk} = \tfrac1N\sum_m x_m\sum_k \omega^{k(j - m)} @@ put in X_k; finite sums swap
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X_k\,\omega^{jk} = x_j} \quad \blacksquare @@ only m = j survives, with N

If the list is N samples of a wave at the spacing 1/r over one repeat, the stepped transform holds its coefficients for the speeds 0 to N − 1, read with 32g’s disguise: the speed k and the speed k − N give the same samples. One example: the list (1, 2, 3, 4) has X = (10, −2 + 2i, −2, −2 − 2i), with ω = i.

32k2Splitting in half

Let N = 2M. Write E for the stepped transform of the even places x₀, x₂, …, and O for that of the odd places x₁, x₃, …, each on M points, with ω² as their equal part of a turn.

Halving-transform technique: for k from 0 to M − 1, X_k = E_k + ω^(−k)O_k and X_(k+M) = E_k − ω^(−k)O_k; so on N = 2ᵖ points the stepped transform costs about N log₂ N steps instead of N².

X_k = \textstyle\sum_{m} x_{2m}\,\omega^{-2mk} + \omega^{-k}\sum_m x_{2m+1}\,\omega^{-2mk} @@ split the sum into even and odd places
= E_k + \omega^{-k}O_k @@ ω² is the part of a turn for M points
E_{k+M} = E_k,\ \ O_{k+M} = O_k,\ \ \omega^{-(k + M)} = -\omega^{-k} @@ (ω²)^M = 1 and ω^M = −1
X_{k+M} = E_k - \omega^{-k}O_k @@ the same two transforms, one sign changed
T(N) = 2T(N/2) + N @@ two half transforms, then M multiplications and N additions, counted as N steps
\htmlClass{new}{T(N) = N\log_2 N + N\,T(1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the unrolling technique (22l1)
  
E = \text{transform of } (1, 3) = (4, -2),\ \ O = \text{transform of } (2, 4) = (6, -2) @@ on two points, ω = −1
X_0 = 4 + 6 = 10,\ \ X_2 = 4 - 6 = -2 @@ k = 0
\htmlClass{new}{X_1 = -2 + (-i)(-2) = -2 + 2i,\ \ X_3 = -2 - (-i)(-2) = -2 - 2i} \quad \blacksquare @@ k = 1, with ω⁻¹ = −i

For N = 1024 the plain way takes about a million steps and the halving way about ten thousand. For one second of music, N = 44 100, about two billion against about seven hundred thousand.

32k3Why forty-four thousand one hundred

Hearing reaches up to about 20 000 Hz in the young, and less with age. The samples-are-coefficients technique (32g1) asks for more than 40 000 samples a second. Before sampling, a filter must cut everything above half the rate, or it comes back in disguise (32g3). No filter of finite cost has gain 1 up to one frequency and 0 just above it: its gain must fall over a band. So the rate is set a little above 40 000, to leave that band.

\text{top of hearing} \approx 20\,000 \text{ Hz} @@ measured, and falling with age
\text{rate} \gt 2 \times 20\,000 = 40\,000 @@ the samples-are-coefficients technique (32g1)
\tfrac{44\,100}{2} = 22\,050 \text{ Hz} @@ half the rate: the filter falls from full gain at 20 000 Hz to none by 22 050 Hz
44\,100 = 3 \times 245 \times 60 = 3 \times 294 \times 50 @@ three samples on each usable line of a video picture, in both of the world’s two video systems
\htmlClass{new}{44\,100 \text{ samples a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first digital recorders stored sound on video tape

So the number is part technique and part history. The technique gives the floor, 40 000, and the need for a falling band gives the margin. The video tape of the late 1970s chose the exact number, and every compact disc and most music files kept it.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The stepped transform is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), and the sampled-turnings technique its inversion formula; ω is a primitive Nth root of unity. The halving-transform technique is the radix-2 Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT); its join of E and O is the butterfly. 44.1 kHz is the Red Book audio standard of the compact disc (1980); the band between 20 and 22.05 kHz is the transition band of the anti-aliasing filter. Multiplying long numbers by transforms is the Schönhage–Strassen method.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss used the halving trick around 1805, in work on the orbits of Pallas and Juno published only after his death. James Cooley and John Tukey published it in 1965, and it spread at once. The 44 100 rate came from Sony’s PCM adaptors of the late 1970s, which stored digital audio on video recorders; Sony and Philips fixed it for the compact disc. Arnold Schönhage and Volker Strassen multiplied long numbers by transforms in 1971, and David Harvey and Joris van der Hoeven reached n log n in 2019.

A proof that is not allowed

“Halving the list halves the cost, so halving it p times divides the cost by 2ᵖ.” Not allowed: each halving gives two half-size transforms, not one, plus a join that costs N. The count is the recurrence T(N) = 2T(N/2) + N, and it gives N log₂ N, not N²/2ᵖ.
