Can a Machine Be Built by Plugging Parts Together?

Can a machine be built by plugging parts together? An engineer draws a radio as boxes joined by wires: a filter, then an amplifier, then a speaker, with two channels side by side and a wire looping back from the output. The drawing is read as a promise that the machine is what its parts do, wired as drawn. This rung’s arrows make that promise exact. Parts in a row are arrows run in a row, parts side by side are a product, and a drawing can be read row by row or column by column with the same answer. A loop closes into one part by algebra. Then two cheap resistor dividers, plugged together, break the promise, and the page finds what the arrows must carry to keep it.

A part that divides every frequency by its own number is an arrow, and parts in a row multiply their numbers. Side by side, two parts make one part whose grid is the two grids in blocks, and a drawing of rows and columns names one part either way it is read. A part with its output fed back and taken from its input is one part, G/(1 + GH). A part that feels what it drives is no arrow of voltages alone: two halving dividers give a fifth, not a quarter. Carry voltage and current together, and each part becomes a 2 by 2 grid that multiplies exactly.

45·eng.1Parts in a Row

32j3’s transfer technique found that the rule y′ + ky = f divides every frequency of its input by its own number: ŷ(ξ) = f̂(ξ)/(k + 2πiξ). Call such a rule a part, and the number it multiplies frequency ξ by, here H(ξ) = 1/(k + 2πiξ), its transfer. A part takes a signal to a signal, as a function takes a member to a member, so parts are arrows, from signals to signals.

Row-of-boxes technique: two parts with transfers H₁ and H₂, run in a row, make one part with transfer H₂H₁; so a chain of parts is one part, and how the chain is bracketed does not change it. Proof: the first part gives ŷ = H₁f̂ at each ξ, and the second gives ẑ = H₂ŷ = H₂H₁f̂. Multiplying numbers can be bracketed either way, which is 45a1’s running in a row for these arrows.

H_1(\xi) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 2\pi i\xi},\ \ H_2(\xi) = \tfrac{1}{4 + 2\pi i\xi} @@ two rules of 32j3, k = 1 and k = 4
H(\xi) = \tfrac{1}{(1 + 2\pi i\xi)(4 + 2\pi i\xi)} @@ the row-of-boxes technique
\htmlClass{new}{|H(0)| = \tfrac14,\ \ |H(1)| \approx \tfrac{1}{6.36 \cdot 7.45} \approx 0.021} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slow part passes, a turning a second is cut to two hundredths

45·eng.2Parts Side by Side

A stereo has two channels, a left signal and a right signal, and each goes through its own parts. The pair of signals is one signal of the product (45d1): the pairs, added entry by entry. Two straight parts f and g, side by side, make the part f ⊕ g that does f to the first entry and g to the second. For parts on lists, its grid is the grid of f in the top left block, the grid of g in the bottom right, and 0 in the other two blocks.

Box-drawing technique: a drawing of parts in rows and side by side names one part, whether it is read row by row or column by column: (g ∘ f) ⊕ (g′ ∘ f′) = (g ⊕ g′) ∘ (f ⊕ f′). Proof: grids in blocks multiply block by block, and the two blocks that are 0 on each side give 0 when multiplied into the rest; so the top left block of the right side is the grid of g times the grid of f, the bottom right is g′ times f′, and the corners are 0, which is the left side.

f = 2,\ g = H_1;\ \ f' = 3,\ g' = H_2 @@ left: gain 2, then 32j3’s stage k = 1; right: gain 3, then k = 4
(g \oplus g') \circ (f \oplus f') = \begin{pmatrix}H_1 & 0\\ 0 & H_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix} @@ column by column: both gains, then both filters
= \begin{pmatrix}2H_1 & 0\\ 0 & 3H_2\end{pmatrix} @@ block by block
\htmlClass{new}{= (g \circ f) \oplus (g' \circ f')} \quad \blacksquare @@ row by row: each channel through, then side by side

The drawing has a second reading in a different shop. Two quantum gates side by side act on the joint state of two qubits, and there side by side means 45h’s tensor product, not the product of pairs: the grid of U next to V is U ⊗ V, of size 4 by 4. The box-drawing technique holds there too, (U′U) ⊗ (V′V) = (U′ ⊗ V′)(U ⊗ V), by 45h1 on the members v ⊗ w. The boxes look alike on paper. Which side by side a drawing means is part of what the drawing says.

45·eng.3Closing a Loop

Feed a part’s output back through a second part and take it from the input: the first part, G, sees u − Hy, where y is its own output and H the part on the way back. 24·eng3’s heater was such a loop, pushing in proportion to the gap between the set point and the room.

Loop-closing technique: a part G whose output is fed back through H and taken from the input is one part, with transfer G/(1 + GH), at every frequency where 1 + GH is not 0. Proof: at each frequency, y = G(u − Hy), so y + GHy = Gu, and y = Gu/(1 + GH).

G = 100\,000,\ \ H = 0.01 @@ a strong, careless amplifier; a feedback part that keeps one hundredth
\tfrac{G}{1 + GH} = \tfrac{100\,000}{1\,001} \approx 99.90 @@ the loop-closing technique
G = 50\,000:\ \ \tfrac{50\,000}{501} \approx 99.80 @@ the amplifier loses half its strength
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the loop moves by a tenth of a percent: the whole is about } 1/H} \quad \blacksquare @@ for GH large, G/(1 + GH) is near 1/H

The loop trades strength for steadiness: the whole is set by the cheap part H, a pair of resistors, and hardly at all by the expensive one. 24·eng3’s droop is the same algebra read at frequency 0: the gap left over is the input divided by 1 + GH, which shrinks as G grows and is never 0. Where 1 + GH is 0 at some frequency, the loop rings at that frequency with no input at all; the winding numbers of 34l are the tool for finding where that can happen.

45·eng.4Parts That Feel What They Drive

Two resistors of R in a row across a voltage give half of it at their middle: by 7·phys’s rule for one path the current is V/(2R), and the lower resistor takes R · V/(2R) = V/2. Call this the halving divider, a part that sends a voltage to half of itself. Two in a row should, by the row-of-boxes technique, send V to a quarter.

Loading technique: two halving dividers of equal resistors R, the second fed from the middle of the first, send V to V/5, not V/4. A part is an arrow on voltages only while what it drives draws no current from it. Proof: the second divider is 2R from the first’s middle to the ground, side by side with the first’s lower R, which by 7·phys’s side-by-side rule is 2R·R/(3R) = 2R/3. The first stage’s middle is then at V · (2R/3)/(R + 2R/3) = 2V/5, and the second halves it.

\text{first stage alone: } \tfrac{R}{R + R} = \tfrac12 @@ nothing drawn from its middle
\text{lower half with the second stage across it: } \tfrac{R \cdot 2R}{R + 2R} = \tfrac{2R}{3} @@ 7·phys, side by side
\text{first stage, loaded: } \tfrac{2R/3}{R + 2R/3} = \tfrac25 @@ one path
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac25 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac15 \ne \tfrac14} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second stage changed the first

Nothing was wrong with either divider. The drawing of two boxes in a row was wrong about what passes between them: the second box takes current from the first, so the first no longer does what it did alone. A shop fixes it by putting a follower between them, an amplifier that copies a voltage while drawing almost no current, and then the row-of-boxes technique holds again, at the price of a part that needs power.

45·eng.5Carry the Current Too

The fix that needs no power is to change what the arrows carry. Let each part take the pair (V, I), the voltage across its output end and the current out of it, back to the pair at its input end. A resistor R along the path has the same current on both sides and adds RI to the voltage. A resistor R across the path has the same voltage on both sides and adds V/R to the current, by 7·phys’s junction law.

Chain-grid technique: with (V, I) carried through each part, a resistor R along the path is the grid with rows (1, R) and (0, 1), a resistor R across it is the grid with rows (1, 0) and (1/R, 1), and parts in a row multiply their grids exactly, loading included. With nothing drawn at the far end, the far voltage is the near voltage divided by the top left number. Proof: the first two grids are 7·phys’s laws written as lists, (V + RI, I) and (V, I + V/R). A part in a row takes the pair the next part hands back, so the grids multiply in drawing order, as the row-of-boxes technique did with numbers. With I = 0 at the far end, the near voltage is the top left number times the far voltage.

D = \begin{pmatrix}1 & R\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1/R & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2 & R\\ 1/R & 1\end{pmatrix} @@ one halving divider: along, then across
D^2 = \begin{pmatrix}2 & R\\ 1/R & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2 & R\\ 1/R & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3R\\ 3/R & 2\end{pmatrix} @@ the chain-grid technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{far voltage} = V/5} \quad \blacksquare @@ the top left number: the loading technique’s fifth, with no extra steps

The arrows are now honest: whatever is plugged on next, the row of grids says what the whole does. The price is that a part is no longer one number per frequency but a 2 by 2 grid, because a wire carries two things, and a drawing of parts is a promise only about what its wires carry.

The accepted names

A drawing of parts joined by wires is a block diagram, and the transfer of a part is its transfer function. Parts in a row are in cascade or in series; the box-drawing technique is the interchange law of a monoidal category, and drawings read by it are string diagrams. The loop-closing technique is the closed-loop gain of negative feedback, and a loop is a trace in the language of categories. The loading technique is the loading effect, set by output and input impedance; the follower is a voltage follower, or buffer. A part with (V, I) at each end is a two-port network, and the chain grid is its transmission or ABCD matrix; n halving dividers in a row are a resistor ladder.

Who said it first

Harold Black sketched the negative-feedback amplifier on a ferry to work in August 1927; his patent, filed in 1928, was granted in 1937. Harry Nyquist gave the test for when a loop rings in 1932. Samuel Mason gave a rule for reading the whole off a drawing of loops in 1953. Saunders Mac Lane proved in 1963 that the bracketings of side by side all agree, and André Joyal and Ross Street proved in 1991 that drawings of boxes and wires are exact proofs in such categories. Jan Willems set out the view of a machine as the behaviours its ends allow from the 1980s. John Baez and Brendan Fong built circuits of resistors as arrows that share ends in 2015.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each divider halves the voltage, and two halvings make a quarter, so the pair sends V to V/4.” Not allowed: “each divider halves the voltage” was measured with nothing plugged on, and the proof uses it with the second divider plugged on. The row-of-boxes technique needs each part to be the same part in the row as alone, which is what the loading technique shows fails here.
