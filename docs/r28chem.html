28·chem · chemistry

Why Does Ice Melt, When Melting Takes Heat In?

Ice left on a counter melts, and melting takes heat in. Coal in air would give heat out by burning, and it sits for years. Whether a change gives heat out does not decide whether it goes, nor how fast.

11·chem wrote ΔH for heat held, 22·comp gave the second law of heat, and 28·then found the most work at a fixed temperature, ΔG. This page draws the energy of a change as a hill, derives from the second law which way a change goes by itself, sorts every change into four cases, and finds the temperature where a change turns round.

At a fixed temperature and pressure, a change goes by itself when ΔH − TΔS is below 0.

28chem1Heat Out, Heat In

11chem8 wrote ΔH for the change in heat held, negative when heat is given out. A change that gives heat out is called exothermic. A change that takes heat in is called endothermic. Burning a mole of carbon has ΔH = −393.5 kJ. Melting a mole of ice has ΔH = +6.01 kJ.

24·then gave a reaction an energy barrier E, and no picture. Here is the picture. The change starts at one level, climbs the barrier, and comes down to another level. ΔH is the finish less the start. E is the top less the start.

Hill technique: on a picture of energy against the progress of a change, ΔH is the finish less the start and the barrier E is the top less the start; walked backwards, ΔH turns sign and the barrier is E − ΔH.

Drawn numbers, to show the shape. The dashed line is the level of the start.

28chem2Heat Out Does Not Decide

It would be easy to guess that a change goes by itself when it gives heat out. Ice on a counter says otherwise: it melts, and melting takes heat in. What decides is 22·comp’s second law of heat. Heat Q passed into a room at temperature T raises the room’s entropy by Q/T, and in anything that happens the total entropy does not fall.

Let a change happen at a fixed temperature T and a fixed pressure. Write ΔS for the change in the entropy of the stuff itself. The change hands heat −ΔH to the room (11chem6: H is heat held at constant pressure).

\Delta S_{\text{room}} = -\Delta H / T @@ heat −ΔH passed into a room at T (22·comp)
\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S - \Delta H / T @@ the stuff’s share plus the room’s
\Delta S - \Delta H / T \ge 0 @@ second law of heat
T\Delta S - \Delta H \ge 0 @@ times T; T is above 0 in kelvins (13n5)
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta H - T\Delta S \le 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ times −1, which turns the order about

From here on, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. It is the ΔG of 28then4, since G = U + PV − TS and H is U + PV, and it is the ΔG that 25·cb7 measured with a cell.

Goes-alone technique: at a fixed temperature and pressure, a change goes by itself when ΔG is below 0, is at rest when ΔG = 0 (25chem4), and goes backwards when ΔG is above 0.

28chem4The Turning Temperature

Hold ΔH and ΔS fixed and let T change. Then ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is 18c’s straight line in T: it starts at ΔH and its step is −ΔS. Where it crosses 0 the change is at rest, and on either side it goes one way or the other.

Turning-temperature technique: when ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, a change is at rest at T = ΔH/ΔS, and goes by itself on the side of it where ΔH − TΔS is below 0.

0 = \Delta H - T\Delta S @@ at rest (25chem4)
T = \Delta H / \Delta S @@ solve for T (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{T = 6010\ \text{J} / 22.0\ \text{J/K} \approx 273\ \text{K}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ice: kilojoules written as joules first

273 K is 0 °C. Above it the line is below 0 and ice melts. Below it the line is above 0 and water freezes. The melting point is where the heat taken in and the entropy gained balance.

ΔH and ΔS are measured near room temperature and drift a little as T changes, so a turning temperature far from room temperature is close, not exact.

The accepted names

Heat held at constant pressure, H, is enthalpy, and ΔH the enthalpy change of reaction; heat out is exothermic and heat in endothermic. The hill is a reaction coordinate diagram (an energy profile), its top the transition state, and the barrier E the activation energy. G = H − TS is the Gibbs free energy, and a change that goes by itself is spontaneous. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation in its simplest form, and the four cases are often drawn as a sign chart. The values on this page are standard values at 298 K and 1 bar, written ΔH° and ΔS°.

What was measured

Every ΔH and ΔS on this page is computed from standard tables of formation heats and entropies, each value checked in two sources; the melting of ice is measured directly. Everything else is derived from 22·comp’s second law and 11·chem’s heat held.
