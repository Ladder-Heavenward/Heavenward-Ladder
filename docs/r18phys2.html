How Thick Must a Shield Be?

How thick must a shield be? A dentist steps out of the room for an X-ray; a hospital’s cancer machine sits behind walls of concrete two metres thick; astronauts bound for Mars will spend months beyond the earth’s magnetic shelter. Each faces the same question: how much matter stops how much radiation. Paper stops some kinds, a sheet of aluminium others, and some are never stopped completely, only thinned. 18·chem found that light through a coloured glass is dimmed by the same share in each equal thickness. This page finds the same law for X-rays and gamma rays, prices shields of lead and concrete, adds distance and time, and says why water beats lead in space.

Charged particles lose energy as they go and stop within a fixed range: alpha in paper, beta in millimetres of aluminium. Gamma and X-rays are removed by the same share in each equal thickness: each half-thickness halves them, so n half-thicknesses leave 2⁻ⁿ. Distance thins radiation from a small source as one over distance squared. In space, hydrogen-rich water and plastic stop heavy fast nuclei with fewer showers of fragments than metal.

Sarah’s school has a smoke detector with a speck of americium, a lab source that gives off fast electrons, and a sealed cobalt source that gives off gamma rays.

A charged particle pulls on every electron it passes and loses energy steadily, so it has a range, past which nothing gets through. A gamma ray passes untouched until, by chance, it hits something; each one is removed or not, all at once.

18phys22The Same Share in Each Thickness

\text{each equal slab removes the same share} @@ 18·chem’s law, for gamma rays
\text{half-thickness } h:\ \text{passes } \tfrac12 @@ measured for each material and energy
x = n h:\ \text{passes } 2^{-n} = 2^{-x/h} @@ n slabs in a row
\htmlClass{new}{\text{to cut by } 1000:\ 2^{-n} = \tfrac{1}{1000},\ n \approx 10} @@ 2¹⁰ = 1024

Half-thickness technique: a gamma or X-ray beam is halved by each half-thickness of shield, so the shield needed grows only with the logarithm of how much the beam must be cut. A thousandfold cut takes ten half-thicknesses; a millionfold, twenty.

18phys23Lead or Concrete?

\text{lead: } h \approx 1.2 \text{ cm};\ \ \text{concrete: } h \approx 6 \text{ cm} @@ measured
\text{cut by } 1000:\ 12 \text{ cm of lead, or } 60 \text{ cm of concrete} @@ ten half-thicknesses
\text{lead is about 5 times denser than concrete} @@ thinning goes mostly with mass in the way
\htmlClass{new}{\text{per kilogram they are close: concrete is cheap, lead is compact}} @@ hospitals use concrete walls, lead in doors

For gamma rays near a million electronvolts, what thins the beam is mostly the mass in its way, so a thick wall of cheap material does what a thin wall of dense material does. A diagnostic X-ray is far less energetic, and a fraction of a millimetre of lead in an apron cuts it many times over.

18phys24Distance and Time

\text{a small source spreads over a sphere: area } 4\pi d^2 @@ the dose rate falls as 1/d²
\text{step from 1 m to 3 m: dose rate} \div 9 @@ free
\text{dose} = \text{rate} \times \text{time} @@ half the time, half the dose
\htmlClass{new}{\text{time, distance, shielding: in that order of cost}} @@ why the dentist steps out

Dose falls as one over distance squared from a small source and in step with the time spent, so stepping back and working quickly cost nothing. A person on the earth takes about 2.4 millisieverts a year from natural sources.

18phys25Shielding Beyond the Earth

\text{galactic cosmic rays: nuclei moving near light speed} @@ from exploded stars
\text{a Mars round trip: about } 0.66 \text{ Sv} @@ measured by Curiosity’s instrument in 2012
\text{heavy nuclei hitting metal shatter into showers of fragments} @@ a thick metal wall can make it worse
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hydrogen-rich water and plastic: fewer fragments per kilogram}} @@ water tanks as walls

Against fast heavy nuclei, light atoms shield better than heavy ones, because they break the incoming nuclei with fewer secondary showers. A round trip to Mars would give about 275 years of a person’s natural dose on the earth; ships are planned with water and food stored round a sheltered room for solar storms.

Accepted names

The half-thickness is the half-value layer (HVL); thinning by a fixed share per thickness is exponential attenuation, with the linear attenuation coefficient μ = ln 2 / HVL. Dose is measured in grays, and its harm in sieverts. The three kinds are alpha, beta and gamma radiation; fragment showers are secondary particles; the free shields are the rule of time, distance, shielding.

Who said it first

Ernest Rutherford named alpha and beta rays in 1899 by how far they went through foil, and Paul Villard found gamma rays in 1900. Cary Zeitlin and colleagues reported the Mars cruise dose from Curiosity’s RAD instrument in 2013.
