Can Two Species Share One Food?

Can two species share one food? Two kinds of beetle in one sack of flour, two kinds of tiny pond animal in one jar of broth: sometimes both thrive side by side, and sometimes one is gone within weeks, though nothing ever attacked it. This page lets each species slow its own growth and the other’s, draws the line where each stops growing (18), finds where the lines cross and why a resting point must exist with 34h, reads from 27i’s pictures whether the crossing holds, and watches two species grown in tubes.

Each species grows until the crowding from itself and from the other uses up its room: N₁ + a N₂ = K₁ for the first, N₂ + b N₁ = K₂ for the second. Each equation is a line; where they cross, both rest. If each crowds itself more than it crowds the other, the crossing holds and both live; if each crowds the other more, the crossing is a pass between two valleys, and one wins.

34bio23Where the Lines Cross

Take K₁ = K₂ = 100. The flow carries the square from 0 to 100 on each side into itself: numbers in it stay in it. A continuous flow of a square into itself has a point that stays put (34h), so a resting point exists. With a = b = 0.5:

N_1 + 0.5 N_2 = 100,\ \ N_2 + 0.5 N_1 = 100 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{N_1 = N_2 = \dfrac{100}{1.5} \approx 67} @@ both live, each below its room alone

34bio24Live Together or One Wins: Which Crossing Holds

Near the crossing, the two rates are close to a linear map (27i), and its stretching numbers say whether nearby numbers fall back or run off. With r = 1:

a = b = 0.5:\ -0.67\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ \text{stretching numbers } -1,\ -0.33 @@ both fall back: the crossing holds
a = b = 1.5:\ \text{crossing at } 40, 40;\ -0.4\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ \text{numbers } -1,\ +0.2 @@ one runs off: a pass
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both live when } a < K_1/K_2 \text{ and } b < K_2/K_1} @@ each crowds itself more than the other

Two species share one food only when each holds itself back more than it holds back the other; otherwise the crossing is a pass, and whichever gets ahead pushes the other out. In nature, species that share a food usually differ in some way, feeding at different depths or hours, which makes a and b small.

Accepted names

The two rates are the Lotka-Volterra competition equations; K is the carrying capacity, a and b the competition coefficients, and the lines isoclines. That two species with exactly the same needs cannot live together is the competitive exclusion principle; the ways species differ are their niches.

Who said it first

Georgy Gause grew two species of Paramecium together in 1934 and saw one push the other out (not yet revisited for the date).
