21r · technique

Curves by turning and reach

A lighthouse beam and a radar screen find a point the way a person giving directions does: face this way, then go this far. That is a second way to name every point of the plane, by how far round and how far out. Curves that are tangled in across-and-up coordinates come out simple this way: flowers with three or four petals, hearts, spirals. This step draws them, finds their slopes, and finds the area each one sweeps.

Every point can be named by how far round to turn and how far out to go. Curves named this way include petals, hearts and spirals. The area a turning reach sweeps out is the total of thin slices, each a sliver of a disc.

21r1A point by turn and reach

From here on, the point with reach r and turn θ is (r cos θ, r sin θ). The pair (r, θ) are its polar coordinates; r may be negative, which reaches backwards.

Turn-and-reach technique: every point other than the origin has reach r = √(x² + y²) and a turn θ with cos θ = x/r and sin θ = y/r; the turn is fixed up to whole turns.

r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} > 0 @@ distance from the origin (18a)
(\tfrac{x}{r}, \tfrac{y}{r}) \text{ is on the circle of radius } 1 @@ its squares add to 1
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ that point is at some arc θ (21b), and θ + 2πk too
x > 0:\ \theta = \arctan\tfrac{y}{x};\ \ x < 0:\ \theta = \arctan\tfrac{y}{x} + \pi @@ arctan only answers on the right half (21m2)

21r2Curves by turn and reach

Polar-graph technique: the graph of r = f(θ) is every point (f(θ) cos θ, f(θ) sin θ); to compare it with an across-and-up equation, multiply through by r and put r² = x² + y², r cos θ = x, r sin θ = y.

r = 2a\cos\theta \Rightarrow r^2 = 2a\,r\cos\theta @@ multiply by r (this adds only the origin, already on the curve)
x^2 + y^2 = 2ax @@ r² = x² + y², r cos θ = x
\htmlClass{new}{(x - a)^2 + y^2 = a^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ complete the square: the circle of radius |a| about (a, 0), through the origin

From here on, r = cos kθ is a rose: k petals when k is odd, 2k when k is even. r = 1 + b cos θ is a limaçon, with a dent when b < 1, a cusp (a heart, the cardioid) when b = 1, and an inner loop when b > 1. r = cθ is a spiral.

21r3Slopes of turning curves

Polar-slope technique: trace r = f(θ) in time with θ as the time; then dy/dx = (dy/dθ)/(dx/dθ), where dx/dθ ≠ 0. And (α cos t, b sin t) traces the oval x²/α² + y²/b² = 1.

x = f(\theta)\cos\theta,\ \ y = f(\theta)\sin\theta @@ the curve in time (18i)
\tfrac{dx}{d\theta} = f'\cos\theta - f\sin\theta,\ \ \tfrac{dy}{d\theta} = f'\sin\theta + f\cos\theta @@ the product rule (13i)
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f'\sin\theta + f\cos\theta}{f'\cos\theta - f\sin\theta}} \quad \blacksquare @@ dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt) (18i)
\big(\tfrac{\alpha\cos t}{\alpha}\big)^2 + \big(\tfrac{b\sin t}{b}\big)^2 = 1 @@ the oval of 18e, traced by the circle technique

21r4The area a reach sweeps

Swept-area technique: as the turn runs from α to β, the reach r = f(θ) sweeps the area ½∫ₐᵝ f(θ)² dθ. Each thin turn dθ sweeps a sliver of a disc of radius f(θ).

A(\theta) = \text{area swept from } \alpha \text{ to } \theta @@ an area function in the turn (16a)
\tfrac12 m^2 h \le A(\theta + h) - A(\theta) \le \tfrac12 M^2 h @@ between slices of discs of the least and greatest reach m, M on [θ, θ + h] (21n3)
A'(\theta) = \tfrac12 f(\theta)^2 @@ m and M close in on f(θ) as h → 0, for continuous f
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = \tfrac12\int_\alpha^\beta f(\theta)^2\,d\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ areas by antiderivatives (16c)
r = 1 + \cos\theta:\ \tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta)\,d\theta = \tfrac12(2\pi + 0 + \pi) = \tfrac{3\pi}{2} @@ the heart’s area, by half arcs (21n2)

The accepted names

Turn and reach are polar coordinates; r = f(θ) is a polar equation and its graph a polar curve. The roses are rhodonea curves; r = cθ is the Archimedean spiral.

Who said it first

In 21r.4.5, the spiral is Archimedes’, On Spirals (c. 225 BC), where he uses it to split angles and to straighten the circle. Pappus of Alexandria (fourth century) sorted problems into plane, solid and linear by the curves they need, and counted a solution by a higher curve than necessary as a fault. That ruler and compass cannot split every angle in three was proved by Pierre Wantzel in 1837.
