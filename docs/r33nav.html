How Is Tomorrow’s Weather Worked Out?

How is tomorrow’s weather worked out? Every few hours, the world’s weather centres feed millions of readings into computers, which step the whole atmosphere forward in time, a few minutes at a step, until they reach next week. Then they do it again, fifty times, from slightly different starts. This page lays the air on a grid as 33k did by hand, steps it forward as 24k stepped a slope field, finds how short each time step must be, finds why small errors grow as 24m warned, and turns fifty runs into a chance of rain with 30’s chances.

Lay the air on a grid of points. Each step, each point’s new value comes from its old value and its neighbours’, moved along by the wind. The step must be shorter than the time the wind takes to cross one cell, or the sums blow up. Small errors in the start double every couple of days, so forecasters run many slightly different starts and count how many bring rain.

33nav1The Air on a Grid

Jean works at a weather centre. The atmosphere is too big to follow every bit of air, so it is sampled on a grid: points 10 km apart across the ground, in layers up through the air. At each point the computer holds a few numbers: temperature, pressure, moisture, and the wind’s three parts.

From here on, on this page, Δx is the spacing of the grid and Δt the time step. The rates in the weather’s statements, which are slopes in space and time, are replaced by differences between neighbouring points, as 33k did for heat.

\dfrac{\partial q}{\partial x} \approx \dfrac{q_i - q_{i-1}}{\Delta x} @@ q any quantity; i counts the points

33nav2Stepping Forward in Time

Take the simplest piece of the weather’s statements: wind u carrying a quantity q along, so that q at a point changes as the upwind air arrives. Step it forward as 24k stepped a slope field: the new value is the old one plus the rate times Δt.

\dfrac{\partial q}{\partial t} = -u\,\dfrac{\partial q}{\partial x} @@ q carried by the wind
q_i^{\text{new}} = q_i - \dfrac{u\,\Delta t}{\Delta x}\,(q_i - q_{i-1}) @@ stepping forward (24k), upwind difference
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each new value is a mix of the old value and its upwind neighbour}} @@ weights 1 − s and s, with s = uΔt/Δx

33nav3The Time Step Must Be Shorter Than the Wind Takes to Cross a Cell

The mix has weights 1 − s and s. While s is at most 1, both weights are at least 0, and each new value lies between its two old ones: nothing can grow. If s passes 1, the weight 1 − s turns negative, small wiggles are magnified at every step, and in a few hundred steps the forecast fills with nonsense.

s = \dfrac{u\,\Delta t}{\Delta x} \le 1 @@ 
u = 50 \text{ m/s},\ \Delta x = 10 \text{ km}:\ \ \Delta t \le 200 \text{ s} @@ the fastest winds, high up
\htmlClass{new}{\text{halving } \Delta x \text{ means 4 times the points a layer and twice the steps: 8 times the work}} @@

Grid-step technique: a forecast stepped on a grid stays sound only while the wind crosses at most one cell per step; a finer grid needs a shorter step, so each halving of the spacing costs about eight times the computing. That is why a sharper forecast needed each new generation of computers.

33nav4Small Errors Grow

The readings that start a forecast are never perfect: a tenth of a degree here, a gust missed there. The weather’s statements are of the kind that 24m found can magnify small differences. In the atmosphere, a small error roughly doubles every two days.

\text{error after } t \text{ days} \approx 0.1 \times 2^{t/2} \;°\text{C} @@ growth (17j)
t = 10:\ 0.1 \times 32 = 3.2\;°\text{C} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{by about two weeks the error is as big as the weather itself}} @@ a limit no computer removes

33nav5Many Runs, Not One: the Chance of Rain From an Ensemble

Since the start is uncertain, run the forecast many times, each from a start nudged within the readings’ errors. Where the runs agree, the forecast is firm; where they spread, it is not.

\text{runs with rain over the city} = 30 \text{ of } 50 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chance of rain} \approx \dfrac{30}{50} = 60\%} @@ a share of equally likely runs (30)

A forecast of “60% chance of rain” means that in about 60% of the runs from starts the readings cannot tell apart, it rains. Jean checks the centre’s forecasts against what happened: over a year, of all the days given 60%, it rained on close to 60%. A forecast that passes this check can be trusted at its word.

Accepted names

This is numerical weather prediction; the step limit is the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition, s the Courant number, and the difference used here an upwind scheme. The growth of small errors is chaos, often called the butterfly effect, and the many runs an ensemble forecast.

Who said it first

Lewis Fry Richardson worked out a forecast by hand in 1922; the step limit was found by Richard Courant, Kurt Friedrichs and Hans Lewy in 1928; the first computer forecast was run in 1950; Edward Lorenz found the growth of small errors in 1963 (not yet revisited for the dates or the doubling time).
