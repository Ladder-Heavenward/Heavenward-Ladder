18e · proof

Conics by Distances

A circle keeps one distance fixed. Three other curves keep a relation between two distances fixed: a distance to a point against a distance to a line, or two distances to two points, added or subtracted. This step proves that each is given by an equation of the second degree, and that the graph of every quadratic since rung 9 is one of them. Then it proves that all three are one family, sorted by one number.

Equally far from a point and a line gives a parabola. A fixed sum of distances to two points gives an ellipse. A fixed difference of distances to two points gives a hyperbola. And a fixed ratio between the distance to a point and the distance to a line gives all three, as the ratio is one, less than one or more than one.

18e1One Point and One Line

For a real p ≠ 0, take the point F = (0, p) and the line ℓ: y = −p. Parabola technique: the set {P : d(P, F) = d(P, ℓ)} is the set of (x, y) with x² = 4py. So for k ≠ 0, the graph of y = kx² is this set with p = 1/(4k), and the graph of every y = k(x − h)² + m is it moved by the displacement (h, m). Its accepted name is the parabola, with F its focus, ℓ its directrix, (0, 0) its vertex and the line x = 0 its axis.

d(P, \ell) = |y + p| @@ nearest-point, with A = 0, B = 1, C = −p
d(P, F) = |y + p| \iff x^2 + (y - p)^2 = (y + p)^2 @@ distance-laws; both sides not negative, so squaring loses nothing (powers-keep-order)
\iff x^2 = 4py @@ adding-keeps-answers: the y² and p² cancel, and −2py moves across
y = kx^2 \iff x^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4k} \cdot y @@ divide by k ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{d(P + w, Q + w) = d(P, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ distance-laws: the differences of coordinates do not change; so moving by w = (h, m) carries the parabola to the graph of y = k(x − h)² + m

So the curves drawn as graphs of quadratics since rung 9, and called by no name there, have a point and a line hidden in them. Exchanging x and y also keeps every distance (distance-laws is symmetric in the two places), so y² = 4px is a parabola too, opening along the x axis, with focus (p, 0) and directrix x = −p.

18e2Two Points, Added

For reals α > c ≥ 0, take F₁ = (−c, 0), F₂ = (c, 0), and b = √(α² − c²). Ellipse technique: the set {P : d(P, F₁) + d(P, F₂) = 2α} is the set of (x, y) with x²/α² + y²/b² = 1. On it, d(P, F₂) = α − (c/α)x and d(P, F₁) = α + (c/α)x. Its accepted name is the ellipse, with F₁ and F₂ its foci, (0, 0) its centre, and the segments from (−α, 0) to (α, 0) and from (0, −b) to (0, b) its major and minor axes. When c = 0 the two foci are one point and the ellipse is the circle of radius α.

d_1 + d_2 = 2\alpha \Rightarrow d_1^2 = 4\alpha^2 - 4\alpha\,d_2 + d_2^2 @@ d₁ = 2α − d₂; square both sides (an implication, so no check needed going this way)
d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (x + c)^2 - (x - c)^2 = 4cx @@ distance-laws: the y² cancels
\alpha\,d_2 = \alpha^2 - cx @@ the two lines together, divided by 4
\alpha^2\big((x - c)^2 + y^2\big) = (\alpha^2 - cx)^2 @@ square again
(\alpha^2 - c^2)x^2 + \alpha^2y^2 = \alpha^2(\alpha^2 - c^2) @@ expand; the −2α²cx cancels
\tfrac{x^2}{\alpha^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 @@ divide by α²b² > 0

Squaring can gain points, as squaring x = −2 gains x = 2. So the converse needs its own proof, and it gives the two distances outright.

\tfrac{x^2}{\alpha^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \Rightarrow |x| \le \alpha @@ y²/b² ≥ 0
d_2^2 = x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + b^2 - \tfrac{b^2}{\alpha^2}x^2 @@ distance-laws, with y² = b² − (b²/α²)x²
= \tfrac{c^2}{\alpha^2}x^2 - 2cx + \alpha^2 = \big(\alpha - \tfrac{c}{\alpha}x\big)^2 @@ b² + c² = α², so 1 − b²/α² = c²/α²
\alpha - \tfrac{c}{\alpha}x \ge \alpha - c \gt  0 \Rightarrow d_2 = \alpha - \tfrac{c}{\alpha}x @@ |x| ≤ α and c < α; powers-keep-order takes the root
\htmlClass{new}{d_1 = \alpha + \tfrac{c}{\alpha}x,\ \ d_1 + d_2 = 2\alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same with −c for c

18e3Two Points, Subtracted

For reals 0 < α < c, keep F₁ = (−c, 0) and F₂ = (c, 0), and take b = √(c² − α²). Hyperbola technique: the set {P : |d(P, F₁) − d(P, F₂)| = 2α} is the set of (x, y) with x²/α² − y²/b² = 1. It has two parts: on the part with x ≥ α, d(P, F₁) − d(P, F₂) = 2α, and on the part with x ≤ −α, d(P, F₂) − d(P, F₁) = 2α. Its accepted name is the hyperbola, with F₁ and F₂ its foci and its two parts its branches.

d_1 = d_2 \pm 2\alpha \Rightarrow \pm \alpha\,d_2 = cx - \alpha^2 @@ square, and subtract d₂² as in ellipse
(\alpha^2 - c^2)x^2 + \alpha^2y^2 = \alpha^2(\alpha^2 - c^2) @@ square again: ellipse's line, letter for letter
\tfrac{x^2}{\alpha^2} - \tfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 @@ now α² − c² = −b² < 0: divide by −α²b²
\text{converse: } d_2^2 = \big(\tfrac{c}{\alpha}x - \alpha\big)^2,\ \ d_1^2 = \big(\tfrac{c}{\alpha}x + \alpha\big)^2 @@ as in ellipse, with y² = (b²/α²)x² − b² and b² − c² = −α²
|x| \ge \alpha \Rightarrow \big|\tfrac{c}{\alpha}x\big| \ge c \gt  \alpha @@ x²/α² = 1 + y²/b² ≥ 1
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{x \ge \alpha:\ d_1 - d_2 = 2\alpha;\ \ x \le -\alpha:\ d_2 - d_1 }\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{= 2\alpha} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ powers-keep-order takes the roots: (c/α)x ± a has the sign of x

So one equation came out of both proofs, and the sign of α² − c² sorted the curve. Far out along a branch the hyperbola runs beside two lines. On the upper right, y = (b/α)√(x² − α²), and the gap to the line y = (b/α)x is (b/α)(x − √(x² − α²)) = (b/α) · α²/(x + √(x² − α²)), which heads to 0 as x grows. So the lines y = ±(b/α)x are its asymptotes, in the sense of 10f carried over to lines that are not level.

18e4One Number for All Three

One-family technique: for a point F, a vertical or horizontal line ℓ not through F, and a real e > 0, the set {P : d(P, F) = e · d(P, ℓ)} is a parabola when e = 1, an ellipse when e < 1, and a hyperbola when e > 1. Conversely, every ellipse or hyperbola written as in this step, with c > 0, is such a set, with e = c/α, F = F₂ and ℓ the line x = α²/c. This e is not 17e’s number e; it has the accepted name eccentricity, and ℓ is a directrix. A circle has eccentricity 0, and no directrix. A slanted ℓ is left for rung 19, which turns the plane.

F = (0, 0),\ \ \ell: x = -k,\ k \gt  0 @@ slide F to (0, 0), and exchange coordinates if ℓ is horizontal; distances are kept (parabola's last line)
x^2 + y^2 = e^2(x + k)^2 \iff (1 - e^2)x^2 - 2e^2kx + y^2 = e^2k^2 @@ distance-laws, nearest-point; both sides not negative (powers-keep-order)
e = 1:\ \ y^2 = 2k\big(x + \tfrac{k}{2}\big) @@ a parabola, by parabola with x and y exchanged
e \ne 1:\ \ (1 - e^2)(x - h)^2 + y^2 = \tfrac{e^2k^2}{1 - e^2},\ \ h = \tfrac{e^2k}{1 - e^2} @@ complete the square (8i)
e \lt 1:\ \tfrac{(x - h)^2}{A^2} + \tfrac{y^2}{B^2} = 1;\ \ e \gt 1:\ \tfrac{(x - h)^2}{A^2} - \tfrac{y^2}{B^2} = 1 @@ divide by e²k²/(1 − e²), positive for e < 1 and negative for e > 1; A = ek/|1 − e²|, B² = e²k²/|1 − e²|: ellipse's equation, or hyperbola's, moved to centre (h, 0)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{converse: } d_2 = \alpha - \tfrac{c}{\alpha}x = \tfrac{c}{\alpha}\big(\tfrac{\alpha^2}{c} - x\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ellipse; on the ellipse x ≤ α < α²/c, so the bracket is d(P, ℓ) by nearest-point; hyperbola the same, branch by branch

18e5The Three, Worked

Moved so its vertex is at (0, 0) and its axis is the x axis, the curve of one-family has the equation y² = 2ℓx − (1 − e²)x², with ℓ = ek. The square y² equals the rectangle 2ℓx when e = 1, falls short of it when e < 1, and exceeds it when e > 1. The length 2ℓ, the chord through the focus at right angles to the axis, has the accepted name the latus rectum, Latin for the upright side, the name a Greek geometer gave it about 200 BCE.

y = \tfrac18 x^2:\ \ x^2 = 4 \cdot 2 \cdot y @@ parabola: focus (0, 2), directrix y = −2
\text{foci } (\pm 4, 0),\ \text{sum } 10:\ \ \tfrac{x^2}{25} + \tfrac{y^2}{9} = 1 @@ ellipse: α = 5, b = √(25 − 16) = 3; e = 4/5
9x^2 - 16y^2 = 144:\ \ \tfrac{x^2}{16} - \tfrac{y^2}{9} = 1 @@ hyperbola: c = 5, foci (±5, 0), asymptotes y = ±3x/4, e = 5/4
d(P, (0, 0)) = \tfrac12\, d(P, \{x = -6\}):\ \ \tfrac{(x - 2)^2}{16} + \tfrac{y^2}{12} = 1 @@ one-family: (3/4)x² − 3x + y² = 9; centre (2, 0), a = 4, c = 2, foci (0, 0) and (4, 0)

Who said it first

The Greek geometer of 18e5 and 18e.4.4 is Apollonius of Perga, who about 200 BCE cut a cone with a plane in his Conics and named the three curves by whether a square equals, falls short of or exceeds a rectangle laid alongside his upright side.

Let the square on the line drawn to the diameter be equal to the rectangle laid alongside the upright side, and call the section a parabola. Where the square falls short of that rectangle, call it an ellipse; where it exceeds it, call it a hyperbola.

Apollonius of Perga, Conics, Book I, propositions 11 to 13 (about 200 BCE); paraphrase
