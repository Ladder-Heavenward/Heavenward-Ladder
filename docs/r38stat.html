How Do You Measure an Ability You Cannot See?

How do you measure an ability you cannot see? No one can look inside a head and read off how well someone reads, or reasons, or climbs a ladder of mathematics. A test gives only answers, right or wrong, and each answer depends on luck as well as ability. This page writes a score as ability plus noise, measures how much a test agrees with itself using 31n’s correlation, draws the chance of a right answer as a logistic curve in ability (17), finds ability as its most likely value with 30r, and finds a few abilities behind many tests with 31q.

A score is a true ability plus noise. If the two halves of a test agree, the noise is small. Better: give each question a difficulty b, and let the chance of a right answer be 1/(1 + e^(−(θ − b))) for ability θ. Given someone’s answers, their ability is the θ that makes those answers most likely. Across many tests, a few hidden abilities explain most of the agreement.

38stat1A Score Is Ability Plus Noise

Evita writes tests. A person’s score on a test is their true ability, the score they would average over endless fair retakes, plus noise: the luck of which questions came up, a lapse, a guess.

X = T + E @@ score, true ability, noise
\text{spread}^2(X) = \text{spread}^2(T) + \text{spread}^2(E) @@ independent: squares add (30h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a test is good when most of its spread is ability}} @@

From here on, on this page, θ is a person’s hidden ability, on a scale with average 0.

38stat2Two Halves Agree: Reliability

Split the test into odd and even questions and score each half. If both halves measure the same ability with independent noise, how closely they agree across people shows how much of each half is ability.

r_{\text{halves}} = 0.7 @@ correlation (31n)
\text{whole test} = \dfrac{2r}{1 + r} = \dfrac{1.4}{1.7} \approx 0.82 @@ twice as long, noise halved
\htmlClass{new}{\text{82\% of the score’s spread is ability}} @@

38stat3The Item Curve: the Chance of a Right Answer as a Logistic in Ability

Look at one question at a time. Its chance of being answered right rises with ability, slowly at first, then steeply, then levelling off near certain.

P(\text{right} \mid \theta) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta - b)}} @@ a logistic curve (17)
\theta = b:\ \ P = \tfrac12 @@ difficulty is the ability at which it is a coin toss
\htmlClass{new}{\theta = b + 2:\ \ P \approx 0.88;\ \ \theta = b - 2:\ \ P \approx 0.12} @@

Item-curve technique: give each question a difficulty b and let the chance of a right answer be the logistic in θ − b; then people and questions sit on one scale, and a person’s ability can be read from any set of questions whose difficulties are known.

38stat4Ability as the Most Likely Value

A climber answers three questions of difficulty −1, 0 and 1: right, right, wrong. Which θ makes that most likely? Take the log of the chance and set its slope to 0 (30r).

\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\ln L = \sum (\text{answer} - P_i) = 0 @@ answer 1 if right, 0 if wrong
P_{-1} + P_0 + P_1 = 2 @@ two right answers
\theta = 0.8:\ 0.86 + 0.69 + 0.45 = 2.00 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \approx 0.8} @@ between the questions passed and the one missed

A person’s ability is the value at which their actual answers are most likely; the more questions, and the closer their difficulties to the person, the sharper it is. The Ladder’s own tests can measure a climber this way: each card dealt has a difficulty fitted from many climbers’ answers, and each answer nudges the climber’s estimate up or down.

38stat5Many Tests, Few Abilities

Give many people six tests: three of words, three of numbers. Their scores correlate: all positively, and words with words most of all. Find the principal directions of the grid of correlations (31q).

\text{first direction: all six together} @@ a general ability, about half the spread
\text{second direction: words against numbers} @@ about a fifth
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two hidden abilities explain most of six tests}} @@ the rest is each test’s own

When many tests correlate, a few hidden abilities often explain most of the agreement, read from the first principal directions of the correlations. Evita uses this to keep a test short: questions that line up with the ability she wants measured are kept, and those that mostly measure something else are cut. A pilot study with a few hundred students shows the fitted difficulties holding steady on a second group, as the model requires.

Accepted names

Score as ability plus noise is classical test theory; agreement of halves is split-half reliability, and 2r/(1 + r) the Spearman-Brown formula. The item curve is item response theory, and the logistic form the Rasch model. Hidden abilities behind many tests are found by factor analysis; choosing questions as you go is computerized adaptive testing.

Who said it first

Charles Spearman found a general factor behind many tests in 1904; Georg Rasch set out his model in 1960 (not yet revisited for the dates).
