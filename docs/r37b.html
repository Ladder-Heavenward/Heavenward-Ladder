37b · proof

How High: Pitch

On rung 6 a bass was tuned by ear, and pitch was a frequency: how many times a second the air swings. That was said of one steady note. A voice, a violin or a door slamming is not one steady note, and this step asks what makes a sound have a pitch at all. The answer is that it repeats, and the pitch is how often. Then the step marks out the window the ear can hear, which is a fact about ears and not about air, and turns pitch into a line on which equal steps are equal ratios, so that every interval of rung 6 becomes a distance you can add.

A sound has a pitch when it repeats, and the pitch is how often it repeats. The ear hears from about twenty swings a second to about twenty thousand. Pitch is measured by ratios, so its natural ruler is the logarithm, and an octave is one step on it.

37b1How Often It Repeats

6·phys took one steady note and called its frequency its pitch. A real note is not one turning: 32·phys1 found a string sounding 440 Hz, 880 Hz, 1320 Hz and every whole multiple at once. The note still has one pitch. What the turnings share is that the sum repeats.

From here on, a sound at a place repeats every T when p(t + T) = p(t) for every t, with T > 0, and its period is the least such T, when there is one. Its pitch is the frequency 1/T, in hertz. A sound with no period has no pitch in this sense: a hiss, a slammed door, the sea.

Repeat-rate technique: a sound that repeats every T is a sum of turnings at the whole multiples of 1/T, and nothing else. Proof: it is a periodic function of period T, and 32a3 writes it as cosines and sines at n/T for whole n; 32b3 says no energy is left over for any other frequency.

p(t + T) = p(t) \text{ for every } t @@ it repeats every T
p(t) = \textstyle\sum_{n} \big(u_n \cos(2\pi n t / T) + v_n \sin(2\pi n t / T)\big) @@ 32a3, cosines and sines
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only the frequencies } n/T \text{ are present; the pitch is } 1/T} \quad \blacksquare @@ 32b3, energy is kept

Notice what the technique does not need: it does not need the turning at 1/T itself to be there. A sum of turnings at 200, 300 and 400 Hz repeats every hundredth of a second, so its pitch is 100 Hz, though nothing at 100 Hz is in it. This rung comes back to that at its end (37j7).

37b2The Window the Ear Hears

The air carries turnings of any frequency. The ear does not hear them all. As 36b3 laid down three curves for the eye, this footing lays down a window for the ear, measured, not derived.

From here on, the hearing window is the interval of frequencies from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz. A young ear hears a turning in that window when it is loud enough (37c), and not outside it. Below the window a turning is felt as a shudder, not heard as a note; above it, a dog or a bat may hear it and a person does not. The top end falls with age, and many adults hear nothing above 15 000 Hz.

So the window is a fact by cases with its rate (36g3): drawn from many listeners, with a spread from one ear to the next. Its ends are soft. What is not soft is the count of octaves it holds.

Ten-octave technique: the hearing window holds just under ten octaves. Proof: an octave is a doubling (6·phys), and the number of doublings from 20 to 20 000 is log₂ 1000 = 3 log₂ 10, which is about 9.97 (17g4, logs to any base).

20 \cdot 2^k = 20\,000 @@ k doublings from the bottom to the top
2^k = 1000 @@ divide by 20
\htmlClass{new}{k = \log_2 1000 = 3\log_2 10 \approx 9.97} \quad \blacksquare @@ a piano’s 27.5 Hz to 4186 Hz is about 7.25 of them

37b3Pitch Goes by Ratios

6·phys found that the ear hears ratios, not differences: 55 Hz to 110 Hz and 220 Hz to 440 Hz are the same step. A ruler on which the same step is the same length is one that turns ratios into differences, and 17c1 built it: the logarithm turns products into sums.

From here on, the pitch line is the real line, and the place of a frequency f on it is h(f) = log₂(f/440), counted in octaves above A₄. A semitone is 1/12 of an octave on the line, and a cent is 1/100 of a semitone.

Pitch-line technique: on the pitch line the distance between two notes depends only on their ratio, and stacking intervals adds their distances.

h(g) - h(f) = \log_2(g/440) - \log_2(f/440) @@ the definition
= \log_2(g/f) @@ 17c2, quotients
\log_2\!\big(\tfrac{g}{f}\cdot\tfrac{k}{g}\big) = \log_2\tfrac{g}{f} + \log_2\tfrac{k}{g} @@ 17c1, products: stacking adds
\htmlClass{new}{\text{distance} = \log_2(\text{ratio}),\ \text{and stacks add}} \quad \blacksquare @@ an octave is 1, a semitone 1/12

So rung 6’s puzzle becomes arithmetic on a line. A pure fifth, 3/2, sits log₂ 1.5 ≈ 0.58496 octaves up, which is 701.955 cents. The fifth of twelve equal semitones is 700 cents. Twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by 12 × 1.955 ≈ 23.46 cents, the comma 6·phys found as 3¹²/2¹⁹.

The accepted names

The turning at 1/T is the fundamental, and the others are harmonics or overtones. The hearing window is the audible range; below it is infrasound, above it ultrasound. The loss of the top end with age is presbycusis. The pitch line in cents is the scale of cents.

Who said it first

The cent was brought in by Alexander Ellis in his notes to an English translation of Helmholtz in 1875 and 1885. Félix Savart and others measured the ends of the hearing window in the 1830s.

A proof that is not allowed

“The sum of turnings at 200, 300 and 400 Hz has nothing at 100 Hz. So its pitch cannot be 100 Hz.” Not allowed: pitch, from 37b1 on, is the repeat rate, and the sum repeats every hundredth of a second; the claim swaps in “the lowest turning present” for the definition.
