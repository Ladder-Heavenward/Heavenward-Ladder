9·cb8 · newsflash for 8i

Pairs that square to −1

8i found that “a square plus one makes nothing” has no answer on the line, because a square on the line is never below 0. 9e built the plane: pairs of reals. Pairs can be added and multiplied by a rule of their own. Under that rule one pair squares to negative one, and the nine rules of a field all still hold.

Pairs of reals add place by place, and multiply by a rule of their own that mixes the four entries. The pair that is nothing across and one up squares to negative one. Every quadratic now has answers.

What this calls back to, and what it stands on

Calls back to 8i (when there is no answer, and its last special challenge). Stands on 9e (the plane ℝ × ℝ), 5c (the nine rules of a field), 5h (sharing out) and 8f (square roots).

9·cb8.1Pairs, added and multiplied

Stands on 9e (the plane) and 5c (the rules of a field).

From here on, for pairs of reals: (a, b) + (c, d) = (a + c, b + d), and (a, b) · (c, d) = (ac − bd, ad + bc).

The rule for multiplying looks strange. It is chosen so that the old numbers sit inside unchanged. The pairs (a, 0) add and multiply exactly as the reals a do.

(a, 0) + (c, 0) = (a + c,\ 0) @@ the adding rule
(a, 0) \cdot (c, 0) = (ac - 0 \cdot 0,\ a \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot c) = (ac,\ 0) @@ the multiplying rule; times-zero
\htmlClass{new}{a \text{ is read as } (a, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the reals keep every sum and product

9·cb8.2A pair whose square is −1

Stands on 9·cb8.1 and 8i (no answer on the line).

The pair (0, 1) times itself is (−1, 0), the number −1. 8i found no real answer to x² + 1 = 0. Among the pairs there are exactly two.

(0, 1) \cdot (0, 1) = (0 - 1,\ 0 + 0) = (-1, 0) @@ the multiplying rule
(a, b)^2 = (a^2 - b^2,\ 2ab) @@ the rule with c = a, d = b
a^2 - b^2 = -1,\ \ 2ab = 0 @@ to equal (−1, 0)
a = 0 \text{ and } b^2 = 1 @@ b = 0 would give a² = −1, which no real does (8i)
\htmlClass{new}{x = (0, 1) \text{ or } (0, -1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two answers

From here on, i names the pair (0, 1), and a + bi names the pair (a, b). Then i² = −1.

9·cb8.3The nine rules still hold

Stands on 9·cb8.2, 5c (the nine rules of a field), 5h (sharing out) and 8f (square roots).

Written a + bi, pairs follow every old rule of adding and multiplying, with one new fact: i² = −1. Share out as on 5h, then put −1 for i². The answer is the multiplying rule.

(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 @@ share out (5h)
= (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,i @@ i² = −1

From here on, a − bi is written , and |a + bi| names √(a² + b²). Their accepted names are the conjugate and the absolute value.

(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2 @@ the bi terms cancel; −b²i² = b²
(a, b) \ne (0, 0) \Rightarrow a^2 + b^2 \ne 0 @@ square-never-negative (8g): a² + b² is 0 only when a = b = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{1}{a + bi} = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ times a − bi over itself

Turn-about, regrouping and sharing out for the pairs come from the same rules for the reals, by multiplying out both sides. So the pairs obey all nine rules. The pairs are a field. Their accepted name is the complex numbers, written ℂ, and the ladder uses that name from here on.

9·cb8.4Quadratics with no answer on the line

Stands on 9·cb8.3 and 8i (completing the sameness, the quadratic formula).

(x + 1)^2 = -4 @@ completing the sameness (8i)
(x + 1)^2 = (2i)^2 = (-2i)^2 @@ i² = −1
\htmlClass{new}{x = -1 + 2i \text{ or } -1 - 2i} @@ the answers of 9·cb8.2.1, slid by −1
(-1 + 2i)^2 + 2(-1 + 2i) + 5 = (-3 - 4i) + (-2 + 4i) + 5 = 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ the check

In ℂ every quadratic with real coefficients has answers: when b² − 4ac < 0 they are the pair −b/2a ± (√(4ac − b²)/2a) i.

The ladder has every piece needed to build these numbers now. It will do nothing more with them for a while. Hold on to them; they come back at 18f.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The pairs a + bi are the complex numbers, ℂ. a is the real part and b the imaginary part. The pairs (a, 0) are the reals inside ℂ; the pairs (0, b) are called imaginary. a − bi is the conjugate of a + bi, and |a + bi| its absolute value, or absolute value.

Who said it first

Gerolamo Cardano (Ars Magna, 1545) met 5 ± √−15 as the two parts of 10 with product 40, and set them aside as useless. Rafael Bombelli (L'Algebra, 1572) gave rules for calculating with them and used them to reach real answers of cubics. René Descartes (La Géométrie, 1637) called them “imaginary”, and the word stuck. William Rowan Hamilton (1837) defined them as pairs of reals with exactly the rules of this step, so that nothing imaginary was left in them.
