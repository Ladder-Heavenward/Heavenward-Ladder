How Sure Can You Be With Only the Sample You Have?

How sure can you be with only the sample you have? For an average, rung 30 gave a formula for how far a sample’s answer tends to miss. For a median, a ratio or anything more tangled, there may be no formula at all. One idea, from 1979, answers them all: treat the sample as if it were the whole population, draw new samples from it, and watch how the answer wobbles. This page does it exactly on a sample of three, checks it against the formula for an average, applies it to a median, and finds the one kind of answer it gets wrong.

Draw n values from the sample, with putting back, many times; work out the answer on each; the spread of those answers stands in for the spread of the answer over real samples. For an average it gives the formula’s spread exactly. Read a range from the middle 95% of the redrawn answers. It fails for answers set by the extremes, like the largest value, which a redraw can never exceed.

31stat21The Sample as a Stand-In

Mike measures how long three batteries last: 1, 4 and 10 days. He wants the median, and how far it might be from the median of all such batteries. Rung 30 would draw fresh samples from the population; he has none. So he draws from his sample instead.

From here on, on this page, a redraw is n values picked from the sample one at a time, each pick equally likely to be any of the n, with putting back, so a value can come twice. With n = 3 there are 3³ = 27 redraws, all equally likely.

31stat22Checked Against the Average

First, a case where the answer is known. Work out the average of each of the 27 redraws, and their spread.

\text{the 27 averages average } 5 @@ the sample’s own average
\text{their spread} \approx 2.16 @@ counted over all 27
\text{the sample’s spread} / \sqrt{3} = 3.74/1.73 \approx 2.16 @@ rung 30’s formula, with the sample’s spread
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same}} @@ exactly, for every sample

Redraw technique: the spread of an answer over redraws from the sample stands in for its spread over fresh samples from the population; for an average it gives the formula’s answer exactly. The redraw needs no formula, so it works where none is known.

31stat23A Median With No Formula

Now the median of each redraw.

\text{median } 4:\ 13 \text{ redraws};\ \ 1:\ 7;\ \ 10:\ 7 @@ count them
\text{average} \approx 4.8,\ \text{spread} \approx 3.3 @@ over all 27
\text{real samples: thousands of redraws, picked at random} @@ n¹⁰ cannot be listed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a range: the middle } 95\% \text{ of the redrawn medians}} @@ read off, with no formula

For any answer worked out from a sample, the redrawn answers give its spread and a range, by the same procedure. With ten values there are 10¹⁰ redraws, so a computer picks a few thousand at random and lets the bell of rung 30 settle their summary.

31stat24Where Redrawing Fails

Try the largest value. Batteries’ longest life is an answer about the edge.

\text{largest} = 10 \text{ unless all three picks miss } 10 @@ each pick misses 10 with chance ⅔
1 - (2/3)^3 = 19/27 \text{ redraws give exactly } 10 @@ none can give more
\text{the true longest life is surely more than } 10 @@ a sample’s largest is below the population’s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{redrawing cannot see past the sample’s edge}} @@ it is too sure, and too low

Redrawing fails for answers decided by the extremes, since no redraw can exceed the sample. It works for answers that each value nudges a little, like averages, medians of large samples, slopes and ratios.

31stat25Why It Works, and When to Trust It

Redrawing swaps the unknown population for the sample’s own spread of values. Two errors remain: the sample is not the population, and a finite number of redraws is not all of them.

\text{sample} \ne \text{population} @@ shrinks as n grows (30h)
\text{some redraws} \ne \text{all redraws} @@ shrinks as more are drawn
\text{second error: as small as you like, for computer time} @@ the cheap one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{first error: as small as the data allow}} @@ the honest limit

With a fair sample of fair size, the redrawn spread is close to the true one; nothing makes a small or slanted sample look like more than it is. Redrawing replaced pages of formulas with a loop, which is why it spread so fast once computers were cheap.

Accepted names

Redrawing from the sample is the bootstrap, a redraw a bootstrap sample, and the range from the middle 95% a percentile interval. Drawing with putting back is resampling with replacement. Answers decided by the extremes are where the bootstrap is inconsistent. An older relative, leaving out one value at a time, is the jackknife.

Who said it first

Bradley Efron introduced the bootstrap in the Annals of Statistics in 1979. Maurice Quenouille and John Tukey had built the jackknife in the 1950s.
