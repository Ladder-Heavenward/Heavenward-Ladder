13e · proof

The chain rule

In a chain, the inside's output is the outside's input. Name the inside, as 9f and 11c did. Change the input a little: the inside changes at its own rate, and the outside changes in turn, at its rate taken where the inside stands. Rates through a chain multiply. The obvious proof divides by the inside’s change, which can be 0 when the input’s change is not. This step builds a helper function that never divides by it, and proves the rule with every line cited. Then comes the d-mark for the slope, read as one mark; the slope of the square root; and what the rule says about a function and its undoing.

Name the inside and the place it stands. Let a helper be the outside’s chord slope from that place, or its slope when the change is 0. Then the chain’s chord slope is the helper times the inside’s chord slope, and both head where they should.

13e1Rates through a chain

Take y = (2x + 1)³. Taken apart from the outside, 9f's way, the last operation is cubing. So the outside is f(r) = r³ and the inside is r = g(x) = 2x + 1. At x = 1 the inside stands at r₀ = g(1) = 3. The inside's rate is 2 (slope-of-a-line): each step of x moves r by 2. The outside's rate, taken where the inside stands, is 3 · 3² = 27 (slope-of-x³): each step of r near 3 moves y by about 27. Two rates in a row should multiply: 27 · 2 = 54. Check by expanding: (2x + 1)³ = 8x³ + 12x² + 6x + 1 (4k's binomial technique, read in ℝ). Its slope at 1 is 24 + 24 + 6 = 54 by polynomial-slope. They agree.

The obvious proof says the same thing with changes. Write Δr = g(p + h) − g(p) and Δy = f(r₀ + Δr) − f(r₀). Then Δy/h = (Δy/Δr) · (Δr/h), the outside's chord slope times the inside's. Let h head to 0, and the two head to f′(r₀) and g′(p). It is almost right.

13e2Where the obvious proof breaks

It divides by Δr. That is licensed only if Δr ≠ 0 for every small h ≠ 0, and nothing promises that.

g(x) = 2:\ \ \Delta r = 2 - 2 = \htmlClass{new}{0} \text{ for every } h @@ a constant inside: the cancelling column divides by 0 at every h
g(x) = x^2 q(x) \text{ at } 0:\ \ \Delta r = h^2 q(h) @@ 13b's slope-at-0-only: g has slope 0 at 0. q is 9a's function
\Delta r = \htmlClass{new}{0} \text{ at every irrational } h @@ q is 0 there; and every window around 0 holds irrationals (everyday-counting-passes, 8e)
\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta r} \text{ names nothing at those } h @@ 5e's nothing-times-0-is-1. No rearrangement of the cancelling column is licensed

The rule's answer is still right in both cases. With the constant inside, f ∘ g is constant and its slope is 0 = f′(2) · 0. Now take f(r) = r² + r and g(x) = x²q(x) at 0. Then f ∘ g is x⁴q(x) + x²q(x), since q(x)² = q(x), its values being 0 and 1. Its chord slope at 0 is h³q(h) + h q(h), squeezed to 0 by |h|³ + |h| (squeezed-to-0). And the rule gives f′(0) · g′(0) = 1 · 0 = 0. The rule is fine. The proof needs mending.

13e3The helper

The mend comes from a well-known modern calculus book. Stop dividing by the inside's change. Instead, build a function that is the outside's chord slope when the change is not 0, and the outside's slope when it is.

Page letters, said out loud. In this step R names a reach of f around r₀: f is defined on (r₀ − R, r₀ + R). k names a change of the outside's input, any real with |k| < R, 0 included. And w names the helper, defined below. r keeps the learner's job from 9f: the inside's output.

Helper technique: let f have a slope at r₀, and be defined on (r₀ − R, r₀ + R). Let w(k) = (f(r₀ + k) − f(r₀))/k when 0 < |k| < R, and w(0) = f′(r₀). Then (i) w is continuous at 0, and (ii) f(r₀ + k) − f(r₀) = w(k) · k for every k with |k| < R. The definition is by cases on k = 0 or not, which three-way-order on ℝ settles: 7d's definitions by cases.

w \text{ is defined on } (-R, R),\ \ 0 \text{ an input} @@ the two cases cover every k with |k| < R (three-way-order on ℝ)
0 \lt |k| \lt R \;\Rightarrow\; w(k) = \dfrac{f(r_0 + k) - f(r_0)}{k} @@ the first case: w agrees with f's chord slope at r₀ off 0
\lim_{k \to 0} w(k) = f'(r_0) = \htmlClass{new}{w(0)} @@ agreeing-near (10a) and 13a's line for f′(r₀); then the second case
\text{(i)}\ \ w \text{ is continuous at } 0 @@ ︿ “continuous” (11a): 0 is an input, w is defined near 0, the limit is the value
k \ne 0:\ \ f(r_0 + k) - f(r_0) = w(k) \cdot k @@ multiply the first case by k: regroup for times on ℝ, reciprocal on ℝ
k = 0:\ \ f(r_0) - f(r_0) = 0 = w(0) \cdot 0 @@ negative on ℝ; anything-times-zero
\text{(ii)}\ \ f(r_0 + k) - f(r_0) = \htmlClass{new}{w(k) \cdot k} \text{ for every } |k| \lt R \quad \blacksquare @@ reasoning by cases on k = 0 ∨ k ≠ 0 (three-way-order on ℝ: rung 7's either-way principle)

No line divided by k where k might be 0. The helper's value at 0 was chosen to be the one value that makes it continuous there, as 11f's mending chose a value; that is all (i) says.

13e4The proof, every line cited

Chain technique for slopes (the chain rule): rates through a chain multiply, the outside's taken where the inside stands, the inside's taken at p. Precisely: let g have a slope at p, and let f have a slope at r₀ = g(p). Then f ∘ g has a slope at p, and (f ∘ g)′(p) = f′(g(p)) · g′(p).

\text{call } R:\ f \text{ defined on } (r_0 - R, r_0 + R) @@ the hypothesis on f, by 13a's line; the helper w of helper for this R
\begin{gathered}\text{call } \delta_0:\ |x - p| \lt \delta_0 \Rightarrow\\ x \in \operatorname{dom} g \land |g(x) - r_0| \lt R\end{gathered} @@ g is continuous at p (slope-means-no-gap, 13b); continuity-unpacked (11a) with ε = R; then the lesser with g's reach (lesser-below-both)
f \circ g \text{ is defined on } (p - \delta_0, p + \delta_0) @@ g(x) lies in f's interval there: 9f's composite-domain
\begin{gathered}0 \lt |h| \lt \delta_0:\ \ \Delta r = g(p + h) - g(p)\\ |\Delta r| \lt R\end{gathered} @@ 13a's Δ, written before the inside's letter
\begin{gathered}(f \circ g)(p + h) - (f \circ g)(p)\\ = f(r_0 + \Delta r) - f(r_0)\end{gathered} @@ substitute for the expression: g(p + h) = r₀ + Δr (adding-keeps-answers), g(p) = r₀
= \htmlClass{new}{w(\Delta r) \cdot \Delta r} @@ helper (ii), with k = Δr. Nothing is divided by Δr
\dfrac{(f \circ g)(p + h) - (f \circ g)(p)}{h} = w(\Delta r) \cdot \dfrac{\Delta r}{h} @@ multiply by h⁻¹ (regroup for times on ℝ); h ≠ 0
\dfrac{\Delta r}{h} \to \htmlClass{keep}{g'(p)} @@ the hypothesis on g
\Delta r \to 0 @@ change-heads-to-0 (13b): g has a slope at w
w(\Delta r) \to w(0) = \htmlClass{keep}{f'(r_0)} @@ limit-passes-inside (11c): the inside h ↦ Δr heads to 0, w is continuous at 0 (helper i), and w(Δr) is defined for 0 < |h| < δ₀
\begin{gathered}(f \circ g)'(p) = f'(r_0) \cdot g'(p)\\ = \htmlClass{new}{f'(g(p)) \cdot g'(p)} \quad \blacksquare\end{gathered} @@ limits-multiply (10c), agreeing-near; substitute for the expression r₀ = g(p)

Look at what each hypothesis paid for. The slope of g paid twice. It paid for Δr/h heading to the inside's slope. And through change-heads-to-0 it paid for Δr heading to 0, which is what lets the helper be read at a change heading to 0. The slope of f paid once, for the helper's continuity at 0. And the cancelling has gone: Δr appears as a factor, never as a bottom.

13e5Name the inside r

In use, the rule is three moves. Name the inside r and find where it stands, r₀. Take the outside's slope as though r were the input, at r₀. Multiply by the inside's slope, and substitute for the expression.

y = (2x + 1)^3:\ \ r = 2x + 1,\ \ y = r^3 @@ take the chain apart from the outside (9f)
\text{at } x = 1:\ \ r_0 = 3,\ \ g'(1) = 2,\ \ f'(3) = 3 \cdot 3^2 = 27 @@ slope-of-a-line; slope-of-x³ at r₀
(f \circ g)'(1) = 27 \cdot 2 = \htmlClass{new}{54} @@ chain for slopes; agrees with polynomial-slope on the expanded cubic
y = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}:\ \ r = x^2 + 1,\ \ y = \dfrac1r @@ the outside is 1/r, defined at every r ≠ 0, and r ≥ 1 here (square-never-negative)
\text{slope at } x:\ \ -\dfrac{1}{r^2}\cdot 2x = \htmlClass{new}{-\dfrac{2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2}} @@ slope-of-one-over-x at r, times slope-of-x²; substitute for the expression r = x² + 1
\text{at } 1:\ \ -\dfrac{2}{4} = -\tfrac12 @@ substitute for the expression x = 1

The second chain needs no knowledge of what a quotient's slope is, only 1/r's. That is one reason 13f builds the quotient rule from this rule.

The square root's slope comes directly from the definition, with 8i's conjugate.

Slope-of-√ technique: at every y > 0, √ has slope 1/(2√y).

0 \lt |h| \lt y:\ \ \dfrac{\sqrt{y + h} - \sqrt{y}}{h} @@ √ is defined on (0, 2y), an open interval around y
= \dfrac{(\sqrt{y + h} - \sqrt{y})(\sqrt{y + h} + \sqrt{y})}{h\,(\sqrt{y + h} + \sqrt{y})} @@ multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (8i), which is positive
= \dfrac{(y + h) - y}{h\,(\sqrt{y + h} + \sqrt{y})} @@ difference-of-squares (5i), and √t · √t = t (8f's line)
= \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{y + h} + \sqrt{y}}} @@ cancel h (fractions-of-rationals i); h ≠ 0
\to \dfrac{1}{\sqrt y + \sqrt y} = \htmlClass{keep}{\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ root-limit (10f): √(y + h) → √y as h → 0; limits-add, limits-divide

So (x^{1/2})′ = ½ x^{−1/2} for x > 0, with 8i's fractional powers: x^{1/2} = √x and x^{−1/2} = 1/√x. 13d said its induction could not reach the power ½; this road does, and the formula has power for slopes's shape. A chain: √(x² + 1) at 2 has r₀ = 5, outside slope 1/(2√5), inside slope 4, so its slope is 4/(2√5) = 2/√5 ≈ 0.894.

And 13d's displaced-slopes is a chain: f(x − s) has the inside x − s, whose slope is 1, so its slope at p is f′(p − s) · 1.

13e6The d-mark

One of the two inventors wrote the slope of y with respect to x as dy/dx. Call it the d-mark. It makes the chain rule look like arithmetic. The ladder keeps the mark and gives it its meaning.

From here on, when y names the outputs of a function f of x, as in y = f(x), dy/dx names f′, and dy/dx at x₀ names f′(x₀). Read it “d y d x”. It is one mark, written as a fraction for history's sake. d is not a name, dy and dx name nothing on their own, and the bar is not 5c's dividing. dy/dx means the limit of the chord slopes Δy/Δx, never a quotient of two numbers. With other letters it is read the same way: dr/dx, dy/dr.

Reuses, said out loud. The letter d has named things before: 4j's divisor d in d ∣ n, 12·phys's distance d, and others in their own steps. Inside dy/dx it is part of the mark and names nothing. The slash and the bar have been 5c's dividing and 5·cb2's quotient set a/∼. In dy/dx they are neither, and what stands around them says which, as 5·cb2 said of its own slash.

In d-marks the chain rule reads

with dy/dr taken at r₀ = g(x₀) and dr/dx at x₀. It looks like cancelling dr. It is not: dy/dr and dr/dx are single marks, and the line that licenses it is chain for slopes, whose proof never divided by Δr. The resemblance is why the marks are worth keeping: they remember the rule for you. It is also why they need watching: they remember a proof that is wrong.

The other inventor marked a flowing quantity's rate with a dot over its letter, and the English kept his dots for a century. The Continent kept the d, which made rules like this one easy to see. The quarrel between their followers, over who had found the method first, lasted longer than either man.

13e7Undoing a function

Undoing's-slope technique: let f have a slope at x₀, and let g undo f near x₀: g(f(x)) = x for every x in some open interval around x₀. If g has a slope at y₀ = f(x₀), then g′(y₀) · f′(x₀) = 1. In d-marks: dx/dy · dy/dx = 1.

(g \circ f)'(x_0) = g'(f(x_0)) \cdot f'(x_0) @@ chain for slopes, with f the inside and g the outside
g \circ f \text{ agrees with } x \mapsto x \text{ on an open interval around } x_0 @@ the hypothesis
(g \circ f)'(x_0) = \htmlClass{keep}{1} @@ slopes-decided-near (13a) and slope-of-a-line: x ↦ x has slope 1
g'(y_0) \cdot f'(x_0) = \htmlClass{new}{1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap axiom; y₀ = f(x₀)

So neither slope is 0, and g′(y₀) = 1/f′(x₀) (5e's nothing-times-0-is-1 and reciprocal, read in ℝ). Check it on 9d's pair, x² kept to (0, ∞) and √: at x₀ = 3, the slope of x² is 6, and the slope of √ at 9 is 1/(2 · 3) = 1/6 by slope-of-√. Their product is 1.

Turned about, undoing's-slope refutes. x³ has slope 0 at 0 (slope-of-x³), and ∛ undoes it everywhere (9d's odd powers, 8i's odd roots). If ∛ had a slope at 0, undoing's-slope would give ∛′(0) · 0 = 1, that is 0 = 1 (anything-times-zero): totally false. So ∛ has no slope at 0. Undoing's-slope assumes that g has a slope; when g has one is a harder question, and 13l asks it.

Who said it first

The helper is from Michael Spivak’s Calculus. The d-mark is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s; the dots are Isaac Newton’s. The Cambridge students of question 13e.4.4 were Charles Babbage, John Herschel and George Peacock. 9a’s function q is Dirichlet’s function.

Spivak builds the helper as a function of h: the outside's chord slope across the inside's change when that change is not 0, and the outside's slope when it is. That function is this step's w(Δr). He stresses that the outer slope must be taken at g(p), where the inside has got to, and warns that Leibniz's notation makes the rule look like the cancelling of fractions, which hides exactly the case the helper handles.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 10

Accepted names: the d-mark is Leibniz notation, and the dot is Newton’s notation; the chain technique for slopes is the chain rule.
