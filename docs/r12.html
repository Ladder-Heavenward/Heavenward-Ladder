Rung 12 · theory

Skipping nothing on the way

Rung 11 asked about one input at a time: does the function land where it was headed, here? This rung asks about a whole stretch of inputs at once. Take a function that is continuous at every input of an interval that keeps both its ends, with no discontinuity anywhere on it. Three things follow, and none of them is a fact about one input. Its outputs take every value between the two end values: it does not skip. They stay below some bound and above another: it does not run off without end. And among them there is a greatest and a least: it reaches its top and its bottom. Each of the three is proved from rung 8's line, at exactly one line of its proof, and each is false on the fractions.

You got this. Rung 11’s one input at a time is yours now, and this rung asks you to hold a whole stretch in your head at once. It is harder, and that is the good part: each fact here is one you could not have reached two rungs ago.

Gather the inputs where something is still true. Take the least upper bound of what you gathered. Then use continuity there to show the something must be true at the bound itself, or a little past it.

The class · read it through, then go to 12a

What it means to say it

To skip a value is to take values on both sides of it without taking it. 11a's step u skips ½, and 1/x skips 0, each at a discontinuity (12a6). The rung's first claim is that a discontinuity is the only way to skip. Take a function continuous on an interval that keeps both its ends, in 11a's interval form. That is, continuous at every input inside, from the right at the left end, and from the left at the right end. It takes every value between its two end values.

“Curve” is 11a's word: the graph of a function kept to an interval, a set of pairs of reals, and nothing about shape. So the title says exactly the claim. If the curve's pairs start with an output below some height and end with an output above it, then one of its pairs has exactly that height. This is the intermediate value technique: the values in the middle are all taken.

Two more techniques ride on the same hypothesis. A function continuous on such an interval is bounded there: some real is above all its outputs, and some real below. And it reaches its bounds: among its outputs there is a greatest and a least, each the output of some input of the interval. That is the highest-and-lowest technique, “extreme” meaning greatest or least.

Each technique needs both of its words. Without “continuous”, a function skips or runs off without end (12b2); without “both ends”, 1/x runs off and x creeps up on 1 and never gets there (12b2, 12c2). The interval that keeps both its ends, which 12a names a closed interval, is the place where all three are true.

Where it comes from

For most of its history the first claim was not thought to need a proof. One philosopher made a whole philosophy out of it: nature makes no leaps, and nothing passes from one state to another without passing through every state between.

A mathematician in Prague, whose definition of continuity rung 11 paraphrased, thought otherwise. His paper of 1817 has the claim in its title. It promises a purely analytic proof that between any two inputs whose outputs have opposite signs there lies at least one zero. His point was that a drawing is not a proof. A drawing shows one curve; the technique is about all of them, including ones nobody can draw. To prove it, he first argued that a collection of reals bounded above has a least upper bound. That is rung 8's no-holes, and he then made it do the work. He had no construction of the reals to stand on. His argument for that first step leaned on a fact about endless sequences that he could not fully justify. The man whom rung 8's survey met in Zürich in 1858 felt the same unease from the other side.

His foundation is rung 8: the reals as cuts, and the technique that every set of reals with a member and an upper bound has a least upper bound. This rung is where that technique earns its keep. The other two claims came later in the century; 12c3 tells where.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves four things.

First, the intermediate value technique. A function continuous on a closed interval, below 0 at its left end and above 0 at its right end, is 0 at some input between. The proof takes the least upper bound of the inputs where the function is still below 0, and continuity forbids the value there to be above 0 or below it (12a4). Then 0 is not special: any value between the two end values is taken, by moving the target.

Second, bounded on a closed interval: such a function stays below some bound and above another. The proof takes the least upper bound of the inputs up to which the function is bounded, and continuity carries it a little past any stopping place short of the right end (12b3).

Third, the highest-and-lowest technique: such a function reaches a greatest and a least value. If no input reached the least upper bound of the outputs, the reciprocal of the gap to it would be continuous, so bounded by the second principle, against “least” (12c3).

Fourth, why having no holes matters. Over the fractions alone, x² − 2 is continuous, negative at 1, positive at 2, and never 0 (12d2). Run the first principle's proof over the fractions, and every line goes through but one: the least upper bound of the gathered inputs does not exist. The line that fails is the line that used rung 8.

What follows

A polynomial whose degree is odd has a zero. Far out it passes every bound both ways, in 10f's words, so on a long enough closed interval the first principle finds a zero (12e8). A polynomial of even degree may have none: x² + 1 is never below 1.

The first principle says that a zero exists, not where. Bisection finds out: test the middle, keep the half whose ends still disagree, and repeat (12e1). Each test halves the stretch the zero is trapped in, so you can say in advance how many tests any accuracy costs, with nothing but 4g's powers of 2.

And a continuous function takes intervals to intervals. On a closed interval, its outputs are exactly every real from its least value to its greatest. The second and third principles give the two ends, and the first fills in everything between (12e9).

What you can do because you know it

You can prove that an equation has an answer without finding it. x³ + x − 3 = 0 has an answer between 1 and 2, because the left side is a polynomial, −1 at 1 and 7 at 2. You can then find the answer to as many decimal places as you like, by hand, with whole-number arithmetic, and know in advance how much arithmetic that will be. You can say that a greatest value exists before you know where it is, and so know that a search for it is not a search for nothing. You can say exactly which hypothesis a failure is missing: a discontinuity, a missing end, or a missing real.

Then 12·phys hangs weights on a bar. The law of the lever says the bar balances on its support when the weights on each side, times their distances from the support, come out the same. The turning effect of the whole load changes continuously as the support slides along the bar. It turns one way at the left end and the other way at the right end. So, by the first principle, there is a place where it balances. Halving finds it, without knowing the load at all.

These are early moves. The ladder has watched you make them, and it has seen only the tip of what you will do.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 12a carries a green box: the middle of a change. A continuous change has a middle, even when no one catches it there. And 12e carries the cloud instead of the raindrop: a zero you cannot write as a dot is given as a trap, with its width.

And 12·econ asks whether a market must have a price where buyers and sellers match, and finds one whenever supply and demand have no jumps. It carries a box on the middle of a change, proved.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down. Each of the three proofs leans on rung 8's no-holes at one line, and no-holes itself is constructive: the least upper bound of a set of cuts is their union. But each proof also splits a real three ways, below, at or above, by rung 8's three-way-order on ℝ. And it turns “not an upper bound” into “some member beyond”, by rung 7's not-every. Both of those lean on rung 7's either-way principle. Constructive mathematicians, who keep rung 1's gap, accept only a weaker form of the first principle: for every positive gap, some input whose output is within the gap of 0. And bisection, run on a polynomial with fraction coefficients, tests only fractions and compares only fractions. 5d's settled-order on ℚ settles those without the either-way principle. Nothing on this rung uses the choosing axiom. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that a continuous curve doesn't skip, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

The philosopher of no leaps is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. The mathematician in Prague is Bernard Bolzano, whose 1817 paper is the “Rein analytischer Beweis”. The one who felt the unease at Zürich is Richard Dedekind, whose cuts are rung 8’s reals. The Berlin teacher is Karl Weierstrass.

Leibniz counts among his greatest and best-confirmed maxims that nature makes no leaps: nothing passes from one state to another without passing through every state between.

Leibniz, New Essays on Human Understanding (written 1704, published 1765), preface

Dedekind says that, teaching the calculus at Zürich in 1858, he found he had to fall back on pictures of magnitudes to prove that a quantity which grows without ceasing but stays below a bound must approach a limit, and that his dissatisfaction was so strong that he resolved to keep thinking until he found a purely arithmetic and rigorous foundation for the principles of analysis.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), preface, after Beman (1901)

Accepted names: the intermediate value technique is the intermediate value theorem (or Bolzano’s theorem); bounded on a closed interval is the boundedness theorem; the highest-and-lowest technique is the extreme value theorem (Weierstrass’s).
