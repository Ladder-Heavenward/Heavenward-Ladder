34e · proof

The same shape

Rung 20 sorted geometries by the moves they keep. Slides and turns keep distances, similarities keep angles, and each bigger family of moves keeps fewer facts. This step takes the biggest family the rung can use: every matching of two spaces, point for point, that carries open sets to open sets both ways. Two spaces matched like that are the same shape. A coffee mug and a ring doughnut are the same shape, and so are a stretch and the whole line. What survives every such matching is what open sets can see: being in one piece, being finitely covered, keeping points apart. Distance does not survive, and neither does completeness. The step uses what survives to tell spaces apart: a closed stretch from an open one, a loop from a stretch, the line from the plane. It also finds a matching that is continuous and still not a matching of shapes, and the condition that rules such matchings out. And it ends on a pair it cannot yet tell apart.

A same-shape map is a bijection that is continuous and whose backwards map is continuous, and same shape is a sameness relation among spaces. A same-shape map carries one piece to one piece, finitely covered to finitely covered, paths to paths and kept-apart points to kept-apart points, so anything defined by open sets alone survives it. Taking a point out of two spaces of the same shape leaves two spaces of the same shape, with the same number of pieces. A continuous bijection from a finitely covered space onto a space that keeps points apart is a same-shape map, and without those two conditions it can fail to be one.

34e1Same shape

A map f from a space X to a space Y is a same-shape map when it is a bijection (9c), it is continuous, and its backwards map f⁻¹ (9d) is continuous. Then f matches the open sets of X with the open sets of Y: U is open exactly when f(U) is. X and Y are the same shape when some same-shape map joins them. The accepted name of such a map is a homeomorphism.

Same-shape technique: same shape is a sameness relation: every space is the same shape as itself, X and Y are the same shape both ways round, and two same-shape maps in a row make one.

\mathrm{id}_X \text{ is a same-shape map} @@ it is its own backwards map, and pulls each open set back to itself
f \text{ same-shape} \Rightarrow f^{-1} \text{ same-shape} @@ a bijection whose backwards map is f (9d), both continuous
g \circ f \text{ continuous},\ \ (g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1} \text{ continuous} @@ the pull-back-chain technique (34b2) and socks-and-shoes (9d)
\htmlClass{new}{X \simeq Y \text{ is a sameness relation}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 2g’s three demands

Some pairs. (−1, 1) and the whole line, by t ↦ t/(1 − |t|), whose backwards map is y ↦ y/(1 + |y|); both are continuous by 11b. So any open stretch is the same shape as the line. A square and a disc, by pushing each point along its ray from the centre. The surface of a cube and a sphere, by the same push. In the sorting of 20h, these are the moves of the largest geometry on this ladder so far, and it keeps the fewest facts.

Anexact, yet rigorous · connections, not families

A potter in Port Townsend throws a mug with one handle, and a baker in Victoria fries a ring doughnut. One is glazed clay and the other is dough. Squeeze the mug’s body into its handle and it becomes a ring. As shapes, they are one thing.

A shape is known by how its points connect, not by what it is made of or how far apart its points are.

Rung 9 defined a function by what it does to each input, not by its formula. A shape is known the same way. Two spaces are the same shape when the matching carries every open set to an open set and back: when they connect the same way. What they are made of, how big they are, and where their corners are does not enter.

That is a strong claim, and an exact one. It says nothing about which mug holds more coffee, and the potter still needs a ruler.

Is a pair of trousers the same shape as a pretzel with two holes? What would you need to check, and what would you not?

34e2What a same-shape map keeps

Kept-by-shape technique: if X and Y are the same shape, then X is in one piece, joined by paths, finitely covered, or keeps points apart exactly when Y is.

f : X \to Y \text{ same-shape},\ \ Y = f(X) @@ f is onto
X \text{ in one piece} \Rightarrow Y \text{ in one piece} @@ the image-one-piece technique (34c2)
X \text{ finitely covered} \Rightarrow Y \text{ finitely covered} @@ the image-covered technique (34d3)
\gamma \text{ a path in } X \Rightarrow f \circ \gamma \text{ a path in } Y @@ the pull-back-chain technique; f is onto, so every pair in Y is reached
x \in U,\ y \in V \text{ separate} \Rightarrow f(x) \in f(U),\ f(y) \in f(V) \text{ separate, open} @@ f⁻¹ is continuous, so f(U) is open; f is one-to-one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{and the same from } Y \text{ to } X} \quad \blacksquare @@ run the lines with f⁻¹

Any property defined by open sets alone is kept this way. Such properties are shape-properties, and they tell spaces apart. [0, 1] is finitely covered and (0, 1) is not, so they are not the same shape, though they differ by two points.

Completeness is not a shape-property. (−1, 1) with |x − y| is not complete, the line is, and they are the same shape. 34a5 promised this. A distance can be stretched until a missing end is pushed off to infinity, and the open sets never notice.

34e3Taking a point out

Point-out technique: if f is a same-shape map from X to Y, then X with x taken out and Y with f(x) taken out are the same shape, and so they have the same number of pieces.

g = f \text{ on } X \setminus \{x\},\ \ g : X \setminus \{x\} \to Y \setminus \{f(x)\} @@ f is one-to-one, so nothing else lands on f(x)
g^{-1}(V \cap (Y \setminus \{f(x)\})) = f^{-1}(V) \cap (X \setminus \{x\}) @@ open in a part (34b5): g is continuous
\text{the same for } g^{-1} @@ f⁻¹ is continuous, and its part is too
\htmlClass{new}{X \setminus \{x\} \simeq Y \setminus \{f(x)\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so their pieces match one for one
  
\text{the line} \setminus \{0\} \text{ is split};\ \ \text{the plane} \setminus \{p\} \text{ is joined by paths} @@ the negatives and positives; go round p (34c3)
\text{the line} \not\simeq \text{the plane} @@ the point-out technique: some point would have to leave both split
\text{the loop} \setminus \{p\} \text{ is joined by paths, for every } p @@ the rest of a loop is an arc
\htmlClass{new}{[0, 1] \not\simeq \text{the loop}} \quad \blacksquare @@ [0, 1] with ½ taken out is split

A figure eight is not a loop either: take out its crossing point and it falls into two pieces, while a loop never does. But take a point out of the plane and out of space, and both remain joined by paths. The point-out technique cannot tell the plane from space. 34g builds a sharper test.

34e4A matching that is not a same-shape map

Wrap [0, 1) round the circle by t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt), as 21b traced it. It is continuous, one-to-one, and onto. It is not a same-shape map: the circle is finitely covered and [0, 1) is not. Its backwards map tears the circle open at (1, 0), where points just below and just above land near 1 and near 0.

Covered-matching technique: a continuous bijection from a finitely covered space onto a space that keeps points apart is a same-shape map.

C \subseteq X \text{ closed} \Rightarrow C \text{ finitely covered} @@ the closed-part-covered technique (34d2)
\Rightarrow f(C) \text{ finitely covered} \Rightarrow f(C) \text{ closed in } Y @@ the image-covered technique (34d3); covered-is-closed (34d2), Y keeps points apart
(f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C) @@ the pull-back of C under f⁻¹ is what f makes of it
\htmlClass{new}{f^{-1} \text{ pulls closed sets back to closed sets, so it is continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ take leftovers: pull-backs of open sets are open

So the loop glued from [0, 1] in 34b5, which is finitely covered as the image of [0, 1], is the same shape as the circle: the glued-map technique makes the wrapping map continuous on it, and it is one-to-one there because 0 and 1 are now one point. The same argument shows that the square with its sides glued in 34k’s ways is the same shape as the surfaces it is meant to be.

The accepted names

A same-shape map is a homeomorphism, and spaces of the same shape are homeomorphic. A shape-property is a topological property, or topological invariant. The covered-matching technique says that a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. Points whose removal splits a space are cut points.

Who said it first

Felix Klein’s programme of 1872 sorted geometries by groups of moves and counted the study of continuous deformations among them. Henri Poincaré named the matchings homeomorphisms in 1895. The coffee cup and the doughnut are a classroom joke of the twentieth century.

A proof that is not allowed

“[0, 1) and the circle are the same shape: wrap the stretch once round and every point lands exactly once.” Not allowed: a same-shape map needs a continuous backwards map. Points of the circle just below (1, 0) are carried back near 1, and (1, 0) itself to 0; the backwards map jumps there.
