38·now · research front

How little energy can a switch spend?

How little energy can a switch spend? 38·eng found that each flip of a switch turns C V² into warmth, half in filling its store and half in emptying it, and that this heat is what stopped the clock. But the half lost in filling came from filling fast: the supply jumped to its full voltage at once, and the whole push fell across the switch. Raise the supply slowly instead, a ramp in place of a step, and the store follows close behind, the push across the switch stays small, and the heat falls as the ramp gets longer. Then the charge can go back into the supply the same way, if the switches that filled the store are still set as they were, which asks for gates that forget nothing. Slowness has its own price, because every switch leaks while it waits, so there is a best speed. And a bit must be held against the jiggle of warmth, which asks for a barrier some tens of kT high. Whether there is a floor that no switch can go below, and what it is, is 39·comp’s question.

Ramp the supply over T in place of a step, and the heat of filling falls to (RC/T) C V². The charge can return to the supply only while the switches that filled it are still set, so the gates must forget nothing. Leak grows with the wait, so the ramp has a best length. A held bit needs a barrier of about 47 kT for ten years.

38now1A ramp in place of a step

38·eng.1 filled a store C through a switch of resistance R from a supply that jumped to V, and lost ½ C V² in the switch. Let the supply rise instead in a straight ramp from 0 to V over a time T. The store’s voltage obeys RC v′ = u − v: it is pulled toward the supply u with time RC, the target technique of 24·then2, with a target that moves.

Slow-ramp technique: filling a store C through R by a supply that ramps from 0 to V over T, with T much longer than RC, warms the switch by about (RC/T) C V², which heads to 0 as T grows.

v = u - RC\,\tfrac{V}{T} \text{ once the start has died away} @@ the lag technique (24·then1): a steady ramp is followed RC behind
I = C v' = \tfrac{CV}{T} @@ the store fills at the ramp’s pace
\text{heat} = I^2 R\,T = \tfrac{C^2 V^2 R}{T} = \tfrac{RC}{T}\,C V^2 @@ power I²R for a time T (7·phys)
RC = 10\text{ ps},\ T = 1\text{ ns}:\ \ 0.01\,C V^2 \text{ (exactly } 0.0099\text{)} @@ the exact heat (RC/T)CV²(1 − (RC/T)(1 − e^(−T/RC))), checked by machine
\htmlClass{new}{50\times \text{ less than the step’s } \tfrac12 C V^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ at 100 times the time

Heat and speed trade one for one: halve the heat by doubling the time. A step has no time, and the lag technique’s small case breaks down: the store is not following, and the full push falls across the switch.

38now2Giving the charge back

The ½ C V² a filled store keeps is not yet heat. 38·eng.1 dumped it to the ground through the lower switch. A ramp of the supply back down, slowly, would pull it back into the supply instead, with heat (RC/T) C V² again. But the charge flows back only along the path it came: the upper switch must still be on while the supply ramps down.

Undo-gate technique: a store filled by a slow ramp through a gate can be emptied back into the supply by a slow ramp only while the gate’s inputs are still as they were; so a circuit that gives its charge back must keep each gate’s inputs until its output is emptied, which a gate that forgets nothing (22·comp5) allows and a not-and alone does not.

\text{output filled through the path that its inputs set} @@ the pair inverter (38e): the upper switches are on exactly when the output is 1
\text{inputs change first} \Rightarrow \text{that path may shut} @@ then the store can empty only to the ground: ½ C V² of heat
\text{inputs kept} \Rightarrow \text{ramp the supply down along the same path} @@ the slow-ramp technique run backwards
\text{a not-and's output does not fix its inputs} @@ three rows give 1 (22·comp5): the inputs cannot be found from the output to rebuild the path
\htmlClass{new}{\text{empty in the reverse order of filling, inputs kept}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a gate whose rows run backwards

So a circuit that recycles its charge runs in waves: each stage is filled while its inputs are held, then held while the next stage fills, then emptied in reverse order. Chips built this way have run, in laboratories since the 1990s, at a small share of the heat of the same circuit filled by steps, and many times slower.

38now3A best length of ramp

A slow ramp spends less in the switch, but every switch of the chip leaks a small power P_ℓ all the time (38·eng.3’s leak floor), and a slower ramp means a longer wait. The two pull opposite ways.

Best-ramp technique: with ramp heat (RC/T) C V² and leak P_ℓ T, the energy of one filling is least at T* = √(RC · C V² / P_ℓ), where it is 2√(RC · C V² · P_ℓ).

E(T) = \tfrac{RC\cdot CV^2}{T} + P_\ell\,T @@ the two costs
E'(T) = -\tfrac{RC\cdot CV^2}{T^2} + P_\ell = 0 \iff T^* = \sqrt{RC\cdot CV^2/P_\ell} @@ slope 0 at the least (13)
E(T^*) = 2\sqrt{RC\cdot CV^2\cdot P_\ell} @@ the two costs are equal there
RC = 10\text{ ps},\ CV^2 = 4.9\cdot10^{-17}\text{ J},\ P_\ell = 0.7\text{ nW}:\ \ T^* \approx 0.84\text{ ns} @@ a leak of 1 nA at 0.7 V
\htmlClass{new}{E \approx 1.2\cdot10^{-18}\text{ J}, \text{ about } 42\times \text{ less than } CV^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked by machine

So recycling is a trade against leaking, and the better the switch holds back its leak, the slower and cooler the best ramp. That is why the hope for chips of this kind rests on switches that leak far less than silicon at room warmth: switches cooled near absolute zero, or made of moving parts.

38now4Holding a bit against warmth

A bit is held when the two states are parted by a barrier of energy E. Warmth tries to lift the state over it about f₀ = 10¹² times a second, the jiggle of atoms, taken as measured, and each try succeeds with share e^(−E/kT), the two-shares technique in its rare form, as 38·phys3 used it.

Holding-barrier technique: a bit held by a barrier E against tries at rate f₀ keeps its state for a time t with chance of a flip about f₀ t e^(−E/kT); for that to be below ε, E must be at least kT ln(f₀ t/ε).

f_0\,t\,e^{-E/kT} \le \varepsilon \iff E \ge kT\ln\tfrac{f_0 t}{\varepsilon} @@ take logs (17)
t = 10 \text{ years} \approx 3.16\cdot10^8\text{ s},\ \varepsilon = 1:\ \ \ln(3.16\cdot10^{20}) \approx 47.2 @@ one flip expected in ten years
E \ge 47.2\,kT \approx 1.22\text{ eV} \approx 2\cdot10^{-19}\text{ J} @@ kT = 0.0259 eV at 300 K
\tfrac12 CV^2 = 2.45\cdot10^{-17}\text{ J} \approx 5900\,kT @@ today’s store, by the switch-heat technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{holding needs about } 47\,kT;\ \text{today’s switch spends about } 12\,000\,kT} \quad \blacksquare @@ CV² per flip, checked by machine

The barrier is not spent: a bit can sit behind it for years for nothing. It sets how deep the store must be, and so how much charge is moved if it is moved fast. What is proved is that the slow-ramp heat goes to 0 as T grows, and that undoing needs the inputs kept. What is open is whether any switch can flip a bit, at a useful speed, near kT, and whether wiping a bit must cost something no ramp can save. That last question has an answer, and 39·comp gives it.

The accepted names

Filling by a ramp is adiabatic charging, and the circuits that give their charge back are adiabatic logic, or charge-recovery logic, run by a power clock; filling and emptying stages in waves is Bennett clocking, or a reversible pipeline. A gate whose rows run backwards is a reversible gate. The barrier against warmth is the Arrhenius, or Néel–Arrhenius, law for thermally activated hopping, with f₀ the attempt frequency; the least energy a held bit needs is the retention barrier of a memory cell. The thermal floor for wiping is Landauer’s principle (39·comp, 22·comp).

Who said it first

Charles Seitz and colleagues described charging by ramps in 1985; William Athas, Lars Svensson and others built adiabatic chips in the 1990s, and Michael Frank has argued for them since. Charles Bennett showed in 1973 that computing can be undone step by step, and Ralph Merkle gave reversible circuits of ramps in 1993. Svante Arrhenius gave the law of warmth and barriers for reactions in 1889.

A proof that is not allowed

“A slow ramp loses (RC/T) C V², which heads to 0, so a chip can compute for no energy at all.” Not allowed: the ramp’s heat heads to 0 only if every switch along the way gives its charge back, which needs the gates to forget nothing, and only if no leak is paid while waiting, which the best-ramp technique forbids. Whether forgetting itself has a price is a different question, and 39·comp answers it.
