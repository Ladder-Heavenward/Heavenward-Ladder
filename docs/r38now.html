Can a Machine Check a Proof?

Can a machine check a proof? A greengrocer stacks oranges in a pyramid, each layer sitting in the hollows of the one below, and nobody can do better: no way of packing equal balls fills more of space. That was guessed in 1611 and proved in 1998, by a proof so long and so dependent on computer runs that the referees, after four years, said they were 99% sure of it and could not be surer. The author then spent eleven more years, with a team, writing the proof again in a form a small program could check line by line, and the check finished in 2014. In 2020 another mathematician wrote that he was not sure his own proof of a technique he cared about was right, and asked for it to be checked the same way; it was, by 2022. What makes the question hard is not the checking. Step 38b showed that whether a list of lines is a proof is settled by a recipe that always stops. The hard parts are getting a real proof into lines at all, knowing that the statement checked says what was meant, and trusting the checker. This page computes the density of the grocer’s stack, shows how a checker is built so that very little of it needs trust, and says where the trust that is left rests, which is where rung 38 left it.

A proof written in a calculus is a finite object, and checking it is a recipe. A checker trusts only a small core that applies the moves; everything else that helps write a proof hands the core lines to check, so a mistake outside the core can fail to find a proof and cannot make a false one pass. What is left to trust is the core, the statement as written, and the calculus itself, and the last of these cannot certify itself.

38now1Checking Is a Recipe

38b’s proof-check technique says that whether a finite list of lines is a proof from a finite collection G is settled by a recipe that always stops: look at each line, and see that it is in G or follows from earlier lines by one of the moves. 38n did it by hand. A machine does it the same way, faster and without getting bored.

The trouble is that proofs as mathematicians write them are not lists of lines. They skip steps, say “similarly”, and lean on ten thousand earlier techniques. 38b’s derived-move technique is the way in: a step that is a known pattern of moves can be used as one move, once the pattern is checked. A library of checked results is a stock of derived moves.

Small-kernel technique: a checker can be split into a small core that applies the moves of the calculus and nothing else, and any number of helpers that search for proofs; if every helper hands the core a list of lines to check, a mistake in a helper can fail to find a proof but cannot make a false statement pass.

\text{a helper outputs lines } \ell_1, \dots, \ell_n \text{ for the statement } \varphi @@ whatever the helper did to find them
\text{the core checks each } \ell_j \text{ is in } G \text{ or follows by one move} @@ the proof-check technique (38b)
\text{if the core accepts, } G \vdash \varphi @@ the lines are a proof, whoever wrote them
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only the core and } G \text{ need trust}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a wrong helper gives lines the core rejects

The cores of the checkers in wide use run to a few thousand lines of program. The helpers, which do the clever work, run to hundreds of thousands, and nobody needs to read them.

38now2The Grocer’s Stack

Stack balls of radius r in layers, each ball in a hollow of the layer below. The stack repeats in cubes: a ball at each corner of a cube and one at the centre of each face, the face-centred stacking of 32·chem. Balls touch along a face’s diagonal.

\text{face diagonal } = s\sqrt2 = 4r @@ a corner ball, the face’s centre ball, the opposite corner ball, touching in a row
\text{balls per cube: } 8 \cdot \tfrac18 + 6 \cdot \tfrac12 = 4 @@ each corner ball is shared by 8 cubes, each face ball by 2
\text{density} = \frac{4 \cdot \tfrac43 \pi r^3}{s^3} = \frac{\tfrac{16}{3}\pi r^3}{(2\sqrt2\, r)^3} = \frac{16\pi}{3 \cdot 16\sqrt2} @@ s = 4r/√2 = 2√2 r
\htmlClass{new}{\text{density} = \tfrac{\pi}{3\sqrt2} = \tfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{18}} \approx 0.7405} \quad \blacksquare @@ π ≈ 3.14159, √18 ≈ 4.24264

So the grocer’s stack fills just over 74% of space. The claim that no packing of equal balls does better, even one with no pattern at all, was proved in 1998. The proof reduced the question to some thousands of local arrangements and settled each with a computer: tens of thousands of inequalities, each checked by a program working with intervals of numbers so that rounding could not mislead it. No referee could check the runs. The formal check, finished in 2014, put both the reasoning and the computer runs through a small core.

38now3The Statement as Written

A checker checks that the formal statement φ is reached from G. Whether φ says what the mathematician meant is not something the checker can see. Every definition that φ uses must be written out too, and a mistake in a definition can make a statement easy to prove and worthless.

Statement-read technique: a checked proof certifies the formal statement and nothing else, so the statement and every definition it uses must be read by a person and matched with what was meant; the proof itself need not be read.

\text{meant: } p \text{ is prime when its only factors are } 1 \text{ and } p @@ as 4j defines it
\text{written: } \forall d\,(d \mid p \Rightarrow d = 1 \lor d = p) @@ forgetting p ≥ 2
d \mid 1 \Rightarrow d = 1:\ \ 1 \text{ is “prime”} @@ the written definition lets 1 in
\htmlClass{new}{\text{“every number above 1 is a product of primes” is checked, and so is a false-looking list}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 · 1 · 1 is a “prime list” of 1, and one-prime-list (4j) fails as written

This is why the statement of the packing result, as checked, is a few lines anyone with rung 38 can read, and why libraries of checked mathematics spend so much care on their definitions: a definition, once checked against many results that use it, is unlikely to be wrong in a way nobody notices.

38now4A Technique Its Author Doubted

In 2019 two mathematicians proved a technique that their whole new theory of spaces rested on. The proof was hard, and one of them wrote in December 2020 that he had lost sleep over it and was not sure every step was right; he asked for it to be checked by machine. A group using a checker of the small-kernel kind did the central part by June 2021, and the whole technique by July 2022.

  • The proof was right. Writing it out found a simpler route through one part, which the authors then used.
  • The library the group built on held tens of thousands of definitions and well over a hundred thousand checked results, written by hundreds of people over years. That stock of derived moves was what made the work possible.
  • In 2023 another technique, proved in November, was checked in about three weeks. A project begun in 2024 aims to check the proof of the statement that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no solutions in positive whole numbers for n ≥ 3, down to results from the 1980s, over five years.

Checking is now quick for results that sit near the library, and slow for results far from it. The cost of writing a proof out in lines is still several times the cost of writing it for a reader.

38now5Where the Trust Rests

After the small-kernel technique, a checked technique rests on three things: the core does what it says, the machine runs the core faithfully, and the calculus with its axioms G is clash-free. The first two are engineering. Cores are small enough to read, and a proof can be checked again by a second core written by other people in another language. The third is mathematics.

G \vdash \varphi \text{, checked} @@ the small-kernel technique
G \text{ clash-free and all of } G \text{ true in some world} \Rightarrow \varphi \text{ true there} @@ the true-everywhere technique (38c)
\text{if } G \text{ reaches } \mathrm{Con}_G \text{, then } G \text{ is not clash-free} @@ the no-self-certificate technique (38j), G strong enough for 38g
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the checker cannot certify its own calculus}} \quad \blacksquare @@ any certificate rests on a stronger G′

So the clash-freeness of the calculus a checker uses is shown, when it is shown, from a stronger collection of axioms, and that one’s from a stronger still. This is the same trust any mathematician extends to the axioms of sets. It is held in trust by everyone, and the machine has not changed it. What the machine has changed is the rest: for a checked technique, the step that a referee might miss is no longer a place a mistake can hide.

Open: whether most of current research mathematics can be checked at reasonable cost; how far helpers can go in writing lines themselves, which is 38·now2’s question; and how to make formal statements readable enough that the statement-read technique is cheap.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The packing claim is the Kepler conjecture, and the formal check of it was the Flyspeck project, done in the proof assistants HOL Light and Isabelle. Checkers of the small-kernel kind are proof assistants, and the small-kernel technique is the de Bruijn criterion; the core is the kernel and the helpers are tactics. The 2022 check was the Liquid Tensor Experiment, done in the proof assistant Lean with its library mathlib. The 2023 technique was the polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture. The statement about xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ is Fermat’s Last Theorem.

Who said it first

Johannes Kepler stated the packing claim in 1611. Thomas Hales announced its proof in 1998, with Samuel Ferguson; it appeared in 2005 with the referees’ note, and Hales led Flyspeck to its end in August 2014. Nicolaas de Bruijn built the checker Automath from 1967 and stated the small-core rule. Georges Gonthier checked the four-colour result in 2005 and, with a team, the odd-order result of group theory in 2012. Peter Scholze and Dustin Clausen proved the 2019 technique; Scholze posed the challenge in December 2020, and Johan Commelin led the group that met it. Timothy Gowers, Ben Green, Freddie Manners and Terence Tao proved the 2023 technique, and Kevin Buzzard leads the project on Fermat’s statement. Mario Carneiro showed in 2019 that Lean’s calculus is clash-free if set theory with enough large sets is.

A proof that is not allowed

“The checker’s core has been checked by the checker itself, so the core is right.” Not allowed: a core that is wrong may well accept a false proof that it is right. A second core written separately is evidence; a core vouching for itself is the shape 38j forbids.
