Rung 18 · theory

Drawing with numbers

Since rung 9 this ladder has drawn pairs of reals as dots on a page, and every time it has said that no shape was meant. The pictures showed two orders at once: less to the left, less below. Distance, straightness, squares as shapes, circles, perpendicular lines, triangles: all of it was kept back. This rung builds it, and it builds it from one definition. Each earlier page that said a shape or a distance waits for rung 18 is answered here. The distance between two points is the square root of a sum of two squares: the square of how far apart they are across, and the square of how far apart they are up. Everything the old geometry proved about figures, and much it could not, then follows by algebra on pairs.

You have drawn shapes on paper all your life. This rung builds the paper. It asks more of you than any rung so far, and gives back more.

A point is a statement that names a pair of numbers, across and up. The distance between two points is the square root of the across-gap squared plus the up-gap squared. Two displacements are perpendicular when the product of their across parts plus the product of their up parts is zero, and then the right-triangle technique holds. Lines are the points where a fixed mix of across and up comes to a fixed total, circles are the points at a fixed distance from a centre, and the conics are the points whose distances to a focus and a line, or to two foci, keep a fixed relation.

What it means to say it

Geometry, as it was first written down, is about figures: points, lines, circles, and what can be said of them by looking and by reasoning from a few things taken as obvious. The claim of this rung is that none of it needs looking. Take the plane to be the set of pairs of reals, ℝ × ℝ, which rung 9 already drew. Give it one more thing, a distance between any two pairs. Then a circle is a set of pairs at one distance from a centre, a line is a set of pairs that satisfy one equation of the first degree, and a right angle is a pair of displacements whose products add to zero. Each statement about figures becomes a statement about real numbers, and the real numbers of rung 8 decide it.

That changes what a proof in geometry is. A figure suggests, and an equation settles. A claim that the three heights of a triangle meet in one point becomes a claim that three equations have a common solution, which is algebra of rung 5. And it changes what counts as a figure: any set of pairs given by an equation is a figure, whether or not anyone can draw it.

Where it comes from

Around 300 BCE, a Greek geometer set out geometry in thirteen books from five postulates and a handful of common notions, and his Elements was the model of a proof for two thousand years. A century later, a Greek geometer of Perga wrote eight books on the curves cut from a cone: the parabola, the ellipse and the hyperbola, named by him. A French mathematician, in a manuscript circulated from 1636, and a French philosopher, in La Géométrie of 1637, each saw that a curve could be described by an equation in two unknowns, so that the problems of the Greeks became problems of algebra. Later mathematicians wrote the conics as equations of the second degree. In the nineteenth century, when questions about the fifth of those postulates showed that the old axioms left gaps, a German geometer rebuilt the foundations, and so did a German mathematician in 1899 and an American mathematician in 1932. The American's version starts, as this rung does, from the real numbers and a distance.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, distance and perpendicular. The distance between two points is the square root of the sum of the squares of the differences of their coordinates. Two displacements are perpendicular when the sum of the products of their coordinates is zero. With these two definitions (18a2), the right-triangle technique is a short calculation: the squares on two perpendicular displacements add up to the square on their sum (18a4).

Second, the triangle inequality. No side of a triangle is longer than the other two together (18b2), and the two together equal it only when the three points lie on one segment (18b3). So the straight way between two points is the shortest, and that is what "straight" means.

Third, lines. A line can be given as a point and a direction, or as an equation Ax + By = C. The two descriptions give the same sets (18c1). Through two different points there is exactly one line (18c2), and through a point not on a line there is exactly one line that never meets it (18c3). The old geometry had to assume that last fact; here it is proved.

Fourth, circles. A circle is the set of points at one distance from a centre (18d1). The points as far from A as from B make a line, perpendicular to the segment AB through its middle (18d2). So any three points not on one line lie on exactly one circle (18d2).

Fifth, the conics. The points whose distance to a fixed point equals their distance to a fixed line make a parabola (18e1). The points whose distances to two fixed points add to a fixed amount make an ellipse (18e2), and those whose distances differ by a fixed amount make a hyperbola (18e3). Each has an equation of the second degree, found by squaring away the roots.

What follows

The old geometry's techniques on triangles, parallels and similar figures can be worked its way, with its superposition named as its own, and checked in coordinates. Lines through given points, parallel and perpendicular lines, distances to a line and regions cut out by inequalities all become equations to solve. Centres and radii of circles come from completing the square. A curve can be traced by a moving point, with its speed and its length found by rungs 13 to 16. The constructions of the Greeks with straightedge and compass can each be checked by solving for where the circles meet. Light passing through a coloured liquid fades in a way set by the length of its path. And rung 13's slopes of curves given by equations return for circles, with the tangent at right angles to the radius.

What you can do because you know it

You can say exactly what a point, a distance, a line, a circle, a right angle and a triangle are, and prove what is true of them. You can prove the right-triangle technique two ways, and use it to find any distance on a map or a plan. You can write the equation of a line or a circle from what you know about it, and find where lines and circles meet. You can recognise a parabola, an ellipse or a hyperbola from its equation and find its foci. You can follow a moving point's path, its speed and its length. You can construct the figures of the old geometry with a straightedge and a compass, and prove that each construction works.

That is the first geometry built on numbers. People who have spent their lives on geometry still call themselves beginners, and you have only just begun.

Who said it first

The Greek geometer of about 300 BCE is Euclid, and his thirteen books are the Elements. The geometer of Perga is Apollonius, who named the parabola, the ellipse and the hyperbola. The two who saw a curve as an equation in two unknowns are Pierre de Fermat, in a manuscript circulated from 1636, and René Descartes, in La Géométrie of 1637. Leonhard Euler was among those who wrote the conics as equations of the second degree.

The foundations were rebuilt by Moritz Pasch, by David Hilbert in 1899, and by George Birkhoff in 1932. Birkhoff's is the version that starts from the real numbers and a distance.

Accepted names: the pairs are Cartesian coordinates, after Descartes; the right-triangle technique is Pythagoras' theorem; the fifth postulate of the old geometry is Euclid's fifth postulate.
