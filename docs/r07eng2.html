Why Does a Plug Have a Third Pin?

Why does a plug have a third pin? Jean’s kettle has three and her phone charger has two. Two wires are enough to carry current out and back (7·phys). The third carries nothing at all on an ordinary day. This page finds what it is waiting for.

Only differences of push exist, so one point is chosen and called zero. The ground under our feet is made that zero, and the third pin ties every metal case to it, so that a fault makes a large current in a wire and not a small one in a person.

7eng21Zero Is Chosen

7·phys measured push between two points. It is always between two. Nobody can say what the push is at one point alone, any more than how high a hilltop is without saying above what.

So pick a point and call it 0, and give every other point its push above or below that. Engineers call the chosen point ground.

\text{the ends are 0 and 9} @@ a 9-volt battery with its lower end called 0
\text{the ends are }-\text{9 and 0} @@ the same battery with its upper end called 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{9}} @@ difference either way

Moving the zero adds the same number to every point, and rung 5 showed that a difference does not change when the same number is added to both. Nothing in the circuit knows which point was picked.

The choice still matters for the working. A circuit with N joining points needs only N − 1 unknown pushes once one is called 0, and the sums are shortest when the zero is the point with the most wires on it.

Zero technique: push is a difference. Choose one point, call it 0, and measure every other point from it. Any point will do, and the differences come out the same.

7eng22The Ground Under Your Feet

A house is fed by two wires, live and neutral, with 240 volts between them. At the supply, the neutral is joined to a metal rod driven into the soil. That is a choice of zero, made for the whole street: neutral and soil are 0, live is 240.

It was made because people stand on soil. A person is a resistor with feet on the zero. Whatever a hand touches, the push across the person is that thing’s push above ground.

\text{0 }-\text{ 0 = 0} @@ hand on neutral. Nothing flows
\htmlClass{new}{\text{240 }-\text{ 0 = 240}} @@ hand on live. Current flows through the person

7eng23The Fault Nobody Sees

Inside Jean’s kettle the live wire works loose and rests against the metal body. With only two pins, nothing happens. The body is now at 240, but it is joined to nothing else, so no current flows, the kettle still boils, and no fuse notices. The fault waits for a hand.

Jean touches it with wet fingers, standing on a stone floor. Take her as 2,000 ohms.

\htmlClass{new}{\text{240 / 2,000 = 0.12 amperes}} @@ through her

That is less than a small lamp draws and far too little to trouble a fuse. It is several times what stops a heart.

Fault technique: a fault that joins a live wire to a loose piece of metal makes no current and gives no sign until something joins that metal to ground.

7eng24The Third Wire Makes the Fault Loud

The third pin is a wire from the metal body straight to ground, with a resistance of about 0.2 ohm. Now run the same fault.

\htmlClass{new}{\text{240 / 0.2 = 1,200 amperes}} @@ the moment the live wire touches the body

The fuse in the live wire is a thin strip made to melt at 8 amperes. It melts at once and the kettle is dead before anyone touches it.

Suppose Jean’s hand is on the kettle in that instant. She and the third wire are two roads from the body to ground, side by side. 7·phys: the flow divides in the inverse ratio of the resistances.

\text{third wire 1 ohm, Jean 10,000 ohms} @@  
\htmlClass{new}{\text{1 part in 10,001}} @@ her share

The fuse does not protect Jean. It is far too slow and too coarse for 0.12 amperes. It protects the wire in the wall from burning. The third wire protects Jean, by turning a silent fault into one large enough for the fuse to end.

Earth technique: join every metal case to ground by a wire of low resistance. A live fault then drives a large current through that wire, which blows the fuse, and a person touching the case carries only their small share.

7eng25Counting What Comes Back

A second guard needs no fault to a case at all. 7·phys’s junction rule: what flows in along the live wire must flow back along the neutral, unless some is leaving by another road.

The guard passes both wires through one iron ring, wound in opposite directions, so that their effects cancel when the two flows are equal.

\text{out 10.00, back 10.00} @@ difference 0. The guard is quiet
\htmlClass{new}{\text{out 10.00, back 9.97}} @@ difference 0.03. Three hundredths of an ampere is going to ground some other way, perhaps through someone. The guard opens the circuit

It works by subtraction and does not need to know where the leak is.

Some things have two pins on purpose. Jean’s charger has a plastic body and a second layer of insulation inside. There is no metal to become live, so there is nothing to tie to ground.

Accepted names

The chosen zero is ground or the reference node; push measured from it is potential or node voltage. The soil connection is earth. The wires are live (or hot), neutral and protective earth. A live wire touching the case is an earth fault; a direct path of almost no resistance is a short circuit. The guard of 7eng25 is a residual current device or ground-fault interrupter. A cased appliance with a third pin is Class I, one with doubled insulation Class II.
