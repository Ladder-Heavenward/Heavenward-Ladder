18i · technique

Curves traced in time

Until now a curve has been a set of points, given by an equation. This step lets a point move. At each time it is at a point, with a horizontal and a vertical coordinate that both change with time. Rung 13 gives the slopes of each coordinate, and the chain technique turns them into the slope of the curve. 18a's distance gives the point's speed. And the length of a curve, which the old geometry never defined, comes out as rung 16's area under the speed.

The path’s slope is the vertical speed over the horizontal speed, wherever the horizontal speed is not zero. The second slope is the slope’s rate of change in time, divided by the horizontal speed. Speed is the square root of the sum of the squared horizontal and vertical speeds. The way travelled is the area under the speed, and the length of a graph is the area under the square root of one plus its squared slope.

18i1A point that moves

Stands on 9 (functions) and 18a (points).

From here on, a path is a function P from an interval I of times to points: P(t) = (x(t), y(t)), where x and y are functions from I to ℝ. Its trace is the set of points it passes, {P(t) : t ∈ I}. The accepted name for a path is a parametric curve, with t its parameter, as for 18c's line X = P + tv.

A path is more than its trace. It says when the point is where, in what order, and how often. On [−1, 1], the paths t ↦ (t, t²) and t ↦ (t³, t⁶) have one trace, an arc of y = x², passed at different speeds. To find the equation of a trace, take t out. From x = t² and y = t³ comes y² = x³, with x ≥ 0.

x = 12t,\ \ y = 16t - 4.9t^2 @@ a stone thrown, the air neglected, in metres and seconds; t = x/12 gives y = (4/3)x − 4.9x²/144, a parabola
x = \tfrac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2},\ \ y = \tfrac{2t}{1 + t^2} @@ (1 − t²)² + (2t)² = (1 + t²)²: the trace is on the circle x² + y² = 1
t = \tfrac{y}{1 + x} @@ takes t back out: every point of that circle except (−1, 0) is reached, once

18i2Slopes of a path

Stands on 18i1, 13 (slopes) and 13l (the inverse dodge).

The pair P′(t) = (x′(t), y′(t)) says how fast each coordinate is changing at t. Its accepted name is the velocity at t. It is a displacement of 18a: the move the point would make in one unit of time, if it kept those rates.

Path-slope technique. Let x and y have slopes at every t of an open interval I. Let x′(t) be above 0 at every t of I, or below 0 at every t of I. Then the trace over I is the graph of a function F, and F′(x(t)) = y′(t)/x′(t). In the marks: dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt).

x \text{ is increasing on } I,\ \text{or decreasing} @@ slope-sign-order
x(I) \text{ is an interval } J @@ slope-means-no-gap: x is continuous; in-between-value
T: J \to I \text{ runs } x \text{ backwards},\ \ T'(x(t)) = \tfrac{1}{x'(t)} @@ inverse-slope
(x(t), y(t)) = \big(u,\ y(T(u))\big) \text{ with } u = x(t) @@ so the trace is the graph of F = y ∘ T
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{F'(x(t)) = y'(T(x(t)))\cdot T'(x(t)) = \tfrac{y'(t)}{x'(t)}} \quad\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ chain technique for slopes, with T(x(t)) = t

The marks dy/dt and dx/dt seem to cancel like fractions. 13e warned that dy/dx is not a quotient, and it still is not. The cancelling is the chain technique, proved; it is not a tech on fractions.

Where x′(t) = 0 and y′(t) ≠ 0, exchange the letters: near there the trace is a graph of x in terms of y, with dx/dy = 0, so it runs straight up and down. Where y′(t) = 0 and x′(t) ≠ 0, it runs level. Where both are 0 the point stops, and the trace can do anything. The trace of (t², t³) has a sharp point at (0, 0), with the accepted name a cusp; the trace of (t³, t⁶) is smooth there.

Second-slope technique. Let x and y have second slopes at every t of an open interval I, with x′ above 0 throughout or below 0 throughout. The second slope of the trace is the slope of y′/x′ in t, divided by x′. In the marks: d²y/dx² = (d/dt (dy/dx))/(dx/dt) = (x′y″ − y′x″)/x′³. Proof: the path t ↦ (x(t), y′(t)/x′(t)) traces the graph of F′, so path-slope applies to it; the quotient technique for slopes gives the last form. Its sign says where the trace bends up (bends-up).

x = 12t,\ y = 16t - 4.9t^2:\ \ \tfrac{dy}{dx} = \tfrac{16 - 9.8t}{12} @@ path-slope: level at t = 16/9.8 ≈ 1.633 s, the top, 13.06 m up
\tfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \tfrac{-9.8}{12}\cdot\tfrac{1}{12} = -\tfrac{4.9}{72} @@ second-slope: below 0 everywhere, so it bends down; y = (4/3)x − 4.9x²/144 agrees
x = t^2,\ y = t^3:\ \ \tfrac{dy}{dx} = \tfrac{3t}{2},\ \ \tfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \tfrac{3/2}{2t} = \tfrac{3}{4t} @@ for t ≠ 0: the upper half bends up, the lower half bends down

18i3Speed

Stands on 18i2 and 18a (distance).

Speed technique. Let x and y have slopes at t. The distance covered per unit of time, d(P(t + h), P(t))/|h|, heads to √(x′(t)² + y′(t)²) as h heads to 0. That number has the accepted name the speed at t, and it is |P′(t)|.

\tfrac{d(P(t + h), P(t))}{|h|} = \sqrt{\Big(\tfrac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h}\Big)^2 + \Big(\tfrac{y(t + h) - y(t)}{h}\Big)^2} @@ distance-laws: |h| = √(h²) goes inside the root
\htmlClass{new}{\longrightarrow \sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each chord slope heads to the slope; sums, squares and the root keep where things head (root-continuity)

The stone's speed is √(144 + (16 − 9.8t)²). It is least, 12 m/s, at the top, where it moves level.

18i4How far it goes

Stands on 18i3, 14c (average-slope) and 15 (integrals).

How long is a curve? The old geometry measured only straight segments, so build the length from them. Take a path P on [r, w] and a partition r = t₀ < t₁ < … < tₙ = w. Its chord sum is the sum of the distances d(P(tᵢ₋₁), P(tᵢ)). Adding a point to the partition never lowers the chord sum: one chord becomes two, and triangle in the plane says two are at least as long as one. From here on, the length of the path on [r, w] is the least upper bound of its chord sums, when they have an upper bound (8c).

Distance-travelled technique. Let x′ and y′ be continuous on [r, w]. The length of the path on [r, w] is the area under its speed, ∫ᵣʷ √(x′² + y′²) dt.

d(P(t_{i-1}), P(t_i)) = \Delta_i \sqrt{x'(s_i)^2 + y'(u_i)^2} @@ average-slope, on x and on y apart: some sᵢ, uᵢ between tᵢ₋₁ and tᵢ; Δᵢ = tᵢ − tᵢ₋₁
\Big|\sqrt{p^2 + q^2} - \sqrt{p^2 + q'^2}\Big| \le |q - q'| @@ triangle in the plane, on (p, q) = (p, q′) + (0, q − q′), and the other way round
|y'(u_i) - y'(s_i)| \lt \varepsilon \text{ when every } \Delta_i \lt \delta @@ evenly-continuous, for y′ on [r, w]
\Big|\text{chord sum} - \textstyle\sum v(s_i)\,\Delta_i\Big| \lt \varepsilon(w - r) @@ v = √(x′² + y′²); add the bounds of the last two lines over i
\Big|\textstyle\sum v(s_i)\,\Delta_i - \int_r^w v\Big| \lt \varepsilon \text{ for every partition fine enough} @@ narrow-strips: v is continuous (root-continuity), and the sᵢ are tags
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{every chord sum} \le \textstyle\int_r^w v,\ \text{and fine ones are }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{within } \varepsilon(w - r + 1)} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ any partition refines to a fine one, no shorter; ε was any number above 0

The length counts every pass. The path t ↦ (t², 0) on [−1, 1] runs from (1, 0) to (0, 0) and back. Its length is ∫|2t| dt = 2, though its trace is a segment of length 1. When a path passes each point of its trace once, its length is the length of the trace.

18i5The length of a graph

Stands on 18i4.

Graph-length technique. Let f′ be continuous on [r, w]. The length of the graph of f from r to w is ∫ᵣʷ √(1 + f′(x)²) dx. It is distance-travelled for the path x = t, y = f(t), which passes each point once. Its accepted name is arc length.

y = x^{3/2} \text{ on } [0, 4]:\ \ \textstyle\int_0^4 \sqrt{1 + \tfrac94 x}\,dx @@ graph-length; f′ = (3/2)x^{1/2}
= \tfrac{8}{27}\Big[\big(1 + \tfrac94 x\big)^{3/2}\Big]_0^4 = \tfrac{8}{27}\big(10\sqrt{10} - 1\big) \approx 9.073 @@ substitution for areas, with u = 1 + 9x/4
y = \tfrac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}:\ \ 1 + f'(x)^2 = f(x)^2 @@ f′ = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ)/2, and 1 + (e²ˣ − 2 + e⁻²ˣ)/4 = (e²ˣ + 2 + e⁻²ˣ)/4
\text{from } -1 \text{ to } 1:\ \ \textstyle\int_{-1}^{1} f = e - e^{-1} \approx 2.350 @@ graph-length; the shape of a hanging chain, with the accepted name catenary

Who said it first

The ratio between straight and curved lines is not known, and I believe cannot be known by men; so nothing exact and certain can be concluded from it.

René Descartes, La Géométrie, second book (1637); paraphrase

The old geometry that never defined the length of a curve is Euclid’s, in his Elements. In 18i.4.4, the geometer is René Descartes. William Neile found the length of an arc of y² = x³ in 1657, Hendrik van Heuraet published a general method in 1659, and Pierre de Fermat had one too. Christiaan Huygens and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz found the catenary's equation in 1691, answering a challenge of Jakob Bernoulli; Galileo had guessed the hanging chain was a parabola.

Accepted names: a path is a parametric curve; path-slope is the parametric derivative; finding a length is rectification, and a path with a length is rectifiable.
