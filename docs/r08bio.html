How Many Must Be Immune to Stop a Spread?

How many must be immune to stop a spread? An infection passes from person to person. If each case passes it to more than one new person, the cases grow generation by generation; if to fewer than one, they die away. Immune people break some of the links. This page counts cases by generations with 4g’s powers, finds how immunity cuts the number each case passes on, and solves an inequality of 8g for the share that must be immune to turn growth into decline. It ends with why measles needs nearly everyone immune and flu far fewer.

Let each case, among people who can catch it, give it to R others. Generation n has Rⁿ times the first. If a share p of people are immune, each case reaches only R(1 − p) who can catch it. The cases die away when R(1 − p) < 1, that is when p > 1 − 1/R.

8bio1Each Case Makes R New Cases

A school has a cold going round. On average, each child who catches it passes it to 3 others before getting better, when nobody else has had it.

From here on, on this page, R is the number of people an average case passes the infection to, in a group where nobody is immune. A generation is one round of passing on: the first cases, the cases they cause, the cases those cause, and so on.

\text{generation 0: } 1 @@ the first case
\text{generation 1: } 3 @@ 
\text{generation 2: } 3 \cdot 3 = 9 @@

8bio2The Generations Grow or Shrink

Rⁿ behaves in one of two ways, depending on which side of 1 R lies (8g).

R > 1:\ R^n \text{ grows past any bound} @@ R = 3: 1, 3, 9, 27, 81, …
R < 1:\ R^n \text{ shrinks toward } 0 @@ R = 1/2: 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, …
\htmlClass{new}{R = 1:\ \text{one case per generation, forever}} @@ the edge between them

An infection whose R is above 1 spreads; one whose R is below 1 dies out. What makes R is the infection, how people meet, and how long a case can pass it on. Hand-washing and staying home lower it; so does immunity.

8bio3Immunity Cuts R

Now suppose a share p of the children are immune, from an earlier bout or a vaccine, and that the immune are mixed evenly among the rest. A case still meets R children it would have infected, but only the share 1 − p of them can catch it.

\text{met: } R @@ as before
\text{can catch it: } 1 - p @@ the share not immune
\htmlClass{new}{R_p = R\,(1 - p)} @@ the new passing-on number

With R = 3 and half the school immune, each case passes it to 1.5 on average: still spreading, but slower.

8bio4The Threshold

The spread dies out when the new number is below 1. Solve that inequality for p, with 8g’s moves.

R\,(1 - p) < 1 @@ die out
1 - p < \tfrac{1}{R} @@ divide both sides by R > 0 (8g)
\htmlClass{new}{p > 1 - \tfrac{1}{R}} @@ move p across, flip nothing

Threshold technique: an infection that each case passes to R others dies out once more than the share 1 − 1/R of people are immune, mixed evenly among the rest. For the cold with R = 3, that is 1 − 1/3: two children in three.

Immune children shield the ones who are not, because a chain of cases that reaches an immune child stops there. Above the threshold, the few who cannot be immunised, such as babies, are protected by the people around them.

8bio5Why Measles Needs More Than Flu

Measles spreads through the air and lingers in a room; in a group with no immunity, each case passes it to about 12 to 18 others. Seasonal flu passes to far fewer, about 1.3 to 2 in most seasons.

infectionRshare that must be immune, 1 − 1/R
measles12 to 18about 92% to 94%
seasonal flu1.3 to 2about 23% to 50%
the school cold3about 67%

The threshold climbs toward 1 as R grows, and it climbs slowly at the top: from R = 12 to R = 18 adds only two points. That is why measles comes back where immunity falls even a few points below the mid-nineties, while flu can be held down with far less.

A health department planning a measles campaign takes R from past outbreaks, works out 1 − 1/R, and sets its target a little above it, because the immune are never mixed perfectly evenly.

8bio6Moves to Watch Out For

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

R = 1 \text{ means the infection dies out}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> at R = 1 each generation is the same size: it neither grows nor dies (8bio2)
p > \tfrac{1}{R}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> solving R(1 − p) < 1 gives p > 1 − 1/R (8bio4)
\text{half immune halves the generations}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> half immune halves R; generation n is multiplied by (1/2)ⁿ (8bio3)
R = 18 \text{ needs twice the immunity of } R = 9\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> 1 − 1/18 ≈ 94% against 1 − 1/9 ≈ 89% (8bio5)
\text{above the threshold, nobody catches it}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> cases still happen; chains just die out instead of growing (8bio4)

Accepted names

R in a group with no immunity is the basic reproduction number, written R₀; with immunity it is the effective reproduction number. Protection of the not-immune by the immune is herd immunity, and 1 − 1/R₀ is the herd immunity threshold.

Who said it first

The threshold 1 − 1/R₀ comes from the mathematical study of epidemics that grew from the work of Ronald Ross and of William Kermack and Anderson McKendrick in the early twentieth century. A 2017 review of 18 studies put measles’ R₀ mostly between 12 and 18.
