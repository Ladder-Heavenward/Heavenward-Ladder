Can a Genome Be Read End to End?

Can a genome be read end to end? A genome is a text in four letters, A, C, G and T, and the human one runs to about three thousand million of them. No machine reads it in one pass. A machine reads pieces, and the text must be put back together from the pieces. Take the clean case: the pieces are all the stretches of k letters in a row, each one read, none wrong. Then the text is a walk on a one-way graph, each piece is one link, and putting the text together is finding a walk that uses every link once. A stretch that repeats is a dot the walk comes back to, and there the pieces stop saying which way to go. Longer pieces unfold the graph until one walk is left. This is why the last part of the human genome, long repeats, waited for machines that read long pieces.

k-words of a text: its stretches of k letters in a row. Dots: the (k − 1)-words. Each k-word is a one-way link from its first k − 1 letters to its last k − 1. A text is a tour of the links, and each tour spells a text with the same k-words. No (k − 1)-word twice: one text. A stretch X of k − 1 letters or more, three times: aXbXcXd and aXcXbXd have the same k-words. The tally of a walk then a walk is the sum of the tallies.

inf88.1A Text Is a Walk

Take the text TCAGCATCAA and read it three letters at a time, sliding one letter along each time: TCA, CAG, AGC, GCA, CAT, ATC, TCA, CAA. From here on, on this page, the k-words of a text are its stretches of k letters in a row, and its k-word tally says how many times each k-word occurs. A text of n letters has n − k + 1 of them. Here the tally has TCA twice and six other words once. The tally keeps no order. Can the text be put back from it?

Make a one-way graph from the tally. Its dots are the words of k − 1 letters. Each k-word in the tally, as many times as it is counted, is a one-way link from its first k − 1 letters to its last k − 1. For the tally above the dots are TC, CA, AG, GC, AT and AA. The word TCA is a link from TC to CA, counted twice, and CAG is a link from CA to AG.

Word-walk technique: the k-words of a text, in order, are a tour of the links of this graph: each link is used once, and each begins at the dot where the one before ended. And every tour of the links spells a text with the same k-word tally.

w_i = x_i x_{i+1} \dots x_{i+k-1},\quad i = 1, \dots, n - k + 1 @@ the k-words of the text x₁ … xₙ, in order
\text{last } k - 1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters }\allowbreak\text{of } w_i = x_{i+1} \dots x_{i+k-1} = \text{first } k - 1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters }\allowbreak\text{of } w_{i+1} @@ so the next word begins at the dot where this one ended
w_1, w_2, \dots, w_{n-k+1} \text{ }\allowbreak\text{uses }\allowbreak\text{each }\allowbreak\text{link }\allowbreak\text{once} @@ the links are the k-words, counted as in the tally
\text{a }\allowbreak\text{tour } u_1, \dots, u_m:\ \text{write } u_1\text{, }\allowbreak\text{then }\allowbreak\text{the }\allowbreak\text{last }\allowbreak\text{letter }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{each } u_i,\ i \ge 2 @@ the other way: spell the tour
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the } k\text{-words }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{of }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{that }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{text }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{are } u_1, \dots, u_m} \quad \blacksquare @@ each word begins where the one before ended

So the texts with a given tally are exactly the spellings of the tours of its graph. In the example CA has three links in and three out. TC has one more out than in, and AA one more in than out, so a tour starts at TC and ends at AA, as 42c’s open tour ran between its two odd dots. From TC the tour goes to CA. There it can take the round by AG and GC back to CA first, or the round by AT and TC. The first spells TCAGCATCAA. The second spells TCATCAGCAA. Two texts, one tally.

inf88.2When the Walk Is Forced

When does a tally give back one text only? One case is plain: the walk never comes to a dot twice.

Forced-walk technique: if no word of k − 1 letters occurs twice in a text, that text is the only one with its k-word tally.

d_1, d_2, \dots, d_m:\ \text{the } (k-1)\text{-words }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{the }\allowbreak\text{text, }\allowbreak\text{in }\allowbreak\text{order, }\allowbreak\text{all }\allowbreak\text{different} @@ the condition; m = n − k + 2
\text{the }\allowbreak\text{links }\allowbreak\text{are } d_i \to d_{i+1},\quad i = 1, \dots, m - 1 @@ the word-walk technique
\text{each }\allowbreak\text{dot }\allowbreak\text{has }\allowbreak\text{at }\allowbreak\text{most }\allowbreak\text{one }\allowbreak\text{link }\allowbreak\text{out, }\allowbreak\text{and } d_1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{has }\allowbreak\text{no }\allowbreak\text{link }\allowbreak\text{in} @@ the dots are all different
\text{a }\allowbreak\text{tour }\allowbreak\text{that }\allowbreak\text{starts }\allowbreak\text{at } d_j,\ j > 1\text{, }\allowbreak\text{never }\allowbreak\text{uses } d_1 \to d_2 @@ links only lead on, to later dots
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{tour }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{is } d_1 \to d_2 \to \dots \to d_m\text{: }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{one }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{text}} \quad \blacksquare @@ start at d₁; each step has one link to take

The graph is then one line of dots with no way to turn. In TCAGCATCAA the word CA occurs three times, and the walk had a choice there. The technique runs one way only. With words of four letters the text has TCA twice among its 3-words, and still only one tour: from TCA the link to CAA must wait until last, since nothing leads on from CAA.

inf88.3What a Repeat Does

Now let a stretch come three times. Write the text as a X b X c X d, where X is the stretch and a, b, c, d are what lies before, between and after. Any of the four may be empty.

Repeat-swap technique: if X has at least k − 1 letters, the texts aXbXcXd and aXcXbXd have the same k-word tally.

\text{a } k\text{-word }\allowbreak\text{across }\allowbreak\text{an } X \text{ }\allowbreak\text{holds } X \text{ }\allowbreak\text{and }\allowbreak\text{a }\allowbreak\text{letter }\allowbreak\text{each }\allowbreak\text{side: } \ge k + 1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters} @@ X has k − 1 letters or more
\text{so }\allowbreak\text{each } k\text{-word }\allowbreak\text{lies }\allowbreak\text{inside } aX,\ XbX,\ XcX \text{ }\allowbreak\text{or } Xd @@ it cannot reach across an X
\text{a } k\text{-word }\allowbreak\text{inside }\allowbreak\text{two }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{these }\allowbreak\text{lies }\allowbreak\text{inside }\allowbreak\text{the } X \text{ }\allowbreak\text{they }\allowbreak\text{share} @@ neighbours share one X
\text{tally}(aXbXcXd) = \text{tally}(aX) + \text{tally}(XbX) + \text{tally}(XcX) + \text{tally}(Xd) - 3\,\text{tally}(X) @@ count each piece; take off what was counted twice
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{right }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{side }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{is }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{the }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{same }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{with } b \text{ }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{and } c \text{ }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{changed }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{about}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a sum does not mind its order

With k = 3, X = CA, a = T, b = G, c = T and d = A this is the pair from the first footing: T·CA·G·CA·T·CA·A and T·CA·T·CA·G·CA·A. On the graph, X is a dot the walk meets three times, and b and c are two rounds from that dot back to it. The tally holds both rounds and not their order.

So pieces must be longer than the repeats. If a stretch of 5000 letters comes three times with different text between, no k up to 5001 gives the text back, however many pieces are read. The stretch between two copies is what tells them apart, and a piece has to hold a whole copy with a letter on each side to carry that across.

inf88.4Longer Words Unfold the Graph

Read TCAGCATCAA four letters at a time: TCAG, CAGC, AGCA, GCAT, CATC, ATCA, TCAA. The dots are now 3-words, and the dot CA of before has come apart into GCA and TCA, which remember the letter before. Send each word of the new graph to its first letters: a dot of k letters to its first k − 1, a link of k + 1 letters to its first k. Links go to links and their ends go along, so a walk goes to a walk. The new graph lies over the old one.

Longer-word technique: two texts with the same (k + 1)-word tally and the same last k letters have the same k-word tally. So longer words never add a text to those that fit; they can only take some away.

\text{each } k\text{-word }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{a }\allowbreak\text{text }\allowbreak\text{but }\allowbreak\text{the }\allowbreak\text{last }\allowbreak\text{is }\allowbreak\text{the }\allowbreak\text{first } k \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{one } (k+1)\text{-word, }\allowbreak\text{at }\allowbreak\text{the }\allowbreak\text{same }\allowbreak\text{place} @@ slide along the text
\text{tally}_k = \text{first-}k\text{-letters }\allowbreak\text{of }\allowbreak\text{tally}_{k+1}\ +\ \text{the }\allowbreak\text{last } k\text{-word} @@ count them
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{tally}_{k+1} \text{ }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{and }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{same }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{last } k \text{ }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{letters} \Rightarrow \text{same }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{tally}_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ both parts of the sum agree

For TCAGCATCAA, three letters at a time leave two texts and four at a time leave one. With k as long as the text there is one link and nothing to put together. Between the two ends, each letter added to k unfolds every dot by one more letter of what came before, and a repeat stops being one dot once k − 1 is longer than it.

inf88.5Read It With Arrows

A one-way graph gives a category (47a1). Its things are the dots. Its arrows are the walks, and two walks run in a row when the second starts where the first ends. The walk of no links at a dot is the do-nothing arrow. A text is then one arrow, from its first k − 1 letters to its last. The counts of links, added link by link, are a category as well, with one thing.

Tally-adds technique: the tally of a walk followed by a walk is the sum of their tallies, and the walk of no links has the empty tally. So the tally is a map of categories from the walks to the counts.

u = u_1 \dots u_p,\quad v = v_1 \dots v_q,\quad u \text{ }\allowbreak\text{then } v = u_1 \dots u_p v_1 \dots v_q @@ walks as rows of links
\text{tally}(u \text{ }\allowbreak\text{then } v) \text{ }\allowbreak\text{counts }\allowbreak\text{each }\allowbreak\text{link }\allowbreak\text{in } u \text{ }\allowbreak\text{and }\allowbreak\text{each }\allowbreak\text{link }\allowbreak\text{in } v @@ the definition
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tally}(u \text{ }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{then } v) = \text{tally}(u) + \text{tally}(v),}\allowbreak\qquad\htmlClass{new}{\text{tally}(\text{no }}\allowbreak\htmlClass{new}{\text{links}) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ rows go to sums

Now every part of the page is one line. Reading a genome from its pieces is undoing this map: given a count, find the arrow over it. Two rounds b and c at one dot run in a row two ways, b then c and c then b, which are different arrows. The map sends both to tally(b) + tally(c), since a sum does not mind its order. That is the repeat-swap technique. The forced-walk technique is the case where a count has one arrow over it. Longer words are a map of categories from the walks at k + 1 to the walks at k, and going up along it the arrows over a count thin out.

See what the arrows let hide. The letters are out of sight: a dot may hold two letters or two thousand, and the argument never opens it. What is left is the plan: the genome is one arrow, the machine hands over a count, and the question is whether the count has one arrow over it. On other pages order hides in the arrows and nothing is lost by it. Here the order that the tally hides is the thing wanted.

inf88.6The Whole Genome

How long must the pieces be for a real genome? A count gives a floor.

Read-floor technique: a text of n letters in which no word of k − 1 letters occurs twice has n − k + 2 ≤ 4k − 1.

\text{the }\allowbreak\text{text }\allowbreak\text{has } n - k + 2 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{words }\allowbreak\text{of } k - 1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters, }\allowbreak\text{all }\allowbreak\text{different} @@ the condition
\text{there }\allowbreak\text{are } 4^{k-1} \text{ }\allowbreak\text{words }\allowbreak\text{of } k - 1 \text{ }\allowbreak\text{letters }\allowbreak\text{in }\allowbreak\text{A, }\allowbreak\text{C, }\allowbreak\text{G, }\allowbreak\text{T} @@ four choices for each letter
\htmlClass{new}{n - k + 2 \le 4^{k-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pigeonhole technique (8·cb5)

For n = 3.055 × 10⁹ the count 4¹⁵ = 1,073,741,824 is too small and 4¹⁶ = 4,294,967,296 is enough, so k − 1 is at least 16 and k at least 17. That is a floor only. Below it some word must repeat; at it nothing says none does. A genome is far from a text of letters drawn by chance, and its repeats run to lengths that no count of this kind foresees.

  • In 2001 a paper proposed putting a genome together by a tour of the links of the graph of k-words, in place of comparing every piece with every other.
  • Until 2022 the reading of the human genome in use covered about 92% of it. Pieces from the machines then common ran to a few hundred letters.
  • In 2022 a reading of one human genome was published with 3.055 thousand million letter pairs and no gaps in any chromosome but Y. It added nearly 200 million letter pairs, and with them 1956 predicted genes, 99 of them predicted to code for a protein. The largest share of what was new is the repeated text around the middle of each chromosome.
  • Two kinds of long piece made it possible: pieces of about 20,000 letters read nearly without error, and pieces of up to a million letters read with modest accuracy.

∞z.oq.1The reading of 2022 is of one genome. How should many whole genomes be held together: as one graph in which each person is a walk? Which graph, and which maps between such graphs, keep what matters?
∞z.oq.2Real pieces have errors, and some places are read less often than others. For a given genome, what is the shortest piece and the fewest pieces that leave one walk, and can that be known before the reading is done?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The pieces came first. A machine could read a few hundred letters in a row, and a genome was put together by finding where pieces overlap.

In 2001 the putting together was restated as a walk: cut every piece into k-words, make the graph, and look for a tour of its links. Repeats then show as dots where the tour has a choice, and the limit of short pieces is visible in the graph itself.

The reading of the human genome in use until 2022 left about 8% out, much of it long repeats. Machines that read pieces of about 20,000 letters, and of up to a million, carried the walk across them, and in 2022 one human genome was read from end to end of every chromosome but one.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A k-word is a k-mer, and the k-word tally is the k-mer spectrum or k-mer multiset. A piece read by a machine is a read. The one-way graph of this page is a de Bruijn graph, and a tour of its links is an Eulerian path. Putting a genome together from reads is assembly; the older way, by overlaps of whole reads, is overlap-layout-consensus. A letter pair is a base pair. The repeated text around the middle of a chromosome is centromeric satellite DNA. The reading of 2022 is T2T-CHM13, T2T for telomere to telomere, end to end. The category of walks on a graph is the free category on it, and the tally is the map to the free commutative monoid on the links. One graph holding many genomes is a pangenome graph.

Who said it first

Pavel Pevzner, Haixu Tang and Michael Waterman, “An Eulerian path approach to DNA fragment assembly”, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 98 (2001). Sergey Nurk and many others, “The complete sequence of a human genome”, Science 376 (2022). The graph is named for Nicolaas de Bruijn, and the tour for Leonhard Euler (42c).
