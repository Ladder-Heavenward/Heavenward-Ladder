41·chem · chemistry

What Is a Flame?

What is a flame? Claire lights a candle. The wax is solid, the flame sits a little above it, and nothing in the flame stays: gas goes in at the bottom and different gas leaves at the top. 6chem10 and 27·chem kept the books of burning, atoms in and atoms out. This page asks what goes on in the bright place between.

A flame is a thin zone where a reaction heats its own reactants fast enough to keep itself going. Heat and lone-electron fragments made in the zone are what light the gas arriving next.

41chem1A Flame Is a Place

Claire holds a cold spoon in the candle flame and it comes out black. She holds it well above and it comes out wet. Wax melts, climbs the wick, and boils off it. The wax vapour moves outward and air moves inward, and they burn where they meet. The flame is that meeting surface. Fuel and air reach it by spreading toward each other, and the flame can burn no faster than they arrive.

Terry’s gas grill works the other way. Gas and air are mixed in the pipe before they reach the burner, and the flame is a thin sheet travelling into the mixture as fast as the mixture flows out. Nothing has to arrive from the side.

Candle, a diesel engine: fuel and air meet at the flame; the meeting sets the pace.

Gas grill, a petrol engine: fuel and air mixed beforehand; the reaction sets the pace.

Flame technique: a flame is the surface where fuel and oxygen react. Ask first whether they were mixed before they reached it or meet there.

41chem2Why It Needs a Match

The grill’s mixture can sit for a year without burning. 21chem2 gave the reason for shapes: there is a hill between. Here the hill stands between fuel with oxygen and their products, and the rate at which molecules pass it depends on temperature as

Ea is the height of the hill, usually 50 to 300 kJ/mol. R is 25·chem’s gas constant.

E_a = 150\text{ kJ/mol} @@ so Ea/R ≈ 18,000 K
e^{-60} @@ at 300 K
e^{-12} @@ at 1500 K
\htmlClass{new}{e^{48} \approx 10^{21}} @@ the ratio

A match heats a little of the mixture. That part reacts and gives out heat (43·chem counts how much), the heat warms the mixture beside it, and the rate there rises by a factor like the one above. So a flame, once lit, lights the next layer itself.

Ignition technique: a mixture burns when the heat its reaction gives out warms the neighbouring mixture faster than that heat leaks away.

41chem3The Chain

Heat is half of it. The other half is fragments. 38chem8 gave an atom’s gaps as arms with one electron each. A hydrogen atom on its own is one such arm with nothing on it. In a flame of hydrogen and oxygen the steps are these.

\text{H + O}_{2}\text{ }\to \text{ OH + O} @@ one fragment in, two out
\text{O + H}_{2}\text{ }\to \text{ OH + H} @@ one in, two out
\htmlClass{new}{\text{OH + H}_{2}\text{ }\to \text{ H}_{2}\text{O + H}} @@ one in, one out, and water made

Run the first step once, the second once and the third twice.

\text{H + O}_{2}\text{ + 3 H}_{2} @@ in
\htmlClass{new}{\text{3 H + 2 H}_{2}\text{O}} @@ out

One loose hydrogen atom has become three. Each of those does the same. That is a chain that branches, and it is how a mixture goes from nothing to everything in a thousandth of a second.

Fragments are also lost: at a wall, or by meeting oxygen and a third molecule together. The mixture explodes when making outruns losing. Evita’s lecture slide shows the odd result. At low pressure the fragments reach the wall and there is no explosion. At middle pressure branching wins. Higher still, three-body meetings win and it is quiet again. Higher again, it explodes once more.

Most flames run on chains that do not branch much. In those each fragment makes one product molecule and one new fragment, about 300 times over before it is lost.

Chain technique: a flame is carried by fragments with a lone electron. Count, for each step, fragments in against fragments out: equal carries the chain, more branches it, fewer ends it.

41chem4Enough Air, Too Little, Too Much

Air is 21 parts oxygen in 100 by count of molecules, so each O₂ comes with 3.773 N₂ that take no part. 27chem8 counted one O₂ for every four gaps in the fuel.

4 + 4 = 8 @@ methane CH₄: 8 gaps, so 2 O₂
48 + 26 = 74 @@ the diesel-like chain C₁₂H₂₆: 74 gaps, so 18.5 O₂
\htmlClass{new}{18.5 \times 32 = 592\text{ g}} @@ by weight: 592 g of oxygen for 170 g of fuel, 3.48 kg per kg, and 11.46 kg of nitrogen with it: 14.94 kg of air for each kg of fuel

For petrol the figure is 14.6. That is the exact mixture. With more air than that the mixture is lean and oxygen is left over. With less it is rich, and the products include CO and H₂: fuel half burnt.

A candle is rich on the inside of its flame and lean on the outside, and exact only on the surface between. A diesel engine runs lean overall, 80 to 1 at idle, and still burns rich at each spray.

41chem5How Hot

The heat of the reaction has nowhere to go at first but into the products, and the nitrogen is one of the products. In a petrol engine the mixture arrives at about 700 K and leaves the flame at about 2800 K.

V \propto T @@ 9·chem: at one pressure the volume of a gas goes as its temperature
\htmlClass{new}{\text{2800 / 700 = 4}} @@ so the burned gas takes four times the room

That swelling is what pushes the piston, and what makes a flame flicker upward.

Two things hold the temperature down. Nitrogen has to be heated and gives nothing back: burning in pure oxygen is far hotter. And near 3000 K the products start to come apart again, CO₂ back toward CO and water toward H and OH, which takes heat in. 25·chem’s reaction that stops halfway applies in a flame.

41chem6Why It Shines

Claire’s spoon came out black. Inside the candle flame the wax vapour is hot and has no oxygen yet. Heat alone breaks it: hydrogen is stripped off and the carbon gathers into specks of soot. The specks are solid and at 1500 K or so, and a hot solid glows (36·phys). That is the yellow of a candle. At the surface of the flame the soot meets oxygen and burns. If it escapes first, the flame smokes.

The grill’s flame is mixed beforehand, makes almost no soot, and is pale blue. Its light comes from single fragments and not from a hot solid: each gives out only its own colours, as 35·chem and 44·chem read them. Engine builders use this the other way round, lighting the flame with a laser at 284 nm to see where the OH is.

A pinch of salt in a flame gives sharp colours too, and the next footing sorts them.

Light technique: a flame’s light is either the glow of hot solid specks, which is spread over all colours, or the light of single atoms and fragments, which comes in a few sharp colours.

41chem7Colouring the Flame

Evita sets out six glass dishes with a little methanol in each, stirs a different salt into five of them, and lights them all.

in the dishflame
methanol aloneblue
a lithium saltpink to red
boric acidgreen
a strontium saltcrimson
table salt (sodium)yellow
a potassium saltlilac

The heat of the flame lifts an electron of the metal atom to a higher level (38·phys). It falls back and gives out the difference as light of one colour. A small drop gives red, a large drop gives blue and violet. Each element has its own set of drops, so each has its own colour, and the colour names the element. 35·chem and 37·chem read molecules the same way.

Visible light runs from 700 nm (1.8 eV, red) to 400 nm (3.1 eV, violet), so the drops that colour a flame are between those.

A firework star is the same demonstration packed dry: something that burns, something that gives up oxygen, and a salt for the colour. So are a road flare (strontium) and a ship’s signal.

Colour technique: a hot atom gives out light only at the colours that match the drops between its own levels, so the colour of a flame names the element in it.

41chem8Heat Without Air

The soot showed that heat alone rearranges a compound. Wood heated with the air kept out does not burn. It gives off gas and tar and leaves charcoal, nearly pure carbon. 33eng31 used charcoal and heat to take the oxygen off iron ore. Limestone heated gives off CO₂ and leaves quicklime. None of these is burning. Heat is the tool, and what it does is break the weakest bonds first.

A fire can also carry its oxygen with it. Saltpetre, KNO₃, gives up oxygen when heated. Ground with charcoal and sulfur it burns with no air at all, and since nothing has to arrive from outside, it burns as fast as its own heat can light the next grain. That is the difference between a log and gunpowder: the log waits for air.

Accepted names

A flame where fuel and air meet is a diffusion flame; mixed beforehand, a premixed flame. The hill is the activation energy, the line the Arrhenius equation. Lone-electron fragments are radicals; the steps are initiation, propagation, branching, termination; the pressure pattern is the explosion limits of hydrogen and oxygen. The exact mixture is stoichiometric; lean and rich are the accepted words; the ratio to exact is the equivalence ratio. The top temperature is the adiabatic flame temperature; products coming apart is dissociation. Breaking by heat alone is pyrolysis; limestone to quicklime is calcination. A flame wave driven by pressure is a detonation. The study of fire and heat as chemical tools is pyrochemistry; making coloured fire is pyrotechnics.
