41a · proof

Dots and links

Three ferry docks on Puget Sound, at Fauntleroy, on Vashon Island and at Southworth, are joined by one run of boats, and each dock can reach each other dock without stopping at the third. Draw the docks as dots and the runs as links, and the drawing has thrown away almost everything: the water, the distances, the timetable, the size of the boats. What is left is only which dock is joined to which. This rung asks how much can be known from that alone, and the answer is a great deal. This first step lays the object down. A graph is a set of dots and a set of links, each link joining two different dots. Counting the links at each dot gives a total that is always even, for a reason that takes one line. A walk goes from dot to dot along links, a path never comes back to a dot, and a cycle comes back only to where it began. And one plain question, whether the dots can be split into two sides with every link crossing, has an answer that a count settles: exactly when no cycle has an odd number of links.

A graph is dots and links, each link a set of two dots. Counting the ends of the links counts each link twice. A walk can be shortened to a path. The dots split into two sides with every link crossing exactly when no cycle is odd.

41a1A graph is its links

A graph G is a set V of dots and a set E of links. Each link is a set {u, w} of two different dots of V, built as 2e builds a set of two. The link {u, w} joins u and w, and u and w are then neighbours. V is finite on this rung unless a page says otherwise.

The word graph is reused. In 9e a graph was the pairs of a function, drawn in the plane, and that meaning stays for functions. Here a graph is a set of dots with a set of links, and nothing is drawn: a drawing is only a way to look at it. The accepted name comes from the Greek graphein, to write, through the chemists of the 1870s, who wrote a molecule as atoms and bonds.

Write d(v), the links at v, for the number of links that hold v. The three docks have d = 2 each. Kₙ is the graph on n dots with every one of the n(n − 1)/2 possible links, by 4k’s choosing-counts-parts technique with k = 2.

Ends-count technique: adding the links at every dot counts every link exactly twice.

P = \{(v, e) : v \in V,\ e \in E,\ v \in e\} @@ the set of ends: a dot together with a link that holds it
|P| = \textstyle\sum_{v \in V} d(v) @@ sort the ends by their dot; each dot v gives d(v) of them (4k, separate piles)
|P| = \textstyle\sum_{e \in E} 2 = 2|E| @@ sort the same ends by their link; each link has exactly two dots
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{v} d(v) = 2|E|} \quad \blacksquare @@ one set, counted two ways

So the number of dots with an odd count of links is even: the even counts add to an even number, and the total is even. At a party where some people shake hands, the people who shook an odd number of hands are an even number of people. No graph has links counts 3, 3, 3, 1, 1.

Anexact, yet rigorous · connections, not families

The Washington ferry map draws Vashon Island to scale, with the water blue and the docks where they are. The timetable leaflet draws the same three docks as three circles on a line. One looks like the Sound and one does not. As graphs they are the same object, since the same docks are joined.

A graph is known by which dots are joined, not by where the dots are drawn or what they are.

Two graphs are the same shape when a matching of their dots (9c) carries links to links and non-links to non-links. Everything this rung proves about a graph is about its links, so it holds for every graph of the same shape. The count of links at each dot is kept by such a matching; so is the number of pieces, and the length of the shortest cycle.

The three docks of the Fauntleroy run and the three corners of a triangle drawn in chalk: one graph, or two?

41a2Walks, paths and cycles

A walk from u to w is a list of dots v₀ = u, v₁, …, vₖ = w, each joined to the next; its length is k, the number of links used. A path is a walk that meets no dot twice. A cycle is a walk of length at least 3 that ends where it starts and meets no other dot twice.

Walk-to-path technique: if a walk joins u to w, a path joins u to w.

\text{among the walks from } u \text{ to } w \text{, take one of least length } k @@ a least member of a set of numbers that has one (4h)
\text{suppose } v_i = v_j \text{ with } i < j @@ the walk meets a dot twice
v_0, \dots, v_i, v_{j+1}, \dots, v_k \text{ is a walk of length } k - (j - i) < k @@ cut out the loop between the two meetings
\htmlClass{new}{\bot:\ \text{the least walk meets no dot twice, so it is a path}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against the choice of the least

Being joined by a walk is a sameness relation on V (2g): every dot is joined to itself by the walk of length 0, a walk can be read backwards, and two walks can be put end to end. Its classes are the pieces of G. G is in one piece when it has one class, the word of 34c carried over: a graph drawn with its links as curves is in one piece in 34c’s sense exactly when it is in one piece here.

The distance from u to w is the least length of a walk between them. It obeys 34a’s three demands, so a graph in one piece is a space with a distance.

41a3Two sides

A graph is two-sided when V splits into two sets X and Y, with no dot in both, so that every link has one dot in X and one in Y. Six houses on a street and three shops, with a link from each house to each shop it buys from, make a two-sided graph. Kₘ,ₙ is the two-sided graph with m dots on one side, n on the other, and every crossing link.

Odd-cycle technique: a graph is two-sided exactly when it has no cycle of odd length.

\text{a cycle } v_0, v_1, \dots, v_k = v_0 \text{ in a two-sided graph} @@ suppose
v_0, v_2, v_4, \dots \in X \text{ and } v_1, v_3, \dots \in Y \text{, if } v_0 \in X @@ every link crosses, so the sides alternate
v_k = v_0 \in X \text{, so } k \text{ is even} @@ the dot at place k is on v₀’s side exactly when k is even (4j, even or odd)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no cycle of a two-sided graph is odd}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 
  
\text{no odd cycle; take one piece and a dot } r \text{ in it} @@ the pieces can be done one at a time
X = \{v : \text{distance}(r, v) \text{ even}\},\ \ Y = \{v : \text{distance}(r, v) \text{ odd}\} @@ the sides, by distance from r
\text{a link } \{u, w\} \text{ inside } X \text{ or inside } Y @@ suppose one does not cross
\text{least paths } r \to u,\ r \to w \text{ and the link make a closed walk of odd length} @@ two lengths of one parity, plus 1
\text{a closed walk of odd length holds an odd cycle} @@ induction on the length: cut at a repeated dot; one of the two closed walks is odd and shorter
\htmlClass{new}{\bot:\ \text{every link crosses, and the graph is two-sided}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against no odd cycle

So a question about every way to split the dots, of which there are 2ⁿ, is settled by looking at the cycles, and by the second proof, at the distances from one dot. A recipe that walks out from r in layers settles it in one pass; 41k builds that recipe.

41a4How many graphs

On the dots {1, …, n}, a graph is a choice of which of the n(n − 1)/2 possible links to keep. 4k’s settled-parts technique counts the choices.

\text{possible links: } \binom{n}{2} = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} @@ choosing-counts-parts (4k)
\text{a graph is a part of the possible links} @@ the definition, with V fixed
\text{graphs on } n \text{ named dots: } 2^{n(n-1)/2} @@ settled-parts (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{n = 4:\ 2^6 = 64;\ \ n = 10:\ 2^{45} \approx 3.5 \times 10^{13}} \quad \blacksquare @@ counted

Many of the 64 graphs on four named dots are the same shape: the six graphs with one link are one shape, only the names moved. How many shapes there are is a different count, harder, since it asks what stays when the names are shuffled. There are 11. 41·cb19 counts them with rung 19’s moves.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Dots are vertices (or nodes) and links are edges; the links at v are its degree. The ends-count technique is the handshake lemma. Neighbours are adjacent vertices. Walks, paths and cycles keep their names, and the pieces are the connected components. A two-sided graph is bipartite, and the odd-cycle technique is the characterization of bipartite graphs. Kₙ is the complete graph and Kₘ,ₙ the complete bipartite graph. Graphs of the same shape are isomorphic. The test asked for in 41a.6.3 is the Havel–Hakimi test.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler counted the ends of bridges in 1736 (see 41c). James Joseph Sylvester used the word graph in 1878, borrowing it from the chemists' graphic notation, and Arthur Cayley counted trees for chemistry in the 1870s. Dénes Kőnig wrote the first book on the subject in 1936 and proved the odd-cycle technique there in the form used here. Václav Havel (1955) and S. Louis Hakimi (1962) found the test for lists of degrees.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each person at a party shakes an even number of hands or an odd number. Pair the odd ones up as they shake hands with each other; so there is an even number of them.” Not allowed: the odd ones need not shake hands with each other at all. The count is even because the total of all the counts is twice the number of handshakes, not because the odd ones pair off.
