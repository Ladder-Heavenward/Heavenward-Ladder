Should We Order Often or Order Big?

Should we order often or order big? Every shop that sells goods off a shelf faces it. Each order costs something whatever its size: a delivery charge, a driver's time, the paperwork. Each bag on the shelf costs something too: the money tied up in it, the space it takes, the chance it spoils. Order often and the delivery charges pile up. Order big and the shelf fills with stock that waits for weeks. 14h found the order that balances the two when sales are steady. Real sales are not steady, and deliveries take days to come. This page asks how much the balance cares about a wrong guess, when to place the next order, and how much to keep back in case.

The daily cost of an order size is the ordering charge, which falls as orders grow, plus the cost of holding stock, which rises. It is least where the two parts are equal. Away from the best size the cost rises slowly: ordering twice the best amount, or half of it, costs only a quarter more. Reorder when the stock falls to what sells while a delivery is on its way, plus a store kept back for the days that sell more.

The balance, from 14h

A feed store in Gonzales sells chicken feed by the bag, about d = 20 bags a day. Each order from the mill costs K = $50 in delivery and handling, whatever its size. Holding one bag for a day costs about h = $0.008, mostly the money tied up in it. Order Q bags at a time. Then there are d/Q orders a day, and the stock falls steadily from Q to 0 between them, so on average Q/2 bags sit on the shelf.

T(Q) = \tfrac{Kd}{Q} + \tfrac{h}{2}Q \text{ on } (0, \infty) @@ ordering cost a day, plus holding cost a day
Q_b = \sqrt{\tfrac{2Kd}{h}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{2 \cdot 50 \cdot 20}{0.008}} = \sqrt{250\,000} = 500 @@ equal-parts (14h), with a = Kd and b = h/2
T(500) = 2 + 2 = \$4 \text{ a day} @@ the two parts equal: an order every 25 days

How much a wrong order costs

K and h are guesses. Nobody knows the true cost of a bag's day on the shelf to the tenth of a cent. So the useful question is how much an order away from Q_b costs.

Kd = \tfrac{h}{2}Q_b^{\,2} @@ Q_b² = 2Kd/h, multiplied by h/2
T(Q) = \tfrac{h}{2}\Big(\tfrac{Q_b^{\,2}}{Q} + Q\Big) = \tfrac{h}{2}Q_b\Big(\tfrac{Q_b}{Q} + \tfrac{Q}{Q_b}\Big) @@ put it in for Kd; take out Q_b
T(Q_b) = hQ_b @@ the line above at Q = Q_b
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{T(Q)}{T(Q_b)} = \tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{Q}{Q_b} + \tfrac{Q_b}{Q}\Big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide

Write x = Q/Q_b. By 8g's means, ½(x + 1/x) ≥ 1, and equals 1 only at x = 1. Near x = 1 it barely rises.

An order half again too big costs about 8% more. Suppose the store guessed K at $100, twice the truth. Its formula then gives √2 Q_b, about 707 bags, and costs ½(√2 + 1/√2) ≈ 1.061 times the least: 6% more for a guess off by a factor of 2. Near the best order, the cost curve is nearly level, so a rough guess of the costs gives nearly the least cost. That is flat-top at work: at Q_b the slope of T is 0, so small misses climb almost nothing.

When to order: the lead time

An order from the mill takes L = 4 days to come. Wait for the shelf to empty and the store sells nothing for 4 days. So the store reorders when the stock falls to what sells in 4 days, the reorder point r = dL = 80 bags. If sales were steady, the delivery would arrive just as the last bag left.

Sales are not steady. A store's books might show four-day sales anywhere from about 60 to just over 100 bags. Reorder at 80, and the shelf runs empty before the truck comes in about half the cycles, every time four days sell more than 80.

The stock kept back

So the store reorders a little earlier, at r = dL + s. The s bags are a store kept back for a busy four days. They are never meant to be sold, and they cost hs every day, all year. Where to put r is read off the books with 9·stat's running total. Count the past lead times. Choose r so that the four-day sales were at most r in as many of them as the store wants covered.

\begin{aligned}&\text{20 past four-day sales, sorted: } 62,\\ &\quad 66, 70, 71, 73, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79,\end{aligned} @@ a year of the store's records, a model
\begin{aligned}&\phantom{\text{20 past four-day sales, sorted: }} 80,\\ &\quad 81, 82, 84, 85, 87, 88, 90, 93, 101\end{aligned} @@ mean 79.9, near dL = 80
F(80) = 11,\ \ F(90) = 18,\ \ F(93) = 19 @@ the running total (9·stat): how many were at most r
r = 93:\ \ s = 13 \text{ bags kept back} @@ covers 19 of 20; costs 13 × 0.008 ≈ $0.10 a day

The stock kept back buys a share of the cycles that do not run out, and each extra bag of it buys less than the last. Going from 19 of 20 covered to 20 of 20 here takes 8 more bags, for one cycle in the records.

Two stores, one shelf

Two stores, each selling d a day with the same K and h, each order Q_b and hold Q_b/2 on average. Together they hold Q_b = √(2Kd/h). Merge them into one store selling 2d: it orders √(2K · 2d/h) = √2 · Q_b and holds half that.

2 \cdot \tfrac12\sqrt{\tfrac{2Kd}{h}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{2Kd}{h}} @@ two stores' average stock
\tfrac12\sqrt{\tfrac{2K \cdot 2d}{h}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{\tfrac{2Kd}{h}} @@ one store, twice the sales
\text{ratio } \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.71} @@ about 29% less stock, for the same sales

Double the sales and the best order grows only by √2. So a big store orders more often, for its size, than a small one, and holds less stock for each bag it sells.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The best-order rule works in a range: sales steady, K and h fixed, the truck on time, the goods kept forever. Past each edge it fails, and in a known direction. Uneven sales empty the shelf at the end of a cycle, never in the middle, so the fix is a store kept back. Goods that spoil cap the order at what sells before they spoil. A price cut for big orders pulls the order up past Q_b.

State the rule, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

Because the cost curve is level near its bottom, the rule survives most of these failures with little loss. Its weak point is the reorder point, not the order size.

A garden centre in Watsonville sells twice as many bags of soil in April as in a usual month. Should its order in April be twice as big?

Who said it first

Ohno likened the stock in a factory to the water in a river. While the water is high it covers the rocks. Lower it, and the rocks show: the machines that break, the parts that come late. Then they can be fixed.

Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System (1978), the image as it is usually told; paraphrase

The engineer of 1913 is Ford W. Harris, in “How many parts to make at once”, in Factory, The Magazine of Management. R. H. Wilson spread the formula in 1934, and many books call it Wilson’s. In 14·logi.4.5, the car maker is Toyota, and the quick change of a press is Shigeo Shingo’s. The factor 1/√2 for pooled stock was set out by David Maister in 1976.

Accepted names: the economic order quantity for Q_b; lead time; reorder point; safety stock for the stock kept back; service level for the share of cycles covered; cycle stock for the rest; inventory position for stock on the shelf plus stock on order; the square-root law for pooling; just-in-time.
