Which Things in Physics Are Arrows?

Which things in physics are arrows? Six physics annexes and two time annexes, from rung 12 to rung 46, each worked with its own things: bars and gears, forces, observers, filters, photons, ticks of a clock. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. A machine is an arrow from effort to load, and a train of machines is a composite. The parts of a force are the arrows out of a product, on any axes at right angles. A change of observer is an arrow, and a row of changes is one change. A filter is an arrow that goes through a number line. An entangled pair is a cup. And a tick of time is an arrow from the state to the state: what one tick keeps, every count of ticks keeps.

Machines in a row: (m₂, d₂) ∘ (m₁, d₁) = (m₂m₁, d₂d₁), and md = 1 for each gives md = 1 for the train. For unit a ⊥ b, F ↦ (F · a, F · b) is a matching of the plane with ℝ × ℝ that keeps adding. Jw ∘ Ju = Ju + w, and each J keeps c²t² − x². P = i ∘ r with r ∘ i = do-nothing, so PP = P, and r₂ ∘ i₁ is the number d₁ · d₂. Φ = cup/√2, and (U ⊗ I)Φ = (I ⊗ Uᵀ)Φ. Q ∘ T = Q gives Q ∘ Tᵏ = Q; s ∘ T = T ∘ s gives s ∘ Tᵏ = Tᵏ ∘ s.

infphys.1A Train of Machines Is a Machine

A crowbar with arms of 1.2 m and 0.1 m multiplies a push by 12 (12·phys7), and its price is distance: the stone rises 1 cm while the hands go down 12 cm (12·phys8). 12·phys9 then put meshes in a row and found that their ratios multiply. Read as arrows, the three footings are one.

A machine is an arrow from its effort end to its load end. It carries two numbers: m, by which it multiplies the force, and d, by which it multiplies the distance moved. Hook the load end of one machine to the effort end of the next and the two are in a row. The second multiplies what the first gives it, so (m₂, d₂) ∘ (m₁, d₁) = (m₂m₁, d₂d₁). A machine is ideal when it passes energy through unchanged, which says md = 1.

Train-ratio technique: a row of machines multiplies force by the product of their m and distance by the product of their d. If every machine in the row is ideal, the row is ideal: its two products multiply to 1.

(m_2, d_2) \circ (m_1, d_1) = (m_2m_1,\ d_2d_1) @@ the second multiplies what the first hands it; for k machines by induction (4c), as in the gear-train technique (12·phys9)
(m_2m_1)(d_2d_1) = (m_2d_2)(m_1d_1) @@ regroup and turn-about for times
= 1 \cdot 1 = 1 @@ each machine is ideal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a row of ideal machines is an ideal machine}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so no train gives a big force and a long distance together
  
(4, \tfrac14) \circ (12, \tfrac{1}{12}) = (48, \tfrac{1}{48}) @@ crowbar first, then a block of four ropes
50 \text{ N} \mapsto 2400 \text{ N},\qquad 48 \text{ cm} \mapsto 1 \text{ cm} @@ 50 · 0.48 = 24 J in, 2400 · 0.01 = 24 J out
\htmlClass{new}{M \mapsto m \text{ and } M \mapsto md \text{ each keep composing: rows of machines to numbers under times}} @@ a map that keeps composing (47b)

What hides is the arrangement: the bars, ropes and gears in the middle of the train. From outside, a train is known by two numbers, and for an ideal train by one. The forearm of 12·phys8 is an arrow with m below 1, and it composes like the rest.

infphys.2Any Axes Will Do

21·phys1 added two ropes’ forces on a boat by adding their parts east and their parts north. 21·phys2 put a block on a slope and took parts along the slope and into it, with no word on why the axes could be changed. Arrows give the reason, and it is the demand on a product (47d).

Parts-fix technique: for any two unit vectors a and b at right angles, the plane with the arrows F ↦ F · a and F ↦ F · b is a product of two number lines: for each pair of numbers (u, v) exactly one vector has those parts. Each arrow keeps adding, so the sum of two forces is the one vector whose parts are the sums of their parts.

(ua + vb) \cdot a = u,\qquad (ua + vb) \cdot b = v @@ a · a = 1 and a · b = 0: a vector with the parts (u, v)
F = (F \cdot a)\,a + (F \cdot b)\,b \text{ for every } F @@ the parts of a vector (21b): so a vector with the parts (u, v) is ua + vb, and there is one only
F \mapsto (F \cdot a,\ F \cdot b) \text{ is a matching of the plane with } \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \quad \blacksquare @@ the demand on a product (47d), by the pair-map technique
(F + G) \cdot a = F \cdot a + G \cdot a,\ \text{and the same for } b @@ the dot spreads over a sum
\htmlClass{new}{F + G \text{ is the one vector with parts } (F \cdot a + G \cdot a,\ F \cdot b + G \cdot b)} \quad \blacksquare @@ on these axes, and on any other pair at right angles
  
300 \text{ N at } 0^\circ:\ (300\cos 30^\circ,\ -300\sin 30^\circ) = (259.8,\ -150) @@ the force lies 30° behind the new first axis
400 \text{ N at } 60^\circ:\ (400\cos 30^\circ,\ 400\sin 30^\circ) = (346.4,\ 200) @@ 30° ahead of it
\text{sum}:\ (606.2,\ 50),\qquad \sqrt{606.2^2 + 50^2} \approx \htmlClass{new}{608.3 \text{ N}} @@ the size found on the east and north axes (21·phys1)

What hides is the arrangement: the choice of axes. The force is a member of the plane before any axes are drawn, and each pair of axes at right angles is one way of seeing the plane as a product. The slope’s axes are as good as east and north, and they make the friction line of 21·phys2 short.

infphys.3No Observer Stands Still

29·phys2 changed from the ground to a moving observer by a grid J, and found that J keeps c²t² − x². Its mixing-turn technique wrote J as a turn Ju with tanh u = v/c and showed that two turns add their u. 29·phys3 used that once, for two speeds. As arrows the same lines say more.

Observer-row technique: u ↦ Ju keeps adding as composing: Jw ∘ Ju = Ju + w, J₀ is do-nothing, and J−u undoes Ju. So the change from any steady observer on the line to any other is one J, any row of changes is one change, and every one of them keeps c²t² − x².

J_w \circ J_u = J_{u + w} @@ the mixing-turn technique (29·phys2)
J_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\qquad J_{-u} \circ J_u = J_0 @@ cosh 0 = 1, sinh 0 = 0; then the line above with w = −u
J_{u_k} \circ \cdots \circ J_{u_1} = J_{u_1 + \cdots + u_k} @@ induction on k (4c)
q \circ J_u = q \text{ for } q = c^2t^2 - x^2 @@ the kept-interval technique (29·phys2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every row of changes is one } J,\ \text{and keeps } q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum of the u is a number, so it is some u
  
\tanh u = 0.6 \Rightarrow u = \ln 2 @@ tanh(ln 2) = (2 − ½)/(2 + ½)
u + u + u = \ln 8 @@ the row is one turn
\htmlClass{new}{v = c\tanh(\ln 8) = \tfrac{8 - 1/8}{8 + 1/8}\,c = \tfrac{63}{65}\,c \approx 0.969c} @@ still below c, as the speeds-add technique (29·phys3) says

What hides is the arrangement and the sequence together. No J in the family is marked as “from the observer at rest”: do-nothing belongs to every observer alike. And since u + w = w + u, the order of the changes in a row does not show in the result. The speeds-add rule of 29·phys3 is the adding of u, read through tanh.

infphys.4Through the Line and Out

31·phys1 found that a filter with line d acts by the shadow map P = ddᵀ, and that P twice is P once. 31·phys2 put filters in a row and got a product of dots. Both come from splitting P into two arrows.

Through-the-line technique: if P = i ∘ r and r ∘ i is do-nothing, then P ∘ P = P. A row of such arrows is the first r, the last i, and between them the arrows rj + 1 ∘ ij; for filters each of those is multiplying by the number dj · dj + 1.

P \circ P = i \circ (r \circ i) \circ r = i \circ r = P \quad \blacksquare @@ regroup (47a); r ∘ i is do-nothing
P_2 \circ P_1 = i_2 \circ (r_2 \circ i_1) \circ r_1 @@ regroup
(r_2 \circ i_1)(s) = (s\,d_1) \cdot d_2 = s\,(d_1 \cdot d_2) @@ a number in, a number out
\htmlClass{new}{P_k \circ \cdots \circ P_1 = i_k \circ (d_{k-1} \cdot d_k) \cdots (d_1 \cdot d_2) \circ r_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on k (4c): the stack technique (31·phys2)
  
(d_1 \cdot d_2)(d_2 \cdot d_3) = \cos 45^\circ \cos 45^\circ = \tfrac12 @@ two numbers between the first r and the last i
\tfrac12 \cdot \big(\tfrac12\big)^2 = \tfrac18 \text{ of the brightness} @@ the mixed-light technique gives the first ½; brightness is the square
\htmlClass{new}{\text{without the middle filter: } d_1 \cdot d_3 = \cos 90^\circ = 0} @@ one number between, and it is 0

What hides is the arrangement in the middle: between two filters the light is one number, and the plane it shakes in is not needed again until the last i. The sequence does not hide here, and the arrows show why. P₂ ∘ P₁ ends with i₂ and P₁ ∘ P₂ ends with i₁, so the two rows send the light out along different lines.

infphys.5A Pair Is a Cup

41·phys2 proved that the state Φ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is not a state of one qubit paired with a state of the other. 41·phys4 proved that whatever is measured in the Seattle lab, the photon in Victoria alone has the grid ½I. Both worked with amplitudes. ∞א‎ has a picture in which Φ is one bent wire.

Cup-state technique: Φ is the cup of ∞א‎ divided by √2. Its grid has rank 2, so Φ is not one product. For every gate U, (U ⊗ I)Φ = (I ⊗ Uᵀ)Φ: a gate on the first qubit of the pair is the same change of state as the gate Uᵀ on the second.

\Phi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(e_0 \otimes e_0 + e_1 \otimes e_1) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,\text{cup} @@ the cup (∞א‎), for two wires of dimension 2
\text{the grid of } \Phi \text{ is } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\ \text{of rank } 2 @@ so Φ is a sum of two products and of no fewer: the fewest-terms technique (47h); the no-split technique (41·phys2) again
(U \otimes I)\,\Phi = (I \otimes U^{\mathsf T})\,\Phi @@ the slide-round-cup technique (∞א‎)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a gate at one end of the cup is a gate at the other}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the box slides round the bend and comes out turned over
  
\rho \mapsto U^{\mathsf T}\rho\,\overline{U} @@ ρ = Σ pᵢψᵢψᵢ* (41·phys4), and each ψᵢ becomes Uᵀψᵢ
U^{\mathsf T}\,\tfrac12 I\,\overline{U} = \tfrac12\,\overline{U^{*}U} = \tfrac12 I @@ a gate keeps sizes: U*U = I
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Victoria’s grid is } \tfrac12 I \text{ before and after}} @@ the half-pair technique (41·phys4), here for a gate in place of a measurement

What hides is the arrangement, the four amplitudes, and one fact of sequence: which lab acted. The state after a gate in Seattle is a state that a gate in Victoria would have made, so nothing in the pair records where the gate was. A cup cannot be cut into a wire for each lab. That is the no-split technique, drawn.

infphys.6Kept at One Tick, Kept at All

29·time2 proved that a stepped law with a sliding symmetry keeps its stepped momentum exactly, however long the tick. 29·time3 proved that one tick keeps area at any step size, and 32·time2 drew from that a spring that turns without growing. 46·phys1 proved, for time that flows, that the total of |ψ|² is kept. Each proof was about one tick, or one moment’s rate. Arrows carry it to every count of ticks.

Let T be the arrow from the state to the state that one tick makes. For the stepped law the state is the pair (xk, xk + 1), and k ticks are Tᵏ. An amount is an arrow Q from states to numbers, and it is kept when Q ∘ T = Q. A symmetry is an arrow s from states to states with s ∘ T = T ∘ s.

Kept-along technique: if Q ∘ T = Q, then Q ∘ Tᵏ = Q for every count k. If s ∘ T = T ∘ s, then s ∘ Tᵏ = Tᵏ ∘ s: a symmetry carries a stepped path to a stepped path. If the slope grid of T has determinant 1 at every state, so has the slope grid of Tᵏ.

Q \circ T^{k+1} = (Q \circ T) \circ T^k = Q \circ T^k = Q @@ regroup (47a); then the case k: induction (4c)
s \circ T^{k+1} = (T \circ s) \circ T^k = T \circ (T^k \circ s) = T^{k+1} \circ s @@ the same
\det(\text{slope grid of } T^{k+1}) = \det(\text{of } T) \cdot \det(\text{of } T^k) = 1 @@ the chain rule multiplies slope grids (28), and det of a product is the product (27e)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{kept by one tick, kept by every count of ticks}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no line used the length of a tick
  
T(x, y) = (y,\ 2y - x),\quad s(x, y) = (x + \varepsilon,\ y + \varepsilon):\quad T(s(x, y)) = (y + \varepsilon,\ 2y - x + \varepsilon) = s(T(x, y)) @@ the slide is a symmetry of the free stepped law (29·time2)
p = \tfrac mh (y - x):\quad p \circ T = \tfrac mh (2y - x - y) = p @@ the stepped momentum is kept
T(x, y) = (y,\ y - x):\quad (1, 0) \mapsto (0, -1) \mapsto (-1, -1) \mapsto (-1, 0) \mapsto (0, 1) \mapsto (1, 1) \mapsto (1, 0) @@ the spring with c = 2 − ω²h² = 1 (29·time4)
\htmlClass{new}{Q = x^2 + y^2 - xy = 1 \text{ at all six}} @@ kept along; six ticks to a turn, as cos θ = ½ says (32·time1)

What hides is the sequence: how many ticks have gone by, and how long each one is. That is why the time annexes could say “exactly, at any step”. Finding the kept amount that belongs to a symmetry is still the work of 29·time2 and of 46·phys1, and arrows do not shorten it. They say only that once it is found for one tick, nothing more is needed.

The accepted names

The number m is the mechanical advantage and d the velocity ratio turned over; a map that keeps composing is a functor, here a homomorphism to numbers under times. Unit vectors at right angles are an orthonormal basis, the parts are components or coordinates, an arrow that keeps adding and scaling is linear, and the demand “exactly one for each pair” is the universal property of the product. The change J is a Lorentz boost, u is the rapidity, c²t² − x² is the interval, and the family Ju is a one-parameter group, part of the Lorentz group; that no observer is marked is the principle of relativity. An arrow with PP = P is an idempotent, or a projector; writing it as i ∘ r with r ∘ i do-nothing is splitting the idempotent, with r a retraction and i a section; the cos² rule for brightness is Malus’s law. Φ is a Bell state; the slide round the cup is the transpose trick; Victoria’s grid is the reduced density matrix, and its not changing is no-signalling. An amount with Q ∘ T = Q is an invariant, or a conserved quantity; an s with s ∘ T = T ∘ s is a symmetry of the dynamical system, and the two properties are invariance and equivariance; a map whose slope grid has determinant 1 is area-preserving, in the plane the same as symplectic. The passage from a symmetry to its kept amount is Noether’s theorem, and 29·time2 is its stepped form.

What the arrows let hide

In the first, second, fourth and fifth footings the arrows hide arrangement: the gears of the train, the choice of axes, the plane between two filters, the amplitudes of the pair. In the third they hide which observer is at rest and the order of the changes. In the sixth they hide the count of ticks and the length of each. In the fourth the sequence does not hide, and the arrows show where it sits: in the last arrow of the row. In each footing the plan is one statement about arrows: two maps that keep composing; a product; a family closed under ∘; an arrow that goes through a smaller thing; a cup; an arrow from a thing to itself, run again and again. No dated claim is made on this page.
