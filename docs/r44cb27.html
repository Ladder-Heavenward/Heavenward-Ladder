44·cb27 · newsflash for 27

Every Matrix Is Nearly Diagonal

Rung twenty seven found the vectors a grid only stretches, and when there are enough of them the grid is diagonal in their basis. Often there are not enough. The grid that slides the plane along, one row of ones above the diagonal, stretches only one direction, and no basis makes it diagonal. Rung twenty seven left it there. Step 44i gives the answer. Let the grid act on vectors, and let the letter x stand for doing the grid once. Then the vectors are a module over the polynomials, and the structure technique splits it into clocks of polynomials. Each clock, in a basis read off from its polynomial, is a grid with a fixed shape. Over the complex numbers each clock is one stretch on the diagonal with ones just above it. So every square grid of complex numbers is, in the right basis, diagonal except for some ones next to the diagonal, and the ones count how far it falls short.

A grid makes its vectors a module over F[x], with x acting as the grid. The structure technique splits that module into clocks F[x]/(d), and each clock has a grid of a fixed shape. Over ℂ each clock is a block with one stretch on the diagonal and ones just above. A grid is diagonal in some basis exactly when its least polynomial has no repeated zero.

44·cb27.1A Grid Makes a Module

Let A be a grid with n rows and n columns over a field F. On the vectors Fⁿ, let a polynomial p stretch a vector v by p · v = p(A)v. The techniques of 44i1 hold because grids add and multiply (27d). So Fⁿ is a module over F[x], and x · v = Av.

Grid-module technique: Fⁿ, with p · v = p(A)v, is a finitely made module over F[x] with no copy of F[x] in it, so it is a copy of F[x]/(d₁) × ⋯ × F[x]/(dₖ) with d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ, each with top coefficient 1.

\text{the unit vectors make } F^n @@ over F, so over F[x]
c(A) = 0 \text{ for } c = \det(A - xI) @@ the own-polynomial technique (27g)
c \cdot v = 0 \text{ for every } v @@ so no member is free, and r = 0 in the structure technique
\htmlClass{new}{F^n \cong F[x]/(d_1) \times \cdots \times F[x]/(d_k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the structure technique (44i), over F[x]

The last dₖ is 27g’s least polynomial: it kills every vector, and nothing smaller does. The product d₁ ⋯ dₖ is the own polynomial, up to sign. Held in trust: that the dᵢ are found by bringing xI − A to a diagonal with 44i’s moves over F[x]; the proof is 44·cb27.6.3.

44·cb27.2Each Clock Has a Fixed Grid

Take one piece F[x]/(d), with d = xᵏ + cₖ₋₁xᵏ⁻¹ + ⋯ + c₀. Its members are the remainders of degree below k (44c2), with basis 1, x, …, xᵏ⁻¹.

Companion technique: on F[x]/(d), with basis 1, x, …, x^(k−1), multiplying by x has the grid with ones just below the diagonal and −c₀, …, −cₖ₋₁ in the last column, and its own polynomial is d, up to sign.

x \cdot x^j = x^{j+1} \text{ for } j < k - 1 @@ the next basis vector: a 1 just below the diagonal
x \cdot x^{k-1} = x^k = -c_0 - c_1 x - \cdots - c_{k-1}x^{k-1} @@ the remainder of xᵏ on dividing by d
\htmlClass{new}{C_d = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & & & -c_0 \\ 1 & 0 & & -c_1 \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ & & 1 & -c_{k-1} \end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the images as columns (27d)

So every grid is, in a suitable basis, a row of these blocks down the diagonal, one for each dᵢ, and zeros elsewhere. Two grids are the same map in two bases exactly when their lists d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ agree: the list is the whole of what a basis cannot change.

44·cb27.3Over ℂ, Nearly Diagonal

Over ℂ every dᵢ splits into straight factors (25d), so by 43c’s remainder technique, read for polynomials that share no factor, each piece is a product of clocks ℂ[x]/((x − λ)ᵐ).

Near-diagonal technique: on ℂ[x]/((x − λ)ᵐ), in the basis (x − λ)^(m−1), …, (x − λ), 1, multiplying by x has λ on the diagonal, 1 just above it, and 0 elsewhere.

x \cdot u = \lambda u + (x - \lambda)u @@ add and take away λu
u = (x - \lambda)^j:\ \ x \cdot u = \lambda (x - \lambda)^j + (x - \lambda)^{j+1} @@ the next basis vector up, or 0 when j + 1 = m
\htmlClass{new}{J_m(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 & & \\ & \lambda & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & \lambda \end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the images as columns, in the basis read from the top power down

So every square grid of complex numbers is, in some basis, a row of such blocks down the diagonal. The stretches λ sit on the diagonal, and each block of size m above 1 holds m − 1 ones. The sliding grid of the header is J₂(0), one block, and no basis does better.

44·cb27.4When It Is Diagonal

Plain-diagonal technique: a grid over ℂ is diagonal in some basis exactly when its least polynomial has no repeated zero. A block Jₘ(λ) with m ≥ 2 has least polynomial (x − λ)ᵐ, and diagonal blocks have least polynomials x − λ; the least polynomial of the whole grid is the largest power of each x − λ that occurs.

A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix},\ \ \det(xI - A) = (x - 2)^3 @@ the top block has (x − 3)(x − 1) + 1 = (x − 2)²
A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ne 0,\ \ (A - 2I)^2 = 0 @@ multiply out
\text{least polynomial } (x - 2)^2 @@ the least-obeyed technique (27g)
\htmlClass{new}{A \text{ is } J_2(2) \text{ beside } J_1(2) \text{ in some basis, and is not diagonal in any}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the list is x − 2, (x − 2)²; the plain-diagonal technique

27f’s straighten-out technique gave a diagonal when the stretches are all different. That is a special case: n different stretches make the least polynomial a product of n different straight factors. The grid above has one stretch, 2, three times, and only a plane of vectors it stretches.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The block of the companion technique is the companion matrix, and the row of companion blocks is the rational canonical form, or Frobenius normal form; d₁, …, dₖ are the invariant factors of A. The blocks Jₘ(λ) are Jordan blocks, and the near-diagonal form is the Jordan normal form; the powers (x − λ)ᵐ are the elementary divisors. The plain-diagonal technique is the criterion for diagonalisability by the minimal polynomial.

Who said it first

Camille Jordan published the near-diagonal form in his Traité des substitutions of 1870, for grids over finite fields. Karl Weierstrass had the same lists of polynomials in 1868, for pairs of quadratic forms, and Georg Frobenius gave the rational form in 1879. The reading of a grid as a module over F[x] is from the school of Emmy Noether in the 1920s.

A proof that is not allowed

“A has the three stretches 2, 2 and 2, so it has three stretched vectors, and they make a basis.” Not allowed: a stretch counted three times in the own polynomial may have fewer free stretched vectors. For the grid of the fourth footing, A − 2I loses only a plane, so there are two free stretched vectors, not three.
