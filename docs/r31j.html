31j · proof

Polynomials Are Everywhere Near

A continuous function can be wild. Its graph can turn sharply, wiggle more and more finely, and have no slope anywhere at all. A polynomial is tame: it has slopes of every order, and it is fixed by finitely many numbers. Yet every continuous function on a closed interval has polynomials as close to it as you like, at every input at once. The proof on this step does not hunt for the polynomial. It tosses coins. Toss a coin that lands heads with chance x, many times, and look up the function at the share of heads. The average of what you find is a polynomial in x, and rung thirty’s averages that settle push it onto the function. So chance builds the polynomial, and the even continuity of rung fifteen locks the closeness at every input together. The step ends on the other side of the same coin: a function with no slope anywhere is still continuous, and polynomials still come as near to it as you like.

Toss n coins, each heads with chance x, and average the value of f at the share of heads. The result is a polynomial of degree n in x. Because shares of heads settle, and f is evenly continuous on [0, 1], these polynomials come within any ε of f at every input at once, however wild f is.

31j1A Polynomial Made of Coin Tosses

Fix x in [0, 1]. Toss n coins, independently, each heads with chance x, and let S be the number of heads. By 4k’s counting of choices and 30f’s techniques, P(S = k) = C(n, k) xᵏ(1 − x)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. For a function f on [0, 1], from here on Bₙf is the polynomial

B_nf(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f\big(\tfrac kn\big)\binom nk x^k(1 - x)^{n-k} = \operatorname{E} f\big(\tfrac Sn\big)

the average of f at the share of heads. It has degree at most n, and its numbers are values of f.

Coin-average technique: the share of heads S/n has average x and spread x(1 − x)/n, which is at most 1/(4n); so Bₙ1 = 1, Bₙx = x and Bₙx² = x² + x(1 − x)/n.

S = X_1 + \cdots + X_n,\ \ X_i = 1 \text{ for heads, } 0 \text{ for tails} @@ one chance variable per coin
\operatorname{E}X_i = x,\ \ \operatorname{Var}X_i = x - x^2 @@ X² = X; the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2)
\operatorname{E}\tfrac Sn = x,\ \ \operatorname{Var}\tfrac Sn = \tfrac{x(1 - x)}{n} @@ the average-adds and independent-spreads-add techniques (30g)
x(1 - x) = \tfrac14 - (x - \tfrac12)^2 \le \tfrac14 @@ completing the square (8i)
\htmlClass{new}{B_nx^2 = \operatorname{E}\big(\tfrac Sn\big)^2 = x^2 + \tfrac{x(1 - x)}{n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ E Y² = (E Y)² + Var Y (30g2)

31j2Near at Every Input at Once

Average-polynomial technique: if f is continuous on [0, 1], then ‖Bₙf − f‖∞ heads to 0: for every ε > 0 some polynomial is within ε of f at every input at once. On any [u, v] the same holds, by the slide and stretch x = u + (v − u)t.

|B_nf(x) - f(x)| = \big|\operatorname{E}\big(f(\tfrac Sn) - f(x)\big)\big| \le \operatorname{E}\big|f(\tfrac Sn) - f(x)\big| @@ Bₙ1 = 1 (31j1); the size of an average is at most the average size

Anexact, yet rigorous · loose, then locked

30d’s box set loose limits, each point settling at its own pace, against a fence that locks them. Here the loose part is chance: at each x, the share of heads settles at x by 30h. The lock is 15f’s even continuity: one δ serves every input, and the spread bound 1/(4n) is the same for every x.

Settling at each point is loose; one bound that serves every point at once locks it, and only the locked claim gives one polynomial for the whole interval.

Without the lock, the averages would still settle at each x, and nothing would be said about all of them at once. With it, one n serves the whole interval.

A survey of salmon counts on every creek between Coos Bay and Tillamook gets more precise as it samples more. What would it take to say one sample size is enough for every creek at once?

31j3Near in Height, Not in Slope

Build one. Let z(x) be the distance from x to the nearest whole number: a zigzag of height ½ and slopes ±1. Let

W(x) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{z(4^kx)}{2^k}

a sum of zigzags, each four times as fine as the last and half as tall. The kth zigzag has slopes ±2ᵏ wherever it has a slope.

Zigzag-sum technique: W is continuous on the whole line, and the polynomials Bₙ come within any ε of it on [0, 1].

\big\|\tfrac{z(4^kx)}{2^k}\big\|_\infty = \tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}},\ \ \textstyle\sum_k \tfrac{1}{2^{k+1}} = 1 @@ the sizes add to something finite (22e)
\text{each term is continuous} @@ a zigzag, squeezed: continuous functions composed (11)
W \text{ is continuous} @@ the size-sum technique (31i1), in the complete space of the sup-complete technique
\htmlClass{new}{\|B_nW - W\|_\infty \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-polynomial technique: it needs only continuity

Does W have a slope anywhere? Over the inputs where the kth zigzag runs straight, its chord slopes are ±2ᵏ, and finer zigzags add slopes that do not cancel. The proof that W has a slope at no input at all is the seam standing question of this step. Each Bₙ W is a polynomial, smooth everywhere, and the list of them comes as near as you like to a curve that has no slope anywhere.

31j4When a Family Comes Near Every Continuous Function

The polynomials come near every continuous function on [u, v] (31j2). What did they need for that? Let 𝒜 be any family of continuous real functions on [u, v] that holds the constants, and holds f + g and fg whenever it holds f and g. Say 𝒜 tells points apart when for any two different points x and y some member has different values at them.

Family technique: if 𝒜 tells points apart, then for every continuous f on [u, v] and every ε > 0 some member of 𝒜 is within ε of f at every input at once.

Write 𝒜′ for the functions that members of 𝒜 come as near to as you like. It holds the constants, sums and products too. The claim is that 𝒜′ is everything.

g \in \mathcal{A}' \Rightarrow |g| \in \mathcal{A}' @@ polynomials come near |t| on [−M, M] (31j2), and a polynomial of g is in 𝒜′
\max(g, h),\ \min(g, h) \in \mathcal{A}' @@ max(g, h) = ½(g + h + |g − h|), and min likewise with a minus
h_{xy}(x) = f(x),\ \ h_{xy}(y) = f(y) @@ some member through any two wanted heights: stretch and lift a member that tells x from y
g_x = \max \text{ of finitely many } h_{xy}:\ \ g_x \gt f - \varepsilon @@ h_xy > f − ε near y; finitely many such stretches cover [u, v] (the least-upper-bound argument of 30a1); and g_x(x) = f(x)
g = \min \text{ of finitely many } g_x:\ \ g \lt f + \varepsilon @@ g_x < f + ε near x; cover again
\htmlClass{new}{f - \varepsilon \lt g \lt f + \varepsilon,\ \ g \in \mathcal{A}'} \quad \blacksquare @@ so f is in 𝒜′

The polynomials are such a family: x itself tells any two points apart. So are the sums of cos nx on [0, π], since cos x alone tells points apart there and cos nx · cos mx is a sum of two cosines (21).

Telling points apart cannot be dropped. The even polynomials on [−1, 1], sums of 1, x², x⁴, …, hold constants, sums and products. No one of them tells x from −x. So every function they come near is even, and they stay at least 1 away from f(x) = x at one of the points 1 and −1.

With turning numbers one more thing is needed. On the circle |z| = 1 the polynomials in z tell points apart, and they do not come near z̄: the average of z · p(z) round the circle is 0 for every polynomial p, and the average of z · z̄ is 1. The family must also hold f̄ whenever it holds f.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Bₙf is the Bernstein polynomial of f, and the average-polynomial technique is the Weierstrass approximation theorem, proved here by Bernstein’s probabilistic method. Near at every input at once is uniform approximation, and a set near to every member of a space is dense. A continuous function with no slope anywhere is a nowhere-differentiable function; W is one of the family of Takagi–van der Waerden functions, and the first such curve is the Weierstrass function. That most continuous functions are of this kind, in the sense of Baire category, is the Banach–Mazurkiewicz theorem. The family technique is the Stone and Weierstrass approximation; a family closed under sums and products is an algebra, and one that tells points apart separates points.

Who said it first

Karl Weierstrass presented a continuous function with no slope anywhere to the Berlin Academy in 1872, and proved in 1885 that every continuous function on a closed interval is a uniform limit of polynomials. Charles Hermite wrote to Thomas Stieltjes in 1893 that he turned away “with fright and horror from this lamentable plague of continuous functions which have no derivatives”, and Henri Poincaré called such functions monsters, made to show the arguments of the fathers wrong. Sergei Bernstein gave the coin proof in 1912. Teiji Takagi built a zigzag sum in 1903, and Bartel van der Waerden the one with factor 4 in 1930. Stefan Banach and Stefan Mazurkiewicz proved in 1931 that the functions with a slope somewhere are, in Baire’s sense, a thin set among the continuous ones.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each Bₙ W has a slope everywhere and Bₙ W heads to W, so W has a slope everywhere.” Not allowed: a list that settles in highest height says nothing about slopes, as 31i2 showed for the wiggles (1/n) sin(nx).
