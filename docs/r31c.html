31c · proof

Straightening a basis

The closest point was easy to find with measuring directions at right angles and slow without them. Most bases do not come at right angles. The powers of x do not, and neither do the columns of a table of data. So this step straightens a basis, one arrow at a time. Keep the first arrow, cut to size one. From the second, take away its shadow on the first, and cut what is left to size one. From the third, take away its shadows on the first two, and so on. Each new arrow stands at right angles to all the ones before it, and the first few always build the same space as the first few of the old basis. Written with grids, straightening says that every grid with free columns is a grid with columns at right angles times a grid shaped like a staircase. That is how a machine finds a best fit without ever forming the slow samenesses of the last step.

From any free list, take away from each arrow its shadows on the straightened arrows before it, and cut what is left to size one. The result is a right-angled list that builds the same spaces, one at a time. As grids: every grid with free columns is a grid with right-angled columns times a staircase.

31c1One arrow at a time

Let v₁, v₂, …, vₙ be free, under some product. Build e₁, e₂, … in turn. Put e₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖. When e₁, …, eₖ₋₁ are built, take wₖ = vₖ − ⟨vₖ, e₁⟩e₁ − ⋯ − ⟨vₖ, eₖ₋₁⟩eₖ₋₁, the miss of vₖ from its shadow on the arrows already built (31b2), and put eₖ = wₖ/‖wₖ‖.

Straightening technique: from free arrows v₁, …, vₙ, the arrows e₁, …, eₙ built this way are a right-angled list, and for every k the first k of them build the same space as v₁, …, vₖ.

k = 1:\ \ e_1 = v_1/\|v_1\|,\ \ \|e_1\| = 1 @@ v₁ ≠ 0, since the list is free; e₁ builds the line v₁ builds
e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1} \text{ right-angled, building } W_{k-1} @@ suppose it, for induction (4b): W₍ₖ₋₁₎ is what v₁, …, vₖ₋₁ build
w_k = v_k - P_{k-1}v_k \perp W_{k-1} @@ the shadow-sum technique (31b2)
w_k \ne 0 @@ else vₖ lies in W₍ₖ₋₁₎, a mix of v₁, …, vₖ₋₁, and the list is not free
e_k = w_k/\|w_k\| \text{ has size } 1,\ \perp e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1} @@ stretching keeps right angles
\htmlClass{new}{e_1, \ldots, e_k \text{ build what } v_1, \ldots, v_k \text{ build}} \quad \blacksquare @@ eₖ is a mix of vₖ and W₍ₖ₋₁₎, and vₖ = wₖ + P₍ₖ₋₁₎vₖ is a mix of the e’s
  
v_1 = (1, 1, 1),\ v_2 = (1, 1, 0),\ v_3 = (1, 0, 0) @@ free, under the dot
e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1) @@ |v₁| = √3
w_2 = (1, 1, 0) - \tfrac23(1, 1, 1) = \tfrac13(1, 1, -2),\ \ e_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, 1, -2) @@ ⟨v₂, e₁⟩e₁ = (2/3)(1, 1, 1)
w_3 = (1, 0, 0) - \tfrac13(1, 1, 1) - \tfrac16(1, 1, -2) = \tfrac12(1, -1, 0) @@ two shadows taken away
\htmlClass{new}{e_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 27i2’s three directions, found from a staircase of 1s

The order matters. Straighten v₃, v₂, v₁ instead and the first arrow is (1, 0, 0): another right-angled list, building the same whole space, with different first pieces.

31c2Straightening the powers of x

Under the area product on [−1, 1], the powers 1, x, x², … are free (a polynomial that is 0 everywhere has every number in front of xᵏ equal to 0) and far from right-angled: ⟨1, x²⟩ = 2/3. Straighten the first three.

e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} @@ ‖1‖² = 2
w_2 = x - \langle x, e_1\rangle e_1 = x,\ \ e_2 = \sqrt{\tfrac32}\,x @@ ⟨x, 1⟩ = 0; ‖x‖² = 2/3
w_3 = x^2 - \langle x^2, e_1\rangle e_1 - \langle x^2, e_2\rangle e_2 = x^2 - \tfrac13 @@ ⟨x², 1⟩ = 2/3, ⟨x², x⟩ = 0
\|x^2 - \tfrac13\|^2 = \tfrac25 - \tfrac49 + \tfrac29 = \tfrac{8}{45} @@ ∫ (x⁴ − (2/3)x² + 1/9) from −1 to 1
\htmlClass{new}{e_3 = \sqrt{\tfrac{45}{8}}\,\big(x^2 - \tfrac13\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ cut to size 1

So the closest parabola to any f on [−1, 1] is three readings, ⟨f, e₁⟩e₁ + ⟨f, e₂⟩e₂ + ⟨f, e₃⟩e₃, and the closest cubic adds one more reading without changing the first three. That is what right angles buy. The polynomials straightened this way have their own family, and 31m works with them.

31c3Right angles times a staircase

Take lists of m numbers with the dot, and a grid X with m rows and n free columns v₁, …, vₙ. Straightening gives e₁, …, eₙ. From here on, Q is the grid with columns e₁, …, eₙ, so QᵀQ = I as in 27m1, and a staircase is a square grid with only 0s below its middle line.

Column-straightening technique: every grid X with free columns is X = QR, where Q has right-angled columns and R is a staircase with numbers above 0 down its middle.

v_k = w_k + \textstyle\sum_{j \lt k}\langle v_k, e_j\rangle e_j = \|w_k\|e_k + \sum_{j \lt k}\langle v_k, e_j\rangle e_j @@ 31c1, turned round
R_{jk} = \langle v_k, e_j\rangle \text{ for } j \lt k,\ \ R_{kk} = \|w_k\| > 0,\ \ R_{jk} = 0 \text{ for } j > k @@ column k uses only e₁, …, eₖ
\text{column } k \text{ of } QR = \textstyle\sum_j R_{jk}e_j = v_k @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d2)
\htmlClass{new}{X = QR} \quad \blacksquare @@ every column agrees

Now the best fit of 31b3 needs no XᵀX. Put X = QR into XᵀXc = Xᵀy: RᵀQᵀQRc = RᵀQᵀy, so RᵀRc = RᵀQᵀy. Rᵀ is undoable, its middle numbers being above 0, so Rc = Qᵀy, solved from the bottom row up.

X = [\,1\ \ x\,],\ \ x = (1, 2, 3, 4) @@ the grid of 31b4
e_1 = \tfrac12(1, 1, 1, 1),\ \ w_2 = x - 5e_1 = (-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5),\ \ \|w_2\| = \sqrt5 @@ ⟨x, e₁⟩ = 5
R = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 0 & \sqrt5\end{pmatrix},\ \ Q^{\mathsf T}y = \big(5,\ \tfrac{3}{\sqrt5}\big) @@ y = (2, 1, 4, 3)
\htmlClass{new}{b = \tfrac{3/\sqrt5}{\sqrt5} = 0.6,\ \ k = \tfrac{5 - 5(0.6)}{2} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ bottom row first, then the top

The accepted names

Straightening is the Gram–Schmidt process (or Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization), and the list it gives is an orthonormal basis. X = QR is the QR decomposition (or QR factorization), with Q having orthonormal columns and R upper triangular; a staircase is an upper triangular matrix. The straightened powers of x on [−1, 1] are, up to size, the Legendre polynomials.

Who said it first

Pierre-Simon Laplace used the steps in 1812, without the name. Jørgen Pedersen Gram set them out in 1883 for fitting series by least squares, and Erhard Schmidt gave the clean form for functions in 1907. Adrien-Marie Legendre met his polynomials in 1782, in the pull of a planet of any shape, long before anyone straightened anything. The machine methods for QR came in the 1950s: Wallace Givens by turns in the plane, and Alston Householder by mirrors, in 1958.

A proof that is not allowed

“e₃ is at right angles to e₂, and e₂ is at right angles to e₁, so e₃ is at right angles to e₁.” Not allowed: right angles do not chain. (1, 0), (0, 1) and (1, 0) chain right angles and the first and last are the same arrow. Each eₖ is at right angles to every earlier e because every shadow was taken away.
