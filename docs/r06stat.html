6·stat · statistics

How do you pick a fair sample?

How do you pick a fair sample? Nobody can ask every voter in Oregon, or test the water in every well. So people ask a few and hope the few speak for the many. The few are a sample, and picking them is a choice in rung six's sense: one group picked out of all the groups that could have been. What makes it hard is that every way of picking is a rule or a draw, and a rule can lean without anyone meaning it to. The people easiest to reach are not like the rest. The people who answer a question are often the ones who care most about it. This page says what a fair pick is, counts what it promises, finds where it still misses, and then looks at a famous poll that heard from over two million people and got the answer wrong.

Call a sample fair when every group of the same size is equally likely to be the one picked. Then each member's chance of being in is the size of the sample over the size of the whole, and the sample's share of anything is right on average. A single fair sample can still miss, so count how often. When the chance of being picked depends on the people, and above all on their answers, the sample leans toward whoever was easier to pick.

A sample is a pick

Oregon has 36 counties. Say a state study can afford to test the well water in 4 of them. The whole set of 36 is the population, and the 4 tested are a sample. A sample of 4 is a part of the population with 4 members. So picking a sample is picking one member from one set: the set of all parts with 4 members.

\binom{36}{4} = \tfrac{36 \cdot 35 \cdot 34 \cdot 33}{4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = \htmlClass{new}{58\,905} @@ choosing-counts-parts: the number of samples of 4

That is one set with 58 905 members, so 6b's finite-choice picks from it with no axiom. Picking is never the problem. The problem is which pick. A rule that picks is easy to write. Take the first four in the alphabet: Baker, Benton, Clackamas and Clatsop. That is 6a's least-chooses, with the alphabet as the order. It names the same four every time, and nothing about the alphabet makes those four like the state.

From here on, on this page, a sample of k from a population of N members is fair when each of the parts with k members is equally likely to be the one picked.

Write all 36 names on tickets, mix them in a drum, and draw 4 without putting any back. Every list of 4 different tickets in the order drawn is equally likely, and each part of 4 comes from 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 24 such lists. So each part is equally likely: a fair sample. A fair draw is a pick that looks at nothing about the members. In 6a's words, it treats them as socks.

Each member's chance

In a fair sample of 4 counties, what is Lane County's chance of being in? Count the parts with Lane in them, and divide by all the parts. Fair-share technique: in a fair sample of k from N, each member's chance of being in is k/N. The proof counts one set of pairs in two ways.

\text{parts holding } x:\ \binom{N-1}{k-1} @@ x is in; pick the other k − 1 from the N − 1 others (choosing-counts-parts)
\text{pairs (a part, one of its members)}:\ \binom{N}{k} \cdot k @@ stages-multiply: the part, then one of its k members
\text{the same pairs}:\ N \cdot \binom{N-1}{k-1} @@ stages-multiply the other way: the member, then a part that holds it
P(x \text{ is in}) = \binom{N-1}{k-1} \Big/ \binom{N}{k} @@ chance as a fraction (5·stat): the parts are equally likely
\htmlClass{new}{P(x \text{ is in}) = \tfrac{k}{N}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two counts are the same, so the bottom is N/k times the top (5b)

For Lane, 6545 of the 58 905 samples hold it, and 6545/58 905 = 4/36 = 1/9. Every county gets the same 1/9. The coast and the high desert are treated alike.

Right on average

Seven of Oregon's counties touch the Pacific: Clatsop, Tillamook, Lincoln, Lane, Douglas, Coos and Curry. The share on the coast is 7/36. How many coastal counties does a fair sample of 4 hold? It varies from sample to sample, so ask for the expected value, 5·stat's average over the equally likely cases. Fair-sample technique: if m of the N members have a property, a fair sample of k holds k · m/N of them on average, so its share is m/N on average.

X = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_m @@ X counts the members with the property in the sample; X_i is 1 when the ith of them is in, 0 when not
E\, X_i = 1 \cdot \tfrac{k}{N} + 0 = \tfrac{k}{N} @@ fair-share; a payout of 1 with chance k/N
E\, X = m \cdot \tfrac{k}{N} @@ expected-values-add, m times
\htmlClass{new}{E\, \tfrac{X}{k} = \tfrac{m}{N}} \quad \blacksquare @@ shift-and-stretch, with r = 1/k and b = 0

For the counties, a fair sample of 4 holds 4 · 7/36 = 7/9 coastal counties on average, and its share is 7/36 on average: the state's own share. No sample holds 7/9 of a county. The average is a fact about all 58 905 samples together, not about any one of them.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting

Right on average is not right every time. Count the samples of 4 by how many coastal counties they hold. A sample with j of them picks j of the 7 coastal and 4 − j of the 29 inland, so stages-multiply gives the count.

samples23 75125 5788526101535

They add to 58 905. Four samples in ten hold no coastal county at all, and 35 hold nothing else. A study that drew one of those would have a fair sample and a strange answer. Fair does not mean typical. It means you know the shape of the pile you drew from.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it.

Bigger samples pile closer to the middle. That is why a sample of a thousand voters can speak for a state, and a sample of 4 counties cannot speak for much.

Draw 18 of the 36 counties fairly. How many coastal counties should you expect, and how likely is a sample with none?

Samples of convenience

Most samples are not drawn from a drum. They are whoever was easy to reach: the people at the ferry terminal in Anacortes, the readers of one newspaper, the people who click on a poll. Such a sample is picked by a rule too, but the rule looks at the people. Some are much more likely to be in than others.

Say a town has 10 000 voters, and 4000 of them favour a new bridge toll. A web poll asks. Those in favour feel strongly, and 1 in 20 of them answers. Those against mostly shrug, and 1 in 100 answers. The town is 40 percent in favour.

4000 \cdot \tfrac{1}{20} = 200 @@ in favour, expected to answer
6000 \cdot \tfrac{1}{100} = 60 @@ against, expected to answer
200 : 60 = 10 : 3 @@ ratios (5i): divide both by 20
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{200}{260} = \tfrac{10}{13} \approx 77\%} @@ the poll's share in favour, against 40% in the town

The expected counts are in the ratio of chance times number. When every member's chance is the same, that ratio is the town's own ratio, which is the fair-sample technique again. When the chance depends on the answer, the ratio tilts. When the chance of being picked depends on the answer, the sample leans toward the answer that is easier to pick. No amount of size fixes it: a million such answers lean exactly as far as 260.

In 1936 an American magazine mailed about 10 million ballots before the presidential election. Its names came largely from telephone books, car registrations and its own subscribers, which in 1936 meant the better-off. About 2.4 million came back, from those who cared to answer. It forecast that the loser would win, with about 57 percent. The winner took about 61 percent of the vote. A much smaller poll, built to be fair, called it right.

The draw must not see the answers

Even a drum can lean. In December 1969 the United States drew the order in which men born from 1944 to 1950 would be called up for the war, by birthday. The 366 birthdays went into capsules, the capsules went into a box one month at a time, and they were drawn by hand. In a fair draw each birthday's number averages 183.5, so each month's average should come out near that. They did not. The December capsules went in last, sat on top, and came out early: December's birthdays averaged about 121.5, and January's about 201.2. The next year's draw used two drums and much more mixing.

So a fair sample needs more than good intentions. It needs a pick that looks at nothing about the members, and the mixing to make that true. Rung six said a pick can be made with no rule. A fair sample says more: for this job a pick must be made with no rule that sees who is being picked.

Who said it first

Anders Kiær, head of Norway's statistics office, argued in 1895 that a well-chosen part could stand for a whole country, and was met with doubt; his "representative method" picked by judgement. Arthur Bowley used drawing by lot in surveys of English towns from 1912. Jerzy Neyman showed in 1934 that a fair draw, unlike picking by judgement, lets you say how far the answer may stray. The magazine of 1936 was the Literary Digest; its poll favoured Alf Landon, and Franklin Roosevelt won. George Gallup's far smaller poll called it. The draft lottery of December 1969 was studied by Stephen Fienberg in 1971, who found the months' averages too far apart for a fair draw. Ronald Fisher made the fair draw the heart of experiments in the 1920s and 1930s; William Gosset, who wrote as Student, argued that a planned, balanced layout of field plots can do better.

Accepted names

A fair sample is a simple random sample, drawn without replacement. Drawing by lot is random sampling. A sample of whoever is easy to reach is a convenience sample; one where people choose to answer is a voluntary response sample, or self-selected. A lean in the expected answer is bias: a picked list that leaves people out has undercoverage, and people who do not answer cause nonresponse bias. The fair-sample technique says the sample share is an unbiased estimator of the population share. The list the sample is drawn from is the sampling frame.
