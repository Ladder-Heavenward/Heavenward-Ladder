42h · technique

Clock Arithmetic by Hand

The proof steps of this rung said what is true on a clock. This step is about how long it takes to find out. A power with an exponent of three hundred digits cannot be reached by multiplying one more time, and one more, since the count of products would be a number of three hundred digits too, and no machine there will ever be could finish. Yet a pocket calculator can do it in a blink. The trick is to square: square once and the exponent doubles, square ten times and it has gone up a thousandfold. Write the exponent in twos, square for each place, and multiply in the places that hold a one. The count of products is about twice the number of places, so a power with a three hundred digit exponent takes about two thousand products. The gcd algorithm is fast for the same kind of reason: its remainders halve at least every two rounds. And a power on a big clock can be split onto small clocks, cut down there, and put back.

Write the exponent in twos; square once per place and multiply where a place holds one. That takes at most about twice the log base two of the exponent in products. The gcd algorithm halves its remainder every two rounds. Splitting a clock into prime power clocks lets each exponent be cut before the work starts.

42h1Square, Then Multiply

4i wrote every number in twos: 218 = 128 + 64 + 16 + 8 + 2, written 11011010. 4g’s powers-add-their-counts technique turns that sum into a product of powers whose exponents are powers of 2, and each of those is the square of the one before.

Square-and-multiply technique: to find aᵏ on the clock of n, square a again and again to get a, a², a⁴, a⁸, …, each on the clock, and multiply together those whose places in k written in twos hold a 1.

3,\ 9,\ 81,\ 6561 \equiv 561,\ 561^2 \equiv 721,\ 721^2 \equiv 841,\ 841^2 \equiv 281,\ 281^2 \equiv 961 @@ 3 to the powers 1, 2, 4, …, 128, each the square of the last, kept below 1000
218 = 2 + 8 + 16 + 64 + 128 @@ the places of 11011010 that hold a 1
9 \cdot 561 = 5049 \equiv 49,\ \ 49 \cdot 721 \equiv 329 @@ the places 2, 8, 16
329 \cdot 281 \equiv 449,\ \ 449 \cdot 961 \equiv 489 @@ the places 64 and 128
\htmlClass{new}{3^{218} \equiv 489 \pmod{1000}} \quad \blacksquare @@ seven squarings and four products

No number above 961 · 961, under a million, was ever needed. The power 3²¹⁸ has 105 digits; its last three are 489.

42h2How Many Products

Power-cost technique: the square-and-multiply technique finds aᵏ on a clock with at most 2⌊log₂ k⌋ products of hours. k written in twos has ⌊log₂ k⌋ + 1 places. There is one squaring for each place after the first, and at most one product for each place after the first that holds a 1.

k \lt 10^{300} \Rightarrow \log_2 k \lt 300 \log_2 10 \approx 997 @@ log of a power (17c); log₂ 10 ≈ 3.32
\text{square-and-multiply: at most about } 1994 \text{ products} @@ the power-cost technique
\text{one product at a time: } k - 1 \text{ products, about } 10^{300} @@ the plain road
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a growth family of } \log k \text{ against one of } k} \quad \blacksquare @@ 39k’s families: the log is far below the power

Each product is of two hours below n, which for n with 600 digits takes some thousands of digit products by 4i’s rule. So the whole power is millions of digit steps, a small fraction of a second for a chip. That is what makes the next three steps possible.

42h3The gcd Is Fast Too

42b found undoes by the gcd algorithm. That would be useless on clocks of hundreds of digits if the algorithm took as many rounds as the numbers are large.

Halving-remainders technique: in the gcd algorithm, each remainder is less than half of the one two rounds before, so the algorithm on a and b, with b ≤ a, ends within about 2 log₂ b rounds.

r_{i-1} = r_i q + r_{i+1},\ \ r_{i+1} \lt r_i @@ one round (4j7)
\text{if } r_i \le r_{i-1}/2:\ \ r_{i+1} \lt r_i \le r_{i-1}/2 @@ the remainder falls below
\text{if } r_i \gt r_{i-1}/2:\ \ q = 1,\ \ r_{i+1} = r_{i-1} - r_i \lt r_{i-1}/2 @@ r_i fits only once
\htmlClass{new}{r_{i+1} \lt r_{i-1}/2} @@ reason by cases
\text{after } 2j \text{ rounds the remainder is below } b/2^j @@ induction on j
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at most } 2\log_2 b + 1 \text{ rounds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a remainder below 1 is 0

For 1071 and 462 it took three rounds; the bound is about 18. The worst pairs are neighbours in the list 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …, where every quotient is 1, and even there the count of rounds grows only like the log.

42h4Split the Clock First

On the clock of 1000 = 8 · 125 the exponent-clock technique cuts the exponent on the clock of 400, and 218 is already below 400. On the two small clocks it cuts further, each on the clock of its own count of units.

c(8) = 4,\ 218 \equiv 2:\ \ 3^{218} \equiv 3^2 = 9 \equiv 1 \pmod 8 @@ the exponent-clock technique (42d3)
c(125) = 100,\ 218 \equiv 18:\ \ 3^{218} \equiv 3^{18} \equiv 114 \pmod{125} @@ the same; 3¹⁸ by square-and-multiply
x \equiv 1 \pmod 8,\ \ x \equiv 114 \pmod{125} @@ put back (42c2)
114 + 125t \equiv 1 \pmod 8:\ \ 2 + 5t \equiv 1,\ \ t \equiv 3 @@ 114 ≡ 2 and 125 ≡ 5 on the clock of 8; [5]⁻¹ = [5]
\htmlClass{new}{x = 114 + 375 = 489} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same last three digits as 42h1

Two roads, one answer, and the answer is checked. Splitting pays when the clock is a product of clocks whose sizes are known and the exponent is far larger than their counts of units. On a clock whose factors nobody knows, the split is not available, and 42i builds a lock on exactly that.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Square-and-multiply is binary exponentiation, or exponentiation by squaring; the power-cost technique says modular exponentiation takes O(log k) multiplications. The halving-remainders technique is a form of Lamé’s theorem (1844): the number of steps of Euclid’s algorithm is at most five times the number of decimal digits of the smaller number, the worst case being consecutive Fibonacci numbers. A shortest road to aᵏ is a shortest addition chain; finding one in general is believed to be hard.

Who said it first

Squaring to reach a high power is in the Chandaḥśāstra of Piṅgala (before 200 BCE), in a rule for counting metres. Gabriel Lamé proved the step bound in 1844, one of the first results counting the steps of an algorithm. Splitting the clock to cut exponents is how RSA is computed in practice (42i).

A proof that is not allowed

“3²¹⁸ on the clock of 1000: 218 = 2 · 109, so 3²¹⁸ = 9¹⁰⁹, and 109 is prime, so the little technique gives 9¹⁰⁸ ≡ 1, and 3²¹⁸ ≡ 9.” Not allowed: the little technique is about a prime clock, and here the prime is the exponent, not the clock. The answer is 489.
