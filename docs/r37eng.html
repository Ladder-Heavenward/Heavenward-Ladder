Why does a room change a song?

Why does a room change a song? Sing in a tiled bathroom and in a carpeted bedroom and you hear two singers. 37e found that walls send back a share of every sound, and in a closed room the bounces never stop at one: they keep coming, weaker each time, and the song rings on after you stop. This page measures how long, from the room’s size and what its surfaces are made of. Then it follows a song through the gear of a small venue in Seattle, from the microphone through every knob to the loudspeaker, counting in decibels, and finds the one mistake that makes a sound system howl.

A room keeps a sound ringing for a time set by its volume over how much its surfaces soak up. Through a chain of gear, levels add in decibels, and each stage has a ceiling and a floor. A microphone turns pressure into voltage and a loudspeaker turns it back. A loop from loudspeaker to microphone howls when its gain round the loop reaches one.

37eng1A room rings on

After a sound stops, its energy in a room is bounced from wall to wall and each surface soaks up a share. From here on, a surface’s soaking share is the share of the sound reaching it that it does not send back: about 0.02 for tile, 0.05 for plaster, 0.3 for carpet, 0.7 for soft ceiling tiles (laid down, measured, varying with frequency). The room’s soaking area A is the sum of each surface’s area times its share.

Ringing technique: the time for a room’s sound to fall by 60 dB after it stops is about 0.161 V/A seconds, with V in cubic metres and A in square metres. Held in trust: that the sound spreads evenly through the room. Given that, the energy falls at its own rate, so by the fading technique (37e1) it falls by a fixed number of decibels a second, and the constant 0.161 comes from how often a sound meets a wall in a room of that size.

V = 10 \times 8 \times 3 = 240 \text{ m}^3 @@ the room
A = 80(0.3) + 80(0.7) + 108(0.05) = 85.4 \text{ m}^2 @@ carpet floor, soft ceiling, plaster walls
\htmlClass{new}{0.161 \times 240 / 85.4 \approx 0.45 \text{ s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ringing technique: good for speech

Speech is clearest when a room rings for about half a second; an orchestra sounds best near two. Paint the ceiling hard (share 0.05) and A falls to 33.4, and the room rings for about 1.2 s: lessons blur.

37eng2Levels add along a chain

A sound system is a chain of gains (37h1). A gain by g is 20 log₁₀ g dB, and gains multiply, so along a chain their decibels add. Voltages are counted against 1 volt, as dBV.

Each stage also has a ceiling, the largest level it passes without clipping (37h5’s limiter, by accident), and a floor, its own hiss. Level-chain technique: along a chain the level in decibels is the sum of the gains, and the sound is clean only where every stage keeps it below its ceiling and well above its floor. Proof: logs of products are sums (17c1), applied stage by stage.

-50 \text{ dBV} @@ the microphone, for a voice near it
+40 \text{ dB preamp},\ -6 \text{ dB fader},\ +26 \text{ dB amplifier} @@ three stages
\htmlClass{new}{-50 + 40 - 6 + 26 = +10 \text{ dBV}} \quad \blacksquare @@ about 3.2 V into the loudspeaker

37eng3Pressure to voltage, and back

A microphone is a gain from pressure to voltage, its sensitivity in volts per pascal; a loudspeaker is a gain back, given as the level it makes at 1 m from 1 W. Both are maps that keep adding and stretching over their working range, so the level-chain technique runs through them.

Loudspeaker technique: a loudspeaker of sensitivity S dB (1 W, 1 m) given P watts makes S + 10 log₁₀ P dB at 1 m, and 20 log₁₀ r dB less at r metres in open air. Proof: power in decibels is 10 log₁₀ (37c2), and spreading (37c3) takes 6 dB a doubling, which is 20 log₁₀ r.

S = 88 \text{ dB},\ P = 100 \text{ W}:\ 88 + 20 = 108 \text{ dB at } 1 \text{ m} @@ 10 log₁₀ 100 = 20
\text{at } 5 \text{ m}:\ 108 - 20\log_{10} 5 \approx 94 \text{ dB} @@ the spreading technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{twice the watts: only } +3 \text{ dB}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ten times the watts for +10

37eng4Why a sound system howls

Put the loudspeaker where the microphone can hear it. A sound at frequency f goes round the loop: microphone, gain, loudspeaker, air, microphone. Let G(f) be the loop’s gain at f, the product of every stage’s, the air included. If the turn round the loop is a whole number of turns, each trip adds to the last in step (37d2).

Howl technique: a frequency whose trips round the loop arrive in step grows without end if |G(f)| ≥ 1 and dies away if |G(f)| < 1. Proof: after n trips its size is |G(f)|ⁿ times the start, the terms of 15i’s geometric list, which grow without end for a ratio of at least 1 and go to 0 for less.

|G(2500)| = 1.12 \text{ in step} @@ a loop that howls at 2500 Hz
\text{a filter taking } 3 \text{ dB at } 2500 \text{ Hz only}:\ 1.12 \times 0.708 \approx 0.79 @@ 37h2, a narrow cut
\htmlClass{new}{|G| < 1:\ \text{the howl dies}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rest of the song is left alone

The loop gain is largest at whatever frequency the room and the gear favour, so a system howls at one note, not all at once. The cure is to cut that note narrowly, move the loudspeaker, or turn the whole loop down.

The accepted names

The ringing is reverberation, and the 60 dB time is RT60, the reverberation time; the soaking share is the absorption coefficient, and A is measured in sabins. Ceiling to floor is headroom and noise floor; keeping the level between them along a chain is gain staging. The howl is acoustic feedback, and |G| ≥ 1 in step is the Barkhausen condition; the narrow cut is a notch filter.

Who said it first

Wallace Clement Sabine found the 0.161 V/A rule in 1898 while fixing the lecture hall of Harvard’s Fogg Art Museum, and was then asked to design Boston’s Symphony Hall (1900). Heinrich Barkhausen gave the loop condition for oscillators in the 1920s.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each trip round the loop adds the sound again, so the total is 1 + G + G² + ⋯, and that always grows. So every system with a microphone near a loudspeaker howls.” Not allowed: 15i’s sum settles at 1/(1 − G) when |G| < 1; the system colours the sound but does not howl.
