10f · technique

Computing limits

Nobody plays the game every time. Limits are known to add, multiply and divide (10c). The value at the input is known not to count (10a's agreeing-near). So most limits are found by rewriting the expression into one you can simply substitute into. This step is the kit of rewrites: when each is licensed, what it does, and the tempting moves that look like rewrites and are not. It also extends the game in two directions rung 9 pointed to. One is inputs that grow past every bound, where 9g's fractions settle. The other is sequences, where 9·cb4's run of one, a half, a third and on is finally said to head somewhere.

Try to substitute. If the bottom comes out 0 and the top does too, the expression is hiding a common factor: rewrite it into something that agrees with it near the input, then substitute into that.

10f1The two licences, and the first move

Stands on 10c (substitution) and 10a5 (agreeing-near).

Every move in this step is licensed by one of two kinds of technique. Licence one, the limit techniques. Substitution says a polynomial, or a fraction of polynomials whose bottom is not 0 at w, heads to its value at w. Limits-add, limits-multiply and limits-divide combine limits you already have. Licence two, agreeing-near: two functions that agree on a neighbourhood of w have the same limits there, whatever they do at w. So the first move is always the same: substitute.

\lim_{x \to -1} \tfrac{x^3 + 2x}{x^2 + 1} @@ the bottom at −1 is 2, not 0: substitution applies
= \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(-1)^3 + 2(-1)}}{\htmlClass{new}{(-1)^2 + 1}} = \tfrac{-3}{2} @@ substitute; arithmetic

Three things can happen when you substitute into a fraction. A real over a bottom that is not 0: done, by substitution. A top that is not 0 over a bottom that is 0: the outputs pass every bound on at least one side, and there is no limit. (See 10e's no-side-at-0, and the line for passing every bound below.) Check each side. Both 0: not an answer but a signal. The top and the bottom are both 0 at w, so by 9g's factor both have the factor x − w hiding in them. Find it, cancel it (agreeing-near licenses the cancel, since it is legal everywhere but at w), and substitute again.

10f2Factor and cancel, through a hole

Stands on 10f1, 5h (factoring) and 9g (polynomial division).

\lim_{x \to 1} \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} @@ substitute: (1 − 1)/(1 − 1), both 0: a signal
= \lim_{x \to 1} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(x - 1)(x + 1)}}{x - 1} @@ difference-of-squares (5h) on the top
= \lim_{x \to 1}\,(x + 1) @@ cancel x − 1: the two agree at every x ≠ 1 (agreeing-near)
= \htmlClass{keep}{2} @@ substitution (or line-limit)
  
\lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{x^2 + x - 6}{x^2 - 4} = \lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(x + 3)(x - 2)}}{\htmlClass{new}{(x + 2)(x - 2)}} @@ both 0 at 2. Top by sum-and-product (product −6, sum 1); bottom by difference-of-squares
= \lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{x + 3}{x + 2} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac54} @@ cancel (agreeing-near); substitution: the bottom is 4 now
\lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{x^2 - 4}{x^2 - x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} \tfrac{x + 2}{x + 1} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac43} @@ 9g's example: it lacks the input 2, and heads to 4/3 there

When the factor is not visible, factor says it is there anyway, and 9g's polynomial-division finds the rest. x³ − 8 is 0 at 2, so x − 2 divides it. Long division gives x³ − 8 = (x − 2)(x² + 2x + 4). So lim_{x→2} (x³ − 8)/(x − 2) = lim_{x→2} (x² + 2x + 4) = 12. Watch the logic of the cancel each time. (x³ − 8)/(x − 2) and x² + 2x + 4 are not the same function: the first has no value at 2, the second has 12 there. They agree everywhere else, and that is all a limit needs.

10f3Roots: the conjugate, and a root’s own limit

Stands on 10f2, 8i (the conjugate) and 5g (one bottom).

A root needs a limit of its own before anything can be substituted into it. Root-limit technique: for reals w and c with w + c > 0, lim_{x→w} √(x + c) = √(w + c). Times the conjugate (8i): √(x + c) − √(w + c) = (x − w)/(√(x + c) + √(w + c)), so for x ≥ −c, |√(x + c) − √(w + c)| ≤ |x − w|/√(w + c). That is 10d's squeezed-by-distance with C = 1/√(w + c), on the neighbourhood of reach w + c, inside the domain. ∎ (10e's root-at-0 is the edge case, from the right.)

When a root sits in a 0/0, the hidden factor is behind it. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate: the same two terms with the sign between them changed. Difference-of-squares clears the root, and the factor shows itself. Multiplying by P/P is multiplying by 1 where P ≠ 0, so nothing changes but the look.

\lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x} @@ substitute: (2 − 2)/0, both 0
= \lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x} \cdot \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}} @@ times 1: √(x + 4) + 2 ≥ 2, never 0
= \lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(x + 4) - 4}}{x\,(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} @@ difference-of-squares on the top; leave the bottom as a product, since you expect to cancel
= \lim_{x \to 0} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2} @@ the top is x; cancel (agreeing-near)
= \tfrac{1}{2 + 2} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac14} @@ root-limit (c = 4, a = 0), limits-add, reciprocal-limit

And a fraction inside a fraction is the same story in a different costume: clear the inner bottoms (5g) and the factor appears.

\lim_{x \to 3} \tfrac{\frac1x - \frac13}{x - 3} = \lim_{x \to 3} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{\frac{3 - x}{3x}}}{x - 3} @@ one bottom 3x for the inner difference (fractions-of-rationals)
= \lim_{x \to 3} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{-(x - 3)}}{3x\,(x - 3)} @@ 3 − x = −(x − 3): the displacement written backwards (negative-of-a-sum)
= \lim_{x \to 3} \tfrac{-1}{3x} = \htmlClass{keep}{-\tfrac19} @@ cancel (agreeing-near); substitution

10f4Squeeze when you cannot rewrite

Stands on 10f3 and 10d (squeezed-to-0).

When there is no algebra to grab, 10d's squeezed-by-distance and squeezed-to-0 do the work. Bound the distance |f(x) − L| by something you know heads to 0. (x − 1)² · q(x), with 9a's q, has |(x − 1)² q(x)| ≤ (x − 1)², which heads to 0 at 1 by substitution. So it heads to 0 at 1, by squeezed-to-0. No rewrite could have found that: q has no formula to rewrite.

10f5Inputs that grow past every bound

Stands on 10f4, 8g (∞ in intervals) and 8e (counting passes any real).

9g said that far out, (2x + 1)/(x − 1) is within any 1/n of 2. That is a game too, with one change: there is no point w to be near, so you answer with a threshold instead of a reach. 8g wrote ∞ in intervals to say “no end on this side”; it gets one more job, inside a mark.

From here on, ∞ may also be written in the mark x → ∞, read “x grows without bound”, and −∞ in the mark x → −∞, read “x falls without bound”. There, as in 8g's intervals, ∞ is not a number. It says only that the inputs pass every real. It is never added, multiplied, compared, divided by, or put in the place of x.

From here on, let f be a function whose inputs and outputs are reals, defined far out: some interval (c, ∞) is a part of dom f. Then lim_{x→∞} f(x) = L means: ∀ε > 0 ∃N : ∀x ∈ dom f (x > N ⇒ |f(x) − L| < ε), with N a real. Read it “the limit of f(x), as x grows without bound, is L”, or “f heads to L far out”; N is called a threshold. lim_{x→−∞} f(x) = L is the same with (−∞, c) and x < N.

One-over-x-far-out technique: lim_{x→∞} 1/x = 0. Given ε, answer N = 1/ε: if x > 1/ε > 0 then 0 < 1/x < ε (positive-reciprocal on ℚ). ∎ That an answer exists for every ε is 8e's counting-passes-any-real at work. 10b's and 10c's proofs carry over with one change of words. Far-out-too technique: One-limit, limits-add, limits-subtract, limits-multiply, constant-factor, power-limit, reciprocal-limit, limits-divide and squeeze are true with x → ∞ in place of x → w. In each column, the window 0 < |x − w| < δ becomes x > N. The least of the reaches becomes the greatest of the thresholds (8b's max), so that x is past all of them. Here is limits-add, carried over:

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \text{call } N_1, N_2 \text{ for } f, g \text{ at } \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ each limit far out, at half the gap
N = \htmlClass{new}{\max(N_1, N_2, c)};\ \ \text{assume } x \gt N @@ the greatest: x is past every threshold, and in the domain
\begin{gathered}|(f + g)(x) - (L + M)|\\ \le |f(x) - L| + |g(x) - M| \lt \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare\end{gathered} @@ as limits-add's column: regroup, triangle, the two promises

With those, a fraction of polynomials far out is decided by its leading terms. Rational-functions-far-out technique: for polynomials p and q of degrees n and m, with leading coefficients a_n and b_m: if n = m, lim_{x→∞} p(x)/q(x) = a_n/b_m; if n < m, the limit is 0. Divide top and bottom by x^m (times 1, for x > 0). Every term but the leading ones becomes a constant times a power of 1/x, which heads to 0 by one-over-x-far-out and power-limit. Then limits-divide, since the bottom heads to b_m ≠ 0. ∎

\lim_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 5} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{3 + \frac{1}{x^2}}}{\htmlClass{new}{1 + \frac{5}{x^2}}} @@ times (1/x²)/(1/x²), which is 1 for x ≠ 0
= \tfrac{3 + 0}{1 + 0} = \htmlClass{keep}{3} @@ 1/x² = (1/x)(1/x) → 0 · 0 (one-over-x-far-out, limits-multiply); far-out-too's limits-add and limits-divide
\tfrac{2x + 1}{x - 1} = 2 + \tfrac{3}{x - 1}:\ \ x \gt \htmlClass{new}{1 + \tfrac{3}{\varepsilon}} \Rightarrow \left|\tfrac{3}{x - 1}\right| \lt \varepsilon @@ 9g's divide first, answered by hand: N = 1 + 3/ε. The limit is 2
\lim_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{5x + 2}{x^2 - 3} = \tfrac{0 + 0}{1 - 0} = \htmlClass{keep}{0} @@ divide by x²: the top's degree is less (rational-functions-far-out)

Big minus big is the trap. √(x² + x) − x has both pieces passing every bound, and it is tempting to call the difference 0. Rationalise instead.

\sqrt{x^2 + x} - x = \tfrac{(x^2 + x) - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + x} + x} = \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{x}}{\sqrt{x^2 + x} + x} @@ times the conjugate over itself; difference-of-squares; for x > 0 the bottom is positive
= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \frac1x} + 1} @@ divide top and bottom by x: for x > 0, √(x² + x) = x√(1 + 1/x) (8i: √(ab) = √a√b, √(x²) = |x| = x)
\left|\sqrt{1 + \tfrac1x} - 1\right| = \tfrac{1/x}{\sqrt{1 + \frac1x} + 1} \le \tfrac1x @@ the conjugate once more: so √(1 + 1/x) heads to 1 far out (squeeze in far-out-too, with one-over-x-far-out)
\lim_{x \to \infty}\big(\sqrt{x^2 + x} - x\big) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 1} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac12} @@ far-out-too's limits-add and reciprocal-limit. Not 0

One more tech, for running a limit far out back to a limit at a point. Swap-to-one-over-x technique: lim_{x→∞} f(x) = L exactly when lim_{t→0⁺} f(1/t) = L. Inputs x > N > 0 are exactly the 1/t with 0 < t < 1/N (positive-reciprocal on ℚ). So a threshold N answers one game exactly when the reach 1/N answers the other. ∎ A limit far out is a one-sided limit at 0 in disguise. The picture uses this to draw it on a finite screen, and 10·bio uses it to read a ceiling off a line.

Some outputs head nowhere because they grow. That needs words that are not a limit.

From here on, “f(x) passes every bound as x → ∞” means: ∀M ∃N : ∀x ∈ dom f (x > N ⇒ f(x) > M), with M and N reals. And “f(x) passes every bound as x → a⁺” means: ∀M ∃δ > 0 : ∀x ∈ dom f (a < x < a + δ ⇒ f(x) > M); likewise as x → a⁻ and as x → a, with those windows, and as x → −∞. “Passes below every bound” is the same with f(x) < M. These are statements about outputs, not limits: no L is claimed, and this ladder never writes them as a limit equal to ∞.

When f(x) heads to L far out, f is said to have the horizontal asymptote y = L. When f(x) passes every bound, or below every bound, as x → w from either side, f has the vertical asymptote x = w. Asymptote is from the Greek for “not falling together”. Passing every bound is also written as a limit equal to ∞, as in lim_{x→w⁺} f(x) = ∞, and called an infinite limit. The mark is shorthand for “passes every bound”. ∞ is not a number, and by passing-every-bound below, a function with an infinite limit has no limit in 10a's sense.

Passing-every-bound technique: if f(x) passes every bound (or below every bound) as x → w⁺, then f has no limit from the right at w; and likewise for the other sides and far out. Suppose it headed to L. Challenge with ε = 1 and call the reach. The bound M = |L| + 1 gives a reach of its own. An input in both windows has f(x) > |L| + 1 and |f(x) − L| < 1, so f(x) < L + 1 ≤ |L| + 1. ⊥. Refute. ∎ Now 9e's sentence is finished. 1/x passes every bound as x → 0⁺: for M > 0 answer δ = 1/M, by positive-reciprocal on ℚ; for M ≤ 0 any δ, since 1/x > 0. And 1/x passes below every bound as x → 0⁻. 9g's 1/(x − 1) does the same at 1. And x² passes every bound as x → ∞ (N = max(1, M), since x² ≥ x past 1), so it has no limit far out.

10f6Sequences, where 9·cb4 left them

Stands on 10f5 and 9·cb4 (sequences).

A sequence is a function on ℕ (9·cb4), so its inputs can only grow. The game is the one far out, with a whole-number threshold.

From here on, for a sequence a of reals, lim_{n→∞} a_n = L, also written a_n → L, means: ∀ε > 0 ∃N ∈ ℕ : ∀n ∈ ℕ (n ≥ N ⇒ |a_n − L| < ε). Read it “a_n heads to L”; the sequence converges to L, and L is its limit. The mark n → ∞ says that the input letter n runs through the numbers past every one.

One-limit's proof works here with n = max(N₁, N₂), so a sequence has at most one limit. The limit techniques carry over as far-out-too says. One-over-n technique: lim_{n→∞} 1/S(n) = 0. Given ε, 8e's everyday-counting-passes gives a number N ≠ 0 with 1/N < ε. For n ≥ N, S(n) > N, so 0 < 1/S(n) < 1/N < ε (positive-reciprocal on ℚ). ∎ That is the sentence 9·cb4 could not finish: the never-rising run of reals 1, ½, ⅓, ⋯ never stands still, and it heads to 0. Cut-offs technique: for a real x ≥ 0 with 8·cb5's cut-offs c_k, lim_{k→∞} c_k = x. 8·cb5's digit rule kept c_k ≤ x < c_k + 10^(−k), so |c_k − x| < 10^(−k) < 1/k for k ≠ 0 (since 10^k > k, by induction). Everyday-counting-passes gives N with 1/N < ε, and k ≥ N does it. ∎ So 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ⋯ heads to √2: 10d's numbers squeezed, said as a limit.

10f7One side at a time

Stands on 10f6, 10e (sides) and 8h (bars by cases).

An absolute value is two formulas glued where its inside is 0, and that is often where a limit is asked. Split by sides (10e), compute each, compare.

\tfrac{|x^2 - 1|}{x - 1} \text{ near } 1 @@ the inside x² − 1 changes sign at 1
x \gt 1:\ \ \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 \to \htmlClass{new}{2} @@ inside positive: the bars change nothing; difference-of-squares, cancel

10f9Running it backwards

Stands on 10f1 to 10f7.

To understand a tech, build something it has to undo. Want a fraction that is 0 over 0 at 4 and heads to 7 there? The simplified form must be 7 at 4, such as x + 3. Hide the factor x − 4 in top and bottom and expand: ((x + 3)(x − 4))/(x − 4) = (x² − x − 12)/(x − 4). Anyone who factors and cancels finds 7. Every 0/0 limit in this step was made that way, by someone who started from the answer.

Who said it first

In 10f.4.4, the textbook is the Marquis de l’Hôpital’s Analyse des infiniment petits (1696), and the sender is Johann Bernoulli; the rule is known as l’Hôpital’s rule. 8e’s counting-passes-any-real is the Archimedean property.

Accepted names: far-out-too is the limit laws at infinity; a sequence that heads to L converges to L; the row y = L and the column x = w of 10f’s lines are the horizontal and vertical asymptotes.
