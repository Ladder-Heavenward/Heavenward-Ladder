Does Water Always Flow Smoothly?

Does water always flow smoothly? Watch the Fraser River where it squeezes through the canyon at Hell’s Gate: the water boils, folds and spins in eddies inside eddies, smaller and smaller, down to swirls too small to see. The rule for moving water has been written down for two hundred years. It is a rate rule in space and time of exactly this rung’s kind: the water carries its own speed along, as on 33e, its speed spreads like heat, as on 33a, and a pressure, a potential of 33j, keeps it from piling up. Nobody knows whether that rule, started from a smooth flow in open space, always stays smooth, or whether somewhere a swirl can tighten until its speed passes every bound in a finite time. What makes the question hard is that the rule’s own books, its energy, are not strong enough to forbid it: zoom in on a small region, and the energy that could stop a blow-up there shrinks with the zoom. In 2000 the question was named one of seven prize problems of mathematics. It is still open. In 2022 a proof, much of it done by a computer that bounded every one of its own roundings, showed that the rule with the stickiness taken away does blow up, at a wall. This page sets out what the rung’s tools can say about both.

The rule of water carries its speed, spreads it, and holds it to a pressure found from the speed by a still-state rule with a source. Energy is only ever lost, but zooming in weakens the energy bound in space, though not in the plane. In one dimension, spreading turns a breaking rule into the heat rule, so it never breaks. In space, whether it ever breaks is open; without the stickiness, at a wall, it does.

33now1The Rule of Flowing Water

Let u(x, t) be the water’s velocity, a vector at each point of space, p its pressure, ρ its density and ν its stickiness per density. Water barely squeezes, so the spread of u is 0. The measured laws of motion and of stickiness, held in trust, give

\(u_t + (u\cdot\nabla)\,u = -\tfrac1\rho\nabla p + \nu\,\Delta u,\qquad \nabla\cdot u = 0.\)

Read it piece by piece. (u · ∇)u is the speed carrying itself along, as on 33e. νΔu is the heat rule for each part of the speed. And −∇p/ρ is a push from a potential. Pressure-from-speed technique: the pressure of a flow with no spread is fixed, at each instant, by the speed: Δp = −ρ Σᵢⱼ (∂ᵢuⱼ)(∂ⱼuᵢ).

\nabla\cdot u_t = (\nabla\cdot u)_t = 0,\ \ \nabla\cdot\Delta u = \Delta(\nabla\cdot u) = 0 @@ take the spread of each term; the order of slopes swaps (28d)
\nabla\cdot\big((u\cdot\nabla)u\big) = \textstyle\sum_{i,j}\partial_i\big(u_j\,\partial_ju_i\big) = \sum_{i,j}(\partial_iu_j)(\partial_ju_i) + u\cdot\nabla(\nabla\cdot u) @@ the product rule
\textstyle\sum_{i,j}(\partial_iu_j)(\partial_ju_i) = -\tfrac1\rho\,\Delta p @@ the spread of the whole rule, with ∇ · u = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta p = -\rho\textstyle\sum_{i,j}(\partial_iu_j)(\partial_ju_i)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a still-state rule with a source (33j)

So the pressure is the speed’s potential, found everywhere at once by a still-state rule, as gravity’s potential is found from mass. That is why the rule is hard: what happens at one point depends at once on the speed everywhere.

33now2Energy Is Only Ever Lost

Let the water fill a closed box and be still at its walls, u = 0 there. Its energy per density is E(t) = ½∫|u|².

Energy-loss technique: E′(t) = −ν∫|∇u|², so the energy of the water never grows.

E^{\prime} = \textstyle\int u\cdot u_t @@ the slope under the total, as on 33c4
\textstyle\int u\cdot(u\cdot\nabla)u = \int (u\cdot\nabla)\tfrac12|u|^2 = -\int \tfrac12|u|^2\,\nabla\cdot u = 0 @@ parts (16e) in each direction; walls at 0 and no spread
\textstyle\int u\cdot\nabla p = -\int p\,\nabla\cdot u = 0 @@ parts again
\textstyle\int u\cdot\Delta u = -\int |\nabla u|^2 @@ parts once more: the sum of the squares of all nine slopes
\htmlClass{new}{E^{\prime}(t) = -\nu\textstyle\int |\nabla u|^2 \le 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the carrying and the pressure move energy about, and only stickiness changes it

The string of 33c4 kept its energy exactly. Water loses it, into heat, wherever its speed changes sharply from place to place. A total that never grows sounds like a guard against blowing up. The next footing shows why it is not enough.

33now3Zooming In

Zoom technique: if u, p solve the rule, so do u_λ(x, t) = λu(λx, λ²t) and p_λ = λ²p(λx, λ²t), for every λ > 0. In space the zoomed energy is E/λ; in the plane it is E.

\partial_t u_\lambda = \lambda^3(u_t),\ \ (u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda = \lambda^3\big((u\cdot\nabla)u\big),\ \ \Delta u_\lambda = \lambda^3(\Delta u) @@ the chain rule: each slope in x brings λ, the slope in t brings λ²
\tfrac1\rho\nabla p_\lambda = \lambda^3\big(\tfrac1\rho\nabla p\big),\ \ \nabla\cdot u_\lambda = \lambda^2(\nabla\cdot u) = 0 @@ the same count
\text{every term is } \lambda^3 \text{ times the old one, read at } (\lambda x, \lambda^2t) @@ so the rule holds for u_λ
\textstyle\int |u_\lambda|^2\,dx = \lambda^2\int |u(\lambda x)|^2\,dx = \lambda^{2 - d}\int |u|^2 @@ put y = λx: dx = λ⁻ᵈ dy in d dimensions (28i)
\htmlClass{new}{E_\lambda = E/\lambda \text{ in space},\ \ E_\lambda = E \text{ in the plane}} \quad \blacksquare @@ d = 3 and d = 2

Now picture a blow-up: a swirl that tightens into a smaller and smaller region, faster and faster. Zoom in on it, with λ large, and it looks like an ordinary flow. In the plane, the zoomed flow has the same energy, and the energy bound is as strong at every zoom: that is the heart of why flows in the plane are proved to stay smooth. In space, the zoomed flow has energy E/λ, as small as you like. The energy that could forbid a blow-up at a small scale is spent before you get there. The energy bound is too weak at exactly the scales that matter.

33now4Where Spreading Wins

In one dimension the question has an answer. Take the breaking rule of 33e3 and add spreading: u_t + uu_x = νu_xx. Without ν, 33e3’s breaking-time technique broke every start that falls somewhere. With ν, something better than a bound happens: the rule turns into the heat rule.

Heat-tames-breaking technique: if φ > 0 solves φ_t = νφ_xx, then u = −2νφ_x/φ solves u_t + uu_x = νu_xx. Since every such φ is smooth for t > 0, so is u, for all time.

u = -2\nu\,\psi_x,\ \ \psi = \ln\varphi @@ the log of a positive answer
\psi_t = \tfrac{\varphi_t}{\varphi} = \nu\tfrac{\varphi_{xx}}{\varphi} = \nu\big(\psi_{xx} + \psi_x^2\big) @@ the heat rule, and φ_xx/φ = ψ_xx + ψ_x² by the chain rule
\psi_{xt} = \nu\psi_{xxx} + 2\nu\psi_x\psi_{xx} @@ the slope along x
u_t = -2\nu\psi_{xt} = \nu u_{xx} - u\,u_x @@ with u = −2νψ_x: −4ν²ψ_xψ_xx = −u u_x
\htmlClass{new}{u_t + u\,u_x = \nu u_{xx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ φ from 33b3’s spreading bell is smooth and above 0 for t > 0, and so is u

So in one dimension spreading always beats steepening. The front that would have broken at t = 1/m becomes a steep but smooth ramp of width about ν over the jump, and moves at the jump speed of 33e4. In space there is no such trick. Nobody has found a change of knobs that turns the rule of water into a rule known to stay smooth, and the zoom says why the energy cannot do the job alone.

33now5What Is Shown, and What Is Open

The prize question of 2000, in the rung’s words: start the rule of water in open space with a smooth speed that has no spread and fades fast far away, with no outside force. Is there always an answer that stays smooth for all time, with finite energy? Or is there one start whose smooth answer stops existing at some finite time, its speed passing every bound? A proof either way wins the prize. Nobody has one.

What is known. In the plane, every such start stays smooth forever. In space, a smooth answer exists for a while, and stays smooth for all time when the start is small enough. A weaker kind of answer, one that keeps the books but may not have slopes, exists for all time from every start, since the 1930s; whether it is smooth, or the only one, is not known. And a rule that keeps the energy books of this one but averages its carrying term differently was shown in 2016 to blow up: so any proof of smoothness must use more than the energy.

Without stickiness, ν = 0, the rule is older. In 2022 a proof was posted that, in a pipe, a smooth swirling flow with no stickiness blows up at the wall in finite time, its slopes passing every bound. The proof finds a flow that keeps its shape while it shrinks, as the zoom technique allows, by computer, and then proves that the true answer stays near it. The second half is a computer proof: every number the machine used was carried with a width that bounds its roundings.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, proved

33i reported a grid answer with a width found by halving the gap: anexact, with a width earned by comparing runs. A computer proof goes one step further. Every number is kept as a pair, a lower and an upper bound, and every sum and product is done on the pairs so that the true value is certain to lie between them.

A number carried with bounds on its roundings is a cloud whose width is proved, and a proof can be built from such clouds.

The blow-up of 2022 was found as a cloud, a shape seen on a fine grid. It was proved by showing that the cloud’s width, computed exactly in the pairs, is smaller than the gap the argument needs. The guess and the proof are the same picture; the proof carries its width.

A grid run on a laptop at Simon Fraser and a run with bounded roundings agree on a blow-up time of 0.003506, the second with a width of 10⁻⁶. Which would you print in a proof, and which in a forecast? Would you trust either for the rule with stickiness put back?

The accepted names

The rule of flowing water is the Navier–Stokes equations for an incompressible fluid, ν the kinematic viscosity, and the rule with ν = 0 is the Euler equations. The pressure-from-speed technique is the pressure Poisson equation. The energy-loss technique is the energy equality (for weak answers, the energy inequality). The zoom is the scaling symmetry, and the energy is supercritical in three dimensions and critical in two. The heat-tames-breaking technique is the Cole–Hopf transformation of the viscous Burgers equation. The weaker answers are Leray–Hopf weak solutions. The prize problem is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems, worth one million dollars. A flow that keeps its shape while it shrinks is self-similar. Computing with pairs of bounds is interval arithmetic.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler wrote the rule without stickiness in 1757. Claude-Louis Navier added stickiness in 1822, and George Stokes derived it properly in 1845. Jean Leray built the weak answers in 1934 and asked whether they blow up. Olga Ladyzhenskaya proved the plane smooth in 1959 and in the books of the 1960s. Eberhard Hopf (1950) and Julian Cole (1951) found the change of knobs. Charles Fefferman wrote the official statement of the prize problem in 2000. Terence Tao showed in 2016 that an averaged form of the rule blows up. Thomas Hou and Guo Luo found the blow-up at a wall on computers in 2014, and Jiajie Chen and Thomas Hou posted the computer-assisted proof in 2022. Tarek Elgindi proved a blow-up for the rule without stickiness, with starts a little rougher than smooth and no wall, in 2021.
