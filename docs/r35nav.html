How Does a Repair Craft Catch a Broken Satellite?

How does a repair craft catch a broken satellite? Two craft in nearly the same orbit, a few hundred metres apart, do not move as two cars on a road do. Push toward the target, and the chaser rises and falls behind; push up, and it loops round and comes back. This page sees the chaser’s motion from the target, in a frame like 28·nav’s; finds that close to the target the motion obeys a linear system, as 27·cb24 solved; finds why pushing forward ends up behind, as 21·nav2 found for whole orbits; finds a loop that comes back to the same place; and matches a tumbling target’s spin before grabbing it, with 27·phys.

Seen from the target, close by, the chaser’s height x and lead y obey x″ = 3n²x + 2ny′ and y″ = −2nx′, with n the orbit’s turning rate. A forward push of Δv lifts the chaser and leaves it falling behind 3Δv per second of orbit; after one orbit it is 6πΔv/n behind. A push straight up makes a closed loop that returns in one orbit. To grab a tumbling target, the chaser circles with it at its spin.

35nav1Motion Seen From the Target

Terry flies a repair craft. The target satellite is in a circular orbit, turning about the earth at n radians a second; for a low orbit of 92 minutes, n = 2π/5520 ≈ 1.14 × 10⁻³ per second.

From here on, on this page, the chaser’s place is given in a frame that rides with the target (28·nav): x straight up, away from the earth; y forward, along the target’s path. The frame turns once an orbit.

35nav2Near the Target the Motion Is Linear

Write the earth’s pull and the turning frame’s pushes (27·phys) for the chaser, and keep only the parts in step with x and y, since both are tiny next to the orbit’s size. What is left is a linear system with fixed numbers, of the kind 27·cb24 solves.

x\prime\prime = 3n^2 x + 2n\,y\prime @@ up: the pull weakens with height, and the turning push
\htmlClass{new}{y\prime\prime = -2n\,x\prime} @@ forward: only the turning push

Near-target technique: within a few kilometres of a target in a circular orbit, the chaser’s height and lead obey a linear system with fixed numbers set by the orbit’s turning rate n; every move is a sum of its few shapes.

35nav3Push Up, End Up Behind

From the target’s place, give a push Δv forward, along y. The solution of the system is:

x(t) = \dfrac{2\Delta v}{n}\,(1 - \cos nt) @@ it rises
y(t) = \dfrac{4\Delta v}{n}\sin nt - 3\Delta v\,t @@ and drifts back
\text{highest: } \dfrac{4\Delta v}{n} \approx 350 \text{ m} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after one orbit: } y = -\dfrac{6\pi\Delta v}{n} \approx -1\,650 \text{ m}} @@ a forward push ends behind

A forward push raises the chaser into a slightly wider, slower orbit, so it falls behind, 1.65 km per orbit for 0.1 m/s. To catch up with a target ahead, a chaser pushes backward and drops below it, as 21·nav2’s supply craft did over a whole orbit.

35nav4A Loop That Comes Back to the Same Place

Instead, give a push Δv straight up, along x.

x(t) = \dfrac{\Delta v}{n}\sin nt @@ 
y(t) = \dfrac{2\Delta v}{n}\,(\cos nt - 1) @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a closed oval, about 88 m up and down and 350 m back: home after one orbit}} @@ no drift

A push straight up, with no forward part, sends the chaser round a closed oval that returns it to its start each orbit. Crews park a chaser on such a loop round a target, because if its engines fail it keeps circling at a safe distance instead of drifting into the target. On real flights, the tracked loops close as the system says, to within a few metres.

35nav5Matching a Spinning Target Before Grabbing It

A broken satellite often tumbles. Spin is kept (27·phys), so it keeps tumbling at a steady rate until something stops it. A handle 2 m from its spin axis, tumbling at 3° a second, moves at 2 × 0.052 ≈ 0.10 m/s, round a circle.

\omega = 3° \text{ a second} \approx 0.052 \text{ per second} @@ 
\text{handle speed} = \omega r \approx 0.10 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the chaser circles at } \omega \text{ to hold the handle still in its own view}} @@ then closes in

Before grabbing a tumbling target, the chaser must turn with it, so the handle stands still in its view; only then can it close the last metre gently. After the grab, the joined craft’s spin is the target’s spin shared over both, and the chaser’s thrusters remove it slowly.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The near-target system is the Clohessy-Wiltshire equations, or Hill’s equations; the riding frame is the local vertical, local horizontal frame; the parking oval is a safety ellipse; and the whole is rendezvous and proximity operations.

Who said it first

George Hill derived the equations for the moon’s motion in 1878; W. H. Clohessy and R. S. Wiltshire applied them to rendezvous in 1960 (not yet revisited for the dates).
