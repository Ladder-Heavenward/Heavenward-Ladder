Why Do a Few Words Do Most of the Work?

Why do a few words do most of the work? Count the words in any long book. A handful, the, of, and, to, a, make up a big share of everything written, while most of the book’s different words turn up only once or twice. The pattern is the same in English, in Japanese, in the texts of the web, and in computer code. This page counts words by rank, finds that count times rank stays about the same, draws the line on log paper with 17 and 31, finds that new words keep coming as the text grows, and asks what such a power law does and does not explain.

Rank a text’s words from commonest down. The count of the word at rank r is about C/r: the second commonest appears half as often as the first, the third a third as often. On log paper, a line of slope −1. Adding up 1/r grows only as a log, so the top hundred words cover about half of any text, and new words keep turning up roughly as the square root of its length.

38comp2Count Times Rank Stays About the Same

1 \times 7\% = 7\%,\ \ 2 \times 3.5\% = 7\%,\ \ 3 \times 2.9\% \approx 8.7\% @@ 
10 \times 0.9\% = 9\%,\ \ 100 \times 0.07\% = 7\% @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{count} \approx \dfrac{C}{r}} @@ about 7% of the text over the rank

Rank-count technique: in a long text, a word’s count is close to a fixed number over its rank; so the share of the text covered by the top R words grows only as the log of R.

38comp3A Line on Log Paper

\log(\text{count}) = \log C - \log r @@ a straight line of slope −1 (17), fitted by least squares (31n)
\text{share of the top } R = \dfrac{1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac1R}{1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac1V} \approx \dfrac{\ln R + 0.58}{\ln V + 0.58} @@ sums against areas (22f)
\htmlClass{new}{V = 30\,000,\ R = 100:\ \dfrac{5.2}{10.9} \approx 0.47} @@ the top hundred words: about half the text

Terry plots his counts against rank on log paper: the points lie close to a straight line of slope −1 from rank 1 to several thousand, then droop where words appear only once or twice. That is why a search engine can ignore the commonest hundred words and save half its index.

38comp4New Words Keep Coming as the Text Grows

Read on through the collection and count how many different words have appeared. The number never stops growing; it just slows.

V(n) \approx K\,n^{\beta},\ \ \beta \approx 0.5 @@ a power law (17)
K = 30:\ n = 10^6 \Rightarrow V \approx 30\,000;\ \ n = 4 \times 10^6 \Rightarrow V \approx 60\,000 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four times the text, twice the different words}} @@ names, rare words, new coinages

Any text, however long, still holds words not yet seen, so a program reading language must always be ready for a word it does not know.

38comp5What a Power Law Does Not Explain

Count-times-rank is so common that it is tempting to think it reveals something deep about language. But a monkey typing letters and spaces at random also produces “words” whose counts fall as roughly a power of their rank, because short strings come up far more often than long ones.

\text{27 keys, space with chance } \tfrac{1}{27}:\ \text{each extra letter makes a word about 26 times rarer} @@ 
\text{and there are 26 times as many words of that length} @@ so rank and count trade off as a power
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a power law fits; nothing about meaning is involved}} @@

A straight line on log paper fits many processes that share little, so the line alone does not show why words are used as they are. It is still useful: it tells a programmer how big a word list must be to cover most of a text, and how much rarer the next words will be.

Accepted names

Count as C/r is Zipf’s law; the growth of new words is Heaps’ law. A word list for a search engine to ignore is a stop list; the sum 1 + ½ + … + 1/V is a harmonic number.

Who said it first

George Kingsley Zipf published the rank law in 1935 and 1949; Harold Heaps the growth of new words in 1978; George Miller showed in 1957 that random typing gives a similar law (not yet revisited for the dates).
