35·chem · chemistry

How do molecules turn and shake?

How do molecules turn and shake? A molecule of carbon monoxide is two atoms joined by a bond. It can spin end over end, and its bond can stretch and squeeze like a spring. No one can watch it do either. Yet chemists measure its bond length to a thousandth of a nanometre and its stiffness to a few parts in a thousand, from the light it takes in. What makes the question hard is that the light is taken in only at certain sharp colours, and the colours have to be read backwards into the motion that made them. The page before found that a held wave has only certain energies. Here a turning molecule has energies that grow like a whole number times the next one, and a shaking molecule has an even ladder of them. Light is taken in when one lump of it carries the molecule up exactly one step. The turning steps fall in the microwaves and the shaking steps in the infrared, which is why the gas of the air warms the earth. And a lump of light with more energy than a bond holds can break the bond outright, which starts the brown haze over a city on a sunny afternoon.

A turning molecule has energies in proportion to J times J plus one, so its microwave lines are evenly spaced, and their spacing gives the bond length. A shaking molecule has an even ladder of energies, and its infrared line gives the bond’s stiffness. One lump of light carries one step, and a lump with more energy than a bond breaks it.

35chem1A molecule that turns

27·phys’s spin-energy technique: a body turning at ω about an axis has energy ½Iω², with spin L = Iω; for two atoms a distance r apart, I = μr², where μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). In the wave law the spin is held to steps: the shapes of a wave over the directions of a sphere, the same that give hydrogen its orbitals, have L² = J(J + 1)ħ² for J = 0, 1, 2, …, with 2J + 1 shapes at each J. That is stated here from 35·phys.6, not proved.

Turning-lines technique: a molecule of two atoms turns with energies J(J + 1)ħ²/(2I), so the light it takes in stepping from J to J + 1 has frequency 2B(J + 1), with B = h/(8π²I): evenly spaced lines, 2B apart.

E_J = \tfrac{L^2}{2I} = \tfrac{J(J+1)\hbar^2}{2I} = hB\,J(J+1) @@ ½Iω² = L²/(2I), with L² held to steps; B = h/(8π²I)
E_{J+1} - E_J = 2hB(J + 1) @@ (J + 1)(J + 2) − J(J + 1) = 2(J + 1)
\text{CO, } J = 0 \to 1:\ \ 115.271\ \text{GHz},\ \text{so } B = 57.636\ \text{GHz} @@ measured; the next lines, 230.54 and 345.81 GHz, are 2B and 3 · 2B
I = \tfrac{h}{8\pi^2B} = 1.456\times10^{-46}\ \text{kg m}^2,\ \ \mu = \tfrac{12.000\times15.995}{27.995}\ \text{u} = 6.856\ \text{u} @@ u = 1.6605 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
\htmlClass{new}{r = \sqrt{I/\mu} = 0.1131\ \text{nm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the length of the bond, from one line

Radio telescopes map the cold gas between the stars by this one line of carbon monoxide, because the molecule turns slowly enough at 10 K to keep it lit, and the gas it marks is where stars are born. The line for HCl’s turning lies far higher, about 21 cm⁻¹ apart in the infrared’s units, because the light hydrogen atom makes I small.

35chem2A molecule that shakes

Near its resting length a bond pulls back like a spring of stiffness κ, and 24·phys’s even-time technique gives it the frequency f = (1/2π)√(κ/μ). 35·phys’s ladder technique gives its energies.

Shaking-line technique: a bond of stiffness κ has energies (v + ½)hf, with f = (1/2π)√(κ/μ), and takes in light of frequency f stepping from v = 0 to v = 1. Infrared chemists write a frequency in waves per centimetre, ν̃ = f/c.

\tilde\nu = 2886\ \text{cm}^{-1} @@ the measured infrared line of HCl
\mu = \tfrac{1.008\times34.969}{35.977}\ \text{u} = 0.9796\ \text{u} @@ hydrogen-1 and chlorine-35
\kappa = \mu\,(2\pi c\tilde\nu)^2 = 481\ \text{N/m} @@ the even-time frequency, turned round
\tfrac{hc\tilde\nu}{kT} = 13.9 \text{ at } 298\ \text{K},\ \ e^{-13.9} \approx 9\times10^{-7} @@ the energy-share technique (30·phys): nearly every molecule sits on the lowest rung
\htmlClass{new}{\text{DCl: same } \kappa,\ \tilde\nu \approx 2886\sqrt{\mu_{\text{H}}/\mu_{\text{D}}} = 2070\ \text{cm}^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ swap hydrogen for its heavy twin: the spring is the same bond; measured, 2091

The next step up, v = 0 to v = 2, should be at twice 2886. It is at 5668, short by 104. A bond is not a perfect spring: stretched far, it pulls back less and less, and at the top it breaks. So the rungs crowd together as they climb, and end. The ladder is a rule for small shakes.

35chem3Microwave and infrared

A lump of light carries one unit of turning. So, as a law measured in every spectrum of two atoms, light is taken in only on steps of J by ±1, and of v by ±1 for a bond near rest. In the infrared, one lump raises v by 1 and moves J up or down by 1 at the same time.

Band technique: the infrared band of a molecule of two atoms is a comb of lines 2B apart on each side of the shake’s frequency, with a gap at the middle, and the lines are brightest at the J with the largest share (2J + 1)e^(−hcBJ(J+1)/kT). The gap is where no line can be: J may not stay put.

\tilde\nu_{J \to J+1} = 2886 + 2B(J + 1),\ \ \tilde\nu_{J \to J-1} = 2886 - 2BJ @@ the two sides of the comb; B = 10.59 cm⁻¹
2B = 21.2\ \text{cm}^{-1} @@ the spacing of the teeth
\tfrac{kT}{hcB} = 19.6 \text{ at } 298\ \text{K} @@ the energy-share technique, with 2J + 1 shapes at each J
(2J + 1)e^{-J(J+1)/19.6}:\ \ 1,\ 2.71,\ 3.68,\ 3.79,\ 3.24 \text{ for } J = 0 \text{ to } 4 @@ the shares, against J = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{brightest at } J = 3 \text{ on each side}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a warmer gas moves the brightest teeth outward

Carbon dioxide has a bend at 667 cm⁻¹, a wavelength of 15.0 μm. The ground, at 288 K, glows most strongly near 10 μm, and much of its glow lies across the bend. Nitrogen and oxygen, which make up almost all of the air, are two like atoms: their stretch does not shift any charge, light cannot take hold of it, and they let the glow through. The few molecules in ten thousand that can bend are the ones that hold the heat in.

35chem4Light that breaks bonds

17·phys found that a lump of light gives its whole energy to one electron. In a molecule it can give it to one bond. If the lump carries more than the bond holds, the bond breaks; if less, no brightness will break it.

Break-threshold technique: light breaks a bond holding D per mole only when its wavelength is below N_A hc / D. One mole of lumps carries N_A hc/λ.

\text{ON–O bond in NO}_2:\ D = 300.5\ \text{kJ/mol} @@ measured
\lambda_{\max} = \tfrac{N_Ahc}{D} = \tfrac{(6.022\times10^{23})(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})}{3.005\times10^{5}}\ \text{m} = 398\ \text{nm} @@ the break-threshold technique
\text{NO}_2 + \text{light} \to \text{NO} + \text{O};\ \ \text{O} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{O}_3 @@ violet and ultraviolet sunlight, below 398 nm, frees an oxygen atom, and it joins O₂
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ozone at ground level, by afternoon}} \quad \blacksquare @@ from car exhaust, which makes the NO₂, and sun

Over the San Joaquin Valley on a still summer day, the NO₂ of the morning traffic, brown and visible, turns to ozone by mid-afternoon, which is invisible and harms lungs. The reaction waits for light below 398 nm. Red light, however bright, does nothing to it.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The turning molecule is the rigid rotor, B its rotational constant, and the lines its rotational, or microwave, spectrum. The shaking-line technique treats the bond as a harmonic oscillator; its line is the fundamental, the line at 5668 cm⁻¹ the first overtone, and the crowding of the rungs anharmonicity. The steps of ±1 are selection rules. The comb of 35·chem.3 is a rotation–vibration band, its two sides the P and R branches. Molecules that take in the ground’s glow are greenhouse gases. The break-threshold technique is the photochemical threshold; NO₂ breaking in light is photolysis, and the brown haze is photochemical smog.

Who said it first

Niels Bjerrum explained in 1912 that the infrared bands of gases come from turning and shaking together. The band of HCl was resolved into its teeth by E. S. Imes in 1919. Carbon monoxide’s line at 115 GHz was first seen from space in 1970, by Robert Wilson, Keith Jefferts and Arno Penzias. John Tyndall measured in 1859 that carbon dioxide and water vapour, and not nitrogen or oxygen, take in heat rays. Arie Haagen-Smit showed in Pasadena in 1952 that the smog of Los Angeles is made by sunlight acting on exhaust.
