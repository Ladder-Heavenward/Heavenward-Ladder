Why Is It Hard to Breathe on a Mountain?

Why is it hard to breathe on a mountain? High up, every breath feels thin, climbers walk a few steps and stop, and on the highest summits most people need bottled oxygen. Yet the air up there is still about a fifth oxygen, just as at sea level. What falls is the air’s push. This page finds how pressure falls with height from the weight of the air above, gets a fixed fraction per step up as in 17j, finds the scale height, shows what that does to the oxygen a lung can take in, and finds when a straight line is close enough, with 23’s matching polynomials.

Each thin layer of air holds up the weight of all the air above it, so the push drops by that layer’s weight going up. Air’s density is in step with its push (9·chem), so the drop is a fixed share of the push per metre: pressure falls by e every scale height, about 8 km. Oxygen keeps its fifth share, but its push falls with the rest.

23bio1Each Layer of Air Holds Up the Layers Above

Evita is a mountain doctor on an expedition. Take a thin layer of air, of thickness dh and one square metre across. The push from below must hold up the push from above plus the layer’s own weight.

From here on, on this page, p is the air’s pressure at height h, ρ its density, and T its temperature in kelvin, taken as fixed at a middle value of 270 K.

dp = -\rho\, g\, dh @@ weight of the layer, per square metre
\rho = \dfrac{pM}{RT} @@ the gas law (9·chem); M = 0.029 kg per mole for air
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{dp}{dh} = -\dfrac{Mg}{RT}\,p} @@ the drop is in step with the push itself

23bio2Pressure Falls by the Same Fraction for Each Step Up

A quantity whose slope is a fixed multiple of itself grows or decays by the same fraction over equal steps (17j). Here it decays.

p(h) = p_0\, e^{-h/H},\ \ H = \dfrac{RT}{Mg} @@ growth and decay (17j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each } H \text{ of height divides the pressure by } e \approx 2.72} @@

Scale-height technique: in air at a steady temperature, pressure falls by the factor e for every scale height RT/(Mg) of climb, so it halves every 0.69 scale heights.

23bio3The Scale Height

H = \dfrac{8.314 \times 270}{0.029 \times 9.81} \approx 7\,900 \text{ m} @@ about 8 km
\text{half height} = H\ln 2 \approx 5.5 \text{ km} @@ half the air is below 5.5 km
\htmlClass{new}{\text{summit at } 8.85 \text{ km}:\ 101.3\,e^{-8.85/7.9} \approx 33 \text{ kPa}} @@ about a third of sea level

On the highest summit, a research team in 1981 carried a barometer to the top and read 253 millimetres of mercury, 33.7 kPa: what this count gives, to within the spread of the day’s weather.

23bio4Oxygen Keeps Its Share, but Its Push Falls

Oxygen is 20.9% of the air at every height. What drives it into the blood is its own share of the push. In the lungs, the air is also wet: water vapour at body heat pushes 6.3 kPa, and takes its place first.

\text{sea level: } 0.209 \times (101.3 - 6.3) \approx 19.9 \text{ kPa} @@ 
\text{summit: } 0.209 \times (33.7 - 6.3) \approx 5.7 \text{ kPa} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{under a third of the push that drives oxygen into the blood}} @@ the wet air takes a bigger share up high

On a mountain the air is just as rich in oxygen, but the push of that oxygen falls with the rest of the air, so the blood takes up less with each breath. The body answers over weeks by breathing harder and making more red cells; a climber who goes up too fast gets mountain sickness.

23bio5Near the Ground, a Straight Line Is Close Enough

For small heights, the curve e^(−h/H) is close to its matching straight line (23a). How close? 23b bounds the error by the next term.

e^{-h/H} \approx 1 - \dfrac{h}{H} @@ the matching line (23a)
\dfrac{h}{H} \approx 0.2:\ \ e^{-0.2} \approx 0.819,\ \ 1 - 0.2 = 0.8 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{error} \le \tfrac12 (h/H)^2 = 0.02} @@ the next term bounds the error (23b)

Up to a couple of kilometres, pressure falls about one eighth for each kilometre; higher, the straight line overshoots and the curve must be used. A pilot’s altimeter is a barometer read through the curve, and is reset to the day’s ground pressure before every landing.

Accepted names

p₀e^(−h/H) is the barometric formula and H the scale height. A gas’s share of the push is its partial pressure, and that the shares add to the whole is Dalton’s law. Too little oxygen push is hypoxia; the body’s answer is acclimatisation.

Who said it first

The summit barometer reading of 253 mmHg was taken by the 1981 American Medical Research Expedition to Everest and published by John West and colleagues in 1983.
