∞49 · newsflash

Can One Shape Tile the Plane Only Without Repeating?

Can one shape tile the plane only without repeating? A floor of square tiles repeats: slide it one tile to the right, or one tile up, and it lands on itself. Every tiling anyone laid for centuries did the same, and in 1961 it was guessed that this always happens: any set of tiles that covers the plane at all can cover it with a repeat. The guess was attractive because it would give a machine a way to decide whether a set of tiles covers the plane. Rung 41 showed that some questions have no such machine, and in 1966 tiling was shown to be one of them, by building tiles whose only coverings copy the run of any machine; so some sets of tiles cover the plane and never repeat. The first such set had more than twenty thousand tiles. The count fell for decades, to two in the 1970s: a kite and a dart, with rules for how their edges meet, in proportions that are not a fraction, so no repeat can hold them. Could one tile do it alone, with no rules beyond fitting together? In late 2022 a hobbyist found one, a shape of eight kites called the hat, and with three mathematicians proved it in 2023. The hat needs its mirror image as well. Two months later the same team found one that does not: the spectre. The newsflash is settled. What is left is how simple such a tile can be.

A tiling kept by two slides in different directions has a lattice of slides, ℤu + ℤv. If every set that tiles also tiles with a lattice, two searches decide tiling; none does, since machines can be built into tiles (1966). Kites and darts: substitution (k, d) ↦ (2k + d, k + d), so kites : darts → φ, not a fraction, so no lattice. The hat: eight kites, grouped into four kinds of cluster that group again without end, so no slide keeps it. The spectre: one shape, no mirror needed. Here φ (little phi) means 23j's ratio, (1 + √5)/2, not 2e's condition.

inf49.1A Repeat Has a Lattice

A tiling covers the plane with copies of finitely many shapes, the tiles, moved by the moves of 20h, overlapping only along their edges. A slide that carries a tiling onto itself is a period. The periods form a group under doing one after another, a part of 20h’s group of slides. A tiling is periodic when it has two periods in different directions, and a set of tiles is aperiodic when it tiles the plane and no tiling by it has any period at all.

Repeat-lattice technique: the periods of a tiling have a shortest nonzero length δ; if they include two in different directions, they are exactly the whole-number sums xu + yv of two of them, u and v. A slide shorter than every tile’s width carries each tile to a tile overlapping it, so to itself, and no slide other than 0 carries a bounded shape to itself; so short slides are not periods. Choose u a shortest period, and v a shortest period not along u.

w \text{ a period: } w = s u + t v \text{ for reals } s, t @@ u and v point in different directions
w' = w - \lfloor s \rceil u - \lfloor t \rceil v = s'u + t'v,\ |s'|, |t'| \le \tfrac12 @@ round s and t to the nearest whole number; w′ is a period
|w'| \le \tfrac12|u| + \tfrac12|v| \le |v|\text{, equal only if } u, v \text{ point the same way} @@ the triangle rule of 18a; |u| ≤ |v|
w' \ne 0 \text{ and } t' \ne 0 \Rightarrow |w'| \lt |v| @@ a period off u’s line shorter than v: refute
t' = 0\text{: } w' = s'u,\ |w'| \le \tfrac12 |u| \lt |u| @@ a nonzero period shorter than u: refute
\htmlClass{new}{w' = 0,\ w = \lfloor s \rceil u + \lfloor t \rceil v} \quad \blacksquare @@ whole-number sums

The square floor of side 1 has periods (1, 0) and (0, 1). The hexagon floor of side 1 has u = (√3, 0) and v = (√3/2, 3/2), both of length √3. A period can be read off any patch: the patch at x and the patch at x + u are the same. That is how a tiling with a lattice can be checked by looking at one parallelogram.

A shift that carries the tiling onto itself is a map that keeps neighbours: tiles that touch go to tiles that touch. Such shifts run in a row and undo, so they form a group, and the repeat-lattice technique says that group is a grid. A tiling with no repeat is one whose group of such shifts is only the do-nothing. The plan is which tiles touch; which tile is which, and the order in which they were laid, are let go.

inf49.2Square Tiles With Coloured Edges

A square tile with coloured edges is a unit square with a colour on each side, read north, east, south, west. Tiles are placed on the grid, never turned, and touching edges must have the same colour. Does a given set of such tiles cover the plane? In 1961 it was guessed that any set that covers the plane covers it with a lattice.

Two-searches technique: if every set of square tiles that covers the plane also covers it with a lattice, then a machine decides whether a set covers the plane. Run two searches side by side. One tries every k by k block of tiles, for k = 1, 2, 3, …, and stops if some k admits no filling. The other tries every repeating block, and stops if one fits with its own copies on all four sides. Exactly one stops.

\text{no filling of a } k \times k \text{ block} \Rightarrow \text{no covering of the plane} @@ a covering would fill it
\text{every } k \times k \text{ block filled} \Rightarrow \text{a covering of the plane} @@ held: 34d’s finitely covered space of all assignments, a thread of fillings that settles
\text{a repeating block that fits its own copies} \Rightarrow \text{a covering with a lattice} @@ slide it by its width and its height
\text{covers} \Rightarrow \text{covers with a lattice} \Rightarrow \text{the second search stops} @@ the guess
\htmlClass{new}{\text{not covers} \Rightarrow \text{the first search stops}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so one always stops, and says which

Two tiles, A with north, east, south, west r, g, b, y and B with b, y, r, g, laid as a chessboard, cover the plane: A’s east g meets B’s west g, A’s south b meets B’s north b, and the other two meetings match too. The 2 by 2 block repeats. Delete B, and A alone cannot fill even a 1 by 2 block, since its east g is not its west y. Small sets settle fast. The guess says every set settles.

inf49.3No Machine Decides Tiling

In 1966 the guess was refuted, and the route was the one of 41e’s reduction-carries technique. Held in trust, as a technique stated: to every tape machine M a set of square tiles can be made that covers the plane exactly when M, started on a blank tape, never stops. Rows of tiles hold successive tapes; the colours on the edges carry the state and the symbol under the head, so the only way to go on tiling is to go on running M. The hard part is forcing the run to start at all: the tiles build a pattern of squares inside squares, at every size, and each square holds a run of its own length.

\text{machine } M \mapsto \text{tile set } T_M\text{: } T_M \text{ covers} \iff M \text{ never stops} @@ stated, 1966
\text{covering is decided} \Rightarrow \text{never stopping is decided} @@ the reduction-carries technique
\text{never stopping is not decided} @@ 41d’s no-stop-decider technique
\text{covering is not decided} @@ refute
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so the guess fails: some set covers, and never with a lattice}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two-searches technique, turned round

The argument says aperiodic sets exist without showing one. The first set shown had 20 426 tiles; within a few years it was cut to 104, then to 6 shapes in 1971, then to 13 square tiles with coloured edges in 1996, and to 11 in 2015, with a search by computer proving that no set of 10 or fewer will do. The question then turned from square tiles to shapes: how few shapes, if the shapes may be anything?

inf49.4Kites and Darts

Take a rhombus with sides φ and angles 72° and 108°, where φ = (1 + √5)/2 is the golden number. Join its two blunt corners to the point of the long diagonal at distance φ from a sharp corner: two pieces. The kite has angles 72°, 72°, 72°, 144°; the dart 36°, 72°, 36°, 216°. Their sides are 1 and φ. The two together tile periodically, as the rhombus does. Marks on the corners, and the rule that marks must match where tiles meet, forbid that. With the rule, they tile only without a lattice (the 1970s).

Every tiling by marked kites and darts can be grouped into bigger kites and darts, φ times as wide, that again obey the rule: held in trust, the grouping step, proved by checking the few ways a corner can be surrounded. Read the other way, each kite splits into 2 kites and 2 half-darts, and each dart into 1 kite and 2 half-darts; two half-darts make a dart.

Not-a-fraction technique: if in every tiling by a set of tiles the numbers of two kinds of tile, counted in ever larger discs, have a ratio tending to a number that is not a fraction, then the set is aperiodic. A periodic tiling repeats one parallelogram holding whole numbers m and n of the two kinds; a disc of radius R holds about πR²/(area) copies, give or take about R copies at its rim, so the ratio tends to m/n.

(k, d) \mapsto (2k + d,\ k + d) @@ one splitting of every tile
(1, 0) \to (2, 1) \to (5, 3) \to (13, 8) \to (34, 21) \to (89, 55) @@ each number the sum of the two before it, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …
\tfrac{89}{55} = 1.61818\ldots,\ \ \tfrac{233}{144} = 1.61805\ldots @@ approaching φ = 1.618034…
\text{areas: kite} = \varphi \cdot \text{dart};\ \ \varphi^2 \cdot \text{kite} = 2\,\text{kite} + \text{dart} @@ φ³ = 2φ + 1, so the split fills the bigger kite exactly
\htmlClass{new}{\text{kites : darts} \to \varphi\text{, not a fraction}} \quad \blacksquare @@ √5 is not a fraction; the not-a-fraction technique

The grouping step also gives the first claim, that a tiling exists at all: split a kite over and over, and 34d’s finitely covered space of patches gives a whole tiling. The marks matter. Without them a kite and a dart make the rhombus, and the rhombus tiles with a lattice.

inf49.5The Hat

Draw the grid of regular hexagons and cut each into six kites by the lines from its centre to the middles of its sides. Glue eight of these kites edge to edge in one particular way, and the result is the hat, a shape with 13 sides. It tiles the plane, and every tiling uses some hats turned over, mirror images of the rest. Every tiling by it is without a lattice (2023).

The proof groups hats into four clusters, the metatiles, called H, T, P and F after their outlines, and shows that every tiling groups into metatiles, and the metatiles into bigger ones of the same four kinds, without end: held in trust, the grouping step, here checked by a computer search through the ways a hat can be surrounded. At each level the metatiles are larger by a fixed factor, so they grow without end.

\text{grouping step: every hat tiling is a metatile tiling, at every scale} @@ held in trust, by computer, 2023
\text{a lattice of hats is a lattice of metatiles at every scale} @@ a period carries each level’s grouping to itself, since the grouping is unique
\text{the metatiles grow without end; a period has a fixed length} @@ a period shorter than a metatile carries it to itself: refute, as in inf49.1
\text{also: a second proof, by hand, for a family of shapes} @@ lengths that would have to stand in a ratio that is not a fraction
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the hat is aperiodic}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one shape, with its mirror image

The first road is the one of inf49.4 run directly: a hierarchy at every size cannot be carried to itself by a slide of fixed length, because some level’s clusters are wider than the slide and only a slide of 0 carries a cluster to itself. The hat came with a second proof that needs no computer, which lets the shape vary: a whole family of polygons with the same pattern of edges, and the counting of edges of two lengths shows a tiling would force a ratio that cannot be a fraction.

inf49.6The Spectre

The hat needs its mirror image: every tiling by hats uses both hands. Is that cheating? A tile and its mirror are the same shape under 20c’s flips, so most say no; others asked for a tile that needs no flip. In May 2023 the same team found one. In the hat’s family of polygons, the member with all edges of equal length, 14 of them, tiles the plane using only slides and turns, with no flipped copies, and then only without a lattice. With flipped copies allowed it also tiles with a lattice. Bending its edges into matching curves forbids the flipped copies outright, and the curved shape is the spectre.

Held in trust, as stated: the spectre tiles the plane, every tiling by it uses copies turned but never flipped, and none has a period. The proof again groups spectres into clusters that group into larger clusters without end.

14 \text{ edges, each of length } 1 @@ an equilateral polygon
\text{flipped copies allowed: a lattice exists} @@ the flaw that the curves remove
\text{only turned copies: no lattice} @@ held in trust, 2023
\htmlClass{new}{\text{curved edges: no flip, and no lattice}} \quad \blacksquare @@ held in trust, 2023

The hat and the spectre answered a question that had been asked since the 1960s, at least in the plane, for tiles in one piece, using no marks. They left harder versions open.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞49.oq.1Is the tile in space right? In September 2026 a preprint claimed a single shape in space, seven cubes glued together with small bumps on its faces, whose every tiling has no period in any direction, with finite checks done by machine and checked again by a proof assistant. Earlier tiles in space always kept a weaker repeat, a slide combined with a turn. As of November 2026 the claim is not refereed. Does it hold, and is there such a tile with no bumps, or a convex one?
∞49.oq.2Is there a convex shape in space that tiles only without a lattice? In the plane the convex shapes that tile have been listed, the last kinds of pentagon in 2017, by a search by computer. In space no such list exists, and nobody knows how to begin one.
∞49.oq.3Which single shapes tile? For square tiles with coloured edges, no machine decides whether a set covers the plane. For one shape in the plane, allowed to slide, turn and flip, nobody knows whether a machine can decide if it tiles at all. In 2023 a related question, for one tile slid about in a space with more directions, was shown to have no machine.
∞49.oq.4How simple can a single aperiodic tile be? The hat has 13 sides. Is there one with fewer? Is there one made of a few copies of a single square or triangle glued together, as the hat is made of kites, and how few?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Patterns that repeat are as old as floors. The guess of 1961, that every set that tiles can tile with a repeat, came from a logician, who saw that if it held, a machine could decide which sets of tiles cover the plane: the two searches of inf49.2.

It did not hold. In 1966 a doctoral thesis showed that tiles could be made to run any machine, so tiling is not decided, and aperiodic sets must exist; the set built had 20 426 tiles, cut to 104 in the same thesis. In 1971 six shapes sufficed, and in 1974 a mathematical physicist found a set of six, and soon after sets of two: the kites and darts, and two rhombi. Their pictures were spread widely by a magazine column in 1977, and the question of one tile was asked aloud.

In the early 1980s a metal alloy was found whose atoms scatter light in a pattern with fivefold symmetry, which no periodic crystal can show. It was doubted for years. The tilings of 1974 gave a model of how such order without repeat can arise, and in 2011 the discovery won a Nobel prize in chemistry. So a question asked about decision machines ended in a material.

For one tile, there were near misses. A tile in space was found in 1988, but its tilings keep a slide combined with a turn. In 2010 a single tile in the plane was found, but only with marks, or with a shape in pieces. In September 2026 a preprint claimed the first tile in space with no repeat of any kind, a chair of seven cubes; others posted notes on it the same month, and as of November 2026 it is not refereed.

In November 2022 a hobbyist in Britain found the hat. With three mathematicians he posted a proof in March 2023, and in May the spectre. The hat paper was published in 2024. Some asked whether a tile that needs its mirror counts. The spectre answered that, and the question is closed in the plane.

The accepted names

The square tiles with coloured edges are Wang tiles, and the 1961 guess is Wang’s conjecture. The undecidability of tiling is the domino problem, proved by Berger. The six notched shapes of 1971 are Robinson’s tiles. The kites and darts, and the two rhombi, are Penrose tilings; the splitting is called deflation or substitution, and the grouping composition. A single aperiodic tile is an einstein, from “one stone” in German. The hat and the spectre are the hat and spectre monotiles; the equal-edged polygon is Tile(1, 1); the clusters are the metatiles H, T, P, F. The 2010 tile is the Socolar–Taylor tile, and the 1988 tile in space is the Schmitt–Conway–Danzer biprism, whose tilings have a screw symmetry. The 2026 claim is of a strongly aperiodic monotile in three dimensions. The 2023 result for one tile slid in more dimensions is the undecidability of translational monotilings; the plane’s convex pentagons are the fifteen pentagon types. The alloy is a quasicrystal.

Who said it first

Hao Wang (1961) asked the question; Robert Berger (1966) proved it undecidable, with 20 426 tiles; Raphael Robinson (1971) gave six; Roger Penrose gave a set of six in 1974, then the kites and darts, popularised in 1977. Karel Culik (1996) gave 13 Wang tiles; Emmanuel Jeandel and Michaël Rao (2015) gave 11 and showed it is the least. Dan Shechtman found the quasicrystal (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2011). Peter Schmitt found the tile in space (1988), extended by John Conway and Ludwig Danzer. Joshua Socolar and Joan Taylor gave the 2010 tile. David Smith found the hat in November 2022; with Joseph Samuel Myers, Craig S. Kaplan and Chaim Goodman-Strauss he proved it aperiodic (2023, published 2024) and found the spectre (May 2023). Ioannis Tsiokos posted the tile in space, Chair44, in September 2026.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every patch of hats anyone has drawn fails to repeat, so the hat is aperiodic.” Not allowed: no finite patch repeats or fails to repeat; a period is a fact about the whole plane. The proof must show that every tiling, however it is laid, groups into larger clusters without end, and that is what the grouping step does.
