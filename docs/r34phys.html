How Do Charges Make Light?

How do charges make light? A charge pushes on other charges across empty space, and a current makes a field that swirls round the wire. Those are pushes and pulls, the stuff of motors and batteries. Light is something else: it carries colour and warmth across a hundred and fifty million kilometres of nothing, from the sun to a field of tulips in the Skagit Valley. What makes the question hard is that nothing in a still charge, or in a steady current, looks anything like a wave. The answer took most of a century of measuring, and then one line of reasoning about what the measured laws allow. This page starts from what 28·phys measured. The flux of a field through a closed surface turns out to count the charge inside, in a way that no stretching of the surface can change. Away from charges the field’s height is still water, which is why no arrangement of fixed charges can hold another charge at rest. Far away, a cluster of charges looks like a single charge, then like a pair. A changing field pushes too. And the measured laws, put side by side, contradict each other unless one more term is added. With that term, the laws carry a wave, at a speed that can be computed from two numbers measured on a bench. It is the speed of light.

The flux of a field out of a closed surface does not change when the surface is slid through space without crossing a charge, so it counts the charge inside. Away from charges the field is the downhill vector of a height that is the average of its neighbours, so it has no lowest point, and fixed charges cannot trap another one. Far away, the height of a cluster is its total charge over the distance, plus its pair moment over the distance squared. A changing magnetic flux pushes charges round a loop. The circulation law must gain a term for changing electric fields, or charge would not be kept. With it, the four laws in empty space give the wave rule, at a speed of one over the root of the two measured constants, and shaken charges send the waves out, the faster shaken the far brighter.

34phys1Counted, Not Measured

28·phys found that the flux of a field out of any closed surface is the charge inside over ε₀. Look at what that does not depend on: the size of the surface, its shape, how it bulges, where it is pinched. Only which charges it holds.

Wrap-count technique: sliding a closed surface through space, never across a charge, never changes the flux of the field out of it.

S_s,\ 0 \le s \le 1,\ \text{a slide of closed surfaces missing every charge} @@ as 34g slid loops, now surfaces
\text{spread of } E = 0 \text{ wherever there is no charge} @@ 28·phys.4: the spread of r/|r|³ is 0 away from 0, for each charge
\text{flux}(S_1) - \text{flux}(S_0) = \textstyle\iiint_{\text{swept}} \text{spread of } E\,dV @@ the boundary rule (29·cb28.4) on the region the surfaces sweep, cut into small boxes
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flux}(S_1) = \text{flux}(S_0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swept region holds no charge

So the flux is a count. A balloon round a charge can be squeezed into any shape at all, and as long as it never touches the charge its flux stays at q/ε₀. Slide it off the charge and the flux drops to 0, all at once, at the moment the surface crosses. A surface that wraps a charge twice, like a balloon twisted round and through itself, has flux 2q/ε₀: the count is how many times the surface goes round, a whole number, which is proved much higher up the ladder.

The earth’s fair-weather field, measured over open ground near Yakima on a still day, is about 130 V/m, pointing down. A closed surface hugging the ground has flux −130 × 4π(6.371 × 10⁶)² ≈ −6.6 × 10¹⁶ V m, so the earth holds a charge of about −8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 6.6 × 10¹⁶ ≈ −5.9 × 10⁵ C, spread over its skin.

34phys2Still Water, and No Trap

A field of charges at rest is the downhill vector of a height, the potential V, with E = −∇V: 28n’s fields with a potential, since the swirl of a point charge’s field is 0. Where there is no charge, the spread of E is 0, so the spread of the uphill vector of V is 0. That is 33d’s rule for still water: V at a point is the average of V over every small sphere about it.

No-trap technique: in a region with no charge, a field made by charges at rest cannot hold a charge in stable rest: the potential has no lowest point and no highest point inside the region.

q\,V \text{ is the energy of a test charge } q @@ the push on it is qE = −q∇V
\text{stable rest at } p \Rightarrow qV \text{ is least at } p \text{ among nearby points} @@ any small move costs energy
V(p) = \text{the average of } V \text{ over a small sphere about } p @@ the average of the neighbours (33d)
\text{an average equal to its least value is constant} @@ if V > V(p) anywhere on the sphere, the average exceeds V(p)
\htmlClass{new}{V \text{ is flat near } p:\ \text{no push back, no stable rest}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for q < 0 and a highest point

So a speck of charged dust cannot be held still by fixed charges alone. Traps that do hold single charged particles swing their fields back and forth millions of times a second, or add a magnetic field. A single electron was kept in such a trap for months at the University of Washington in Seattle, in the 1970s and 1980s, and its magnetism measured to twelve places.

34phys3Far Away, and Inside Matter

Charges qᵢ sit at points rᵢ near 0. Far away, at r with |r| = R much larger than every |rᵢ|, what does their potential look like?

Far-field technique: far from a cluster of charges, its potential is K Q/R + K (p · r̂)/R² plus terms that fall faster, where Q is the total charge and p = Σ qᵢ rᵢ is its pair moment.

V(r) = K\textstyle\sum_i \tfrac{q_i}{|r - r_i|} @@ the height of each charge, added (28·phys.4)
|r - r_i|^{-1} = R^{-1}\big(1 - 2\tfrac{r\cdot r_i}{R^2} + \tfrac{|r_i|^2}{R^2}\big)^{-1/2} @@ |r − rᵢ|² = R² − 2r · rᵢ + |rᵢ|²
= R^{-1} + \tfrac{\hat r\cdot r_i}{R^2} + \cdots @@ the binomial series (23h), (1 + u)^(−½) = 1 − u/2 + ⋯, with r̂ = r/R
\htmlClass{new}{V(r) = \tfrac{KQ}{R} + \tfrac{K\,p\cdot\hat r}{R^2} + \cdots} \quad \blacksquare @@ sum over i

A water molecule has Q = 0 and a pair moment of 6.2 × 10⁻³⁰ C m, its oxygen end negative. So its field falls off as 1/R³, not 1/R²: along its axis at 1 nm it is 2Kp/R³ ≈ 1.1 × 10⁸ V/m, against 1.4 × 10⁹ V/m from a single electron’s charge at the same distance. In a field, the molecules of liquid water turn to line their pair moments up, and their own fields cancel most of it. That is 28·phys’s measured factor κ, the field drop inside matter. For water it is about 80. Two ions of salt in water pull on each other 80 times more weakly than in air, and that is why salt dissolves in water and not in oil, whose κ is about 2.

34phys4A Changing Field Pushes, and a Missing Term

24·then met the measured law of induction: round a loop of wire, the push that drives the current is −dΦ/dt, where Φ is the magnetic flux through the loop. Written for any loop in space, wire or not: the circulation of E round a loop is minus the rate of change of the flux of B through it. By the curved-swirl technique of 29·cb28, the swirl of E is −∂B/∂t. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field that swirls.

28·phys.5 measured the other way round, for steady currents: the swirl of B is μ₀J. Now put the measured laws side by side.

Missing-term technique: the steady current law, swirl of B = μ₀J, cannot hold where charge piles up; with the term μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t added, it keeps charge, and it reduces to the old law for steady currents.

\text{spread of swirl of } B = 0 @@ twice-nothing (29·cb28.3)
\text{spread of } J = -\partial\rho/\partial t @@ charge is kept: what flows out of a small box is what the box loses
\text{a capacitor charging: } \partial\rho/\partial t \ne 0 \text{ on its plates} @@ so spread of μ₀J ≠ 0: the steady law fails there
\text{spread of } \varepsilon_0 E = \rho \Rightarrow \text{spread of } \varepsilon_0\,\partial E/\partial t = \partial\rho/\partial t @@ the enclosed-charge technique, on small boxes; rates through slopes (28d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{swirl of } B = \mu_0 J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial E/\partial t} \quad \blacksquare @@ now both sides have spread 0

The new term was not measured first. It was required, by the logic of charge being kept, and it was too small to see on any bench of the day. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field that swirls, just as a changing magnetic field makes an electric one.

34phys5A Wave at the Speed of Light

In empty space there is no charge and no current. The four laws now read: spread of E = 0, spread of B = 0, swirl of E = −∂B/∂t, swirl of B = μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t.

Light-speed technique: in empty space, each entry of E and of B obeys 33c’s wave rule, with speed 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s.

\text{swirl of swirl of } E = -\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\,\text{swirl of } B = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\tfrac{\partial^2 E}{\partial t^2} @@ swirl both sides of the third law; mixed slopes agree (28d)
\text{swirl of swirl of } E = \nabla(\text{spread of } E) - \Delta E = -\Delta E @@ partial slopes written out, entry by entry (28a); spread of E = 0
\Delta E = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\tfrac{\partial^2 E}{\partial t^2} @@ the two lines
\htmlClass{new}{c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 1/\sqrt{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times 8.854\times10^{-12}} = 2.998\times10^8} \quad \blacksquare @@ the wave rule (33c) with c² = 1/(μ₀ε₀); the same for B

Two numbers, one from the push between two charges and one from the push between two currents, measured on a bench with wires and coils, give the speed that light had already been measured to travel. In 1862 the agreement was within the errors of both. Light is a wave of the electric and magnetic fields.

Take a wave moving along z, E = E₀ cos(kz − ωt) with ω = ck. Spread of E = 0 says E₀ has no z entry: the field shakes across the direction of travel, never along it. B = (1/c) times the turn of E by a quarter about z. The direction in which E shakes is the light’s polarization, the vector 31·phys’s filters project.

34phys6Shaken Charges Shine

A charge at rest makes a field that falls as 1/R². A charge moving steadily makes the same, seen by an observer moving with it (29·phys). A charge that speeds up makes a field that falls only as 1/R across its line of sight, and that part carries energy away: q a sin θ/(4πε₀c²R), at angle θ from its push, with a its rate of speeding up. This comes from the four laws, and its proof is long; it is held in trust here. Adding the energy flow over a sphere gives the power sent out, q²a²/(6πε₀c³).

Shaken-charge technique: a charge shaken to and fro along a line, x₀ cos ωt, sends out on average q²ω⁴x₀²/(12πε₀c³) of power, so twice the pace sends sixteen times the light.

a(t) = -\omega^2 x_0\cos\omega t @@ two slopes of x₀ cos ωt (21g)
P(t) = \tfrac{q^2\omega^4x_0^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}\cos^2\omega t @@ the power held in trust above
\text{the average of } \cos^2 \text{ over a swing is } \tfrac12 @@ cos² = (1 + cos 2ωt)/2 (21d)
\htmlClass{new}{\bar P = \tfrac{q^2\omega^4x_0^2}{12\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fourth power of the pace

Sunlight shakes the electrons of the air’s molecules at the pace of the light, and they shine it out again sideways. Blue light at 450 nm shakes them (700/450)⁴ ≈ 5.9 times as hard, in power, as red at 700 nm. So the sky away from the sun is blue, and the sun low over the Olympic Mountains, seen through the long path of air that has lost its blue, is red. An FM antenna on Cougar Mountain shakes its electrons 100 million times a second, and radio is the same light at a pace a few million times slower.

The four laws are one statement in spacetime. With 29·cb28’s forms and 29·phys’s ruler, the fields make one form F of degree 2 in four variables: its six entries are the three of E and the three of B. Two laws are dF = 0, the other two are d⋆F = J, where ⋆ turns each pair of directions into the other two by the ruler. Twice-nothing then gives dJ = 0: charge is kept, as the missing term required.

The accepted names

The enclosed-charge technique is Gauss’s law, and the wrap-count technique is its topological form. The potential obeys Laplace’s equation in empty space and Poisson’s equation with charge; the no-trap technique is Earnshaw’s theorem. The far-field technique begins the multipole expansion, with p the dipole moment. The field drop κ is the relative permittivity, or dielectric constant. The law of induction is Faraday’s law, the missing term is Maxwell’s displacement current, and the four laws are Maxwell’s equations. The power of a speeding charge is the Larmor formula, and the blue sky is Rayleigh scattering. In spacetime the field is the Faraday form F, and ⋆ is the Hodge star.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the flux law in 1813 and 1839. Samuel Earnshaw proved that charges cannot be trapped by fixed charges in 1842. Michael Faraday measured induction in 1831. James Clerk Maxwell added the displacement current and computed the speed of the waves in 1861–62, and Heinrich Hertz made and caught the waves in 1887. Joseph Larmor found the power of a speeding charge in 1897, and John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh, explained the blue sky in 1871. Hans Dehmelt held a single electron in a Penning trap at the University of Washington from 1973, and shared the Nobel Prize for it in 1989.

Wrong moves, worked

“Two equal positive charges sit on a line. Halfway between them the pushes cancel, so a third positive charge rests there, trapped.” Not allowed: it rests, but not stably. Along the line it is pushed back toward the middle, and across the line it is pushed away. The potential at the middle is a saddle, as the no-trap technique says it must be.
