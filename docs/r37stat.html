Did You Hear It, or Just Say Yes?

Did you hear it, or just say yes? In a hearing test, faint beeps come through headphones and you press a button when you hear one. Someone eager presses at every faint hiss and catches every beep; someone cautious waits to be sure and misses some. Who hears better? Counting the beeps caught cannot say. This page counts hits and false alarms, puts two bell curves and one line for saying yes on 30j’s bell curve, measures how far apart the curves sit, moves the line to trade hits for false alarms, and measures hearing by the area under the trade-off curve, with 15.

Each trial gives the listener an inner reading: noise alone spreads as one bell curve, a beep in noise as the same curve shifted. The listener says yes past some line. How far apart the curves sit, d′, is the hearing; where the line sits is the caution. d′ = z(hits) − z(false alarms). Moving the line traces a curve of hits against false alarms, and the area under it is the chance of telling a beep trial from a silent one.

37stat1Hits and False Alarms

Mike is an audiologist. In each trial of his test, a faint beep is played or not, at random, in a hiss of noise, and the listener says yes or no.

From here on, on this page, the hit share is the share of beep trials answered yes, and the false alarm share the share of silent trials answered yes. An eager listener raises both; a cautious one lowers both. Hits alone cannot tell hearing from eagerness.

37stat2Two Bell Curves and One Line for Saying Yes

Model each trial as an inner reading. With noise alone, it spreads as a bell curve with mean 0 and spread 1 (30j); with a beep, the same curve shifted up by d′. The listener says yes when the reading passes a line c.

\text{false alarm share} = P(Z > c) @@ Z a standard bell curve (30j)
\text{hit share} = P(Z > c - d\prime) @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{d\prime \text{ is the hearing; } c \text{ is the caution}} @@ two separate things

37stat3How Far Apart the Curves Sit

Turn each share back into a position on the bell curve, z, the point with that share above it, and subtract.

d\prime = z(\text{hits}) - z(\text{false alarms}) @@ 
z(0.84) \approx 1,\ \ z(0.16) \approx -1 @@ the bell curve’s table
\htmlClass{new}{d\prime = 1 - (-1) = 2} @@ the curves sit two spreads apart

Separation technique: measure how well a signal can be told from noise by how many spreads apart the two bell curves sit, d′ = z(hits) − z(false alarms), which does not change with the listener’s caution.

37stat4Moving the Line Trades Hits for False Alarms

Ask the same listener to be more willing to say yes. Their d′ stays 2, but the line moves down.

1.5 (cautious)69%7%

−0.5 (eager)99%69%

For a fixed hearing, every gain in hits costs false alarms; the line can only choose where on the trade-off to sit. Mike tells two listeners apart this way: one with many hits and many false alarms, one with fewer of both, can turn out to have exactly the same d′, and so the same hearing.

37stat5The Area Under the Trade-Off Curve

Plot hits against false alarms as the line sweeps from very cautious to very eager. The curve runs from (0, 0) to (1, 1), bowing up more the bigger d′ is. Its area (15) is a single measure of hearing that uses every line at once.

\text{area} = P(\text{beep reading} > \text{silent reading}) = P\!\left(Z < \dfrac{d\prime}{\sqrt 2}\right) @@ the difference of two readings has spread √2
\htmlClass{new}{d\prime = 2:\ P(Z < 1.41) \approx 0.92} @@ 0.5 for no hearing at all, 1 for perfect

The area under the trade-off curve is the chance that a beep trial reads higher than a silent one, a measure of telling signal from noise that ignores caution. The same area grades medical tests, radar screens, and machine sorters. Mike’s hearing booth, run with many listeners at several cautions, gives points that lie on the curve for each listener’s d′, as the two-curve model says.

Accepted names

This is signal detection theory; d′ is read “d prime”, c the criterion; the trade-off curve is the receiver operating characteristic, ROC, and its area the AUC.

Who said it first

Wilson Tanner and John Swets set out signal detection for human seeing and hearing in 1954, carried over from the study of radar operators in the Second World War (not yet revisited for the date).
