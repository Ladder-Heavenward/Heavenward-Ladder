How Close Is Close Enough?

How close is close enough? No machine makes a part exactly to size. A lathe cuts a shaft a few thousandths of a millimetre fat or thin, and no two are the same. Yet the shaft must turn in its hole, the pump must not leak, and a part made in one town must fit a machine built in another. People asked how close is close enough as soon as parts were made in one place and fitted in another. What makes it hard is that “close” has to be a number, agreed before the part is made, and checked by tools that are not exact either. Rung 8 built the real line and 8h's distance on it. This page uses that distance three ways: to say when a part is good, to say what happens when good parts are put together, and to say what a measurement can and cannot prove.

A part is good when its size is within the agreed tolerance of the size on the drawing. When parts are added or subtracted, their tolerances add, by the triangle technique. A reading proves the part good only when it is within the tolerance less the tool’s own error. Two readings of the same part must agree within twice the tool’s error, or one of them is wrong.

8eng1A Size and a Distance from It

Sizes here are in millimetres: 1 mm is a thousandth of a metre. Machinists also count in thousandths of a millimetre. This page writes them as μm, read “micrometres”: 1 μm = 0.001 mm. A hair is roughly 70 μm thick.

From here on, on this page, n ± d, with d ≥ 0, names the stretch of the line within d of n: the reals x with |x − n| ≤ d. A part whose drawing says n ± d is good when its size x has |x − n| ≤ d. The number n is the nominal size and d the tolerance.

By 8h's unpack-the-bars, |x − n| ≤ d says n − d ≤ x ≤ n + d. So n ± d is the closed interval [n − d, n + d], and a tolerance can be given either way. A part of 20 ± 0.01 mm may be anything from 19.99 to 20.01 mm. A tolerance is a distance on the line: the part is good when its size is no farther than d from the nominal size.

8eng2A Hole and a Shaft That Must Fit

A shaft that must turn freely in a hole needs a gap all round, but not much of one. Engineers agree on standard pairs. In the standard fit called 20 H7/g6, used for a shaft turning in a bearing, the hole is 20.000 to 20.021 mm across and the shaft 19.980 to 19.993 mm. Call the hole's size h and the shaft's s. The clearance is h − s.

20.000 \le h \le 20.021 @@ the hole's drawing
19.980 \le s \le 19.993 @@ the shaft's drawing
-19.993 \le -s \le -19.980 @@ multiply by −1: the order turns about (8g)
\htmlClass{new}{0.007 \le h - s \le 0.041} @@ same-direction-add: least with least, greatest with greatest

So every good shaft fits every good hole, with between 7 and 41 μm to spare. Written about the middle, the hole is 20.0105 ± 0.0105, the shaft 19.9865 ± 0.0065, and the clearance 0.024 ± 0.017. The clearance's tolerance is the sum of the other two. That is no accident.

8eng3Tolerances Add

Tolerances-add technique: when sizes are added or subtracted, the tolerance of the result is the sum of their tolerances. Let x be within d of p, and let y be within e of q. Then x + y is within d + e of p + q, and so is x − y of p − q.

|x - a| \le d,\ \ |y - b| \le e @@ given
(x + y) - (a + b) = (x - a) + (y - b) @@ regroup on ℝ (8d)
|(x + y) - (a + b)| \le |x - a| + |y - b| @@ triangle technique (8h)
\le d + e @@ same-direction-add (8g)
(x - y) - (a - b) = (x - a) + (-(y - b)) @@ regroup; and |−(y − b)| = |y − b| by size-of-a-negative
\htmlClass{new}{|(x - y) - (a - b)| \le d + e} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same two lines again

By induction on the number of parts, the same is true for any number of them. A chain of parts set end to end, or packed on one shaft, has a length whose tolerance is the sum of all theirs. This is the stack-up. Five spacers of 10.00 ± 0.05 mm make a stack of 50.00 ± 0.25 mm.

Is the sum too careful? No: it is reached. If every spacer is 10.05 mm, the stack is 50.25 mm. So no smaller number can be promised for every set of good parts. The worst case of a stack is the sum of the tolerances, and good parts can reach it. A designer who needs the stack within ±0.1 mm must make each spacer within ±0.02 mm, or use fewer parts.

8eng4Measuring Is Not Exact Either

A part is checked with a tool, and the tool has an error too. A good dial caliper reads within about 0.02 mm of the true size, and a micrometer within about 0.002 mm. Say a reading r is within e of the true size x. When does r prove that x is good?

Guard-band technique: if |r − x| ≤ e and |r − n| ≤ d − e, then |x − n| ≤ d. Take x − n = (x − r) + (r − n), and use the triangle technique: |x − n| ≤ e + (d − e) = d. So the reading must land inside a narrower band, the tolerance less the tool's error.

d = 0.0105,\ \ e = 0.02 @@ the hole's tolerance about its middle; a caliper
d - e = -0.0095 \lt 0 @@ no reading has a negative distance
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no caliper reading proves the hole good}} @@ use a tool with e well below d

Machinists put it as a habit: use a tool ten times finer than the tolerance. A micrometer, with e = 0.002, leaves a band of 0.0085 mm each side.

And the old saying: measure twice, cut once. Two readings r₁ and r₂ of one part, each within e of its true size, are within 2e of each other: |r₁ − r₂| ≤ |r₁ − x| + |x − r₂| ≤ 2e. Turned about, if two readings differ by more than 2e, one of them is wrong. A second reading cannot make the first exact, but it can catch a mistake. A burr, a speck of chip, or a tool held askew shows up as two readings that disagree.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

5e set beside the forbidden division a principle of the second kind of care: a statement comes with its range, and the direction it fails past the edge. 5·nav found it in the calendar, good to four places for a few thousand years. A drawing's n ± d is that principle written as a mark. The nominal size is the statement, and the tolerance is its range. Past the edge the failure has a known direction: a shaft too big binds, and a shaft too small rattles.

± is not an admission of error: it marks out where you can't be certain, so that you can hedge against being wrong.

8·cb5 gave √2's decimals a width and 8·phys gave a measurement one. Here widths are added on purpose, and the stack-up says how far they reach together. The guard band goes one step further. It takes the tool's own width off before a reading is trusted.

Who said it first

Parts made to fit any copy of a machine were pushed by Honoré Blanc for French muskets in the 1780s, and by the United States armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry in the early 1800s. Gauges that say “go” and “no go” for the two ends of a band came in through the nineteenth century. Joseph Whitworth, in Manchester, built machines from the 1840s that could detect a millionth of an inch. The standard fits such as H7/g6 are set by the ISO system of fits, ISO 286. The engineers of 8·eng.6.4 began writing about stacks that do not reach the worst case in the 1950s; Arthur Bender's rule of 1962 added a safety factor of 1.5 to their figure.

Accepted names

The nominal size is the basic size, and the distances of the band's ends from it are the upper and lower deviations. A fit whose clearance is always positive is a clearance fit; always negative, an interference fit or press fit; sometimes either, a transition fit. The sum of tolerances is worst-case tolerance analysis, and the engineers' smaller figure, the root of the sum of the squared tolerances, is root-sum-square or statistical tolerancing. The narrower band for readings is a guard band, and the habit of a tool ten times finer is the ten-to-one rule. A tool's error band is its accuracy.
