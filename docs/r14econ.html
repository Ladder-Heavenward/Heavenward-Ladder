How Much Should a Factory Make?

How much should a factory make? Each unit it sells brings money in, and each unit it makes costs money. Make too few, and the plant stands idle while buyers go elsewhere. Make too many, and the last units cost more to make than they bring in. Owners asked this long before anyone wrote down a slope, and most answered by habit: last year's output, and a little more. It is hard because nobody sees the whole cost curve or the whole curve of what buyers will pay. A plant knows its costs at the few outputs it has run. This page takes curves fitted to such numbers as premises. Then it asks where 14a's flat tops put the best output, how the answer moves when one seller has the market to itself, and what a tax does to it.

Profit is what sales bring in, less what making costs. Inside the range of outputs, a greatest profit needs its slope to be 0, which is the slope of sales equal to the slope of cost: the slope of revenue meets the slope of cost. A seller among many takes the price as fixed, so one more sale brings in the price. A seller alone must drop the price to sell more, so one more sale brings in less than the price, and it makes less. A tax on each unit lifts the slope of cost and moves the best output. A fixed tax does not.

14econ1Where Profit Is Greatest

A plant chooses q, the number of units it makes and sells in a week. R(q) is the money in, its revenue. C(q) is the money out, its cost. On this page both are premises: curves fitted to a plant's books. What is left is the profit, written here G(q) = R(q) − C(q), G for gain. 13·econ read the slopes: C′(q) is close to the cost of one more unit, and R′(q) to the money that one more unit brings in.

G(q) = R(q) - C(q) @@ the goal, in dollars a week
G'(q) = R'(q) - C'(q) @@ slopes-add, constant-factor for slopes (13d)
G \text{ has a top at } q \text{ inside the range} \;\Rightarrow\; G'(q) = 0 @@ flat-top (14a)
\htmlClass{new}{R'(q) = C'(q)} @@ the slope of revenue meets the slope of cost
G''(q) = R''(q) - C''(q) \lt 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{a top} @@ second-test (14f); one-critical-point makes it the greatest

Make one more unit while it brings in more than it costs, and stop where the two slopes meet. The condition only finds the inside candidates. The ends are candidates too: making nothing, and running the plant flat out (candidates, 14a).

14econ2A Grower Who Takes the Price

Processing tomatoes from Yolo County are sold to the canneries on contract, at a price per ton set before the season. In 2022 it was about $100 a ton. One grower is far too small to move it. So a grower's revenue from y tons an acre is 100y, and its slope is the price itself.

Take as a premise a field's cost per acre, C(y) = 1500 + y² dollars at a yield of y tons an acre. The 1500 is paid whatever the yield: rent, seed, planting. The y² is the cost of pushing the yield up: more water, more fertiliser, more picking. Each extra ton costs more than the last.

G(y) = 100y - 1500 - y^2 @@ the goal, dollars an acre
G'(y) = 100 - 2y = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; y = 50 @@ polynomial-slope: the price meets the slope of cost
G''(y) = -2 \lt 0 @@ second-test: a top; one-critical-point: the greatest on [0, ∞)
G(50) = 5000 - 1500 - 2500 = \htmlClass{new}{\$1000 \text{ an acre}} @@ near the yield California's growers do get

At 50 tons, the average cost is 4000/50 = $80 a ton, well below the price. But the 51st ton costs about $101 to grow. So the grower stops at 50. Among many sellers, each one makes where the price equals the slope of its own cost.

14econ3A Seller Alone

A seller with no rivals faces the whole curve of buyers: q tons sell only at the price p(q), and p falls as q grows. Take a ready-mix concrete plant near Gonzales, the only one within a day's haul. As premises, buyers take q tons a week at p(q) = 60 − 0.02q dollars a ton, and the plant's cost is C(q) = 2000 + 20q + 0.005q².

R(q) = q\,p(q) = 60q - 0.02q^2 @@ every ton sells at the price that q tons fetch
R'(q) = 60 - 0.04q @@ polynomial-slope: below the price 60 − 0.02q for every q > 0
C'(q) = 20 + 0.01q @@ polynomial-slope
60 - 0.04q = 20 + 0.01q \;\Leftrightarrow\; q = 800 @@ the slopes meet
G''(q) = -0.04 - 0.01 \lt 0 @@ second-test: a top; one-critical-point: the greatest
p(800) = \$44,\ \ G(800) = 35\,200 - 21\,200 = \htmlClass{new}{\$14\,000} @@ a week

Why does the slope of revenue sit below the price? By product for slopes, R′(q) = p(q) + q p′(q). Selling one more ton earns its price, p(q). But it lowers the price of every ton already sold, and that is the q p′(q), which is negative.

Now give the same buyers and the same cost curve to a market of many small plants. None of them can move the price. So, like the grower, they make where the price meets the slope of cost.

p(q) = C'(q):\ \ 60 - 0.02q = 20 + 0.01q @@ the grower's technique
q = 1333\tfrac13,\ \ p = \$33\tfrac13 @@ more concrete, at a lower price

A seller alone makes less and charges more than many sellers would, facing the same buyers with the same costs. Its accepted name is a monopoly.

14econ4A Tax on Each Ton

Suppose the county puts a tax of t dollars on each ton sold. The plant's cost becomes C(q) + tq, and the slope of cost becomes C′(q) + t.

60 - 0.04q = 20 + 0.01q + t @@ the slopes meet again
q = 800 - 20t,\ \ \ p = 44 + 0.4t @@ solve; then the price from p(q)
t = 5:\ \ q = 700,\ \ p = \$46 @@ buyers pay $2 of each $5 in a higher price
C(q) + T \text{ for a fixed tax } T:\ \ \text{slope } C'(q) @@ constant-has-slope-0: the best output does not move
(1 - s)\,G(q):\ \ \text{flat where } G \text{ is flat} @@ constant-factor for slopes: a share s of profit moves nothing

A tax moves the best output only when it changes the slope of cost. A fixed tax can still close a plant. That happens when the best profit falls below what the plant would lose by making nothing.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

13a set beside the slope a second kind of care. Things do not jump between states; they pass through a middle. A plant's books show its cost at the outputs it has run, a before and an after. The slope of cost is the middle between them: what one more ton costs at this output.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

An average is a before-and-after number. The grower's average cost at 50 tons is $80 a ton, and it says nothing about the 51st ton. An owner who decides by the average stops in the wrong place.

A bakery in Salinas sells 300 loaves a day at $5. Its average cost is $3 a loaf. What would you need to know before baking a 301st?

Who said it first

Cournot imagined the owner of a mineral spring, alone in selling its waters and paying nothing to draw them. He showed that the owner would not fix the highest price, nor sell the most, but would fix the price at which the slope of his receipts is zero.

Antoine Augustin Cournot, Recherches sur les principes mathématiques de la théorie des richesses (1838); paraphrase

In 14·econ.6.4, the economists of 1939 are Robert Hall and Charles Hitch, at Oxford. The survey of 1946 is Richard Lester's, and Fritz Machlup answered it the same year. The billiard player is Milton Friedman's, from his essay on method of 1953.

Accepted names: marginal revenue for R′, marginal cost for C′, profit maximisation, a price-taker, perfect competition, monopoly. The output the market loses to a monopoly carries a loss to buyers and sellers together called the deadweight loss. Who pays a tax is its incidence.
