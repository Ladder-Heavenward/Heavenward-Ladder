Adding up Slices

Spin a curve around a line and it sweeps out a solid: a vase, a bowl, a ball. Cut that solid across, like a loaf, and every slice is a disc. Now that the disc has an area, the solid’s volume is the sum of its slices. This step builds the cylinder, the cone and the ball this way. It ends with a result an ancient geometer was proud enough of to want carved on his grave: the ball takes up exactly two thirds of the can that just holds it.

A solid spun round a line is a stack of discs, so its volume is the total of their areas. The cone is a third of its cylinder, and the ball two thirds of its. The ball’s skin is how fast its volume grows as it swells.

21o1Discs

Disc-stack technique: spin the region under y = f(x) ≥ 0, from x = a to x = b, about the floor, and the solid has volume ∫ₐᵇ π f(x)² dx. The slice at x is a disc of radius f(x).

\text{slice at } x = \text{disc of radius } f(x),\ \text{area } \pi f(x)^2 @@ the disc technique (21n3)
\text{tight: least disc inside, greatest disc around} @@ on each piece, the disc of the least radius sits in every slice and the greatest holds them all; their gap totals U − L for πf² (15c)
V = \int_a^b \pi f(x)^2\,dx @@ cross-sections (16j2)
f(x) = \tfrac{r}{h}x,\ 0 \le x \le h:\ \ V = \pi\tfrac{r^2}{h^2}\cdot\tfrac{h^3}{3} @@ a cone of radius r and height h, lying on its side
\htmlClass{new}{V_{\text{cone}} = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h} \quad \blacksquare @@ a third of its cylinder, πr²h, as 16j found for pyramids

21o2Washers

Washer technique: spin the region between y = f(x) and y = g(x), with f ≥ g ≥ 0, about the floor, and the solid has volume ∫ₐᵇ π(f(x)² − g(x)²) dx. Each slice is a disc with a smaller disc taken out.

\text{slice at } x: \pi f(x)^2 - \pi g(x)^2 @@ the outer disc less the hole
\text{tight: the narrowest washer inside, the widest around} @@ on each piece, the washer from the largest g to the smallest f sits in every slice, and the one from the smallest g to the largest f holds them all
\htmlClass{new}{V = \int_a^b \pi\big(f(x)^2 - g(x)^2\big)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ cross-sections (16j2)
\pi(f^2 - g^2) \ne \pi(f - g)^2 @@ the hole is taken from the disc, not the gap squared

21o3The Ball Is Two Thirds of Its Can

Ball technique: the ball of radius r has volume (4/3)πr³, two thirds of the cylinder of radius r and height 2r that just holds it.

f(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2},\ \ -r \le x \le r @@ spin the upper half disc about the floor
V = \int_{-r}^{r} \pi(r^2 - x^2)\,dx = \pi\Big[r^2x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{-r}^{r} @@ disc stack
\htmlClass{new}{V_{\text{ball}} = \tfrac43\pi r^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ π(2r³ − 2r³/3)
\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \div (\pi r^2 \cdot 2r) = \tfrac23 @@ the can

Slice by slice: at height t, the half ball’s slice has area π(r² − t²), which is the can’s slice πr² less the cone’s slice πt². So half ball = can − cone = πr³ − ⅓πr³ = ⅔πr³.

21o4The Ball’s Skin

From here on, the area of the sphere of radius r is the slope of the ball’s volume as its radius grows: S(r) = V′(r).

Skin technique: the sphere of radius r has area 4πr², four times the disc through its centre. A ball grown from r to r + h gains a shell, of thickness h, wrapped round the sphere; for small h that shell is the area times h.

V(r + h) - V(r) = \text{shell of thickness } h @@ the bigger ball less the smaller
\frac{V(r + h) - V(r)}{h} \to V'(r) @@ the slope at r (13a)
\htmlClass{new}{S(r) = \big(\tfrac43\pi r^3\big)' = 4\pi r^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power technique (13d)
4\pi r^2 = 2\pi r \cdot 2r @@ the can’s side, unrolled: a rectangle 2πr round and 2r high

The accepted names

The disc-stack and washer techniques are the disc method and the washer method; slicing across, with equal slices giving equal volumes, is Cavalieri’s principle. The ball is the solid sphere; the sphere is its surface. The ring in 21o5.1 is a torus.

Who said it first

The ball as two thirds of its cylinder is Archimedes’, On the Sphere and Cylinder (c. 225 BC); Cicero found the sphere and cylinder carved on his tomb in Syracuse. In 21o.4.5, the endless horn is Evangelista Torricelli’s (1641), now called Gabriel’s horn. Its finite volume set off a long philosophical argument, Thomas Hobbes among those who objected.
