Why Do Power Stations Run Two Engines?

Why do power stations run two engines? The best gas-fired power stations burn fuel in a jet engine bolted to the ground, then pass its hot exhaust through a boiler to run a steam turbine too. Together they turn over 60% of the fuel’s heat into electricity, where either alone manages about 40%. This page measures a cycle’s work as the area inside its loop (15), recalls 28·then’s ceiling on any engine, finds the gas turbine’s efficiency from its pressure ratio with 24’s separated variables, adds a second engine on its waste heat, and shows why stacking engines closes in on the ceiling but never reaches it.

An engine’s work each cycle is the area inside its loop on a pressure-volume chart, and no engine between Th and Tc beats 1 − Tc/Th. A gas turbine’s efficiency is 1 − r^(−(γ−1)/γ), set by how hard it squeezes the air; its exhaust is still hot. A steam cycle run on that exhaust turns a share η₂ of what the first threw away, so together η₁ + η₂ − η₁η₂.

29eng21A Cycle as a Loop Whose Work Is the Area Inside

Claire runs a gas-fired power station. In each of its engines a working fluid, air or steam, is squeezed, heated, let expand and cooled, and comes back to where it started: a loop on a chart of pressure against volume.

\text{work done by the fluid} = \int p\,dV @@ area under the path (15)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{over a whole loop: the area inside it}} @@ out at high pressure, back at low

From here on, on this page, an engine’s efficiency η is the work it gives over the heat it takes in.

29eng22The Hot-and-Cold Ceiling

\eta \le 1 - \dfrac{T_c}{T_h} @@ no engine beats it (28·then)
\htmlClass{new}{T_h = 1\,700 \text{ K},\ T_c = 300 \text{ K}:\ \eta \le 0.82} @@ gas at its hottest, the river that cools the plant

The hotter the heat comes in and the colder it leaves, the higher the ceiling. A steam boiler cannot run at 1,700 K, and a gas turbine cannot let its exhaust out near 300 K. Each alone uses only part of the range.

29eng23The Gas Turbine’s Efficiency From Its Pressure Ratio

Air squeezed quickly has no time to lose heat, so all the work of squeezing goes into warming it. Take one mole of air: its energy is c_v T and its push is p = RT/V (9·chem). Squeezing it by dV adds −p dV to its energy, and the variables separate (24f).

c_v\,\dfrac{dT}{T} = -R\,\dfrac{dV}{V} @@ separating the variables (24f)
\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = r^{(\gamma - 1)/\gamma},\ \ r = \dfrac{p_2}{p_1},\ \ \gamma = 1.4 \text{ for air} @@ logs of both sides (17)
\eta_1 = 1 - r^{-(\gamma - 1)/\gamma} @@ heat in and out at the same temperature ratio
\htmlClass{new}{r = 20:\ \eta_1 = 1 - 20^{-0.286} \approx 1 - 0.425 = 0.575} @@ losses bring a real one to about 0.40

A real gas turbine loses in its squeezer, its turbine and its seals, and gives about 40%. Its exhaust leaves at about 600 °C: a great deal of heat still hot enough to boil water.

29eng24Its Hot Exhaust Runs a Steam Cycle

Pass the exhaust through a boiler. A steam cycle turns a share η₂ of the heat it receives into work, and it receives what the gas turbine threw away.

\text{heat in: } 1;\ \ \text{gas turbine work } \eta_1;\ \ \text{thrown away } 1 - \eta_1 @@ 
\text{steam work} = \eta_2\,(1 - \eta_1) @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\eta = \eta_1 + \eta_2 - \eta_1\eta_2} @@ the stacked-cycle technique
\eta_1 = 0.40,\ \eta_2 = 0.35:\ \eta = 0.40 + 0.35 - 0.14 = 0.61 @@

Stacked-cycle technique: an engine run on another’s waste heat turns a share of what the first threw away, so the pair gives η₁ + η₂ − η₁η₂; what both throw away is (1 − η₁)(1 − η₂). On test, the best plants of this kind have been measured turning just over 60% of their fuel’s heat into electricity, as the count says.

29eng25Reheating Closes on the Ceiling but Never Reaches It

Stack more stages, or reheat the steam between turbines, and each extra stage takes a share of what is left.

\text{thrown away} = (1 - \eta_1)(1 - \eta_2)(1 - \eta_3)\cdots @@ each stage multiplies it down
0.60 \times 0.65 = 0.39;\ \ \times 0.90 = 0.35 @@ a small third stage
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every stage is under its own ceiling, so the total stays under } 1 - T_c/T_h} @@ 0.82 here

Stacking engines and reheating move a plant toward the hot-and-cold ceiling, but each extra stage gains less, and no stack can pass it. A plant engineer weighs each added stage’s small gain against its cost in steel and upkeep, and today’s best plants stop at two engines with reheat.

Accepted names

The gas turbine runs the Brayton cycle and the steam engine the Rankine cycle; the stack is a combined cycle, and the boiler on the exhaust a heat recovery steam generator. Squeezing with no heat lost is adiabatic compression, and γ the ratio of specific heats. Selling the last heat is combined heat and power.

Who said it first

Combined-cycle plants spread from the 1970s; makers announced plants measured above 62% in 2016 and above 63% in 2018 (not yet revisited for the figures).
