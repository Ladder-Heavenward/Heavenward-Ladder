13f · proof

A joint has no slope

11e proved the size of a number continuous at 0, with pieces that climb at the rates negative one and one, and said that rung 13 would ask what the difference in rates costs. This step answers. Its chords from 0 have slope one on the right and negative one on the left, and a limit cannot head two ways, so it has no slope at 0. In general, at a joint a function has a slope exactly when its pieces arrive with the same slope. That is the second condition 11f promised for gluing pieces. Last, the quotient rule, built from the product and chain rules, and with it the slopes of the negative powers.

At a joint, take the chords from each side separately. Each side's chord slopes head to its piece's slope. The slope exists exactly when the two sides agree.

13f1|x| at 0

Size-has-no-slope-at-0 technique: |x| is continuous at 0 and has no slope there. Its chord slopes from 0 are 1 for every h > 0 and −1 for every h < 0.

h \gt 0:\ \ \dfrac{|0 + h| - |0|}{h} = \dfrac{h}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{1} @@ 8h's line for |a| by cases: |h| = h when 0 ≤ h; reciprocal on ℝ
h \lt 0:\ \ \dfrac{|0 + h| - |0|}{h} = \dfrac{-h}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{-1} @@ |h| = −h when h < 0; negative-out on ℚ, reciprocal on ℝ
\lim_{h \to 0^+} (\cdots) = 1,\ \ \lim_{h \to 0^-} (\cdots) = -1 @@ agreeing-near side by side (10e): each side agrees with a constant, which heads to itself
1 \ne -1 @@ three-way on ℝ, read for 1 and −1
\text{the chord slopes have no limit at } 0 @@ two-sides-differ (10e): two sides that differ, no limit
|x| \text{ has no slope at } 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ 13a's line. And |x| is continuous at 0: size-is-continuous (11e)

So the converse of 13b's slope-means-no-gap fails again, this time with no wild function in sight. And |x| has a slope at every other input. At w > 0 it agrees with x on (0, 2w), so its slope is 1 (slopes-decided-near, slope-of-a-line). At w < 0 it agrees with −x on (2w, 0), so its slope is −1. Continuous at every real, with a slope at every real but one.

13f2Slopes from the two sides

The two sides deserve names of their own, since they exist even where the slope does not.

From here on, take a function f with w an input, and f defined on some interval [w, w + r) with r > 0. The slope from the right of f at w is lim_{h→0⁺} (f(w + h) − f(w))/h, when that one-sided limit exists. Likewise, take f defined on some (w − r, w]. The slope from the left of f at w is lim_{h→0⁻} (f(w + h) − f(w))/h, when it exists. These are 10e's one-sided limits of 13a's chord slopes. No new mark is used: the step writes the limits out.

Two-side-slopes technique: a function has a slope exactly when its slopes from the two sides exist and agree. Precisely: let f be defined on an open interval around w. f has a slope at w exactly when it has a slope from the left and a slope from the right at w, and the two are the same. Then f′(w) is their common value. It is 10e's two-sides-agree, read for the chord-slope function of h at 0. |x| at 0 has the slope −1 from the left and 1 from the right: 11e's two rates, now each a one-sided slope.

13f3A joint’s slope

|x| at 0 is the plainest joint. The general one has two pieces, each with a slope of its own.

Joint-slopes technique: at a joint, a function has a slope exactly when its pieces arrive with the same slope. Precisely: let f equal f₁ on (w − r, w) and f₂ on (w, w + r), where f₁ and f₂ are functions with slopes at w. Let f(w) = f₁(w) = f₂(w), so that w is a joint of f. Then the slope of f from the left at w is f₁′(w), and from the right is f₂′(w). So f has a slope at w exactly when f₁′(w) = f₂′(w), and then f′(w) is that value. The low marks 1 and 2 name the two pieces, as 3d's low marks name things.

-r \lt h \lt 0:\ \ \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \dfrac{f_1(w + h) - f_1(w)}{h} @@ f agrees with f₁ at w + h and at w: the swap axiom, twice
\dfrac{f_1(w + h) - f_1(w)}{h} \to f_1'(w) \text{ as } h \to 0 @@ the hypothesis: f₁ has a slope at w
\lim_{h \to 0^-} \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{f_1'(w)} @@ two-sides-agree's free direction (a two-sided limit gives both one-sided ones), then agreeing-near side by side
\lim_{h \to 0^+} \dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \htmlClass{new}{f_2'(w)} @@ the same column on (0, r), with f₂
f \text{ has a slope at } w \;\Leftrightarrow\; f_1'(w) = f_2'(w) \quad \blacksquare @@ two-side-slopes

That is the cost 11e asked about. A joint costs nothing in continuity: the pieces need only arrive at the same height. It costs the slope, unless the pieces also arrive with the same slope. 11e's glued-functions asked how the pieces arrive; joint-slopes asks how they leave.

2x + 1\ (x \le 1),\ \ 4 - x\ (x \gt 1) @@ 11e's joint at 1: heights 3 and 3
\text{slopes at } 1:\ 2 \text{ and } -1 @@ slope-of-a-line for each piece. joint-slopes: no slope at 1
|x - 2| + |x + 1| = -2x + 1,\ \ 3,\ \ 2x - 1 @@ 11e's pieces on (−∞, −1), [−1, 2], (2, ∞)
\text{at } -1:\ -2 \text{ and } 0;\ \ \text{at } 2:\ 0 \text{ and } 2 @@ no slope at either joint; slope −2, 0 or 2 everywhere else
x|x| = -x^2\ (x \lt 0),\ \ x^2\ (x \ge 0) @@ 8h's line by cases
\text{at } 0:\ \ (-x^2)'(0) = 0,\ \ (x^2)'(0) = 0 @@ slope-of-x² and constant-factor for slopes. The same slope: joint-slopes gives x|x| the slope 0 at 0
(x|x|)' = 2|x| @@ −2a for a < 0, 2a for a > 0, 0 at 0: a slope everywhere. Its slope function 2|x| has none at 0 (size-has-no-slope-at-0, constant-factor for slopes)
u:\ \ 0\ (x \lt 0),\ \ 1\ (x \ge 0) @@ both pieces have slope 0 at 0, but they arrive at 0 and 1: not a joint, a jump. No slope, by slope-means-no-gap turned about

The last line is a warning about the order of the two conditions. Equal slopes are not enough: joint-slopes starts from a joint, equal heights arriving and the value there. Without that, 13b already says no.

Take as given a measured heating curve: 100 g of ice, heated steadily through melting and boiling to steam. Its temperature, as a function of the heat put in, is continuous with four joints, and its five pieces climb at about 4.78, 0, 2.39, 0 and 4.98 °C per kJ. (The annex 11·chem measures it.) No two neighbours agree, so the temperature has no slope at any of its four joints.

13f4The second condition at a joint

11f glued mx + 1, for x < 2, to x² − b, for x ≥ 2. It found that the pieces arrive at one height exactly when 2m + 1 = 4 − b: a whole family of pairs (m, b), among them (1, 1), (0, 3) and (3/2, 0). It said a second condition of another kind would pin down one pair, and that rung 13 supplies the usual one. Here it is.

2m + 1 = 4 - b @@ the joint: glued-functions (11e), as 11f found
\text{left slope at } 2 = m,\ \ \text{right slope at } 2 = 2 \cdot 2 = 4 @@ slope-of-a-line; slope-of-x² and lifting for x² − b
m = \htmlClass{new}{4} @@ joint-slopes: a slope at 2 exactly when the slopes agree
4 - b = 2 \cdot 4 + 1 = 9,\ \ \text{so } b = \htmlClass{new}{-5} @@ the first condition, solved (adding-keeps-answers, 5g)
4x + 1\ (x \lt 2),\ \ x^2 + 5\ (x \ge 2) @@ heights 9 and 9; slopes 4 and 4; f′(2) = 4

Look at the left piece: 4x + 1 is the tangent line of x² + 5 at 2, since 9 + 4(x − 2) = 4x + 1. Pieces that join with a slope meet as a piece meets its tangent line. The other pairs of 11f's family, such as m = 1 and b = 1, give a joint with slopes 1 and 4: continuous at 2, no slope there.

13f5Continuous everywhere, a slope nowhere

|x| lacks a slope at one input, and |x − 2| + |x + 1| at two. A function with a joint at every whole number, such as the distance from x to the nearest whole number, lacks one at endlessly many. How far can it go? In 1872 a function continuous at every real with a slope at none was presented to the Berlin Academy, and it was published in 1875. It is an endless sum, built from functions this ladder builds on rung 21. However near you look to any input, its chords from that input never settle on a slope. Earlier, the author of 12a's paper of 1817 had built a continuous function lacking slopes at endlessly many inputs. It was in his manuscript of the 1830s, the one 12c mentioned, which was not printed until 1930. Mathematicians of the time had taken for granted that a continuous function has a slope at most of its inputs. Slope-means-no-gap is all that survives of that belief: a slope means continuity, and never the other way.

13f6The quotient rule, from the product and chain rules

13e's chains already found the slope of 1/(x² + 1) using only 1/r's slope. That is the general tech.

Reciprocal technique for slopes: if g has a slope at w and g(w) ≠ 0, then 1/g has a slope at w, and (1/g)′(w) = −g′(w)/g(w)².

g \text{ continuous at } w,\ \ g(w) \ne 0 @@ slope-means-no-gap (13b); the hypothesis
g(x) \ne 0 \text{ on an open interval around } w @@ away-from-zero (10c), with M = g(w): 1/g is defined there (10c's line for 1/g)
\tfrac1g:\ \ r = g(x),\ \ y = \tfrac1r @@ name the inside r; the outside is 1/r (equal-functions)
\big(\tfrac1g\big)'(w) = -\dfrac{1}{g(w)^2}\cdot g'(w) \quad \blacksquare @@ chain for slopes (13e), with slope-of-one-over-x (13a) at r₀ = g(w) ≠ 0

Quotient technique for slopes (the quotient rule): if f and g have slopes at w and g(w) ≠ 0, then f/g has a slope at w, and (f/g)′(w) = (f′(w)g(w) − f(w)g′(w))/g(w)².

\dfrac{f}{g} = f \cdot \dfrac{1}{g} \text{ on an open interval around } w @@ 5c's line for dividing, read in ℝ: f(x)/g(x) = f(x) · g(x)⁻¹; equal-functions; the interval of reciprocal for slopes
\Big(\dfrac{f}{g}\Big)'(w) = f'(w)\cdot\dfrac{1}{g(w)} + f(w)\cdot\Big(-\dfrac{g'(w)}{g(w)^2}\Big) @@ slopes-decided-near, then product for slopes (13c) with reciprocal for slopes
= \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{f'(w)\,g(w) - f(w)\,g'(w)}{g(w)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one bottom (5h), read in ℝ; negative-out on ℚ

Built, not assumed: the quotient rule needs nothing beyond the product rule, the chain rule and the slope of 1/x. The order in the top matters, because subtraction cannot be turned about: the slope of the top times the bottom, less the top times the slope of the bottom.

\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} \text{ at } 2:\ \ \dfrac{4 \cdot 1 - 5 \cdot 1}{1^2} = \htmlClass{new}{-1} @@ quotient for slopes: f(2) = 5, f′(2) = 4, g(2) = 1, g′(2) = 1
\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1} = x + 1 + \dfrac{2}{x - 1} @@ 9g's polynomial-division, as a check
\text{slope at } 2:\ \ 1 - \dfrac{2}{(2 - 1)^2} = -1 @@ slopes-add, slope-of-a-line, and reciprocal for slopes with constant-factor for slopes: the same
\dfrac{2x + 3}{x^2 + 1} \text{ at } 1:\ \ \dfrac{2 \cdot 2 - 5 \cdot 2}{2^2} = -\tfrac32 @@ quotient for slopes

Negative-powers technique: for every number n ≥ 1 and every w ≠ 0, x^{−n} has a slope at w, and it is −n w^{−n−1}. By 8i's line x^{−n} is 1/xⁿ. Reciprocal for slopes with power for slopes gives −n w^{n−1}/(wⁿ)², and 8i's whole-exponent makes that −n w^{−n−1}. With n = 1 it is slope-of-one-over-x again. So the power rule's shape, n x^{n−1}, gives the slope of every whole power (power for slopes). It gives it for every negative integer power away from 0 (negative-powers), and for the power ½ (13e's slope-of-√).

13f8Running it backwards

Handed a function in pieces, find each joint by glued-functions, then compare the pieces' slopes there by joint-slopes: equal, a slope; unequal, none. Handed a family of pieces to tune, write the two conditions, equal heights and equal slopes, and solve them together, as 5g solved two equations at once. Handed a quotient-rule answer, (f′g − fg′)/g², read off f and g from the bottom and the shape of the top. Found upward; written downward.

That completes the core of rung 13. The slope at a point is a limit of chord slopes. A slope means no gap. Products, powers, chains and quotients have rules, each proved once from the definition. And at a joint the pieces must arrive with one slope for the function to have one.
