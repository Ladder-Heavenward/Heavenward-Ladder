29h · support

Spaces flat up close

No single flat map shows the whole earth without a tear. Latitude and longitude break at the poles, where every longitude meets. An atlas solves this with many pages. Each page shows a piece of the earth flat, the pieces overlap, and the overlaps say how to turn from one page to the next. This step makes that exact. A space that is flat up close is one covered by such pages. Its ruler, its lengths and its bends are worked out page by page, and the overlaps make sure every page agrees.

A surface is a space covered by charts, flat pages that each show a piece of it, whose overlaps can be undone with undoable slope grids. Everything rung 29 measures can be measured chart by chart, and the charts agree.

29h1Pages of an atlas

A chart of a surface is a one-to-one map from a piece of it onto an open region of the plane, which can be undone by a smoothly sloped patch r(u, v) whose ruler grid is undoable, EG − F² > 0. A collection of charts that covers the whole surface is an atlas.

Latitude and longitude are not a chart at the poles. There G = R² cos² φ = 0, so EG − F² = 0, and every longitude names the same point.

Two-charts technique: the unit sphere is covered by two charts: from the north pole, (x, y, z) ↦ (x, y)/(1 − z), on every point but the north pole; and from the south pole, (x, y, z) ↦ (x, y)/(1 + z), on every point but the south pole.

(X, Y) = \frac{(x, y)}{1 - z},\ \ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1,\ z \ne 1 @@ the line from the north pole through the point meets the plane z = 0 there
X^2 + Y^2 = \frac{1 - z^2}{(1 - z)^2} = \frac{1 + z}{1 - z} @@ x² + y² = 1 − z²
z = \frac{X^2 + Y^2 - 1}{X^2 + Y^2 + 1},\ \ (x, y) = \frac{2(X, Y)}{X^2 + Y^2 + 1} @@ solve the line above for z; then 1 − z = 2/(X² + Y² + 1)
\text{every } (X, Y) \text{ comes from exactly one point; the undoing is smoothly sloped} @@ the formulas above, a quotient with a bottom never 0
E = G = \frac{4}{(1 + X^2 + Y^2)^2},\ \ F = 0 @@ the slopes of the undoing, dotted (29d1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a chart on all but the north pole; the south one the same, with } -z} \quad \blacksquare @@ EG − F² > 0 everywhere; together they cover the sphere

Look at that ruler: 4(dX² + dY²)/(1 + X² + Y²)². It is the disc plane’s ruler of 29d4 with one sign changed. The round geometry and the saddle geometry are mirror images of each other on paper.

29h2Where the pages overlap

Where two charts both show a point, the surface gives a way to go from one page to the other: undo the first chart, then apply the second. That is the change of page.

Page-change technique: for the sphere’s two charts, the change of page is (X, Y) ↦ (X, Y)/(X² + Y²), which is undoable and has an undoable slope grid at every point of the overlap.

\text{north page } (X, Y),\ \text{south page } (X^{\prime}, Y^{\prime}) = \frac{(x, y)}{1 + z} @@ the two charts
(1 - z)(1 + z) = 1 - z^2 = x^2 + y^2 @@ on the sphere
(X^{\prime}, Y^{\prime}) = \frac{(x, y)(1 - z)}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{(X, Y)}{X^2 + Y^2} @@ multiply top and bottom by 1 − z; then (x, y)/(1 − z) = (X, Y) and x² + y² = (X² + Y²)(1 − z)²
\text{as a complex number, } w \mapsto 1/\bar w @@ (X + iY)/(X² + Y²) = 1/(X − iY) (9·cb8)
\text{it is its own undoing, and its slope grid has determinant } -1/(X^2 + Y^2)^2 \ne 0 @@ apply it twice; slopes of the quotient (28a)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the change of page is undoable, with an undoable slope grid}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the overlap leaves out both poles, so X² + Y² ≠ 0

29h3Every page tells the same story

A surface, or a space flat up close, is a set with an atlas whose changes of page are all undoable with undoable slope grids. A ruler on it is a ruler grid on each page, and where pages overlap, the grids must match by 29d3: g on one page is Jᵀ g J on the other, with J the slope grid of the change.

Page-agree technique: on a surface with a ruler, the length of a curve, the angle between two curves, the area of a region and the surface bend at a point come out the same on every page that shows them.

\text{the two grids are } g \text{ and } J^{\mathsf T} g J @@ how rulers on overlapping pages must match
\text{lengths and angles agree} @@ ruler-change (29d3)
\sqrt{\det(J^{\mathsf T} g J)} = |\det J|\,\sqrt{\det g} @@ det-product (27e); areas agree, as in 28n’s patch technique
\text{K is built from the ruler alone} @@ remarkable technique (29e4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every page agrees on length, angle, area and bend}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for K: the grid and its slopes are matched point by point, and the formula reads the same surface

So a surface needs no room around it. The disc plane is a surface with one page and a ruler. A creature living on a surface can build an atlas, measure, and find the surface bend, without ever knowing whether there is a space outside. Rung 29’s questions about space itself can be asked the same way, from inside.

29h4Illegal moves

\text{latitude and longitude are one chart of the whole sphere}\,? @@ at the poles every longitude names the same point, and EG − F² = 0
\text{a chart keeps lengths}\,? @@ a chart is only one-to-one and smooth; the ruler on the page says what the lengths are
\text{the page grids of two charts are equal on the overlap}\,? @@ they match through Jᵀ g J, not equality
\text{a surface must sit inside space}\,? @@ an atlas and a ruler are enough

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A chart is a coordinate chart, an atlas is an atlas, and the change of page is the transition map. A set with an atlas whose transition maps are smooth with invertible Jacobians is a smooth manifold; with a ruler on it, a Riemannian manifold. The two charts of the sphere are stereographic projections; stereographic projection keeps angles, which is why its ruler has F = 0 and E = G.

Who said it first

Stereographic projection was used by Hipparchus in the second century BCE and described by Ptolemy for star maps; the astrolabe is built on it. Bernhard Riemann spoke of spaces described piece by piece in 1854. Hermann Weyl gave the modern definition by charts and their overlaps in The Concept of a Riemann Surface in 1913, and Hassler Whitney proved in 1936 that every such space can be placed inside a flat space of high enough dimension.
