Counting Alone Says How Things Connect

A road map, a family of molecules, a list of who knows whom at a party, the hospitals and the doctors who rank them: each is a set of things and a list of which pairs are joined. Forget where the things are and what they are made of, and what is left is a graph, dots and links. This rung asks what can be known about a graph from counting alone. It finds that counting decides a great deal. Adding the links at each dot counts every link twice, and from that one fact come the shape of trees, which walks can use every link once, why some graphs cannot be drawn flat, and why five colours suffice for any map. Counting the neighbours of every group decides when everyone can be matched, and counting the room across every cut decides how much a network can carry; the same count, turned into prices, finds the best plan of a factory and the best way to play a game. Then counting turns against disorder: put enough things in few enough boxes, or colour the links of a large enough party, and some order must appear, even where no one can say where.

Adding the links at each dot counts every link twice; from that, a tree has one link fewer than dots, a walk uses every link once only when the odd dots are none or two, and a flat graph has at most 3V − 6 links and five colours. Everyone can be matched exactly when no group has too few neighbours, the most a network carries is its smallest cut, and the best plan meets the least price. Large enough disorder always holds order.

What it means to say it

To say that counting alone says how things connect is to say that, for a graph, questions about what exists (a path, a tour, a flat drawing, a colouring, a matching, a flow, a best plan, an orderly group) are settled by counts: of the links at each dot, of dots, links and faces, of the neighbours of each group, of the room across each cut, of the ways things can fall into boxes. In each case a count that is easy to make rules out, or forces, something that would be hard to find.

The links at the dots add to twice the links; from that, trees have n − 1 links and number nⁿ⁻², a tour of every link exists exactly when no dot or two dots are odd, and flat graphs have V − E + F = 2 and five colours; a two-sided graph matches one side exactly when no group has fewer neighbours than members, the largest flow equals the smallest cut, the best plan equals the least covering price, and every game of gain against loss has a value; and in every red and blue colouring of a large enough party some k guests are joined in one colour, while some colourings of 2^(k/2) guests have no such group. The rung proves it, and one step, that a closed curve splits the sheet in two, is held in trust.

Where it comes from

In 1736 a walk over the seven bridges of a Baltic city was shown impossible by counting the bridges at each bank, and the counting of dots, links and faces of solids came in 1750 and 1752. Trees were counted in 1857, for chemistry in 1874, and named trees in 1889. Four colours for maps were guessed in 1852, and five were proved in 1890. In 1928 every game of gain against loss was shown to have a value. The marriage technique came in 1935, and the same year order in every large enough disorder, which had been proved for one case in 1930. Flows and cuts came in 1956, with best plans in factories and shipping from 1939 to 1947, and the method that finds them by walking corners in 1947. Random colourings were used to prove a colouring exists in 1947. Four colours were proved by computer in 1976. The names are in the basements of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things.

First, every link has two ends, so the links at the dots add to twice the links. A walk can be cut to a path, and a graph splits into two sides exactly when it has no cycle of odd length.

Second, a tree is a graph in one piece with no cycle; it has n − 1 links, and any two of in one piece, no cycle and n − 1 links give the third. There are nⁿ⁻² trees on n named dots. A walk uses every link once exactly when at most two dots have an odd count of links. Rounds through every dot are another matter, and enough links at each dot are enough for one.

Third, a flat drawing in one piece has V − E + F = 2, so a flat graph has at most 3V − 6 links, a dot with at most five, and five colours; K₅ and K₃,₃ are not flat.

Fourth, on a two-sided graph, one side can be matched exactly when every group has at least as many neighbours as members; the most links in a matching equals the fewest dots that cover every link. In a network, the largest flow equals the smallest cut. A linear program and its twin of prices meet at the same number, and from that every game of gain against loss has a value that each side can guarantee.

Fifth, more than n · k things in n boxes put more than k in one; counting by overlaps counts what lies in none of several sets. Every red and blue colouring of the links of a party of six has a one-colour triangle, and for every k some size of party forces k guests joined in one colour; and an average below one proves that some colouring of a party of 2^(k/2) has none, though no one can name it.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 41k finds shortest paths and cheapest spanning trees by greedy choices and says why greed works there. 41l finds best paths and best alignments of strings from best pieces. 41m keeps dots findable with heaps and hash tables, and 41n runs the simplex method by hand. The call back to rung 19 counts necklaces and graph shapes up to symmetry. The annexes count molecules and their orbitals, build trees of life from distances, let tiny magnets agree on a grid, find what holds cloth and rope together, ship at the least cost, pair doctors with hospitals so no two would rather swap, and end at two research fronts: a proof by computer no one can read, and how large order must be.

What you can do because you know it

You can turn a map, a molecule, a timetable or a market into dots and links and ask the question that counts. You can say when a tour of every road exists, why a graph cannot be drawn flat, and why five colours suffice. You can find the largest matching, the largest flow and the smallest cut, and prove each largest by showing the cut or the cover. You can write a plan as a program and prove it best by prices, and find the value of a game. You can find order that must exist and count how much disorder can hide it.

And you can say exactly what the counts do not do: they say what exists, often without saying how to find it fast, and for rounds through every dot no fast way is known.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 4’s counting, rung 7’s proofs by contradiction and by cases, rung 9’s graphs and matchings, rung 27’s matrices and rung 30’s chances, rung 34’s count on a sphere, and rung 39’s greedy choices, tables and heaps. One thing is held in trust: on 41d, that a closed curve drawn without crossing itself splits the sheet into an inside and an outside. Four green boxes stand on the rung: connections, not families on 41a and 41·chem; counting on 41j; and laws with a known exception rate on 41·bio. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say counting alone says how things connect, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
