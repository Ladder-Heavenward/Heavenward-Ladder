Why Does a Metal Bend Before It Breaks?

Why does a metal bend before it breaks? Randy bends a paperclip open and it stays open. Jean bends a stick of chalk and it snaps. Both are crystals. This page finds what a metal has that chalk has not, why bending a metal makes it harder, and why heat makes it soft again.

A metal changes shape by passing lines through its crystals, one row of bonds at a time. Whatever stops the lines makes the metal hard, and heat that lets atoms change places makes it soft again.

27phys21A Paperclip and a Stick of Chalk

Randy bends a paperclip open and it stays open. Jean bends a stick of chalk and it snaps. Both are crystals: atoms in rows, rows in planes (32·chem). A crystal that springs back has only stretched its bonds. One that stays bent has moved planes of atoms past each other by whole steps, and after a whole step every atom has neighbours again, so the lattice is the same lattice in a new outline.

Slip technique: a crystal changes shape for keeps when one plane of atoms slides over the next by a whole lattice step.

Staying bent comes in two tests. Joy draws a rod of copper through a die and it comes out as wire a hundred times longer: it stayed whole under a pull. Terry hammers a bead of gold into a leaf thin enough to see light through: it stayed whole under blows. Both are slip, plane over plane, with the metal’s bonds re-made at every step.

A metal can pass one test better than the other. Lead hammers into sheet easily and tears when drawn into wire, because under a pull a soft metal necks down at one place and parts there.

27phys22The Sum That Came Out Too Strong

Work out the push needed to slide one whole plane over the next, breaking every bond across the plane at once. The answer is far above what a metal takes before it bends. A first course puts the measured strength at about a tenth of the calculated one. A course in metallurgy puts the shortfall for a careful single crystal at ten thousand to a hundred thousand times. A prediction that fails this badly says one of its givens is false. The given that goes is “all at once”.

27phys23A Line That Moves Like a Ruck in a Carpet

Nobody drags a carpet across a room. They push a ruck along it. In a crystal the ruck is a line: an extra half-plane of atoms that ends inside the crystal. The line moves one step by breaking one row of bonds and making one row, and when it has crossed the crystal the top has slid one step over the bottom. Now walk a loop of lattice steps: five right, five down, five left, five up. In a whole crystal the arrows sum to zero and the loop closes (27). Round the line it does not close.

The arrow that closes it is the same wherever the loop is drawn round that line, and the same all along the line.

Closing-arrow technique: walk a loop of lattice steps that would close in a whole crystal; if an arrow is needed to close it, the loop circles a line of this kind, and that arrow is the step the crystal slips by when the line passes.

If the arrow is at right angles to the line, the line is the edge of the half-plane. If it runs along the line, the planes wind round it like a ramp in a car park. In between, split the arrow into its two parts (27’s components). Such a line cannot end inside a crystal: it runs out to a surface or closes on itself.

27phys24Which Planes Slip

Mike hangs a weight on a single-crystal wire. The pull is along the wire, and slip needs a push along a plane. Take the pull per area, and two angles: one between the pull and the arrow at right angles to the plane, one between the pull and the direction of slip. The push along the plane, in the direction of slip, is the pull per area times the cosine of the first times the cosine of the second (27’s dot product, twice).

It is greatest, one half, when both angles are 45 degrees, and nothing when the plane lies across the pull or along it. Planes packed closest slip most easily, so a crystal has a short list of planes and directions, and the one with the largest product goes first.

Slip-plane technique: of the planes and directions a crystal can slip on, the first to go is the one where cos times cos is largest.

27phys25Why Bending Makes It Harder

Bend the paperclip back and forth and it stiffens. Bending makes more lines, from a few thousand million for each square centimetre of cut surface to a hundred thousand times that, and lines on crossing planes tangle and pin each other. Anything that stops a line makes the metal harder to bend, and there are four such things: other lines; the walls between grains, where the rows change direction; stray atoms of another kind, which do not fit; and small hard particles, which a line has to bow round.

Stop-the-line technique: a metal is made harder by whatever stops its lines moving: more lines, smaller grains, stray atoms, small particles.

Chalk and salt snap because their lines cannot move at all: one step of slip would put like charges beside like (6chem9), so the crystal cracks first (24eng27).

27phys26Heat Undoes It

A smith who has hammered a bar hard heats it to a dull glow and lets it cool, and it is soft again. Heat lets atoms change places. First the tangled lines sort themselves into low walls. Then new grains with almost no lines in them start and grow through the worked metal. This happens in a temperature range of about three tenths to one half of the melting temperature, counted in kelvins.

The rate follows 17’s e: one over the time taken is A times e to the minus Q over RT, so a small rise in temperature shortens the time greatly. Left hot, the new grains go on growing, their size as the square root of the time.

Soften technique: heat between about 0.3 and 0.5 of the melting temperature in kelvins replaces worked grains with new ones, at a rate that grows as e to the minus Q over RT.

Accepted names

Springing back is elastic deformation and staying bent is plastic deformation. The line is a dislocation, edge or screw, and its closing arrow the Burgers vector. Cos times cos is the Schmid factor. Hardening by bending is work hardening; the others are grain-boundary, solid-solution and precipitation strengthening. Softening by heat is annealing: recovery, recrystallization, grain growth. Staying whole under a pull is ductility, under blows or squeezing malleability.
