Why Does a Slope Slide?

Why does a slope slide? A hillside of soil can stand for decades and then, after a week of rain, slump onto the road below. Nothing about its shape changed. Soil holds by friction between its grains, and water in the gaps pushes the grains apart. This page splits the weight on a slope into arrows (21·phys), lets the water carry part of the load, turns stress through an angle with 21’s double angles to draw a circle, finds where soil fails, and measures how safe a slope is, dry and wet.

On any cut through soil, the push across the cut and the slide along it depend on the cut’s angle through cos 2θ and sin 2θ, so together they trace a circle. Soil fails where that circle reaches the grip line, slide = push × tan φ. Water in the pores takes away part of the push, so the same circle slides further toward failure.

23eng21Weight Down the Slope Against the Grip

Randy is checking a cutting beside a new road. Think of a block of soil on a slope at angle β. Split its weight W into an arrow along the slope and an arrow square to it (21·phys).

From here on, on this page, the soil’s grip angle φ is the steepest slope it can stand at when dry and loose: the slide it resists is the push across the surface times tan φ. For Randy’s sandy soil, φ = 35°.

\text{along the slope} = W\sin\beta,\ \ \text{pressing in} = W\cos\beta @@ arrows split (21b)
\text{grip} = W\cos\beta\,\tan\varphi @@ 
\text{factor of safety} = \dfrac{\text{grip}}{\text{pull}} = \dfrac{\tan\varphi}{\tan\beta} @@ W cancels
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\tan 35°}{\tan 25°} = \dfrac{0.700}{0.466} \approx 1.50} @@ the grip is half again the pull

23eng22Water in the Pores Carries Part of the Load

Soil is grains with gaps between them. When the gaps fill with water, the water pushes outward on the grains from every side, and that push is taken away from the push of grain on grain. Only the grain-on-grain push makes grip.

\sigma\prime = \sigma - u @@ grain push = total push − water push
\text{slope soaked to the surface, water seeping down it:}\ \ \dfrac{\sigma\prime}{\sigma} \approx \dfrac{20 - 9.81}{20} \approx 0.51 @@ soil 20 kN per m³, water 9.81
\htmlClass{new}{\text{factor of safety} \approx 0.51 \times 1.50 \approx 0.76} @@ below 1: the slope slides

The same slope that stood with half again the grip it needed now has only three quarters. Nothing was added to the soil; the water simply took over half of the push that held it.

23eng23Stress Turned Through an Angle: the Stress Circle

Deep in the soil, a point is squeezed hardest in one direction, σ₁, and least in the one square to it, σ₃. On a cut at angle θ to the direction of least squeeze, the push across the cut, σ, and the slide along it, τ, follow from splitting the arrows and the double-angle formulas (21d).

\sigma = \tfrac12(\sigma_1 + \sigma_3) + \tfrac12(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\cos 2\theta @@ double angles (21d)
\tau = \tfrac12(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)\sin 2\theta @@ 
(\sigma - 140)^2 + \tau^2 = 60^2 @@ cos² + sin² = 1 (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every cut is a point on one circle: centre } 140, \text{ radius } 60} @@ turning the cut by θ goes 2θ round the circle

Stress-circle technique: the push and slide on every cut through a point lie on one circle, centred at the average of the largest and smallest squeeze, with radius half their difference. One drawing shows every cut at once.

23eng24Failure Where the Circle Touches the Grip Line

A cut slips when its slide reaches its grip: τ = σ tan φ. On the drawing that is a straight line from the origin at angle φ. The soil holds while its circle stays below the line, and fails when the circle touches it.

\text{distance from the centre to the line} = 140\sin\varphi @@ the line through the origin at angle φ
\text{touches when } 60 = 140\sin\varphi:\ \ \sin\varphi \approx 0.43,\ \varphi \approx 25° @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = 35°:\ 140\sin 35° \approx 80 > 60} @@ the circle is clear of the line: safe

In a soil laboratory a technician squeezes three samples of the same soil in a sealed cell at three side pressures, raising the top load until each fails. Drawn on one sheet, the three failure circles all touch a single straight line at the soil’s grip angle, as the technique says.

23eng25The Factor of Safety, and Why Rain Brings Slopes Down

The factor of safety is the grip available over the grip used. Engineers ask for about 1.3 to 1.5 on a permanent slope, to allow for what the samples missed.

state of the slopegrain push sharefactor of safety
dry11.50
soaked halfway downabout 0.75about 1.1
soaked to the surface, seepingabout 0.51about 0.76

Rain does not make soil heavier so much as it takes away the grain push that grips, so a slope safe when dry can fail when soaked. That is why road cuttings get drains to keep water out of the pores, and why landslides follow long rains. An engineer signs off a cutting only after checking it soaked, not dry.

Accepted names

The stress circle is Mohr’s circle; the grain push σ − u is the effective stress and u the pore water pressure. The grip line is the Mohr-Coulomb failure envelope, φ the angle of internal friction, and the squeeze test a triaxial test. The slope worked here is an infinite slope analysis.

Who said it first

Otto Mohr published the stress circle in 1882; Karl Terzaghi set out effective stress in the 1920s, founding soil mechanics (not yet revisited for the dates).
