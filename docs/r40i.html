40i · proof

Chance in computing

Is a polynomial written as a long product the same as another written as a long sum? Multiplying out can take more terms than there are atoms. There is a quicker way. Pick numbers at random, put them in for the letters, and compare the two values. If the two polynomials differ, they agree at only a few points, so a random point gives them away almost always. The answer can be wrong. The chance that it is wrong can be computed before the coins are thrown, and pushed as low as anyone likes by throwing again. This step gives the tape machine a supply of fair coins, and asks what that buys. It buys speed on real questions: checking an identity, checking a product of two large tables of numbers in far fewer steps than computing it. It does not buy the power to decide what could not be decided, and it is not known to buy anything that patience without coins could not also do fast. The statistics floor said that an answer with a stated chance of being wrong is still an answer. Here that becomes a class of questions.

A coin machine is wrong at a known rate, and repeating it with a majority vote pushes the rate down. A polynomial that is not zero is zero at a random point with chance at most its degree over the size of the set the point is drawn from. Chance checks a product of tables quickly. Every coin machine can be run on all its coins in small room.

40i1A machine with coins

A coin machine is a tape machine with an extra tape of coin flips, each 0 or 1 with chance one half, all independent (30f). On an input its answer is now a chance event. A set A is in the class BPP when a coin machine runs within a power of n steps and, on every input, gives the wrong answer about A with chance at most 1/3.

One third looks loose. It is not, because the machine can be run again.

Majority-vote technique: if a coin machine is wrong with chance at most 1/3 on each run, then k independent runs, answered by the majority, are wrong with chance at most 8/k.

X = \text{the }\allowbreak \text{number }\allowbreak \text{of }\allowbreak \text{right }\allowbreak \text{runs }\allowbreak \text{out }\allowbreak \text{of } k @@ a sum of k independent counts, each 1 with chance p ≥ 2/3
E X = kp \ge \tfrac{2k}{3},\ \allowbreak \ \text{Var}\,X = kp(1 - p) \le \tfrac{2k}{9} @@ 30g, with p(1 − p) at most 2/9 for p ≥ 2/3
\text{the }\allowbreak \text{majority }\allowbreak \text{is }\allowbreak \text{wrong }\allowbreak \text{only }\allowbreak \text{if } X \le \tfrac k2\text{, so } |X - E X| \ge \tfrac k6 @@ 2k/3 − k/2 = k/6
P(|X - E X| \ge \tfrac k6) \le \frac{2k/9}{(k/6)^2} @@ the spread-bound technique (30h)
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac{8}{k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (2k/9) · 36/k²
Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

A coin machine never makes its answer certain. It makes the chance of error a number fixed before it runs, the same on every input, and it lowers the number by repetition, at a cost in steps that is also fixed in advance.

A coin machine is judged by its chance of error, and that chance is stated before the coins are thrown, for every input alike.

This bound is crude: for an error of one in a million it asks for eight million runs. A sharper bound, named in the basement, shows the chance falls by a fixed factor with each run, so a few hundred runs bring it below the chance that a cosmic ray flips a bit in the machine’s memory during the run.

A test for whether a number is prime, run at a bank in Seattle, is wrong with chance at most 1/4 each run. How many runs would you ask for before signing a key with it, and against what other risk would you set the chance you accept?

40i2Is this identity true?

A polynomial in letters x₁, …, xₖ may be given as a formula that is short to write and long to multiply out: (x₁ + y₁)(x₂ + y₂)⋯(x₄₀ + y₄₀) has 2⁴⁰, about a million million, terms. Its value at given numbers is quick to compute. The degree of a term is the sum of its letters’ powers, and the polynomial’s degree is the largest.

Random-point technique: if a polynomial p in k letters has degree at most d and is not the zero polynomial, and each letter is given a value drawn at random from a set of s numbers, then p is 0 at that point with chance at most d/s.

k = 1\text{: } p \text{ has }\allowbreak \text{at }\allowbreak \text{most } d \text{ roots, }\allowbreak \text{so }\allowbreak \text{chance }\allowbreak \text{at }\allowbreak \text{most } d/s @@ the root technique (25d): p = u(x − r₁)⋯(x − r_d)
p = \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{j} x_1^i\, q_i(x_2, \dots, x_k),\ \allowbreak \ q_j \ne 0 @@ sort by the power of x₁; q_j has degree at most d − j
P(q_j = 0) \le \tfrac{d - j}{s} @@ the claim for k − 1 letters (induction, 4c)
\text{if } q_j \ne 0 \text{ at }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{drawn } x_2, \dots\text{, } p \text{ is }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{nonzero }\allowbreak \text{polynomial }\allowbreak \text{in } x_1 \text{ of }\allowbreak \text{degree } j @@ so 0 with chance at most j/s, by the case k = 1
\htmlClass{new}{P(p = 0) \le \tfrac{d - j}{s} + \tfrac{j}{s} = \tfrac{d}{s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chance of an or is at most the sum (30f)

To test whether two formulas f and g give the same polynomial, test p = f − g. If they are the same, p is 0 everywhere and the test always says so. If not, it says so wrongly with chance at most d/s. For (x + y)³ against x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + y³, the degree is 3; draw from 1 to 1000 and a wrong pair slips through with chance at most 3/1000. No fast method without coins is known for this question in general.

40i3Checking a product of tables

Multiplying two n × n tables of numbers in the plain way takes about n³ multiplications, as 27j’s elimination did. Checking a claimed product AB = C can be done in about n² with coins.

Random-column technique: if AB ≠ C and r is a column of n entries, each 0 or 1 at random, then A(Br) = Cr with chance at most 1/2.

D = AB - C \ne 0\text{, with } d_{ij} \ne 0 @@ some entry differs
(Dr)_i = d_{ij} r_j + \textstyle\sum_{l \ne j} d_{il} r_l @@ the ith entry of Dr
\text{fix }\allowbreak \text{every } r_l \text{ with } l \ne j\text{; }\allowbreak \text{at }\allowbreak \text{most }\allowbreak \text{one }\allowbreak \text{of } r_j = 0,\ \allowbreak r_j = 1 \text{ makes }\allowbreak \text{it } 0 @@ the two values differ by d_ij ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{P(Dr = 0) \le \tfrac12} \quad \blacksquare @@ whole chance (30f), over the other entries
  
A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix},\ \allowbreak B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \allowbreak C = \begin{pmatrix}1&3\\3&8\end{pmatrix} @@ the true product has 7 in the corner
r = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}:\ Br = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ \allowbreak A(Br) = \begin{pmatrix}3\\7\end{pmatrix},\ \allowbreak Cr = \begin{pmatrix}3\\8\end{pmatrix} @@ about n² steps each
\htmlClass{new}{A(Br) \ne Cr:\ C \text{ is wrong}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with r = (1, 0) the check would have passed

Twenty columns drawn independently miss a wrong C with chance at most 2⁻²⁰, about one in a million, for 20 · 3n² steps, far below n³ once n is in the thousands.

40i4Where chance sits

All-coins technique: every set in BPP is in PSPACE.

\text{the }\allowbreak \text{coin }\allowbreak \text{machine }\allowbreak \text{runs }\allowbreak \text{within } n^a \text{ steps, }\allowbreak \text{so }\allowbreak \text{it }\allowbreak \text{reads }\allowbreak \text{at }\allowbreak \text{most } n^a \text{ coins} @@ one coin a step at most
\text{run }\allowbreak \text{it }\allowbreak \text{on }\allowbreak \text{every }\allowbreak \text{string }\allowbreak \text{of } n^a \text{ coins, }\allowbreak \text{one }\allowbreak \text{after }\allowbreak \text{another, }\allowbreak \text{reusing }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{room} @@ 2 to the n^a runs, each in room at most n^a
\text{count }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{strings }\allowbreak \text{that }\allowbreak \text{give }\allowbreak \text{yes, }\allowbreak \text{in }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{counter }\allowbreak \text{of } n^a + 1 \text{ digits} @@ room again a power of n
\htmlClass{new}{\text{say }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{yes }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{when }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{count }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{passes }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{half}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the right answer has chance at least 2/3, so more than half the strings give it

So P sits in BPP, which sits in PSPACE. Coins do not decide anything undecidable either: run the coin machine on every coin string and the answer comes out without chance, slowly. What coins buy is speed, and whether they buy it in the end is open. For decades the best quick test of whether a number is prime used coins, and in 2002 a test without coins was found that is fast. Many now expect BPP to equal P, with coins replaced by numbers that only look random. That is not proved.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A coin machine is a probabilistic Turing machine, and BPP is bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time. The majority-vote technique is amplification; the sharper bound is the Chernoff bound, which gives an error below e^(−k/18) for k runs. The random-point technique is the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, and testing whether a formula is the zero polynomial is polynomial identity testing. The random-column technique is Freivalds’ algorithm. The all-coins technique is BPP ⊆ PSPACE; it is also known that BPP lies in the second level of the polynomial hierarchy.

Who said it first

Rūsiņš Freivalds gave his check in 1977. Jacob Schwartz (1980) and Richard Zippel (1979) proved the random-point bound, with an earlier form by Richard DeMillo and Richard Lipton (1978). Robert Solovay and Volker Strassen (1977) and Michael Rabin (1980, after Gary Miller, 1976) gave coin tests for primes; Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena (2002) gave a fast test without coins. John Gill defined BPP in 1977. Russell Impagliazzo and Avi Wigderson (1997) showed that BPP equals P if some problems are hard enough in a precise sense.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each run is wrong with chance 1/3, so two runs are both wrong with chance 1/9, and the answer of two runs is wrong with chance 1/9.” Not allowed: with two runs that disagree there is no majority, and the chance both are wrong is not the chance the answer is wrong. Take an odd number of runs, and bound the majority, as the technique does.
