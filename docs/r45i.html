45i · technique

Universal Properties by Hand

A universal property is quick to write and slow to check. This step checks some by hand, in three places where the answer is not the one a first guess gives. In the counting numbers ordered by dividing, the product of two numbers is not their product: it is their greatest common divisor, and their sum is the least number both divide. Among groups, the sum of two copies of the whole numbers is not the pairs of whole numbers, and the way to prove it is to count the maps into a small group and find the counts disagree. And among fields, some pairs have no product at all, because a map of fields cannot change whether one plus one is nothing. The recipe each time is the same: guess a thing, find the arrows out of it, prove that every rival runs through it in one way, and when the guess fails, count arrows to show that nothing could pass.

Guess from sets, check that every rival runs through the guess, and check that it runs in only one way. In the dividing order the product is the greatest common divisor and the sum the least common multiple. Counting maps into the shuffles of three shows that the pairs of whole numbers are not the sum of two copies among groups. A map of fields keeps whether one plus one is nothing, so the clock of two and the fractions have no product among fields.

45i1Products in the Dividing Order

Order the counting numbers by dividing: one arrow m → n exactly when m divides n. 45a2’s order-category technique makes this a category, since n | n and dividing is transitive (4j). A product of m and n is then a number p that divides both, such that every number dividing both divides p.

Divides-order technique: in the dividing order, the product of two counting numbers is their greatest common divisor and their sum is the least number both divide; 1 has an arrow to every number and 0 has an arrow from every number.

12 = 2^2 \cdot 3,\ \ 18 = 2 \cdot 3^2 @@ unique factorisation (4j)
d \mid 12 \land d \mid 18 \iff d = 2^i 3^j,\ i \le 1,\ j \le 1 @@ a divisor’s prime list sits inside each; least power of each prime
\text{the common divisors are } 1, 2, 3, 6,\ \text{and each divides } 6 @@ every one of them divides 2¹3¹
\text{product of } 12 \text{ and } 18 = 6 @@ 6 divides both, and every rival divides 6; there is at most one arrow, so “exactly one” is free
12 \mid m \land 18 \mid m \iff 2^2 3^2 \mid m @@ greatest power of each prime
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sum of } 12 \text{ and } 18 = 36,\ \ 1 \mid n,\ \ n \mid 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ 36 is divided by both and divides every rival; 1 divides every n, and every n divides 0

In an order, “exactly one” never needs checking: there is at most one arrow. All the work is in finding a thing that every rival runs through. Here the greatest common divisor is greatest in the dividing order, not only in size, and that is what 4j’s prime lists show.

45i2Counting Arrows to Tell Things Apart

A guess fails when nothing passes the property. To show a thing is not a sum, it is often enough to count arrows. If S is a sum of A and B, the maps out of S into X match with pairs of maps, one out of A and one out of B. So the count of maps S → X is the count from A times the count from B, for every X.

Count-the-arrows technique: if S is a sum of A and B, the number of arrows from S to X is the number from A to X times the number from B to X, for every X; a thing whose counts disagree for one X is no sum of A and B.

|\mathbf{Grp}(\mathbb Z, S_3)| = 6 @@ the arrows-from-one technique (45g1): maps from ℤ are members
\text{a sum } S \text{ of } \mathbb Z \text{ and } \mathbb Z:\ \ |\mathbf{Grp}(S, S_3)| = 6 \cdot 6 = 36 @@ the property: a map out of S is a pair of maps
\mathbf{Grp}(\mathbb Z \times \mathbb Z, S_3) \leftrightarrow \text{pairs } (g, h) \text{ with } g h = h g @@ (1, 0) and (0, 1) change places in ℤ × ℤ, so their images must
\text{pairs that change places in } S_3:\ \ 6 + 2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 2 = 18 @@ e with all 6; each turn with the 3 powers of r; each flip with e and itself
\htmlClass{new}{18 \ne 36,\ \text{so } \mathbb Z \times \mathbb Z \text{ is no sum of } \mathbb Z \text{ and } \mathbb Z \text{ in } \mathbf{Grp}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum is the free group on two letters (45f4), with 36

In the category of turn-about groups the same count runs the other way. There every X changes places with itself, so every pair of maps from ℤ into a turn-about X gives one map from ℤ × ℤ, and ℤ × ℤ is the sum. The thing is the same; the category, and so the answer, is not.

45i3When Nothing Passes

Fields, with the ring maps between them (43a): a ring map keeps sums, products and 1. A product of two fields would be a field P with a ring map to each.

No-common-field technique: no field has a ring map both to the clock of two and to the fractions, so the two have no product among fields.

\varphi : P \to \mathbb Z/2,\ \ \psi : P \to \mathbb Q \text{ ring maps} @@ suppose a product, or any field with both
\varphi(1 + 1) = 1 + 1 = 0,\ \ \psi(1 + 1) = 2 @@ ring maps keep 1 and sums
1 + 1 = 0 \text{ in } P \Rightarrow 2 = \psi(0) = 0 \text{ in } \mathbb Q @@ case one: against 5d
1 + 1 \ne 0 \text{ in } P \Rightarrow \text{some } u \in P,\ u(1 + 1) = 1 @@ case two: P is a field, so 1 + 1 has a reciprocal
\varphi(u) \cdot 0 = \varphi(u)\varphi(1 + 1) = \varphi(1) = 1 \text{ in } \mathbb Z/2 @@ ring maps keep products
\htmlClass{new}{0 = 1 \text{ in } \mathbb Z/2,\ \text{totally false either way}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 5e: x · 0 = 0; both cases end at ⊥

The recipe ends here: guess, check every rival runs through, check it runs in one way, and if no guess survives, find what every arrow must keep. Fields keep whether 1 + 1 is 0, and so all arrows stay inside fields that agree on it.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

In the divisibility poset, meet is gcd and join is lcm; 1 is the initial object (bottom) and 0 the terminal object (top). The count-the-arrows technique is the use of Hom-sets into a test object as invariants; the sum of groups is the free product, and in abelian groups the coproduct of finitely many is the direct sum, which is also the product. Fields have no initial object and no products in general, because ring maps preserve the characteristic. ℤ is the initial ring.

Who said it first

The free product of groups is from Otto Schreier (1927); Saunders Mac Lane read it as the dual of the direct product in 1950. Ernst Steinitz’s 1910 paper on fields defined the characteristic, the fact the no-common-field technique spends.

A proof that is not allowed

“ℤ × ℤ is the sum of ℤ and ℤ, since a map out of it is fixed by where (1, 0) and (0, 1) go.” Not allowed in Grp: it is fixed by them, but they cannot go anywhere: their images must change places. The sum’s property asks that every pair of maps out of the two copies runs through, and the pair sending 1 to two flips of S₃ that do not change places does not.
