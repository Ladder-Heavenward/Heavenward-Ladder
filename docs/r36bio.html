36·bio · biology

True Blue Is Rare in Nature

True blue is rare in nature: why should that be? Walk a trail in the forests of the Olympic Peninsula and count colours. Greens everywhere, browns and greys, the yellow of a banana slug, the red of a salmonberry, the orange of a chanterelle. Then a Steller’s jay lands on a branch, deep blue from crest to tail, and it is one of the few blue things on the walk. Birds, butterflies and flowers are blue far less often than they are red, yellow or brown, and when they are, the blue is often not a pigment at all. What makes the question hard is that blue light is plentiful. The sky is full of it. So the shortage is not in the light but in the living things, and the question is what it takes for a living thing to send blue back to an eye. This page answers with the rung’s tools: a photon’s gap, the three numbers, and a test by cases with its rate.

A pigment looks blue when it takes away the long end of the window, and that takes a small energy gap, which few living molecules have. So most blue in animals is made by structure instead: tiny pockets in a feather or a wing scale, spaced so that short waves come back in step. Crush the structure and the blue goes; crush a pigment and its colour stays. And small things scatter short waves far more than long ones, which is why the sky is blue and why the blue of a jay is not in any molecule of it. A safe blue pigment is a long chain of making, and its cost grows like a power of the steps; layers that bend light make blue for almost nothing, and their blue moves toward violet as they tilt.

36bio1What a Blue Pigment Must Take Away

A pigment’s colour is what it leaves. To look blue, it must leave the short end of the window and take away the long end: the oranges and reds near 600 nm and beyond. By 36·phys’s gap-to-wavelength technique, a photon at 600 nm has energy hc/λ ≈ 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ / 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 3.31 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. So the pigment’s molecule needs a gap that small or smaller.

Small-gap technique: a pigment that looks blue by taking away light at 600 nm or longer has an energy gap of at most about 3.31 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Proof: the gap-to-wavelength technique, with λ ≥ 600 nm, and 5j3: a longer wavelength is a smaller energy.

Here the chemistry is a fact by cases with its rate (36g3). Small gaps come from electrons shared along long chains of bonds. The common pigments of living things have larger gaps: the carotenoids that make a chanterelle orange take away blue and green; the chlorophyll of a leaf takes away red and blue and leaves green; melanin takes away nearly everything and leaves brown and black. Flowers that look blue, like the camas of a Willamette Valley meadow, mostly use pigments that are red or purple in acid, made blue by a less acid sap or by metal held in the molecule. So blue pigments are made, and they are made rarely, at a cost.

36bio2Blue Made by Structure

A Steller’s jay has no blue pigment. Inside each barb of a blue feather is a spongy layer of keratin full of air pockets, far smaller than the wavelengths of the window and spaced fairly evenly, over a floor of dark melanin. Light that bounces off many pockets comes back as many waves. Where the spacing makes the waves of one wavelength come back in step, they add, as 32’s turnings in step add; for other wavelengths they come back out of step and cancel. With the jay’s spacing, the waves that add are blue. The melanin beneath takes away what passes through, so the blue is not washed out.

The test is a fact by cases. Crush a blue jay feather and the blue goes: the pockets are gone, and what is left is the grey-brown of the melanin. Crush a red feather of a house finch, coloured by carotenoids from its food, and the red stays, since every crumb still holds the molecule. Wet a structural blue feather with a liquid that bends light as keratin does, and the pockets stop sending light back, and the blue fades until it dries. Across the feathers that have been tried, the rate is high and not perfect: some feathers mix a pigment with a structure.

So the rung’s mix is at work. The structure sends back a spectrum heavy at the short end, the melanin takes away the rest, and col of what reaches the eye is a blue that no molecule of the bird is.

36bio3Small Things Scatter Short Waves

A thing much smaller than the wavelength scatters light, and measurements over a long time give a law with its range: the share scattered goes as 1/λ⁴, while the thing stays small against λ. It holds for the molecules of the air and for the finest pockets in a feather; it fails for drops as large as the wavelength or larger, which scatter every colour alike and make clouds white.

Short-wave-share technique: if the share scattered goes as 1/λ⁴, light at λ₁ is scattered (λ₂/λ₁)⁴ times as much as light at λ₂. Proof: divide 1/λ₁⁴ by 1/λ₂⁴ (5b), and 13m’s shares through a power.

That is why the sky is blue, and why it is not violet: the sun sends less violet than blue, and the three curves of 36b3 take little at 400 nm. And it is why blue is the easy colour for structure. A structure only has to be small and fine to send back the short end, while a pigment has to find a small gap. Living things, it seems, have found the fine structure far more often than the small gap.

36bio4The Price of a Safe Blue

A blue pigment needs a small gap (36·bio1), and a small gap is a danger to the living thing that holds it. A molecule that takes visible light easily is lifted up its gap again and again in daylight, and a lifted molecule can hand its energy to the oxygen around it. Oxygen lifted that way attacks the molecules of a cell. Dyes that do this well are used on purpose to kill germs under a lamp; one of them is a blue dye. So a living thing that makes a blue pigment must also make it safe: hold it inside a protein, bind a metal to it, or keep it where little light reaches it. Each of those is more making.

How much more can be put as a model. Suppose a substance is made in n steps in a row, and each step turns a share p of what reaches it into the next stuff and loses the rest. The steps are a path through 30l’s tree.

Chain-cost technique: a substance made by n steps in a row, each keeping a share p of what reaches it, keeps pⁿ of the start, so one unit of it costs 1/pⁿ units of the start. Proof: the branch-product technique (30l), with every branch’s share p, gives pⁿ; to have 1 at the end, the start must be 1/pⁿ (5b).

p = 0.9,\ \allowbreak \ n = 20:\ \ 1/0.9^{20} \approx 8.23 @@ the same, with a metal and a protein to make it safe

The shares and step counts are made up to show the shape of the argument; the shape is what the technique proves. A cost that grows like a power punishes long chains, and a safe blue pigment is a long chain. A structure costs almost nothing past the keratin a feather is made of anyway: its pockets form as the keratin sets, the way oil and water part. 36bio5Blue Made by Layers That Bend Light

A morpho butterfly’s wing is blue from layers, not pockets. Each scale carries ridges, and each ridge a stack of thin shelves of chitin with air between them, the stack repeating with a period: a shelf of chitin of thickness d₁ and index n₁ ≈ 1.56, then air of thickness d₂ and index n₂ = 1. Light crossing into a shelf bends by 23·phys’s kept-sine technique, n sin θ the same on both sides, so inside the chitin it runs at the angle θ₁ with sin θ₁ = sin θ/1.56 for light that meets the stack at θ from straight on.

What comes back is many waves, one from each period, and 21·then’s two-slit technique counts when they are in step. Light sent back by one period and by the next differ in path by 2(n₁d₁ cos θ₁ + n₂d₂ cos θ₂), which is laid down here from the geometry of the shelves; where that difference is a whole number m of wavelengths, the waves add, and the stack sends that wavelength back strongly.

Tilt-shift technique: a stack that sends back λ = 2(n₁d₁ + n₂d₂)/m straight on sends back a shorter wavelength the more it is tilted. Proof: as θ grows from 0, sin θ grows, so sin θᵢ = sin θ/nᵢ grows in each layer and cos θᵢ falls (21b); every term nᵢdᵢ cos θᵢ falls, and so does λ = 2(n₁d₁ cos θ₁ + n₂d₂ cos θ₂)/m.

\theta = 0:\ \ 2(1.56 \cdot 75 + 118) = 2(117 + 118) = 470\ \text{nm} @@ m = 1: a blue
\theta = 30^\circ:\ \ \sin\theta_1 = 0.5/1.56 \approx 0.321,\ \allowbreak \ \cos\theta_1 \approx 0.947,\ \allowbreak \ \cos 30^\circ \approx 0.866 @@ kept-sine (23·phys), then 21b
2(117 \cdot 0.947 + 118 \cdot 0.866) \approx 426\ \text{nm} @@ a violet-blue

So a morpho flashes blue and darkens to violet as it turns, and a jay’s pockets, which have no one direction, stay the same blue from every side. Both are blue made without a blue molecule, by the arithmetic of path lengths, and the layers use bending to do it. Wet the wing with alcohol, whose index of about 1.36 lies between air’s and chitin’s, and the air terms grow: 2(117 + 1.36 · 118) ≈ 555 nm, and the wing turns green until it dries. A liquid of index 1.56 would leave nothing between the shelves to bend the light, and the blue would go: the test by cases of 36·bio2 again.

The accepted names

Blue made by structure is structural colour; the jay’s spongy keratin makes non-iridescent structural colour by coherent scattering, and the even layers of a butterfly’s scales make iridescent colour by thin-film interference. Scattering by things much smaller than the wavelength is Rayleigh scattering, and the 1/λ⁴ law is its law; scattering by drops the size of the wavelength is Mie scattering. The common pigments named here are carotenoids, chlorophylls, melanins and anthocyanins. The blue of a cornflower is a metal complex of anthocyanin. A molecule that hands its energy to oxygen is a photosensitizer, the lifted oxygen is singlet oxygen, and using it to kill germs or cells under a lamp is photodynamic therapy; methylene blue is one such dye. The blue pigment held in a protein in blue-green algae is phycocyanin. The morpho’s stack is a multilayer reflector, its colour iridescent, and the in-step rule is constructive interference in a thin-film stack.

Who said it first

John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh, gave the 1/λ⁴ law for small scatterers in 1871 and with it the blue of the sky. Robert Hooke and Isaac Newton both looked at the colours of peacock feathers in the seventeenth century and took them for structure. The jay’s spongy keratin and its even spacing were shown by electron microscopes and by measuring the scattered light, in work through the twentieth century and into the 2000s. The shelves on the ridges of morpho scales were seen with electron microscopes from the 1940s on.
