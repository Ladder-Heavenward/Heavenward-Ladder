28·bio · biology

Why Do Foxes and Rabbits Take Turns?

Why do foxes and rabbits take turns? For about a hundred years the fur traders of northern Canada counted the pelts they bought, and the counts rise and fall in waves about ten years apart. Snowshoe hares boom, and a year or two later the lynx that eat them boom. Then the hares crash, and the lynx crash after them, and the wave starts again. Foxes and rabbits are the old picture of the same dance. 27·then wrote the simplest pair of rules for it, found the one point where both counts would rest, and stepped forward from a start near it. The steps went round and round. But rung twenty-seven could not say whether they truly close. Its grid of slopes at the rest sat exactly on the border line, where a path might spiral in slowly, spiral out slowly, or close up for ever, and the grid cannot tell which. What makes the question hard is that the pictures look the same for a long time. A spiral that drifts in by a hair each turn and a loop that closes are hard to tell apart by stepping, because each step makes an error of its own. This page settles it with the flat maps of rung twenty-eight. It finds a quantity that never changes along any path, shows that its level curves are loops round the rest, and so proves that the paths close. Then it asks what a cull does: why trapping both all year leaves more prey, and why a single cull made at the wrong moment makes every swing after it wider.

Rabbits feed foxes, and foxes eat rabbits. Write the two rates down and look at the vector from the resting point to where the counts are now. That vector always turns the same way: rabbits peak, then foxes peak, then rabbits are at their lowest, then foxes are. So the counts take turns. Turning is not yet closing. The trick is to weigh each count by a slope that grows with it, and then the two rates cancel exactly: one sum of a straight part and a logarithm, in each count, never changes along any path. That quantity is lowest at the rest and rises in every direction from it, so each of its level curves is a single loop round the rest. A path stays on its loop and keeps turning, so it comes back to where it started, and does it again for ever. Averaged over a loop, each count is exactly its resting value. A cull moves the counts onto a different loop: a smaller one if made at the right moment, a larger one if made at the wrong moment. And add a little crowding among the rabbits, and the loops become spirals in: the closing was a balance as fine as the border line. Counted by age, a population is a grid times last year’s, and it grows in the long run by the grid’s one positive stretch.

28bio1The Vector From the Rest Always Turns One Way

Rabbits, x of them in thousands, grow on their own at the rate a, and are eaten at a rate in proportion to how often they meet foxes. Foxes, y of them in hundreds, die off at the rate c, and grow by what they eat. The numbers are 27·then’s, for snowshoe hares and lynx in the forests of the North Cascades: a = 0.8, b = 0.2, c = 0.6 and d = 0.03 a year.

x' = x(a - by),\ \ y' = y(-c + dx),\ \ x^* = \tfrac cd = 20,\ \ y^* = \tfrac ab = 4 @@ the rules, and the rest (the crossed-rest technique, 27·then.3)
\tfrac{\partial x'}{\partial x} = a - by,\ \ \tfrac{\partial x'}{\partial y} = -bx,\ \ \tfrac{\partial y'}{\partial x} = dy,\ \ \tfrac{\partial y'}{\partial y} = -c + dx @@ partial slopes (28a): hold one count still
\text{at the rest: } \begin{pmatrix}0 & -4\\ 0.12 & 0\end{pmatrix},\ \ T = 0,\ \ \Delta = 0.48 @@ the slope grid sits on the border line (27·cb24.3, 27·cb24.4)
u = x - x^*,\ w = y - y^*:\ \ x' = -bx\,w,\ \ y' = dy\,u @@ a = by* and c = dx*
uw' - wu' = dy\,u^2 + bx\,w^2 @@ u′ = x′ and w′ = y′
\htmlClass{new}{uw' - wu' \gt 0 \text{ at every point with } x, y \gt 0 \text{ other than the rest}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each part is at least 0, and they are not both 0

Write the vector from the rest as u = r cos θ, w = r sin θ. The slopes of cos and sin (21) give uw′ − wu′ = r²θ′. Always-round technique: away from the rest, the vector from the rest to the counts always turns the same way, from more rabbits toward more foxes, and never stops turning. So the four marks come round in order: most rabbits, then most foxes, then fewest rabbits, then fewest foxes. That is the taking of turns. The foxes peak a quarter turn after the rabbits.

Turning is not closing. A path that turns for ever can still spiral in to the rest, or out toward the axes. The slope grid cannot settle which, because it sits on the border line.

28bio2A Quantity Kept Along Every Path

Weigh the rabbits’ rate by d − c/x, which is below 0 when rabbits are few and above 0 when they are many. Weigh the foxes’ rate by b − a/y in the same way. The two weighted rates are then the same product, with opposite signs.

V(x, y) = dx - c\ln x + by - a\ln y,\ \ x, y \gt 0 @@ one function of the two counts
\tfrac{\partial V}{\partial x} = d - \tfrac cx,\ \ \tfrac{\partial V}{\partial y} = b - \tfrac ay @@ partial slopes (28a), with the slope of ln (17)
V' = \tfrac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,x' + \tfrac{\partial V}{\partial y}\,y' @@ the roads-add technique (28c), with x and y both moving in time
= \big(d - \tfrac cx\big)\,x(a - by) + \big(b - \tfrac ay\big)\,y(dx - c) @@ the two rules
= (dx - c)(a - by) + (by - a)(dx - c) @@ multiply out each x and each y
\htmlClass{new}{V' = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two products cancel

Kept-quantity technique: along every path of the rules, V = dx − c ln x + by − a ln y never changes, so each path stays on one level curve of V. The same V works for any a, b, c and d above 0. With 27·then’s numbers, V = 0.03x − 0.6 ln x + 0.2y − 0.8 ln y. At the rest it is −1.506; at 30 thousand rabbits and 4 hundred foxes it is −1.450. So the path through (30, 4) can never come near the rest.

Where did V come from? Divide each weighted rate by the other’s bracket and the two are equal. Read backwards, the weights are the slopes of dx − c ln x and of by − a ln y. A kept quantity is a guess that has to be checked, and the check is the column above.

28bio3Level Curves That Are Loops

A level curve of V is every point where V has one value k (28g). Each path lies on one. The question is the shape of those curves.

\varphi(s) = V(x^* + sp,\ y^* + sq),\ \ s \ge 0 @@ V along a ray from the rest, in the direction (p, q) ≠ (0, 0)
\varphi'(s) = \big(d - \tfrac cx\big)p + \big(b - \tfrac ay\big)q,\ \ \varphi'(0) = 0 @@ the roads-add technique (28c); both partial slopes are 0 at the rest
\varphi''(s) = \tfrac{c\,p^2}{x^2} + \tfrac{a\,q^2}{y^2} \gt 0 @@ the roads-add technique again
\varphi \text{ rises for } s \gt 0,\ \text{to no ceiling as the ray nears an axis or runs out} @@ φ′ increases from 0 (14g); −c ln x has no ceiling as x falls to 0, and dx beats c ln x (17d)
\text{each ray meets } V = k \text{ once, at a distance } \rho(\theta) \text{ that changes smoothly with } \theta @@ for k above V at the rest: in-between values (12a); φ′ ≠ 0 there, so the equation fixes ρ (28f)
\theta' \ge m \gt 0 \text{ on that curve} @@ θ′ = (dy u² + bx w²)/r² is above 0 and continuous in θ on [0, 2π] (12c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{within } \tfrac{2\pi}{m} \text{ the path is back where it started, and it then repeats}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one point of the curve on each ray; the rules fix the future from there (24d)

Level-loop technique: every level curve of V above its lowest value is one loop round the rest, and every path other than the rest goes round its loop and comes back to its start, again and again. So the rest is a true centre of the rules. 27·cb24.4’s border line is settled here, not by the slope grid, but by a quantity the grid never saw.

The loop through (30, 4) runs from 12.5 to 30 thousand rabbits and from 2.68 to 5.70 hundred foxes, and takes 9.19 years. Small loops near the rest take 2π/√(ac) ≈ 9.07 years, the pace of the slope grid. Larger loops are slower: the loop through (60, 4) takes 10.3 years.

28bio4Averaged Over a Loop, Each Count Rests

The counts swing round a loop that may be far from the rest. Yet the rest still rules them, on average.

(\ln x)' = \tfrac{x'}{x} = a - by @@ the rabbits’ rule, divided by x
\ln x(P) - \ln x(0) = aP - b\textstyle\int_0^P y\,dt @@ over one time round the loop, P (16c)
0 = aP - b\textstyle\int_0^P y\,dt @@ x(P) = x(0): the path is back
\tfrac1P\textstyle\int_0^P y\,dt = \tfrac ab,\ \ \tfrac1P\textstyle\int_0^P x\,dt = \tfrac cd @@ the same for the foxes, from (ln y)′ = dx − c
\htmlClass{new}{\text{trap both at } h:\ \text{average rabbits } \tfrac{c + h}{d},\ \text{average foxes } \tfrac{a - h}{b}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a becomes a − h and c becomes c + h; the loops still close

Loop-average technique: over every loop, the average number of rabbits is c/d and the average number of foxes is a/b, however wide the loop; so steady killing of both leaves more rabbits on average and fewer foxes. 27·then.3 moved the rest. This moves the average of every loop with it.

California grew it as a lesson. From 1889, a ladybird beetle brought from Australia kept the cottony cushion scale, a sap-sucking insect, down in the citrus groves. In the 1940s growers sprayed DDT, which kills both. The scale came back in force where the spray had killed the beetles.

28bio5A Cull at the Wrong Moment

A one-off cull is not steady trapping. It jumps the counts from one loop to another, and the rules carry on from there. Which loop it lands on depends on when it is made.

f(x) = dx - c\ln x,\ \ f'(x) = d - \tfrac cx @@ the rabbits’ part of V
f' \lt 0 \text{ below } \tfrac cd,\ \ f' \gt 0 \text{ above } \tfrac cd @@ the slope-sign-order technique (14d): f falls, then rises
\text{a cull from } x \text{ to } x_1 \lt x \text{ with } x \le \tfrac cd:\ \ f(x_1) \gt f(x) @@ both below c/d, where f falls
\text{a cull from } x \text{ to } x_1 \text{ with } \tfrac cd \le x_1 \lt x:\ \ f(x_1) \lt f(x) @@ both above c/d, where f rises
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a cull of rabbits while they are at or below } \tfrac cd \text{ lands on a larger loop}} \quad \blacksquare @@ V rises; the loops sit inside one another, since V rises along each ray (28·bio.3)

Wrong-moment technique: a cull of rabbits made while they are at or below their average c/d moves every later swing outward, to more rabbits at the peak and fewer at the low; a cull made above c/d that leaves them no lower than c/d moves every swing inward. The foxes obey the same rule with a/b.

Take the loop through (30, 4). Cull a third of the rabbits at their peak, 30 thousand, and they land on 20 thousand with 4 hundred foxes: the rest itself. The swings stop. Cull the same third at their low, 12.5 thousand, and they land on 8.3 thousand. The new loop runs from 8.3 to 39.4 thousand rabbits, and the foxes from 1.90 to 7.26 hundred. A cull meant to keep rabbits down gives a peak of rabbits a third higher, nine years on and every nine years after.

28bio6A Little Crowding, and the Loops Are Gone

Real rabbits run short of grass. Add a crowding term to their rule: x′ = ax − ex² − bxy. Alone, the rabbits would level off at a/e. Take e = 0.004, so that alone they would level off at 200 thousand, ten times the rest. Here e means how hard the rabbits crowd one another, not 17e's number e.

x^* = \tfrac cd,\ \ y^* = \tfrac{a - ex^*}{b} @@ the new rest: (20, 3.6)
W = dx - c\ln x + by - by^*\ln y @@ V, with a replaced by by*
W' = (dx - c)(a - ex - by) + (by - by^*)(dx - c) @@ the roads-add technique (28c), as in 28·bio.2
= (dx - c)(a - ex - by^*) = (dx - c)(ex^* - ex) @@ a − by* = ex*
\htmlClass{new}{W' = -\tfrac ed\,(dx - c)^2 \le 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ ex* − ex = −(e/d)(dx − c)

Crowding-spiral technique: with any crowding e above 0, W only falls along a path, so no path but the rest can close up, and the paths spiral in toward the rest. W stays level only for a moment, as the path crosses the line x = c/d. The slope grid at the new rest has T = −0.08 and Δ = 0.432: a spiral in, off the border line, as 27·cb24.3 reads it. From (30, 4) the rabbit peaks fall from 27.1 to 24.7, 23.1, 22.1 and 21.4 thousand, about a third of the swing lost each time round.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the border line, settled

The kept quantity proved that the paths of these rules close. It did not prove that hares and lynx cycle. The closing rests on an exact cancellation of two products, and one small crowding term breaks it. Rules that close only on the border line are a knife edge: any small change in them tips the paths in or out.

When a result rests on an exact balance in the rules, check whether it survives a small change in them before reading it as a fact about the world.

So the loops of 28·bio.3 are a fact about these rules, not a forecast. A model that cycles only because of an exact balance has not explained a cycle. The fur records cycle with peaks of much the same height for about a hundred years, which neither a spiral in nor a spiral out can do for long. Something in the world keeps the cycle going.

Some prey hide when they are few: a few rabbits can always find a burrow the foxes cannot reach. Which way would hiding tip the loops, in or out, and how would you test your guess?

28bio8Counting by Age, a Grid at a Time

Foxes and rabbits were counted as two totals. A population can also be counted by age: x₁ this year’s young, x₂ the one-year-olds, x₃ the two-year-olds. Each year a share sᵢ of age i lives on to age i + 1, and each of age i leaves fᵢ young. Then next year’s counts are a grid times this year’s, x′ = Mx, with f₁, f₂, f₃ along the top row and s₁, s₂ just under the middle, and after k years the counts are Mᵏx: the powers of 27l2.

Age-grid technique: for such a grid with each sᵢ above 0 and some fᵢ above 0, exactly one stretch λ is a positive number, the root of 1 = f₁/λ + f₂s₁/λ² + f₃s₁s₂/λ³; it only stretches the age vector (1, s₁/λ, s₁s₂/λ²); and when it is larger in size than every other stretch, the population grows in the long run by the factor λ a year, in those age shares, whatever its start.

Mv = \lambda v,\ \allowbreak \ v = (1, v_2, v_3):\ \ s_1 = \lambda v_2,\ \allowbreak \ s_2v_2 = \lambda v_3 @@ the rows under the top: the survivors
v_2 = \tfrac{s_1}{\lambda},\ \allowbreak \ v_3 = \tfrac{s_1s_2}{\lambda^2} @@ the age vector
f_1 + f_2v_2 + f_3v_3 = \lambda \iff 1 = \tfrac{f_1}{\lambda} + \tfrac{f_2s_1}{\lambda^2} + \tfrac{f_3s_1s_2}{\lambda^3} @@ the top row: the young
\text{the }\allowbreak \text{right }\allowbreak \text{side }\allowbreak \text{falls, }\allowbreak \text{from }\allowbreak \text{past }\allowbreak \text{every }\allowbreak \text{bound }\allowbreak \text{near } 0 \text{ to } 0 \text{ far out} @@ each term falls as λ grows; the in-between-value technique (12) gives a root, the fall makes it the only one
\htmlClass{new}{M^kx \approx c\,\lambda^k v} \quad \blacksquare @@ the leading-stretch technique (27l2), when λ is the largest in size

The northern spotted owl of the old forests of Oregon and Washington has been modelled by stages: young, not yet breeding, and adults, which stay adults. With 0.33 young a year for each adult female, 18% of the young living to the next stage, 71% of those to adulthood and 94% of adults living from year to year, the grid has rows (0, 0, 0.33), (0.18, 0, 0), (0, 0.71, 0.94). Its stretches, by 27e’s determinant, are the roots of λ³ − 0.94λ² − 0.042 = 0: one is 0.984, and the other two are a flipped pair of size 0.207. So the owls shrink by about 1.6% a year, settling on about 24% young, 4% not yet breeding and 72% adults, and the start is forgotten fast, by 0.207/0.984 ≈ 0.21 each year. Raise the young’s survival to 30%, which is where the forest left standing matters, and λ = 1.009: the same birds, a growing population.

The accepted names

The rules x′ = ax − bxy, y′ = −cy + dxy are the Lotka–Volterra equations. A quantity kept along every path is a first integral, or a conserved quantity, and V is the Lotka–Volterra first integral. A rest with closed loops round it is a centre, and the loops are periodic orbits. The loop-average technique and its trapping corollary are Volterra’s principle. The crowding term turns the prey’s growth into logistic growth, and the slope grid is the Jacobian matrix. Rules whose kind of paths changes under any small change are structurally unstable. A grid of births and survivals by age is a Leslie matrix, and by stage, with a stage that keeps a share, a stage-structured (Lefkovitch) matrix. λ is the asymptotic growth rate, v the stable age distribution, and 1 = Σ fᵢlᵢ/λⁱ the Euler–Lotka equation; that one positive stretch exists and leads is the Perron–Frobenius theorem.

Who said it first

Alfred Lotka wrote the rules in 1925, for chemistry and for living things. Vito Volterra found them on his own in 1926, with the kept quantity and the loop averages. His future son-in-law Umberto D’Ancona had noticed that the fish markets of the Adriatic sold more sharks and other predators during the First World War, when fishing nearly stopped, and asked why. Charles Elton read the Hudson’s Bay Company’s fur records in 1924. Georgii Gause put the protist Didinium with its prey Paramecium in tubes of broth in 1934, and the predators ate every prey. Carl Huffaker spread six-spotted mites and the predatory mites that eat them over oranges in 1958, and found cycles once the world was patchy enough. Albert Koebele brought the vedalia beetle from Australia to California in 1888 to fight the cottony cushion scale, and Paul DeBach reported the scale’s return after DDT in the 1940s. Patrick Leslie gave the age grid in 1945, after Harro Bernardelli (1941) and Edwin Lewis (1942). Leonhard Euler wrote the equation for ages in 1760, and Alfred Lotka found it again in 1911. Russell Lande modelled the spotted owl by stages in 1988; the numbers on this page follow a textbook version of such models.
