37c · proof

How loud: intensity

A whisper and a jet engine differ by a factor of about a hundred thousand million in the power that reaches your ear, and the ear takes in both. This step says what that power is: the average of the square of the sound pressure, which is rung 32’s energy measured in the air. It then counts loudness the way rung 17 counted it, by powers of ten, so that the huge range fits on a ruler a person can read. It follows a sound as it spreads out from where it was made, and gets weaker with the square of the distance. And it ends with a fact about ears: how loud a sound seems is not how much power it carries, a loud sound hides a quiet one beside it, and a loud enough sound deafens.

The power a sound carries is the average of its pressure squared. Decibels count that power by tens. Spread from one place, it weakens with the square of the distance. The ear does not hear power as such: it hears more keenly in the middle of its window, a loud sound hides a quieter neighbour, and past a point sound hurts and deafens.

37c1Intensity is the square

A sound carries power: it can shake a window or a drum skin. From here on, the intensity I of a sound at a place is the power it carries through each square metre facing the way it goes, in watts a square metre. For a sound running one way in a medium of density ρ and speed c, the work done by the pressure on the moving air gives I = ⟨p²⟩ / (ρc), held in trust from the stiffness and motion laws of 37a2, where ⟨p²⟩ is the average of p² over a long time. For air at 20 °C, ρc ≈ 413.

So intensity is 32b3’s energy of the sound, measured per second at one place. A steady note p₀ cos(2πft) has ⟨p²⟩ = p₀²/2, since the average of cos² over a turn is one half.

Adding-power technique: when sounds at different frequencies arrive together, their intensities add. Proof: the turnings at different frequencies are at right angles (32a1), so the average of the product of any two is 0, and the average of the square of the sum is the sum of the averages of the squares (31a4, the right-angle case).

\langle (p + q)^2 \rangle = \langle p^2 \rangle + 2\langle pq \rangle + \langle q^2 \rangle @@ squaring out, and averages add
\langle pq \rangle = 0 \text{ for turnings at different frequencies} @@ 32a1, at right angles
\htmlClass{new}{I_{p + q} = I_p + I_q} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by ρc

The same frequency is another matter. Two equal turnings in step add to one of twice the pressure, so four times the intensity; half a turn apart they cancel (32·eng). Intensities add only when the cross term averages away.

37c2Decibels

17·then counted sound level by tens: L = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) decibels, with I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m², about the faintest sound heard. The difference here is that a microphone measures pressure, not power, and since I is a square, the level in pressure is twenty times a log.

Pressure-level technique: a sound’s level in decibels is 20 log₁₀(p_rms / p_ref), with p_rms = √⟨p²⟩ and p_ref = 20 micropascals, and it agrees with 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) to within a small fraction of a decibel.

10\log_{10}\tfrac{I}{I_0} = 10\log_{10}\tfrac{p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/(\rho c)}{I_0} @@ 37c1
p_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 / (\rho c) = (2 \times 10^{-5})^2 / 413 \approx 0.97 \times 10^{-12} @@ so p_ref is the pressure of I₀, nearly
\htmlClass{new}{L \approx 10\log_{10}\tfrac{p_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}^2} = 20\log_{10}\tfrac{p_{\mathrm{rms}}}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ off by 10 log₁₀ 0.97 ≈ 0.14 dB

Speech at a metre, p_rms ≈ 0.02 Pa, is 20 log₁₀ 1000 = 60 dB. Doubling the pressure adds 6 dB; doubling the power adds 3 dB. Two equal voices at different pitches are 3 dB louder than one, by the adding-power technique; two equal notes in step are 6 dB louder.

37c3Spreading from a point

A small source in open air sends its power out evenly in every direction. At distance r the power is spread over a sphere of area 4πr², so the intensity there is the source’s power W divided by that area.

Spreading technique: in open air, a sound from a small source loses 6 dB each time the distance doubles.

I(r) = W / (4\pi r^2) @@ the power spread over the sphere
I(2r) / I(r) = 1/4 @@ the square of the distance
\htmlClass{new}{10\log_{10}\tfrac14 \approx -6.02 \text{ dB}} \quad \blacksquare @@ per doubling of distance

A jackhammer gives off about 1 W of sound. At 10 m, I = 1/(4π·100) ≈ 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ W/m², which is 10 log₁₀(8.0 × 10⁸) ≈ 89 dB; at 20 m about 83 dB; at 160 m about 65 dB. In a street the walls send some back (37e), and the fall is slower.

37c4Loudness is not intensity

Two notes at the same level in decibels need not sound equally loud. As with the hearing window, what follows is laid down, measured from many listeners, a fact by cases with its rate (36g3).

First, the ear is keenest between about 2000 and 5000 Hz. At low levels a note at 50 Hz must be some 40 dB above a note at 3000 Hz to seem as loud. Second, a loud sound hides a quieter one near it in frequency: a 1000 Hz note at 80 dB makes a 1100 Hz note at 40 dB disappear for most listeners. Third, at about 120 to 130 dB sound hurts, and long hours far below that wear the ear down for good.

The ear keeps the power of a sound only in part: it weighs it by frequency, it drops what a louder neighbour hides, and past a point it is deafened. That is the rung’s name said once in plain words. Each later step says what else the ear drops, and the rung’s last technique (37j7) says what can still be told from what is kept.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The curves of equal loudness and the reach of a masking note are drawn from listeners, and every listener differs a little.

A law with a known exception rate is still a law: it says how often it fails and in which direction.

The accepted names

Intensity is sound intensity; ρc is the characteristic acoustic impedance of the medium. The level in pressure is the sound pressure level, SPL, in dB SPL. The curves of equal loudness are the equal-loudness contours, and the unit of loudness that follows them is the phon. Hiding by a louder neighbour is masking. The fall of 6 dB a doubling is the inverse-square law.

Who said it first

Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson at Bell Laboratories drew the first equal-loudness curves in 1933; a revised set is an international standard (ISO 226). The bel was named at Bell Laboratories in the 1920s for Alexander Graham Bell.

A proof that is not allowed

“A 50 Hz hum and a 3000 Hz beep are both 40 dB. So they are equally loud, and turning down either one helps equally.” Not allowed: the level counts power; loudness weighs it by frequency, laid down in 37c4. At 40 dB the hum is hardly heard at all.
