Complete Spaces With a Size

Not every useful size comes from a product. The highest height of a curve does not, and the total size does not. Spaces with such sizes have no angles and no shadows, but if they have no holes, a great deal still works. A sum whose sizes add to something finite settles. The continuous functions, sized by their highest height, have no holes, which is rung twenty three’s settling everywhere at once seen as a fact about one space. Then the maps between such spaces. A straight map that never stretches anything by more than a fixed amount moves vectors that are close to vectors that are close, and the two demands turn out to be one. The slope is not such a map: a tiny wiggle can have a huge slope. Last, rung twenty seven’s readings come back, as the straight maps from a space to the numbers. In a complete product space every such reading is the product with one fixed vector, so the readings of that space are the space again.

A space with a size and no holes lets every sum with finite total size settle. The continuous functions under their highest height are one. A straight map is continuous exactly when it stretches by at most a fixed amount, and in a complete product space every continuous reading is a product with one vector.

31i1No Holes, Without a Product

From here on, a complete space with a size is a space of vectors with a size in which every crowding list settles. L¹ and L² are complete (31g4). So is the space of continuous functions on [u, v] with the highest height, though it has no product (31g1).

Sup-complete technique: the continuous functions on [u, v], sized by ‖f‖∞, form a complete space with a size.

\|f_n - f_m\|_\infty \to 0 @@ the list crowds under the highest height
|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \le \|f_n - f_m\|_\infty \text{ at each } x @@ one height is at most the highest
f_n(x) \to f(x) \text{ at each } x @@ the crowd technique (22c), at each x
|f_n(x) - f(x)| \le \varepsilon \text{ for every } x \text{ once } n \ge N @@ let m grow in the crowding bound
f \text{ continuous} @@ fₙ settles everywhere at once (23d), and the kept-continuity technique (23d3)
\htmlClass{new}{\|f_n - f\|_\infty \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above, for every ε

Size-sum technique: in a complete space with a size, if ‖g₁‖ + ‖g₂‖ + ⋯ settles, then g₁ + g₂ + ⋯ settles. The running sums Sₙ crowd: ‖Sₘ − Sₙ‖ ≤ ‖gₙ₊₁‖ + ⋯ + ‖gₘ‖ by the triangle, a tail of a settling sum. Then they settle, since the space has no holes. With the highest height it is 23d4’s comparison test, read in one line.

31i2Maps That Stretch by at Most a Fixed Amount

Let L be a straight map between spaces with sizes. From here on, L is bounded when some C has ‖Lf‖ ≤ C‖f‖ for every f, and the least such C is its stretch size ‖L‖, as 31f3 found σ₁ for a grid. L is continuous when fₙ → f gives Lfₙ → Lf.

Bounded-is-continuous technique: a straight map between spaces with sizes is continuous exactly when it is bounded.

\|Lf_n - Lf\| = \|L(f_n - f)\| \le C\|f_n - f\| \to 0 @@ bounded gives continuous: L is straight
\text{not bounded} \Rightarrow \|Lh_n\| > n\|h_n\| \text{ for some } h_n \ne 0 @@ no C works, so n fails for each n
g_n = \tfrac{h_n}{\sqrt n\,\|h_n\|},\ \ \|g_n\| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n} \to 0 @@ stretch hₙ down
\|Lg_n\| > \sqrt n \not\to 0 = \|L0\| @@ straight in gₙ
\htmlClass{new}{L \text{ continuous} \iff L \text{ bounded}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last two lines: gₙ → 0 and Lgₙ does not
  
(If)(x) = \textstyle\int_u^x f,\ \ |(If)(x)| \le (x - u)\,\|f\|_\infty @@ the area under a height of at most ‖f‖∞ (15)
\|I\| \le v - u @@ bounded on the continuous functions on [u, v], under ‖·‖∞
f_n = \tfrac1n\sin(nx),\ \ \|f_n\|_\infty = \tfrac1n,\ \ \|f_n'\|_\infty = \|\cos(nx)\|_\infty = 1 @@ on [0, 2π]; the slope (21g)
\htmlClass{new}{f \mapsto f' \text{ is not bounded}} \quad \blacksquare @@ fₙ → 0 and its slopes do not

Area smooths and slope roughens. The area of a function is never larger than its heights allow, while a wiggle too small to see can have a slope as large as you like. Every hard question about rate rules, on rungs 24 and 33, comes back to this difference.

31i3Readings of a Function Space

Reading-is-product technique: in a complete product space, every bounded reading φ is φ(f) = ⟨f, g⟩ for exactly one vector g, and ‖φ‖ = ‖g‖. This φ (little phi) is not 2e's condition.

N = \{f : \varphi(f) = 0\} \text{ is a closed space} @@ what φ loses (27c1); closed, since φ is continuous (31i2)
\varphi \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{some } z \ne 0,\ z \perp N @@ take any f outside N, and z = f minus its closest point in N (31h4)
f - \tfrac{\varphi(f)}{\varphi(z)}z \in N \text{ for every } f @@ φ of it is 0; φ(z) ≠ 0, since z is not in N
\langle f, z\rangle = \tfrac{\varphi(f)}{\varphi(z)}\|z\|^2 @@ take the product with z: z is at right angles to N
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi(f) = \langle f, g\rangle,\ \ g = \tfrac{\overline{\varphi(z)}}{\|z\|^2}z} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve for φ(f); two such g differ by a vector at right angles to everything, so 0

The accepted names

A complete space with a size is a Banach space, and the continuous functions with the sup norm are C[u, v]. The size-sum technique is the statement that absolutely convergent series converge in a Banach space. A bounded straight map is a bounded linear operator, its stretch size the operator norm, and the bounded-is-continuous technique the equivalence of boundedness and continuity for linear maps. The bounded readings form the dual space, and the reading-is-product technique is the Riesz representation theorem (Fréchet–Riesz). The slope map is an unbounded operator.

Who said it first

Stefan Banach laid down complete spaces with a size in his thesis of 1920, published 1922, and his book of 1932 made them a field; Norbert Wiener and Hans Hahn found the same idea about the same time. Maurice Fréchet and Frigyes Riesz proved the reading-is-product technique independently in 1907. Vito Volterra and Ivar Fredholm studied maps like K, built from integrals, in the 1890s and 1900s.

A proof that is not allowed

“A reading on L² is fixed by its values on the right-angled basis, so it is the product with the list of those values.” Not allowed until the list of values is shown to be in ℓ²; that needs the reading bounded, and the proof above never needs the basis at all.
