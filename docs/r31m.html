31m · technique

Interpolation and orthogonal polynomials

A tide table gives the height at each hour, and someone wants it at twenty past. A table of a function gives a few values, and a machine needs all of them. The oldest answer is to draw the one polynomial that passes through the given points exactly, and it works well in the middle of the table. At the ends it can go badly wrong: with many points spaced evenly, the curve through them swings wildly between the last few, even for a smooth bell-shaped function. The swing has a cause that can be written down, a product of the distances to the chosen points, and that product can be made as small as possible by choosing the points well. The best points crowd towards the ends, where the trouble was, and they come from the cosine. The same polynomials, straightened under the right product, stand at right angles to each other, and any family of straightened polynomials is built by a short recipe with three terms.

Through n + 1 points there is one polynomial of degree at most n. Its miss from a smooth f is a high slope of f times the product of the distances to the points. Evenly spaced points make that product large near the ends; points from the cosine make it as small as it can be. Straightened polynomials obey a three-term recipe.

31m1Through the given points

Given n + 1 inputs x₀, …, xₙ, all different, and values y₀, …, yₙ, a polynomial p of degree at most n passes through them when p(xⱼ) = yⱼ for every j.

Through-the-points technique: exactly one polynomial of degree at most n passes through n + 1 points with different inputs, and it is p = Σⱼ yⱼLⱼ, where Lⱼ(x) is the product over k ≠ j of (x − xₖ)/(xⱼ − xₖ).

L_j(x_j) = 1,\ \ L_j(x_k) = 0 \text{ for } k \ne j @@ each factor is 1 at xⱼ; one factor is 0 at xₖ
p(x_k) = \textstyle\sum_j y_jL_j(x_k) = y_k @@ only the kth term survives
p, q \text{ both pass} \Rightarrow p - q \text{ has } n + 1 \text{ roots} @@ p − q is 0 at every xₖ
\htmlClass{new}{p - q = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a polynomial of degree at most n that is not 0 has at most n zeros (the at-most-n-zeros technique, 9g)
  
(0, 1),\ (1, 3),\ (2, 7) @@ the table
L_0 = \tfrac{(x - 1)(x - 2)}{2},\ \ L_1 = -x(x - 2),\ \ L_2 = \tfrac{x(x - 1)}{2} @@ the products
\htmlClass{new}{p = L_0 + 3L_1 + 7L_2 = 1 + x + x^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out; check p(1) = 3

31m2The miss, and the wiggle at the ends

Interpolation-miss technique: if f has n + 1 slopes on an interval holding x and every xₖ, and p passes through the points (xₖ, f(xₖ)), then f(x) − p(x) = f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(ξ)/(n + 1)! times (x − x₀)(x − x₁)⋯(x − xₙ), for some ξ in the interval.

\omega(t) = (t - x_0)\cdots(t - x_n),\ \ x \ne \text{every } x_k @@ the product of distances
g(t) = f(t) - p(t) - K\omega(t),\ \ K \text{ chosen so that } g(x) = 0 @@ ω(x) ≠ 0
g \text{ is } 0 \text{ at } n + 2 \text{ inputs} @@ at x and at every xₖ
g^{(n+1)}(\xi) = 0 \text{ for some } \xi @@ the level-ends technique (14b) between neighbours, n + 1 times, each time one root fewer
g^{(n+1)} = f^{(n+1)} - 0 - K(n + 1)! @@ p has degree at most n; ω starts with tⁿ⁺¹
\htmlClass{new}{f(x) - p(x) = K\omega(x) = \tfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}\,\omega(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ g(x) = 0

The slope part depends on f. The product ω depends only on the points chosen, and it is the part that can be changed. With 11 evenly spaced points on [−1, 1], |ω| is below 0.00007 at x = 0.05 and reaches 0.0085 near the ends, over a hundred times more.

f(x) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 25x^2} \text{ on } [-1, 1] @@ smooth, and at most 1
4 \text{ even gaps: largest miss } \approx 0.44 @@ a machine’s check over 20 001 inputs
10 \text{ even gaps: largest miss } \approx 1.92,\ \text{near } x = \pm 0.94 @@ worse
\htmlClass{new}{16 \text{ even gaps: largest miss } \approx 14.4} \quad \blacksquare @@ more points, a worse fit at the ends

31j promised polynomials near every continuous function. It never promised that the polynomials through evenly spaced points are the near ones, and they are not. The slopes of this bell grow like (n + 1)! times 5ⁿ⁺¹, faster than the product shrinks.

31m3Families at right angles

31c2 straightened 1, x, x² under the area product on [−1, 1], and got multiples of 1, x and x² − 1/3. A weight w(x) > 0 gives another product, ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ f g w, and another family. Straighten 1, x, x², … under it and keep each pₙ of degree n with leading number 1: the family of the weight.

Three-term technique: in the family of any weight, x pₙ = pₙ₊₁ + βₙpₙ + γₙpₙ₋₁ for numbers βₙ and γₙ: each new member needs only the last two.

xp_n - p_{n+1} \text{ has degree at most } n @@ both start with xⁿ⁺¹
xp_n - p_{n+1} = \textstyle\sum_{k \le n} c_kp_k @@ p₀, …, pₙ build the polynomials of degree at most n (31c1)
c_k\|p_k\|^2 = \langle xp_n, p_k\rangle = \langle p_n, xp_k\rangle @@ pₙ₊₁ ⊥ pₖ; the weight moves x across the product
xp_k \text{ has degree } k + 1 < n \text{ for } k \le n - 2 @@ and pₙ is at right angles to every polynomial of lower degree
\htmlClass{new}{c_k = 0 \text{ for } k \le n - 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ only cₙ and cₙ₋₁ survive

With the weight 1/√(1 − x²) on (−1, 1), put x = cos θ. Then ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₀^π f(cos θ) g(cos θ) dθ. On this step Tₙ names a new thing, the cosine polynomial; 23a’s Tₙ, the polynomial that matches slopes, keeps its meaning everywhere else. The cosine polynomials Tₙ, with Tₙ(cos θ) = cos nθ, are polynomials in x by 21k: cos(n + 1)θ + cos(n − 1)θ = 2 cos θ cos nθ, so Tₙ₊₁ = 2xTₙ − Tₙ₋₁, a three-term recipe, from T₀ = 1 and T₁ = x. And ∫₀^π cos nθ cos mθ dθ = 0 for n ≠ m by 21k again. So they are this weight’s family, up to their leading numbers 2ⁿ⁻¹: T₂ = 2x² − 1, T₃ = 4x³ − 3x.

31m4The best points to choose

Cosine-points technique: among all choices of n + 1 points in [−1, 1], the roots of Tₙ₊₁, xₖ = cos((2k + 1)π/(2n + 2)), make the largest size of ω on [−1, 1] as small as it can be, 2⁻ⁿ.

\omega = T_{n+1}/2^n @@ both start with xⁿ⁺¹ and have the same roots
|\omega(x)| = |\cos((n + 1)\theta)|/2^n \le 2^{-n} @@ x = cos θ
\omega = \pm 2^{-n} \text{ at } x = \cos\tfrac{j\pi}{n + 1},\ j = 0, \ldots, n + 1,\ \text{signs turning} @@ n + 2 inputs where cos((n + 1)θ) = ±1
q \text{ starts with } x^{n+1},\ |q| < 2^{-n} \text{ on } [-1, 1] \Rightarrow \omega - q \text{ changes sign } n + 1 \text{ times} @@ q is smaller than ω at each of those n + 2 inputs
\htmlClass{new}{\omega - q \text{ has degree} \le n \text{ and } n + 1 \text{ roots, so } \omega = q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the intermediate value technique (12a), then 9g; refuted, so no choice beats 2⁻ⁿ

For the bell of 31m2, the cosine points give a largest miss of about 0.40 with 5 points, 0.11 with 11 and 0.033 with 17. The points crowd towards ±1, the shadows of points evenly spaced round a half circle, and the wiggle at the ends is gone.

The accepted names

The polynomial through the points is the interpolating polynomial, and the Lⱼ form is Lagrange interpolation. The interpolation-miss technique is the interpolation error formula. The wiggle at the ends is Runge’s phenomenon, and 1/(1 + 25x²) is Runge’s function. A family of straightened polynomials under a weight is a family of orthogonal polynomials, and the three-term technique is the three-term recurrence. The Tₙ are the Chebyshev polynomials, their roots the Chebyshev nodes, and the cosine-points technique the minimax property of Chebyshev polynomials. Weight 1 gives the Legendre polynomials.

Who said it first

Edward Waring published the Lⱼ form in 1779, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange again in 1795; Isaac Newton had a form of interpolation by differences in 1676. Pafnuty Chebyshev found his polynomials in 1854 while designing linkages for steam engines. Carl Runge showed the wiggle at the ends in 1901.
