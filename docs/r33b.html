33b · proof

Heat Smooths and Forgets

A rod that starts with a hot spot and a cold spot does not keep them. The hot spot spreads and sinks, the cold spot fills in, and before long the rod has forgotten where either one was. This step proves three things about that forgetting. First, a rod never gets hotter than it was somewhere at the start or at its ends: the heat rule cannot make a new hottest point inside. From that comes the second, that a start and the ends fix the whole future, and that two starts that are close stay close. Third, on a line with no ends, the rule has one answer that every other answer is built from: a bell that spreads, the same bell as the crowd of random walkers on rung 30. Then the step turns round and asks for the past. The rule that forgets so gently forward cannot be run backward, because the past it forgot could have been almost anything.

Inside a rod, the heat rule makes no new hottest or coldest point, so a start and the ends fix the future, and nearby starts stay near. On a line with no ends every answer is a spreading bell laid over the start. Detail fades fastest, so the future is easy and the past is lost.

33b1No New Hottest Point

Let u be continuous on the closed rectangle R of points (x, t) with 0 ≤ x ≤ L and 0 ≤ t ≤ t₁, and let it solve u_t = κu_xx at every point of R with 0 < x < L and t > 0. Call the three sides t = 0, x = 0 and x = L the floor and walls of R. The fourth side, t = t₁, is the lid.

Hottest-at-the-edge technique: the greatest value of u on R is taken on the floor or the walls. So is the least value.

v = u + \varepsilon x^2,\ \ \varepsilon > 0 @@ a small tilt that bends up
v_t - \kappa v_{xx} = u_t - \kappa u_{xx} - 2\kappa\varepsilon = -2\kappa\varepsilon < 0 @@ inside R and on the lid
v \text{ has a greatest value on } R \text{ at some } (x_0, t_0) @@ R is closed and bounded, v is continuous (25d)
\text{if } 0 < x_0 < L,\ t_0 > 0:\ \ v_{xx}(x_0, t_0) \le 0,\ \ v_t(x_0, t_0) \ge 0 @@ a top along x, so no bend up there (14a, 14f); along t a top or the lid, so chords from below never fall
v_t - \kappa v_{xx} \ge 0 \text{ there},\ \text{ a clash with } -2\kappa\varepsilon < 0 @@ so (x₀, t₀) lies on the floor or a wall
\max_R u \le \max_R v = \max_{\text{floor, walls}} v \le \max_{\text{floor, walls}} u + \varepsilon L^2 @@ v ≥ u, and εx² ≤ εL²
\htmlClass{new}{\max_R u = \max_{\text{floor, walls}} u} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε was any positive number; for the least value, use −u

So heat never piles up into a new peak. A rod that starts below 60 °C everywhere, with its ends held below 60 °C, stays below 60 °C everywhere, for as long as the rule holds.

33b2One Start, One Future

One-future technique: two answers of the heat rule on R with the same start and the same ends are the same answer. If their starts and ends differ by at most δ, the answers differ by at most δ everywhere in R.

w = u_1 - u_2 \text{ solves } w_t = \kappa w_{xx} @@ the rule is straight in u (33a3)
|w| \le \delta \text{ on the floor and walls} @@ the starts and the ends differ by at most δ
\max_R w \le \delta,\ \ \min_R w \ge -\delta @@ the hottest-at-the-edge technique, for its greatest and its least value
\htmlClass{new}{|u_1 - u_2| \le \delta \text{ on } R;\ \ \delta = 0 \Rightarrow u_1 = u_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same start and ends give δ = 0

So the fading-series answer of 33a3 is the only answer. Separation found one; the one-future technique says there is no other to find. And a thermometer reading off by a tenth of a degree at the start makes a forecast off by at most a tenth of a degree, ever. Rung 24’s drift technique let two nearby starts drift apart by a growing factor. Here they cannot drift apart at all.

33b3The Spreading Bell

Now take a rod so long that its ends never matter: the whole line. For t > 0 let

\(\Phi(x, t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\kappa t}}\,e^{-x^2/(4\kappa t)}.\)

Spreading-bell technique: Φ solves the heat rule for every t > 0, totals 1 along the line at every t, and is the bell with spread 2κt.

\ln\Phi = -\tfrac{x^2}{4\kappa t} - \tfrac12\ln(4\pi\kappa t) @@ where Φ > 0, which is everywhere
\tfrac{\Phi_t}{\Phi} = \tfrac{x^2}{4\kappa t^2} - \tfrac{1}{2t} @@ the slope of ln along t (17b), one knob at a time (28a)
\tfrac{\Phi_x}{\Phi} = -\tfrac{x}{2\kappa t},\ \ \Phi_{xx} = \Phi\big(\tfrac{x^2}{4\kappa^2t^2} - \tfrac{1}{2\kappa t}\big) @@ along x, then the product rule
\kappa\Phi_{xx} = \Phi_t @@ the two brackets agree after multiplying by κ
\textstyle\int \Phi\,dx = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\kappa t}}\int e^{-s^2}\sqrt{4\kappa t}\,ds = 1 @@ put x = √(4κt) s (16d), then the bell-total technique (30j)
\htmlClass{new}{\Phi(\cdot, t) \text{ is the bell with average } 0 \text{ and spread } 2\kappa t} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stretched-density technique (30m), with σ = √(2κt)

Every other answer on the line is built from Φ. If the start f is bounded and continuous, the answer is the start smoothed by the bell:

\(u(x, t) = \int \Phi(x - y, t)\,f(y)\,dy.\)

This is 32d’s convolution of f with Φ. 32e gives the reason it works. The transform turns a slope along x into a multiplication, so the heat rule becomes û_t = −κξ²û for each frequency ξ, a rule of rung 24 alone, with answer û = e^(−κξ²t) f̂. The bell is its own transform (32e), so e^(−κξ²t) is the transform of Φ, and a product of transforms is the transform of a convolution (32d). High frequencies, the fine detail, fade like e^(−κξ²t): the fastest of all.

The spread 2κt is 30n’s 2Dt. Heat is a crowd of walkers. Start every walker at 0, and after a time t their share near x is Φ(x, t). The heat rule is what the crowd does when no one walker can be followed.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change, at once

12a proved that a continuous change passes through a middle. The heat rule makes every change continuous at once. Start a long iron bar at 0 °C for x < 0 and 100 °C for x > 0, a jump. For every t > 0, however small, the answer is the jump smoothed by a bell of width √(2κt), with a slope at every point.

The heat rule smooths every jump at once, and it does so by being slightly wrong about what happens far away.

There is a cost. Φ is above 0 at every x, for every t > 0. So some heat from the hot half has reached every point of the bar, however far, at once. No heat moves that fast in iron. The rule is a law of the crowd, true where the crowd is large and false for the fastest few walkers.

The jump in the iron bar smooths over a width of about √(2κt). With κ = 2.3 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s, how wide is the middle after one second? How far away would you have to look before the rule’s heat there is too small for any thermometer, and does it matter that the rule puts some there at all?

33b4Forward Is Easy, Backward Is Lost

Forgetting technique: on a rod with both ends at 0, if Σ|b_n| settles, then |u(x, t)| ≤ (Σ|b_n|) e^(−κπ²t/L²) for every x and every t ≥ 0, and the shapes with two or more arches shrink against the first by at least e^(−3κπ²t/L²).

|u(x, t)| \le \textstyle\sum |b_n|\,e^{-\kappa n^2\pi^2t/L^2} @@ the fading-series technique, and |sin| ≤ 1
e^{-\kappa n^2\pi^2t/L^2} \le e^{-\kappa\pi^2t/L^2} @@ n² ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{|u(x, t)| \le \big(\textstyle\sum |b_n|\big)\,e^{-\kappa\pi^2t/L^2}} @@ every answer heads to 0 at least this fast
\tfrac{e^{-\kappa n^2\pi^2t/L^2}}{e^{-\kappa\pi^2t/L^2}} = e^{-\kappa(n^2 - 1)\pi^2t/L^2} \le e^{-3\kappa\pi^2t/L^2} \text{ for } n \ge 2 @@ n² − 1 ≥ 3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the rest shrinks against the first shape by at least } e^{-3\kappa\pi^2t/L^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide the tail’s bound by the first term’s factor

Now ask the other question. The rod is measured at a time t₁, and the start is wanted. A shape with n arches and size δ at t₁ had size δe^(κn²π²t₁/L²) at the start. For the copper rod of 33a2, half a metre long, one minute back, and ten arches, that factor is about e^27.5, nearly a million million. A wobble too small for any thermometer at t₁ can come from a wobble of thousands of degrees at the start. Two very different pasts give almost the same present.

So the one-future technique runs one way. Forward, nearby starts give nearby futures. Backward, nearby presents can come from pasts as far apart as you like. The heat rule forgets, and what it forgets is the detail.

The accepted names

The hottest-at-the-edge technique is the weak maximum principle for the heat equation; the floor and walls are the parabolic boundary. The one-future technique is uniqueness and stability, or well-posedness, of the initial-boundary value problem. Φ is the heat kernel, or fundamental solution; the answer Φ * f is the solution of the Cauchy problem on the line. The jump smoothed by the bell is written with the error function, erf. That the heat equation cannot be run backward is the ill-posedness of the backward heat equation. E(t) = ∫w² in the reroute is an energy method.

Who said it first

Pierre-Simon Laplace used the spreading bell in 1809, and Joseph Fourier solved the heat equation on the whole line with it by 1811. The maximum principle for the heat equation was proved in the twentieth century; Louis Nirenberg gave the strong form in 1953. Jacques Hadamard named well-posed problems in 1902 and showed what goes wrong without them. Albert Einstein tied the heat equation to the walk of a crowd of particles in 1905, and Paul Langevin did it another way in 1908.

A proof that is not allowed

“At the hottest point inside, u_xx < 0, so u_t = κu_xx < 0 and the point is cooling. So it was hotter a moment before, and the hottest point was earlier. Keep going back to t = 0.” Not allowed: at a top u_xx can be 0, not below 0, so u_t can be 0, and the line ‘it was hotter before’ fails. The tilt εx² is there to make the inequality strict.
