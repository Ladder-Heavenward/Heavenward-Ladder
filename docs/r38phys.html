Why Can't Two Electrons Share a Place?

Why can't two electrons share a place? A neon sign in a window in Portland glows red because of what the electrons of neon cannot do. Hydrogen has one electron, and 35·phys found its orbitals. Neon has ten, and they do not all crowd into the lowest orbital, as a heap of marbles would roll to the bottom of a bowl. They fill the orbitals two to each, from the bottom up, and the tenth one closes a shell. That is why neon hardly reacts, why sodium, with one electron more, reacts with almost anything, and why the table of the elements has rows of 2, 8, 8 and 18. Nobody has seen an electron refuse to share. The rule was guessed in 1925 from the lines atoms give off, and every measurement since has kept it. What makes the question hard is that electrons have no labels. Two of them cannot be told apart even in principle, and that alone limits what their joint state can be. This page shows how the guess about swapping works, counts the shells it gives, estimates the energy of helium two ways, and ends with the two ways a crowd of identical particles can share out energy, one of which gives the glow of a hot body.

An electron carries a two-way arrow, its spin. Swapping two electrons turns their joint state into its negative, so two in the same state give nothing at all. Filling orbitals two by two gives the shells. The same rule, and its opposite for light, give two ways to share energy, and the second gives the law of the glow. Inner shells screen the nucleus, so the pull on the outermost electron rises along a row and falls down a column, and a spectrum of knocked-out electrons counts the shells one by one.

38phys1Spin, and Two Electrons

An electron’s state, measured, has more to it than its wave in space. In a magnet a beam of silver atoms splits in two, never more, never fewer. So the electron carries an arrow in a space with two directions, called spin: up, written ↑, and down, ↓, perpendicular in 31a’s sense. Its state is a wave in space times a spin arrow.

Two electrons have a joint state ψ(1, 2), where 1 and 2 stand for the place and spin of each. Electrons have no labels, so swapping them can change ψ only by a factor, and swapping twice changes nothing: the factor squared is 1. It is +1 or −1. Every measurement of atoms since 1925 fits −1 for electrons. That is a law, from guessing under the small case, with no measured exception.

Swap-sign technique: if a joint state changes sign when two electrons are swapped, then two electrons in the same one-electron state give the zero state.

\psi(1, 2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\varphi(1)\chi(2) - \chi(1)\varphi(2)\big) @@ the joint state of one electron in φ and one in χ that changes sign under a swap
\psi(2, 1) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\varphi(2)\chi(1) - \chi(2)\varphi(1)\big) = -\psi(1, 2) @@ swap the labels
\chi = \varphi:\ \ \psi(1, 2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\varphi(1)\varphi(2) - \varphi(1)\varphi(2)\big) @@ put both in the same state
\htmlClass{new}{\psi = 0:\ \text{no state at all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two terms cancel

So two electrons can share an orbital only with different spins: φ↑ and φ↓ are different one-electron states. That is the exclusion rule. It is not a force pushing them apart. It is a fact about which joint states exist.

38phys2Shells

35·phys found hydrogen’s orbitals: at level n, the shapes ℓ = 0, 1, …, n − 1, and for each ℓ, 2ℓ + 1 orbitals. Fill them with electrons, two to an orbital, from the lowest energy up.

Shell-count technique: level n of hydrogen’s orbitals holds 2n² electrons.

\textstyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1} (2\ell + 1) = 2\cdot\tfrac{(n-1)n}{2} + n = n^2 @@ the sum of the first n − 1 numbers (4k), plus n ones
\text{two spins to each orbital} @@ the swap-sign technique
\htmlClass{new}{2n^2:\ \ 2,\ 8,\ 18,\ 32} \quad \blacksquare @@ n = 1, 2, 3, 4

Helium, with 2 electrons, fills level 1. Neon, with 2 + 8 = 10, fills level 2. Both hardly react. In atoms with more electrons the shapes of one level no longer share one energy, since the inner electrons screen the nucleus more for some shapes than others, and the order of filling shifts. The closed shells fall at 2, 10, 18, 36, 54 and 86 electrons: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. The rows of the table, 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, are those gaps.

38phys3Helium, Two Ways

Helium has charge 2 and two electrons. Without the push between the electrons, each sits in hydrogen’s lowest orbital scaled to charge 2, with energy −4 × 13.6 eV, and the two together −108.8 eV. The push raises it. Measured, it takes 24.6 eV to remove one electron and 54.4 eV more to remove the other: the energy is −79.0 eV.

First estimate: keep the old state and add the average of the push in it. That average works out to (5/4) × 2 × 13.6 = 34.0 eV, giving −74.8 eV. Its accepted name is first-order perturbation theory. Second estimate: give each electron a trial orbital with a charge Z′ in place of 2, as if the other electron screened part of the nucleus, and make the average energy as low as Z′ allows.

Upper-bound technique: the average energy in any trial state is at least the lowest energy.

\psi = \textstyle\sum_i c_i\,u_i,\ \ \sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1 @@ the energy is a symmetric map, so it has perpendicular axes uᵢ with energies Eᵢ (31d)
\langle E \rangle = \textstyle\sum_i |c_i|^2 E_i @@ the average, axis by axis
\htmlClass{new}{\langle E \rangle \ge E_0 \textstyle\sum_i |c_i|^2 = E_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ every Eᵢ is at least the lowest, E₀
  
\langle E \rangle (Z') = 27.2\,\big(Z'^2 - 2 \cdot 2\,Z' + \tfrac58 Z'\big) \text{ eV} @@ the average energy in the trial state with charge Z′, worked out from 35·phys’s orbital
\tfrac{d}{dZ'}:\ 2Z' - \tfrac{27}{8} = 0,\ \ Z' = \tfrac{27}{16} = 1.6875 @@ the lowest point (14a)
\langle E \rangle = -27.2 \cdot (\tfrac{27}{16})^2 \approx -77.5 \text{ eV} @@ put it back
\htmlClass{new}{-79.0 \le -77.5:\ \text{a bound from above, 1.9\% high}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the upper-bound technique; the measured value

38phys4Two Ways to Share

30·phys’s grand-share technique gave the share of a site that holds one molecule or none: one over (1 + e^(−(ε₀ + μ)/kT)). Read “site” as one state of energy E, and the molecule as an electron. By the swap-sign technique the state holds 0 or 1 electrons, never more. Particles of light obey the other sign, +1, and a state may hold any number of them.

Two-shares technique: a state of energy E holds, on average, 1/(e^((E − μ)/kT) + 1) particles of the sign −1, and 1/(e^((E − μ)/kT) − 1) of the sign +1.

\text{sign } -1:\ \ \bar n = \tfrac{0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot q}{1 + q},\ \ q = e^{-(E - \mu)/kT} @@ the grand-share technique: weights 1 for empty, q for one
= \tfrac{1}{e^{(E - \mu)/kT} + 1} @@ divide top and bottom by q
\text{sign } +1:\ \ \bar n = \tfrac{\sum_n n\,q^n}{\sum_n q^n} = \tfrac{q/(1 - q)^2}{1/(1 - q)} = \tfrac{q}{1 - q} @@ endless geometric sums and their slopes (22), with q below 1
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac{1}{e^{(E - \mu)/kT} - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by q

At high energy both become e^(−(E − μ)/kT), 30·phys’s share: when states are mostly empty, the sign does not show. At low energy they part. Electrons fill the low states one to each and stop: that is why a metal’s electrons keep a great deal of energy even near absolute zero. Light piles up in the low states.

38phys5The Glow's Law

Light in a closed hot box is a crowd of particles of sign +1, each of energy hν, with μ = 0, since the walls make and swallow them freely. How many states have frequency near ν? The waves that fit a box of side L are counted by three whole numbers, as in 35·phys’s box, with frequency (c/2L)√(n₁² + n₂² + n₃²). Each has two directions of shaking (31·phys).

\text{states up to } \nu:\ \ 2 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot \tfrac43\pi\big(\tfrac{2L\nu}{c}\big)^3 = \tfrac{8\pi L^3 \nu^3}{3c^3} @@ whole-number points in an eighth of a ball of radius 2Lν/c, two each
\text{states per unit } \nu \text{, per unit volume}:\ \tfrac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3} @@ the slope along ν, over L³
\text{energy per state}:\ \tfrac{h\nu}{e^{h\nu/kT} - 1} @@ the two-shares technique, sign +1, μ = 0, times hν
\htmlClass{new}{u(\nu) = \tfrac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\,\tfrac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT} - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy per unit volume per unit frequency

Glow technique: the energy of the glow per unit frequency is 8πhν³/c³ times 1/(e^(hν/kT) − 1), and its total grows as the fourth power of the temperature. Put x = hν/kT in the total of u over every ν: it becomes T⁴ times a number that does not depend on T.

The glow is brightest per unit of wavelength at λ = 2.898 mm·K / T. The Sun’s surface, at 5772 K, peaks at 502 nm, green light near the middle of what the eye sees. A person at 310 K glows brightest at 9.3 μm, in the infrared that a forehead thermometer in a clinic in Eugene reads.

38phys7Inner Shells Screen the Nucleus

An orbital of level n scaled to a charge Z holds its electron by 13.6 Z²/n² eV (35·phys, and 38·phys.3). In a larger atom the outermost electron feels the nucleus through the electrons nearer in, which cancel part of its charge. Write Z_eff for the charge it feels, its effective charge, so that it is held by about 13.6 Z_eff²/n² eV. Turned round, the measured energy I that removes it reads Z_eff = n√(I/13.6 eV).

Screened-charge technique: if each electron of an inner level cancels a whole charge for an outer electron, and each other electron of the same level cancels only a share σ below 1, then along a row the outer electron’s effective charge rises by 1 − σ with each element, and its pull rises with it. Proof: with k electrons in the outer level n and the other Z − k within, Z_eff = Z − (Z − k) − σ(k − 1) = k − σ(k − 1). The next element has one more of Z and of k, so Z_eff gains 1 − σ, above 0, and with n the same, 13.6 Z_eff²/n² rises.

\text{Li } 5.39 \to 1.26,\ \allowbreak \ \text{Be } 9.32 \to 1.66,\ \allowbreak \ \text{B } 8.30 \to 1.56,\ \allowbreak \ \text{C } 11.26 \to 1.82 @@ I in eV, then Z_eff = 2√(I/13.6): level 2
\text{N } 14.53 \to 2.07,\ \allowbreak \ \text{O } 13.62 \to 2.00,\ \allowbreak \ \text{F } 17.42 \to 2.26,\ \allowbreak \ \text{Ne } 21.56 \to 2.52 @@ rising along the row, by about 0.18 a step
\text{Li } 0.63,\ \allowbreak \ \text{Na } 0.61,\ \allowbreak \ \text{K } 0.56,\ \allowbreak \ \text{Rb } 0.55,\ \allowbreak \ \text{Cs } 0.53 @@ down a column: Z_eff/n = √(I/13.6), for n = 2 to 6
\htmlClass{new}{I = 13.6\,(Z_{\text{eff}}/n)^2:\ \text{rises }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{along }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{a }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{row, }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{falls }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{down }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{a }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{column}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the screened-charge technique, and a closed level between

Down a column, each new row puts a whole closed level between the outer electron and the nucleus. The added charges are screened almost entirely: Z_eff creeps up from 1.26 for lithium to 3.21 for caesium, while n grows from 2 to 6 faster, so the pull falls, and caesium gives up its electron for 3.89 eV against lithium’s 5.39. Two dips break the row. Boron’s fifth electron starts the shape ℓ = 1 of 35·phys, which the inner shapes screen more than ℓ = 0; oxygen’s eighth is the first to share a p orbital with another, and 38·phys.3’s push between two electrons in one place costs it. An electron held weakly ranges far, so atoms shrink along a row and grow down a column: the same trend, read as size.

38phys8Shells Read off a Spectrum

The shells of 38·phys.2 can be counted, level by level. Shine X-rays of one known energy hν on a gas. By 17·phys’s photoelectric effect a lump of light gives all its energy to one electron, which spends the energy I that holds it and keeps hν − I as motion. Count the electrons that come off at each energy of motion. Each level and shape of the atom gives a peak of its own, at its own I.

Shell-reading technique: in the counts of electrons knocked out by light of one energy, each level and shape gives one peak, at the energy that holds it; and if each electron is as likely as any other to take the light, the sizes of the peaks stand in the ratio of the numbers of electrons in each. Proof: an electron of shape ℓ at level n leaves with hν − I for that shape, the same for all of them; the average count of a peak is the sum, over its electrons, of the same chance (30g’s averages add).

\text{neon: }\allowbreak \text{peaks }\allowbreak \text{at } 870.6,\ \allowbreak 48.5,\ \allowbreak 21.6 \text{ eV} @@ measured: level 1, level 2 with ℓ = 0, level 2 with ℓ = 1
\text{sizes } 2 : 2 : 6 @@ two to an orbital; one orbital of ℓ = 0 in each level, three of ℓ = 1
1\cdot\sqrt{870.6/13.6} \approx 8.0,\ \allowbreak \ 2\sqrt{48.5/13.6} \approx 3.8,\ \allowbreak \ 2\sqrt{21.6/13.6} \approx 2.5 @@ the screened-charge reading, peak by peak
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{deepest }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{pair }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{feels }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{8 }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{of }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{neon’s }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{10 }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{charges; }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{outer }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{six }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{feel }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{2.5}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shell-reading technique: the shells, counted

Light of 1486.6 eV, a common X-ray source, sends the deepest electrons of neon off with 1486.6 − 870.6 = 616 eV and the outermost with 1465 eV. The three peaks, their sizes 2, 2 and 6, and the gap of more than 800 eV between level 1 and level 2 are the shell-count technique read straight off a counter. Sodium adds a fourth peak, of size 1, near 5.1 eV: the single electron that makes it react. The premise about equal chances is only near the truth, and real peaks differ in size a little more than the counts say, so the counts are read from the order of the peaks and their rough sizes together.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The swap sign is the symmetrization postulate; the exclusion rule is the Pauli exclusion principle, and particles of sign −1 are fermions, of sign +1 bosons. The joint state that changes sign is a Slater determinant. The silver-atom beam is the Stern–Gerlach experiment. The upper-bound technique is the variational principle; the trial with charge 27/16 is the standard variational estimate for helium. The two shares are the Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions. The glow’s law is Planck’s law of black-body radiation; the T⁴ total is the Stefan–Boltzmann law, and the peak in wavelength is Wien’s displacement law. Z_eff is the effective nuclear charge, the cancelling of charge by nearer electrons is shielding, or screening, and σ is a screening constant. The energy that removes the outermost electron is the first ionization energy; its rise along a period and fall down a group, with the atom’s size going the other way, are the periodic trends. Counting electrons knocked out by light of one energy is photoelectron spectroscopy (PES, or XPS for X-rays), and the energy holding each electron is its binding energy.

Who said it first

Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach split the silver beam in 1922. Wolfgang Pauli stated exclusion in 1925, and George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit proposed spin the same year. Werner Heisenberg and Paul Dirac tied exclusion to the sign of the joint state in 1926, and Enrico Fermi and Dirac found the share for sign −1 that year. Satyendra Nath Bose derived the glow’s law by counting light in 1924, and Albert Einstein extended the count to atoms. Max Planck found the law in 1900. Egil Hylleraas pushed the helium estimate to great accuracy from 1929. Josef Stefan found the T⁴ law from measurements in 1879, Ludwig Boltzmann derived it in 1884, and Wilhelm Wien the shift of the peak in 1893. John Slater gave rules for screening constants in 1930. Kai Siegbahn built photoelectron spectroscopy into a way of reading the levels of atoms in the 1950s and 1960s, and shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for it in 1981.

A proof that is not allowed

“The trial with Z′ = 1.6875 gives −77.5 eV. A better trial gives a lower number. So the lowest number any trial gives is helium’s energy, and any trial below −79.0 eV shows the measurement is wrong.” Not allowed as stated: every trial is above the lowest energy, so no trial can go below −79.0 eV if the measurement is right. A trial below it would show the model of helium is wrong, or the arithmetic.
