How Tightly Can Balls Be Packed?

How tightly can balls be packed? Pour coins on a table and push them together, and they fall into rows where each coin touches six others. Stack oranges, and the grocer puts each one in a hollow of the layer below. Both look best, and both took a very long time to prove best. For coins, the claim that nothing beats the honeycomb was printed in 1890 with a proof that readers found incomplete, and a full proof came in 1940; this page proves it by hand. For oranges, the claim was written down in 1611 and settled in 1998 by a proof so long that its referees, after years of work, could only say they were almost certain, and it was then checked line by line by a machine, finished in 2014. Above three dimensions almost nothing is known, except in two places. In 2003 two mathematicians found a way to bound every packing with a single function: if a function and its transform have the right signs, they cap the share of space any packing can fill. In March 2016 a function of exactly the right kind was found for dimension 8, and a week later, with four coauthors, for dimension 24. In those two dimensions the best packing is now known, and it is a grid. In every other dimension above three it is not, and in some dimensions the best packing anyone has found is not a grid at all.

Density is the share of space the balls fill. In the plane no packing of equal discs passes π/√12 ≈ 0.9069, by cutting the plane into triangles between centres: every triangle has area at least √3 and holds exactly π/2 of disc. If f ≤ 0 past distance r and f̂ ≥ 0, then a grid with no two points closer than r has density at most vol(B_{r/2}) · f(0)/f̂(0), by summing f over the grid two ways (Σ f(x) = (1/V) Σ f̂(y)). The bound is exact when f and f̂ vanish on every nonzero grid point: in dimension 1 by a tent, in 8 and 24 by functions found in 2016.

inf47.1The Share of Space Filled

Put balls of radius ρ in space so that no two overlap: their centres are at least 2ρ apart. Take a box of side L centred anywhere, count the balls inside it, and divide their total size by the size of the box, Lⁿ, as 30a measures sizes. The density of the packing is where that share settles as L grows. For a packing that repeats, one cell tells all.

Box-share technique: if a packing repeats by a grid whose cell has size V and each cell holds k balls of size B, its density is kB/V. A box of side L holds about Lⁿ/V cells. The cells cut by the box’s faces number at most a fixed multiple of Lⁿ⁻¹, so they change the count by a share that falls like 1/L.

\text{discs of radius } 1 \text{ in a square grid: cell } 2 \times 2 = 4 @@ one disc per cell
\pi/4 \approx 0.7854 @@ the box-share technique, B = π
\text{rows shifted by half: cell } 2 \times \sqrt 3 = 2\sqrt3 @@ the next row sits √3 higher, in the hollows
\pi/(2\sqrt3) = \pi/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.9069 @@ the honeycomb
\text{balls of radius } 1 \text{ in a cubic grid: cell } 8,\ B = \tfrac43\pi @@ π/6 ≈ 0.5236
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the grocer’s stack: } \pi/\sqrt{18} \approx 0.7405} \quad \blacksquare @@ 38·now’s count, four balls to a cube of side 2√2

38·now found the grocer’s 74%. Not every packing repeats, and the question is about all of them. A packing can be made of patches that change from place to place, or have no pattern at all; the density is still the settled share, when it settles, and the upper limit of the share when it does not.

inf47.2Triangles Between the Centres

Now a proof that no packing of discs in the plane beats the honeycomb, pattern or not. Take discs of radius 1. Call the packing full when no further disc fits anywhere. Adding discs never lowers the share, so it is enough to bound full packings. Join the centres into triangles so that no centre lies strictly inside the circle through a triangle’s three corners, the circle of 18d. Such a cutting exists; held in trust, it is built by flipping the shared side of any two triangles that break the rule until none do.

Largest-angle technique: in a full packing of discs of radius 1, every triangle of the cutting has its largest angle between π/3 and 2π/3, and area at least √3.

R \ge 2 \Rightarrow \text{the circle’s centre is } \ge 2 \text{ from every centre} @@ no centre lies inside the circle
\Rightarrow \text{a new disc fits there; so } R < 2 @@ the packing is full
\text{side } = 2R\sin(\text{opposite angle}) \ge 2 \Rightarrow \sin\theta > \tfrac12 \text{ for every angle} @@ the sine rule; sides ≥ 2 since discs do not overlap
\text{every angle} > \pi/6;\ \ \text{largest} \ge \pi/3 @@ three angles add to π
\text{largest} > 2\pi/3 \Rightarrow \text{the other two add to} < \pi/3 @@ but each is above π/6: impossible
\text{area} = \tfrac12 \cdot b \cdot c \cdot \sin\theta_{\max} \ge \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} @@ b, c the sides beside the largest angle; sin ≥ √3/2 on [π/3, 2π/3]
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} \ge \sqrt3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the largest-angle technique

Checks. The honeycomb’s triangles are equilateral with side 2: area √3 exactly. The square grid’s triangles are half-squares with legs 2: largest angle π/2, area 2. A thin triangle with sides 2, 2 and 3.9 has a largest angle near 154°, and the proof says it cannot appear: its circle has radius about 4.5, room for a new disc at its centre.

inf47.3No Packing of Discs Beats the Honeycomb

Each disc is split by the triangles round its centre into slices, one per triangle, whose angles add to a full turn. So the discs’ total size is the total of the slices, triangle by triangle.

Honeycomb-bound technique: no packing of equal discs in the plane has density above π/√12.

\text{slices in one triangle: angles add to } \pi,\ \text{size } \tfrac12 \cdot \pi \cdot 1^2 = \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ a slice of angle θ has size θ/2 (21n3’s disc, cut)
\text{the slices stay in the triangle} @@ a corner is c sin θ ≥ 2 · ½ = 1 from the far side, θ the angle between them
\text{share in one triangle} = \tfrac{\pi/2}{\text{area}} \le \tfrac{\pi/2}{\sqrt3} = \tfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{12}} @@ the largest-angle technique
\text{share in a region} = \tfrac{\sum \pi/2}{\sum \text{areas}} \le \tfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{12}} @@ a share of totals is at most the largest share
\htmlClass{new}{\text{density} \le \pi/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.9069} \quad \blacksquare @@ the triangles cut by the box’s edge fall away as in inf47.1

Equality needs every triangle to have area √3 exactly, and the largest-angle technique then forces it equilateral with side 2: the honeycomb. A computer check of 200 000 random triangles with sides at least 2 and circle radius below 2 found the largest share 0.876, below 0.907. The same idea in space fails at once: the regular solid with four triangle faces cannot fill space, five of them round an edge leave a gap of about 7.36°, and space cannot be cut into perfect pieces as the plane is.

inf47.4Summing Over a Grid Two Ways

The next bound needs one tool from 32. Take f on the line, with f and its transform f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x)e^(−2πixξ) dx of 32e both falling fast. Wrap f round a circle of length 1: F(x) = Σ f(x + n) over all whole n. F repeats every 1, so it is a sum of turnings e^(2πikx).

Grid-sum technique: if f and f̂ fall fast enough that the sums below settle and F has a slope at 0, then Σ f(n) = Σ f̂(k), both over all whole numbers.

c_k = \int_0^1 F(x)\,e^{-2\pi i k x}\,dx = \sum_n \int_n^{n+1} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i k x}\,dx @@ 32a’s shadow, period 1; e^(−2πikn) = 1
= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i k x}\,dx = \hat f(k) @@ the pieces join; swapping sum and total is held in trust
F(0) = \sum_k c_k @@ 32c’s slope-returns technique
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_n f(n) = \sum_k \hat f(k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the grid-sum technique
  
f(x) = e^{-\pi t x^2},\ \ \hat f(\xi) = t^{-1/2} e^{-\pi \xi^2/t} @@ 32e’s own-transform technique, stretched
t = 2:\ \ \sum e^{-2\pi n^2} = 1.003735 @@ the terms n = 0, ±1 nearly all of it
\htmlClass{new}{2^{-1/2}\sum e^{-\pi k^2/2} = 1.003735} \quad \blacksquare @@ both sums run to |n| = 50 by script

The same holds on any grid in n dimensions. Write the grid Λ as M times the whole-number points, with cell size V = |det M|. Its partner grid Λ* is Mᵀ’s undo times the whole-number points, and Σ_{x∈Λ} f(x) = (1/V) Σ_{y∈Λ*} f̂(y). Held in trust: the proof runs the one above in each coordinate, then changes variables by M as 28 does.

Summing over a grid two ways is ∞20.5’s transform on the group of the line: the turnings that are 1 at every grid point are the points of the dual grid, and the sum of f over the grid equals the sum of its transform over the dual grid. In higher dimension the same pairing of a grid with its dual is what the sign-pattern technique uses. The plan is the pairing; where the balls sit inside a cell, and the order of the grid points, are let go.

inf47.5One Function Caps Every Grid

Take a grid Λ of cell size V whose points are at least r apart; balls of radius r/2 at its points do not overlap, and by the box-share technique the density is vol(B_{r/2})/V. A function now bounds V from below.

Sign-pattern technique: if f is real and even, f(x) ≤ 0 whenever |x| ≥ r, f̂(y) ≥ 0 for every y, and f̂(0) > 0, then every grid packing with points at least r apart has density at most vol(B_{r/2}) · f(0)/f̂(0).

\sum_{x \in \Lambda} f(x) \le f(0) @@ every other point has |x| ≥ r, where f ≤ 0
\sum_{x \in \Lambda} f(x) = \tfrac1V \sum_{y \in \Lambda^*} \hat f(y) \ge \tfrac1V \hat f(0) @@ the grid-sum technique; every f̂(y) ≥ 0
f(0) \ge \hat f(0)/V \Rightarrow V \ge \hat f(0)/f(0) @@ join the two lines
\htmlClass{new}{\text{density} = \tfrac{\mathrm{vol}(B_{r/2})}{V} \le \mathrm{vol}(B_{r/2})\,\tfrac{f(0)}{\hat f(0)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sign-pattern technique
  
f(x) = 1 - |x| \text{ for } |x| \le 1,\ 0 \text{ beyond};\ \ r = 1 @@ f ≤ 0 (in fact 0) past 1
\hat f(\xi) = \Big(\tfrac{\sin \pi\xi}{\pi\xi}\Big)^2 \ge 0,\ \ \hat f(0) = 1 = f(0) @@ by script: f̂(½) = 0.405285 = 4/π²
\text{bound} = \mathrm{vol}(B_{1/2}) \cdot 1 = 1 @@ the ball of radius ½ on the line has length 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{segments of length 1 at the whole numbers fill the line: the bound is met}} \quad \blacksquare @@ f = 0 at every n ≠ 0, and f̂ = 0 at every k ≠ 0

The same proof bounds packings that repeat with several balls per cell, and from them every packing; held in trust, the sums then carry cross terms, and the signs still do the work. So one good function, found once, caps every packing in its dimension. Finding it is the hard part.

inf47.6Why Eight and Twenty-Four

Read the tent backwards. The bound is met only if the two inequalities are equalities: f(x) = 0 at every grid point but 0, and f̂(y) = 0 at every partner point but 0. So a function that proves a grid best must vanish on that grid, and its transform must vanish on the partner grid, while keeping the signs.

In dimension 8 there is a grid E₈: the whole-number points of 8 coordinates with an even total, together with the same points shifted by ½ in every coordinate. Scaled so that its cell has size 1, it is its own partner, and its points lie at distances √2, √4, √6, … from 0.

\text{points at distance } \sqrt2,\ \sqrt4,\ \sqrt6:\ \ 240,\ 2160,\ 6720 @@ counted by script
240 \cdot 1,\ \ 240 \cdot 9,\ \ 240 \cdot 28 @@ 240 times the total of the cubes of the divisors of 1, 2, 3
f = 0 \text{ and } \hat f = 0 \text{ at every } |x| = \sqrt{2k},\ k \ge 1 @@ equality in both lines
f \le 0 \text{ past } \sqrt2:\ \text{a sign change at } \sqrt2,\ \text{a touch at } \sqrt4, \sqrt6, \ldots @@ zeros past √2 must not cross
\htmlClass{new}{\text{density} = \mathrm{vol}(B_{\sqrt2/2}) = \tfrac{\pi^4}{24}\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big)^8 = \tfrac{\pi^4}{384} \approx 0.2537} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ball in 8 dimensions has size π⁴ρ⁸/24

Such an f was found in March 2016, built from functions with a great deal of symmetry under the moves of the upper half plane, which this ladder names only. A week later the same idea, with a grid of dimension 24 whose points sit at √4, √6, …, gave density π¹²/12! ≈ 0.00193 there. Why only these two: in each, a grid is its own partner and its distances squared run through every even number from a start, which is exactly the pattern of zeros those symmetric functions can be made to have. In dimension 3 the grocer’s stack is not its own partner, and no function is known that meets it.

inf47.7A Proof Nobody Could Referee

In three dimensions the sign-pattern technique falls short, and the 1998 proof went another way, the way of 42·now’s four colours: reduce the question to finitely many local arrangements, then settle each by computer. A ball’s neighbours were scored; the stack wins if no arrangement scores above a set value; and the arrangements, after cutting, numbered in the thousands, each settled by inequalities checked with intervals of numbers so that rounding could not mislead.

1998:\ \ \text{a reasoned reduction, plus computer runs} @@ the proof as announced
2003:\ \ \text{a panel of 12 referees},\ \text{“99\% certain”} @@ they could not check every run
2005:\ \ \text{the reasoning printed; the runs vouched for, not refereed} @@ the journal’s compromise
\htmlClass{new}{2014:\ \text{every line and every run through a small checking core}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 38·now’s small-kernel technique

38·now told how the formal check, begun in 2003, put reasoning and runs together through a checker. In February 2026 the eight-dimensional proof of 2016 was checked the same way, in a different checker, the last steps written by a program that fills in formal proofs; the 24-dimensional proof followed within weeks. The two halves of the packing story now meet: a proof too long to read, and a proof short enough to read in an afternoon, both certified by a machine that reads neither for meaning.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞47.oq.1What is the densest packing of balls in dimension 4, or 5, or any dimension other than 1, 2, 3, 8 and 24?
Why it is hard: a proof needs a ceiling that meets a packing, and no function with the sign pattern is known to meet one in those dimensions. This page has not revisited the latest computed bounds.
∞47.oq.2Is the best packing ever not a grid? In dimension 10 the densest packing known repeats with several balls per cell and is not a grid. Nobody has proved that no grid does as well, or that something better does not exist.
Why it is hard: proving a packing best needs a matching upper bound, and in dimension 10 the bounds are far above the packings.
∞47.oq.3How does the best density fall as the dimension n grows? The packings known fill a share that roughly halves with each dimension added, improved by a factor growing like a power of n in 2025, while the best ceilings fall exponentially but more slowly.
Why it is hard: the gap between floor and ceiling is exponential in n, and nobody knows which is nearer the truth. This page has not revisited the exact constants.
∞47.oq.4Can the three-dimensional proof be made short enough for a person to check? The 2014 check settles its truth for those who trust the checker; a proof a reader can follow, of the kind found for 8 and 24, is not known.
Why it is hard: in three dimensions the sign-pattern bound does not meet the stack, so some other single idea would be needed, and none has been found in four hundred years.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question came from cannonballs and snowflakes. In 1611 a short Latin pamphlet on the six-cornered snowflake claimed, with no proof, that the grocer’s stack is the tightest way to pack equal balls. In 1831 the stack was proved best among grids. In 1900 the question went onto a famous Paris list of problems for the coming century, as part of its eighteenth. That a pattern is best was believed by everyone and proved by nobody.

The plane went first, and not cleanly. A proof for discs was printed in 1890 and is widely judged incomplete. A complete one appeared in 1940, and since then simpler ones have been given, among them the triangle proof on this page, written out in 2010. In 1953 the three-dimensional question was reduced in principle to a finite, if enormous, calculation, at a time when no computer could do it.

In the early 1990s a proof by hand was claimed and published; specialists disputed it, and it is not now accepted. The proof announced in August 1998 was of the other kind, a reasoned reduction plus a vast computer check. Referees worked on it for years. In 2003 they reported that they were 99% certain and could not be more, and the journal printed the reasoning in 2005 while vouching for the programs only as programs. Some said a proof that cannot be refereed is not yet a proof; others said that every long proof by hand carries the same risk, only better hidden.

The answer of the author was to remove the doubt instead of arguing about it. The formal check, begun in 2003 and estimated at twenty years, was finished in August 2014, with two proof checkers, and published in 2017. It showed that a proof resting on computer runs can be passed, runs and all, through a checking core small enough for people to read.

Meanwhile the higher dimensions changed shape. A 2003 paper turned the question into one about a single function. Thirteen years later, in March 2016, a proof for dimension 8 appeared; dimension 24 followed within a week, by a group of five. In 2022 the proof for dimension 8 was cited in the award of the best-known prize in mathematics.

What is argued now is where to go next. In 2026 both of the 2016 proofs were checked by machine, the last stretch written by a program that turns proofs into formal steps; the meaning still rests on the statement as written, which people must read. In most dimensions the bounds and the packings remain far apart.

The accepted names

The claim about the grocer’s stack is the Kepler conjecture, now Hales’ theorem; π/√18 is the density of the face-centred cubic packing. The honeycomb-bound technique is Thue’s theorem, and the cutting of the plane used here is the Delaunay triangulation; a full packing is a saturated packing. The proof on this page follows Chang and Wang (2010). The grid-sum technique is the Poisson summation formula, and the partner grid is the dual lattice. The sign-pattern technique is the Cohn–Elkies linear programming bound, and a function meeting it is a magic function. E₈ and the Leech lattice are the grids in dimensions 8 and 24; their shell counts come from the theta series, 240σ₃(k) for E₈. The functions of 2016 are built from modular forms and quasimodular forms. The densest packing known in dimension 10 is Best’s packing P₁₀c. The exponential ceiling is the Kabatiansky–Levenshtein bound. The 2014 check is the Flyspeck project, in HOL Light and Isabelle; the 2026 checks were done in Lean.

Who said it first

Johannes Kepler stated the conjecture in Strena seu de nive sexangula (1611). Carl Friedrich Gauss proved it for lattices (1831). David Hilbert listed it in his eighteenth problem (1900). Axel Thue gave a proof for the plane in 1890; László Fejes Tóth gave a complete proof in 1940 and in 1953 reduced the three-dimensional problem to a finite one. Wu-Yi Hsiang claimed a proof in the early 1990s. Thomas Hales announced his proof, with Samuel Ferguson, in 1998; the referee panel was led by Gábor Fejes Tóth; Hales led Flyspeck to completion in 2014. Hai-Chau Chang and Lih-Chung Wang published the short proof of Thue’s theorem in 2010. Henry Cohn and Noam Elkies proved the bound (2003). Maryna Viazovska proved the case of dimension 8 (14 March 2016), and with Henry Cohn, Abhinav Kumar, Stephen D. Miller and Danylo Radchenko the case of dimension 24 (21 March 2016). Viazovska received a Fields Medal in 2022, its citation naming the proof for dimension 8. The 2026 formalisation was led by Sidharth Hariharan and Viazovska, with Math, Inc.’s system Gauss.

A proof that is not allowed

“Around each disc draw its region of points nearer to it than to any other centre; each region contains a hexagon of area 2√3, so the density is at most π/(2√3).” Not allowed as it stands: the regions of a general packing need not contain a regular hexagon, and showing each has area at least 2√3 is the whole difficulty. The triangles of this page do that work.
