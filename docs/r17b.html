17b · proof

Its slope

17a defined ln by an area and bounded it. This step finds its slope, and the slope is the one 16f could not find a function for: one over the input. That fills the gap in rung 16's table of antiderivatives, and closes the flag 16f left open. It also opens a question 17a left shut, what to do with one over the input when the input is negative, and closes it with the logarithm of the input’s size. And it shows that the logarithm bends down everywhere, so its tangent at one lies above it: the line that 17a met as a bound.

The slope of the logarithm is one over the input, by area-slope. The logarithm of the input’s size has the same slope on both sides of zero, by chain for slopes on the left. So the area under one over the input is the logarithm of its size, plus a constant, on any interval that misses zero. The slope of the slope is negative, so the logarithm bends down.

17b1The slope of ln

Slope-of-log technique: ln is differentiable on (0, ∞), and ln′(x) = 1/x.

\ln x = \textstyle\int_1^x \tfrac{dt}{t} @@ ﹀ ln (17a)
\tfrac1t \text{ is continuous at every } x \gt 0 @@ one-over-x-is-continuous (11b)
\htmlClass{new}{\ln'(x) = \tfrac1x} \quad \blacksquare @@ area-slope (16b): the area function's slope is the height at its end

16f proved that no power xr+1/(r + 1) has slope x⁻¹; no-power-for-1/x said the table has a hole at r = −1. Slope-of-log fills it, with a function that is not a power at all.

\textstyle\int x^r\,dx = \tfrac{x^{r+1}}{r+1} + C,\ \ r \ne -1 @@ 16f's power line, for rational r
\textstyle\int x^{-1}\,dx = \ln x + C \quad (x \gt 0) @@ slope-of-log and all-antiderivatives (14e)

17b2When x is negative

1/x is defined and continuous for x < 0 too, so antiderivative-exists says it has an antiderivative there. ln x has no value there. ln(−x) does.

Area-under-1/x technique: for x ≠ 0, the slope of ln|x| is 1/x. So on any interval that does not contain 0, ∫ dx/x = ln|x| + C.

x \gt 0:\ \ \ln|x| = \ln x,\ \ \text{slope } \tfrac1x @@ slope-of-log
x \lt 0:\ \ \ln|x| = \ln(-x),\ \ \text{slope } \tfrac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \tfrac1x @@ chain for slopes (13e): the outside's slope at −x, times the inside's slope −1
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int \tfrac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C} \quad \blacksquare @@ all-antiderivatives on the interval; an interval that misses 0 lies on one side

On all of x ≠ 0 at once the C need not be one constant. Same-slopes needs an interval, and (−∞, 0) and (0, ∞) are two. Any function that is ln|x| + C₁ on the left and ln|x| + C₂ on the right has slope 1/x everywhere it is defined.

In 1712 one of the first workers in the calculus argued to another that the log of −x must equal the log of x. The two have the same slope, d(−x)/(−x) = dx/x. The other answered that the logs of negative numbers are not real at all. The equal slopes, he said, prove nothing about the values.

17b3ln bends down

Log-bends-down technique: ln″(x) = −1/x² < 0 for x > 0; the graph of ln lies below each of its tangent lines, touching only at the point of contact.

\ln''(x) = (x^{-1})' = -x^{-2} \lt 0 @@ slope-of-log, then the power rule (13h)
\ln' \text{ falls on } (0, \infty) @@ slope-sign-order (14d) applied to ln′
\htmlClass{new}{\ln \text{ bends down}} @@ bends-up (14g), run with the slope falling
\text{tangent at } 1:\ \ y = 0 + 1\cdot(x - 1) = x - 1 @@ ln 1 = 0, ln′(1) = 1
\ln x \le x - 1 \quad \blacksquare @@ the curve stays under its tangent; 17a's upper bound, seen a second way
  
\textstyle\int_2^8 \tfrac{dx}{x} = \ln 8 - \ln 2 @@ area-is-a-change (16c) with G = ln
\textstyle\int_{-4}^{-1} \tfrac{dx}{x} = \ln|{-1}| - \ln|{-4}| = -\ln 4 @@ area-is-a-change with G = ln|x|; [−4, −1] misses 0
\tfrac{d}{dx}\ln(x^2 + 1) = \tfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1} @@ chain for slopes; x² + 1 > 0 always
\textstyle\int \tfrac{dx}{3x - 6} = \tfrac13 \ln|3x - 6| + C @@ substitution for areas (16d), r = 3x − 6, on x < 2 or on x > 2
\lim_{h \to 0} \tfrac{\ln(1 + h)}{h} = 1 @@ the slope of ln at 1, written as 13a's limit

Who said it first

Johann Bernoulli argued to Leibniz that the log of −x must equal the log of x, since the two have the same slope, d(−x)/(−x) = dx/x. Leibniz answered that the logs of negative numbers are not real at all, and that the equal slopes prove nothing about the values.

Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli, letters of 1712–1713; paraphrase

In 17b.4.5, the one who said 0 is Johann Bernoulli, and the one who said “not real” is Leibniz.
