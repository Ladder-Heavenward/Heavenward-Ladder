28p · technique

How Errors Spread

Every measured number comes with a doubt: a ruler good to a fifth of a millimetre, a stopwatch good to a hundredth of a second. When measured numbers go into a formula, the doubts go in too, and something comes out the other side. Rung thirteen followed a doubt through a single power. With several inputs, each doubt travels by its own road, weighted by how much the formula cares about that input, and the roads add. This step puts a true ceiling on what comes out, finds the quick rule for products and powers, where the doubts as shares simply add, and then runs the question backwards. When the thing you want is found by undoing a formula, as a position is found from distances, a small doubt can grow large, and the determinant of the slope grid says when.

The doubt in the output is at most the sum of each input’s doubt times the largest partial slope along it. For products of powers, doubts measured as shares add, each times its power. Undoing a formula multiplies the doubts by the undone slope grid, which is large when the determinant is small.

28p1A Ceiling on the Spread

Let x₁, …, xₙ be measured as p₁, …, pₙ, each within a known doubt: the true value is pᵢ + hᵢ with |hᵢ| ≤ Δᵢ. The true inputs then lie in the box of points within Δᵢ of pᵢ in each place. The question is how far f(p + h) can be from f(p). This Δ (capital delta) is not 13a's mark for a change.

Error-bound technique: let f be smoothly sloped on the box. Suppose |∂f/∂xᵢ| ≤ Mᵢ at every point of the box. Then |f(p + h) − f(p)| ≤ M₁Δ₁ + ⋯ + MₙΔₙ for every true point p + h in the box.

g(t) = f(p + th),\ \ 0 \le t \le 1 @@ along the segment from the measured point to the true one, which stays in the box
f(p + h) - f(p) = g(1) - g(0) = g'(c),\ \ 0 < c < 1 @@ the average-slope technique (14c), the same walk as the segment-bound technique (28e1)
g'(c) = \textstyle\sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(p + ch)\,h_i @@ the roads-add technique (28c3)
|g'(c)| \le \textstyle\sum_i M_i\,|h_i| @@ the triangle technique (8h), term by term
\htmlClass{new}{|f(p + h) - f(p)| \le M_1\Delta_1 + \cdots + M_n\Delta_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ |hᵢ| ≤ Δᵢ
  
V = \pi r^2h,\ \ r = 3.30 \pm 0.02\ \text{cm},\ \ h = 12.2 \pm 0.1\ \text{cm} @@ a can measured with a ruler
V = 417.4\ \text{cm}^3,\ \ V_r = 2\pi rh \approx 253.0,\ \ V_h = \pi r^2 \approx 34.2 @@ the one-knob technique (28a2), at the measured point
\text{flat estimate: } 253.0(0.02) + 34.2(0.1) \approx 5.06 + 3.42 = 8.48 @@ the best flat map (28b1), with each doubt taken the bad way
M_r = 2\pi(3.32)(12.3) \approx 256.6,\ \ M_h = \pi(3.32)^2 \approx 34.6 @@ the largest partial slopes on the box
\htmlClass{new}{|\Delta V| \le 5.13 + 3.46 = 8.59\ \text{cm}^3:\ \ V = 417 \pm 9\ \text{cm}^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the error-bound technique; the true worst is 8.54

The flat estimate with the slopes at the measured point is what people use every day, and its own error shrinks faster than the doubts (28b1). The ceiling with the largest slopes on the box costs a little more and is never wrong. Both treat each doubt as if it fell the bad way. Doubts that fall at random partly cancel, and how much they cancel is a question about chance, for rung thirty.

28p2Shares Add

13m defined the relative error of a measured x as Δx/x, the doubt as a share, and proved that through a power k the share is multiplied by k. Many laws of science are products of powers, and for them the shares simply add.

Relative-errors-add technique: let f = c x₁^k₁ ⋯ xₙ^kₙ with c > 0 and every xᵢ > 0. If xᵢ is measured with relative error eᵢ, the relative error of f is, to first order, at most |k₁|e₁ + ⋯ + |kₙ|eₙ. To first order means: by the best flat map, whose own error shrinks faster than the doubts.

\ln f = \ln c + k_1\ln x_1 + \cdots + k_n\ln x_n @@ the log-of-a-product and log-of-a-power techniques (17c), real powers by 17g
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\ln f = \tfrac{k_i}{x_i} @@ the one-knob technique (28a2) and the slope-of-log technique (17b)
\Delta(\ln f) \approx k_1\tfrac{\Delta x_1}{x_1} + \cdots + k_n\tfrac{\Delta x_n}{x_n} @@ the best flat map of ln f (28b1)
\Delta(\ln f) = \ln\big(1 + \tfrac{\Delta f}{f}\big) \approx \tfrac{\Delta f}{f} @@ the best flat map of ln at 1, of slope 1 (17b)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{|\Delta f|}{f} \lesssim |k_1|e_1 + \cdots + |k_n|e_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the triangle technique (8h); ≲ marks a bound to first order
  
g = \tfrac{4\pi^2L}{T^2},\ \ L = 1.000 \pm 0.002\ \text{m},\ \ T = 2.006 \pm 0.005\ \text{s} @@ a pendulum’s length and the time of one swing out and back
e_L = 0.2\%,\ \ e_T = 0.249\% @@ shares of the measured values
e_g \lesssim 1\cdot0.2\% + 2\cdot0.249\% \approx 0.70\% @@ the relative-errors-add technique: L to the power 1, T to the power −2
\htmlClass{new}{g = 9.811 \pm 0.069\ \text{m/s}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the time counts twice: improve the stopwatch first

The powers tell you where to spend effort. The time enters squared, so its share counts twice. Timing twenty swings and dividing by twenty cuts the time’s doubt twentyfold with the same stopwatch, and then the length becomes the weak link.

28p3Measuring Backwards

Often the thing wanted is not measured. Something else is, y = F(x), and x is found by undoing F. Two radio beacons give your distances to each, and your position is the point with those distances. Then the doubts in y pass backwards through the undoing.

Undo-error technique: let F be smoothly sloped near x, with slope grid J = DF(x) undoable. If x is found by undoing F from a measured y, then, to first order, the error in x is J⁻¹ times the error in y. Every entry of J⁻¹ is a stand-in of J divided by det J, so when det J is near 0, small doubts in y make large doubts in x.

G(F(x)) = x \text{ near } x,\ \ G \text{ smoothly sloped} @@ the local-undo technique (28e2): det J ≠ 0
DG(y) = DF(x)^{-1} = J^{-1} @@ the undo-slope technique (28e3), at y = F(x)
\Delta x = G(y + \Delta y) - G(y) \approx J^{-1}\Delta y @@ the best flat map of G (28b1)
J^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{\det J}\,S @@ the stand-in technique (27g2): S is the grid of stand-ins
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta x \approx \tfrac{1}{\det J}\,S\,\Delta y} \quad \blacksquare @@ small det J, large Δx
  
F(x, y) = \big(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2},\ \sqrt{(x - 10)^2 + y^2}\big) @@ distances, in km, to beacons at (0, 0) and (10, 0)
\text{at } (5, 12):\ \ J = \tfrac{1}{13}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 12 \\ -5 & 12 \end{pmatrix},\ \ \det J = \tfrac{120}{169} \approx 0.71 @@ the slope-grid technique (28b2): x/d₁, y/d₁ and so on
J^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.3 & -1.3 \\ 0.542 & 0.542 \end{pmatrix} @@ the stand-in technique (27g2)
\text{doubts } \pm 0.01:\ \ |\Delta x| \le 0.026,\ \ |\Delta y| \le 0.011 @@ add the sizes along each row of J⁻¹, times 0.01
\text{at } (5, 1):\ \ \det J = \tfrac{10}{26} \approx 0.38,\ \ |\Delta y| \le 0.051 @@ the same work: near the line through the beacons
\htmlClass{new}{\text{close to the line through the beacons, the north is poorly fixed}} \quad \blacksquare @@ det J = 10y/(d₁d₂) heads to 0 as y does

The same happens with no curves at all. Undoing a grid whose determinant is small, as in 27j’s elimination, multiplies the doubts in the right-hand side by the large entries of the undone grid. Two samenesses that are nearly the same sameness fix their answer badly, however carefully they are solved.

The accepted names

Following doubts through a formula is the propagation of error (or of uncertainty). The flat estimate is the total differential, df = fx dx + fy dy, used as a linear error estimate; the error-bound technique is a form of the mean value inequality. A share is a relative (or fractional, or percentage) error; the relative-errors-add technique is the rule for products and quotients, found by logarithmic differentiation. For errors that are random and independent, the sum is replaced by the square root of the sum of squares, adding in quadrature. How much an undoing magnifies doubts is measured by the condition number of the matrix; a system with a large one is ill-conditioned. In navigation by beacons or satellites, the magnification by the geometry is the dilution of precision.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss built a theory of errors in observation in 1809 and 1821 to 1823, for astronomy and for surveying the kingdom of Hanover. Alan Turing named the condition number in 1948, in a paper on the rounding errors of matrix work. Henry Kater measured the pull of gravity with a reversible pendulum in 1817.
