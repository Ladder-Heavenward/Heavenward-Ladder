How Much of the Wind Can a Turbine Take?

How much of the wind can a turbine take? A turbine takes energy from the wind by slowing it down. Stop the wind dead behind the blades and nothing more can flow through, so the turbine takes nothing; leave it at full speed and it takes nothing either. Somewhere between is a best amount of slowing. This page finds the wind’s power through the blades, writes the turbine’s share as a function of how much it slows the wind, finds the top of that function with 14h, and gets a limit no turbine of any design can pass: 16/27 of the wind’s power.

Wind of speed V through a disc of area A carries power ½ρAV³. A turbine that slows the wind at the disc to V(1 − a) slows it far downstream to V(1 − 2a), and takes 4a(1 − a)² of the wind’s power. The slope of that is 0 at a = 1/3, where the share is 16/27, about 59%.

14eng1The Wind’s Power Grows as the Cube of Its Speed

Claire’s town is planning a wind turbine whose blades sweep a disc of area A. Wind at speed V carries air through that disc: each second, a column V metres long and A square metres across, of mass ρAV, where ρ, the air’s density, is about 1.2 kg per cubic metre.

\text{mass per second} = \rho A V @@ a column V long, each second
\text{energy of motion per kg} = \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 @@ 14·phys
\htmlClass{new}{P_{\text{wind}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A V^3} @@ power, the cube of the speed

Twice the wind speed, eight times the power. A site with winds 10% stronger gives about a third more power, which is why turbines go on ridges and out at sea, and why their towers are tall, where the wind is faster.

14eng2A Turbine Takes Energy by Slowing the Wind

The turbine takes energy by slowing the air. Say it slows the wind at the disc to V(1 − a), where a, the slowing, is a share between 0 and 1. Momentum (14·phys) and energy together fix what happens downstream: far behind the blades, the wind’s speed is V(1 − 2a), and the speed at the disc is the average of the speeds in front and far behind.

\text{mass per second through the disc} = \rho A V(1 - a) @@ slower air, less of it
\text{energy lost per kg} = \tfrac{1}{2}V^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}V^2(1 - 2a)^2 = 2V^2 a(1 - a) @@ 
P = \rho A V(1 - a) \cdot 2V^2 a (1 - a) = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A V^3 \cdot 4a(1 - a)^2 @@ mass per second times energy lost
\htmlClass{new}{\text{share taken: } C(a) = 4a(1 - a)^2} @@ of the wind’s power

The share is a cubic in a, a function on the slowings from 0 to 1/2 (footing 3 says why it stops there).

14eng3Slow It Too Much and the Wind Goes Round

Taking more energy means slowing the air more. But slowed air piles up in front of the disc, and some of the oncoming wind goes round the turbine instead of through it.

a = 0:\ C = 0 @@ no slowing, nothing taken
a = \tfrac{1}{2}:\ \text{wind far behind} = V(1 - 2a) = 0 @@ the air behind would stop
C(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{a > \tfrac{1}{2}:\ \text{the formula gives backward wind, and fails}} @@ the model holds for 0 ≤ a ≤ 1/2

Past a = 1/2, the air behind would have to blow back toward the turbine, which it doesn’t: the flow breaks up into churning eddies. The best slowing lies somewhere between 0 and 1/2.

14eng4The Best Slowing, and the Limit 16/27

C is a smooth function on a closed interval, so its greatest value is at an end or where its slope is 0 (14h).

C(a) = 4a(1 - a)^2 @@ 
C\prime(a) = 4(1 - a)^2 - 8a(1 - a) = 4(1 - a)(1 - 3a) @@ product technique (13c)
C\prime(a) = 0:\ a = \tfrac{1}{3} @@ a = 1 is outside the interval
C(\tfrac{1}{3}) = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \tfrac{4}{9} = \tfrac{16}{27} \approx 0.593 @@ above both ends, 0 and 1/2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no turbine takes more than } \tfrac{16}{27} \text{ of the wind’s power}} @@ whatever its blades

Best-slowing technique: a turbine takes the most from the wind when it slows the air at its blades to two thirds of the wind’s speed, and then it takes 16/27 of the wind’s power; no design can take more. The limit comes from mass, momentum and energy alone. Blade shape, number and material decide only how close a turbine gets.

14eng5Real Turbines Against the Limit

Real blades lose some energy to drag, to the swirl they leave behind, and at their tips. The best large turbines reach shares of about 0.45 to 0.5 at their best wind speeds: four fifths of the way to the limit.

\text{blades } 50 \text{ m long: } A \approx 7\,850 \text{ m}^2 @@ the disc they sweep
V = 10 \text{ m/s}:\ P_{\text{wind}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.2 \cdot 7\,850 \cdot 1000 \approx 4.7 \text{ MW} @@ 
\text{limit: } \tfrac{16}{27} \cdot 4.7 \approx 2.8 \text{ MW} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at a share of } 0.45:\ \approx 2.1 \text{ MW}} @@ what a good turbine makes

The limit tells an engineer how much room is left: a turbine at 0.45 has little to gain from better blades, and more from a windier site or a bigger disc. When a wind-tunnel team tests a model rotor, a measured share above 16/27 means the test is wrong, usually because the tunnel walls are squeezing the flow.

Accepted names

The slowing a is the axial induction factor, the share C is the power coefficient C_P, and the disc model is actuator disc or momentum theory. The limit 16/27 is the Betz limit. The slowed wind behind the turbine is its wake.

Who said it first

Albert Betz published the limit in 1920; Frederick Lanchester in 1915 and Nikolay Zhukovsky in 1920 reached it as well.
