Can Teleportation Be Drawn?

Can teleportation be drawn? An engineer draws a radio as boxes joined by wires, and rung 47 showed that such a drawing names one part whichever way it is read. Quantum circuits are drawn the same way, with side by side meaning the tensor product. Add two more pieces of wire: a cup, which starts two wires together out of nothing, and a cap, which ends two wires together. Then a wire that bends round a cup and back round a cap can be pulled straight, and the algebra says the same. In those drawings the entangled pair of rung 41 is a cup, and a joint reading of two qubits is a cap. Teleporting a qubit, which was proposed in 1993 and done in a laboratory in 1997, becomes a wire pulled straight, with a small box left on it that only an ordinary message can remove. This way of drawing was made a foundation in 2004. In 2008 a calculus of two kinds of dot was built on it, and in 2018 it was shown that every true equation between qubit circuits can be derived in it by redrawing. Finding the cheapest circuit is still hard, and was shown to be as hard as the hardest checkable problems in 2024.

Boxes are maps, wires are spaces, side by side is ⊗. A cup is Σ eᵢ ⊗ eᵢ, a cap is eᵢ ⊗ ej ↦ [i = j]. Yanking: (cap ⊗ I)(I ⊗ cup) = I. Sliding: (A ⊗ I) cup = (I ⊗ Aᵀ) cup. With Φ = cup/√2 and the four readings (σ ⊗ I)Φ, the receiver’s qubit after reading σ is ½ σᵀψ: the wire is straight, and σᵀ waits for two bits.

inf12.1Boxes, Wires, Side by Side

Draw a linear map as a box, its input space as a wire coming in from below and its output space as a wire going out above. Boxes in a row are maps in a row. 47·eng.2 already noted that two quantum gates side by side mean 47h1’s tensor product, U ⊗ V, a 4 by 4 grid for two qubits, and that the box-drawing technique holds there: (U′U) ⊗ (V′V) = (U′ ⊗ V′)(U ⊗ V).

So a drawing of gates is read as one grid, whether it is read row by row or column by column. A bare wire is the identity I. The Hadamard gate H = (1, 1; 1, −1)/√2 on the first qubit only is H ⊗ I: on |00⟩ it gives (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2.

Wire-slide technique: a box on one wire slides past a box on another: (A ⊗ I)(I ⊗ B) = A ⊗ B = (I ⊗ B)(A ⊗ I).

(A \otimes I)(I \otimes B) = (A\,I) \otimes (I\,B) @@ the box-drawing technique for ⊗ (47·eng.2)
\phantom{(A \otimes I)(I \otimes B)} = A \otimes B @@ I does nothing
\phantom{A \otimes B} = (I\,A) \otimes (B\,I) = (I \otimes B)(A \otimes I) @@ the same, read the other way
\htmlClass{new}{(A \otimes I)(I \otimes B) = (I \otimes B)(A \otimes I)} \quad \blacksquare @@ boxes on separate wires do not wait for each other

inf12.2A Cup and a Cap

Let V have basis e₁, …, eₙ. The cup is the vector Σ eᵢ ⊗ eᵢ of V ⊗ V, drawn as a wire bent into a U opening upward: two wires that start together. The cap is the reading V ⊗ V → numbers that sends eᵢ ⊗ ej to 1 when i = j and to 0 otherwise, drawn as an upside-down U: two wires that end together. On real lists the cap of v ⊗ w is 27i1’s dot v · w, one of 31a1’s products.

For a qubit, n = 2 and the cup is |00⟩ + |11⟩. That is √2 times 41·phys.2’s entangled state Φ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, which the no-split technique showed is no pair of single states. So an entangled pair is drawn as a cup: two wires born joined. The cap, scaled by 1/√2, is the reading “is the pair in the state Φ?”, one outcome of a joint reading of two qubits.

Cup-cap technique: the cap of the cup is n, the dimension: a loop of wire is the number n.

\mathrm{cap}(\textstyle\sum_i e_i \otimes e_i) = \sum_i \mathrm{cap}(e_i \otimes e_i) @@ the cap is linear
\phantom{\mathrm{cap}(\textstyle\sum_i e_i \otimes e_i)} = \textstyle\sum_i 1 @@ i = i each time
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{cap} \circ \mathrm{cup} = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ a closed loop is a number

inf12.3Pulling the Wire Straight

Draw a wire that comes up, bends over a cap to the left, comes down the middle, and bends round a cup back up the right. On paper it is a snake. Pull its ends, and it is a straight wire. The algebra agrees.

Yanking technique: (cap ⊗ I)(I ⊗ cup) = I and (I ⊗ cap)(cup ⊗ I) = I: a wire bent round a cup and a cap pulls straight.

(I \otimes \mathrm{cup})(e_k) = e_k \otimes \textstyle\sum_i e_i \otimes e_i @@ a cup beside the wire
\phantom{(I \otimes \mathrm{cup})(e_k)} = \textstyle\sum_i e_k \otimes e_i \otimes e_i @@ ⊗ is straight in each slot (47h1)
(\mathrm{cap} \otimes I)(\cdots) = \textstyle\sum_i [k = i]\; e_i @@ the cap on the first two
\htmlClass{new}{(\mathrm{cap} \otimes I)(I \otimes \mathrm{cup})(e_k) = e_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ only i = k survives; a linear map fixed on a basis is fixed (27d1)

Check it with grids for a qubit: I ⊗ cup is the 8 by 2 grid sending |0⟩ to |000⟩ + |011⟩ and |1⟩ to |100⟩ + |111⟩; cap ⊗ I is the 2 by 8 grid reading the first two qubits equal and passing the third. Their product, 2 by 2, is I. A script multiplies them out.

inf12.4A Box Slides Round a Cup

A box on one leg of a cup can move to the other leg, turned over. Its turn-over is the transpose Aᵀ of 27d’s grid: rows become columns.

Slide-round-cup technique: (A ⊗ I) cup = (I ⊗ Aᵀ) cup, for every grid A.

(A \otimes I)\,\textstyle\sum_i e_i \otimes e_i = \sum_i A e_i \otimes e_i = \sum_{i,j} A_{ji}\; e_j \otimes e_i @@ A eᵢ is column i of A
(I \otimes A^{T})\,\textstyle\sum_j e_j \otimes e_j = \sum_j e_j \otimes A^{T} e_j = \sum_{i,j} (A^{T})_{ij}\; e_j \otimes e_i @@ Aᵀ eⱼ is column j of Aᵀ
(A^{T})_{ij} = A_{ji} @@ the transpose
\htmlClass{new}{(A \otimes I)\,\mathrm{cup} = (I \otimes A^{T})\,\mathrm{cup}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same entry at every eⱼ ⊗ eᵢ

With A = (1, 2; 3, 4), both sides are |00⟩ + 3|10⟩ + 2|01⟩ + 4|11⟩. For the gates X = (0, 1; 1, 0) and Z = (1, 0; 0, −1), Xᵀ = X and Zᵀ = Z; for XZ, (XZ)ᵀ = ZX = −XZ.

inf12.5Teleportation in Three Pictures

The sender has a qubit in a state ψ she does not know, and one half of Φ; the receiver has the other half. The sender reads her two qubits along the four states (σ ⊗ I)Φ, for σ = I, X, Z, XZ: a right-angled basis of the four-dimensional space. Each reading is a cap with a box σ on one leg. By 41·phys.1’s square-chance law, the reading σ comes with chance |½ σᵀψ|².

First picture: the cup of Φ with ψ beside it. Second: the sender’s reading closes ψ’s wire and the cup’s left leg with a cap carrying σ; slide σ round the cup, and it sits on the receiver’s leg as σᵀ. Third: the wire from ψ through the cap and the cup to the receiver is a snake; pull it straight. The receiver’s qubit is σᵀψ, scaled by ½.

Teleport technique: after the sender’s reading σ, which comes with chance ¼ for each σ, the receiver’s qubit is in the state σᵀψ; applying (σᵀ)⁻¹ returns ψ.

(\langle (\sigma \otimes I)\Phi | \otimes I)(\psi \otimes \Phi) = \tfrac12\,(\mathrm{cap} \otimes I)(\sigma^{T} \otimes I \otimes I)(\psi \otimes \mathrm{cup}) @@ Φ = cup/√2 twice; the box on the cap
\phantom{(\cdots)(\psi \otimes \Phi)} = \tfrac12\,(\mathrm{cap} \otimes I)(I \otimes I \otimes \sigma^{T})(\psi \otimes \mathrm{cup}) @@ slide round the cup
\phantom{(\cdots)(\psi \otimes \Phi)} = \tfrac12\,\sigma^{T}\,(\mathrm{cap} \otimes I)(I \otimes \mathrm{cup})\,\psi = \tfrac12\,\sigma^{T}\psi @@ the wire-slide technique, then yanking
|\tfrac12\,\sigma^{T}\psi|^2 = \tfrac14 @@ σᵀ keeps lengths, and |ψ| = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the receiver holds } \sigma^{T}\psi;\ (\sigma^{T})^{-1}\sigma^{T}\psi = \psi} \quad \blacksquare @@ he undoes the box he is told about

With ψ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + ½|1⟩, the 30° photon of 41·phys.1, and the sender reading σ = X, the receiver holds ½|0⟩ + (√3/2)|1⟩ until he applies X. The box on the receiver’s wire is the whole difference between the receiver holding ψ and the receiver holding nothing useful. Without being told σ, the receiver holds σᵀψ with chance ¼ for each σ, whose density grid is ½I, as 41·phys.4 requires: no message has moved. Two ordinary bits, naming σ, finish the job.

inf12.6Two Kinds of Dot

A calculus of drawings for qubits uses two kinds of dot. A green dot with m wires in and k out, carrying an angle α, is the map |0…0⟩ ↦ |0…0⟩, |1…1⟩ ↦ eiα|1…1⟩, everything else to 0: it copies along |0⟩, |1⟩. A red dot is the same along the right-angled pair (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2. The cup is a green dot with no wires in, two out and angle 0. The copy gate of 41·phys.5 is a green dot followed by a red one.

Dot-fusion technique: two green dots joined by a wire are one green dot, and their angles add; here for one wire in and one out.

\text{green}(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\alpha} \end{pmatrix} @@ |0⟩ ↦ |0⟩, |1⟩ ↦ e^{iα}|1⟩
\text{green}(\beta)\,\text{green}(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\beta} e^{i\alpha} \end{pmatrix} @@ diagonal grids multiply entry by entry
\htmlClass{new}{= \text{green}(\alpha + \beta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^{iβ}e^{iα} = e^{i(α+β)} (25)

Every redrawing rule of the calculus is an equation between grids, so every redrawing is sound: a drawing derived from another names the same map. The converse is the deep part. In 2018 two groups showed that with a suitable list of rules every equation between qubit circuits that is true as grids can be derived by redrawing alone. That is told here, not proved.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞12.oq.1Finding the circuit with the fewest of the expensive gates for a given task was shown in 2024 to be at least as hard as the hardest problems whose answers can be checked quickly (41j). It is not known to be no harder. How hard is it exactly, and do redrawings in the calculus of dots give good circuits fast, even when they cannot give the best?
∞12.oq.2Which redrawing rules are really needed? A rule that can be derived from the others can be dropped. A preprint of August 2026 claims to have found complete lists of rules from which no rule can be dropped, settling a question open since about 2017. Whether the claim stands is not yet revisited here. If it does, what do the rules that cannot be dropped say about qubits?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Teleportation was proposed in 1993 by a group of six physicists, as a way to move an unknown quantum state using a shared entangled pair and two ordinary bits. The name drew attention and some scorn: nothing travels, and the original is destroyed by the reading. In December 1997 a team in Innsbruck reported doing it with photons.

In 2004 two computer scientists recast quantum protocols in the language of categories in which every space has a cup and a cap. In that setting teleportation, and the swapping of entanglement, came out of the yanking rule and a few others, with the arithmetic of grids left out. The pictures were shorter than the matrix proofs they replaced.

In 2008 the two kinds of dot were introduced, and the calculus grew. The question was whether it was complete: could every true equation be derived? Partial answers came from 2013 on, for the circuits that can be simulated efficiently. In 2018 two groups gave complete lists of rules, first for a fragment enough to approximate every circuit, then for all of them.

The calculus is now used in software that simplifies circuits before they run on machines, where each expensive gate costs. A 2024 paper showed that cutting the number of the most expensive gates to the minimum is as hard as the hardest checkable problems, so redrawing can help but cannot be expected always to win. A preprint of August 2026 claims that the lists of rules can be cut to ones in which every rule is needed; it has not yet been checked by others.

The accepted names

The cup and cap are the unit and counit of a compact closed category; the yanking rule is the snake equation or zigzag identity; the four readings (σ ⊗ I)Φ are the Bell basis, and σ ∈ {I, X, Z, XZ} are the Pauli corrections. The setting of 2004 is categorical quantum mechanics; the calculus of two dots is the ZX-calculus, its dots are spiders, and dot fusion is spider fusion. The expensive gate is the T gate, and the count is the T-count.

Who said it first

Charles Bennett, Gilles Brassard, Claude Crépeau, Richard Jozsa, Asher Peres and William Wootters proposed teleportation (1993). Dik Bouwmeester, Jian-Wei Pan, Klaus Mattle, Manfred Eibl, Harald Weinfurter and Anton Zeilinger reported it at Innsbruck (Nature, December 1997); Samson Abramsky and Bob Coecke, “A categorical semantics of quantum protocols” (LICS 2004). Bob Coecke and Ross Duncan introduced the ZX-calculus (2008). Miriam Backens, completeness for stabilizer circuits (2013); Emmanuel Jeandel, Simon Perdrix and Renaud Vilmart (2018), and Amar Hadzihasanovic, Kang Feng Ng and Quanlong Wang (2018), completeness. John van de Wetering and Matthew Amy, “Optimising quantum circuits is generally hard” (2024). Harry Stoltz and Renaud Vilmart, “Minimality of the pure qubit ZX calculus” (preprint, 2026; not yet revisited).
