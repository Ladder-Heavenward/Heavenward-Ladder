39i · proof

Adding with gates

Rung four wrote numbers in twos and added them in columns, carrying from each place to the next. Its computing annex built that column sum out of gates and chained the gates place by place. This step lays the sum down as a lesson, from the arithmetic of the first step of this rung, and then asks the question the annex did not: how long does it take? A carry can travel the whole width of the number, place by place, and every place costs two gates’ worth of waiting, so a wide adder is slow, and the clock of the last step must wait for the slowest sum. The cure is to stop waiting for carries one at a time. Each place can say, before any carry arrives, whether it will make a carry of its own and whether it will pass one along. Those two facts combine in pairs, the pairs in pairs again, and a tree of combinations reaches the top place after about as many levels as the number of times the width can be halved. Then the same adder subtracts, once negative numbers are written the way a car’s odometer would write them running backwards, and the step ends with what happens when a sum is too big for its row of catches.

Three bits add to a sum bit, their exactly-one, and a carry, at-least-two. Chained, the carry waits two gates a place. Each place says whether it makes a carry and whether it passes one; those pairs combine in a tree of about the log of the width levels. Negatives are the complement plus one, so the adder subtracts, and a sum too big wraps round.

39i1Three bits in, two out

In a column of a sum in twos (4i12, 4i14) there are three bits to add: one from each number and the carry in, c. The total is 0, 1, 2 or 3, two bits: a sum bit, worth 1, and a carry out, worth 2. 39a3 found the two-bit case: x + y = 2xy + (x ⊕ y). 4·comp built the three-bit case from two two-bit adders as an annex; here it is laid down from the arithmetic.

Three-bit-sum technique: x + y + c is twice “at least two of x, y, c”, plus x ⊕ y ⊕ c.

x + y = 2xy + (x \oplus y) @@ the remainder-by-two technique (39a3)
x + y + c = 2xy + (x \oplus y) + c = 2xy + 2(x \oplus y)c + (x \oplus y \oplus c) @@ the same technique on x ⊕ y and c
xy \text{ and } (x \oplus y)c \text{ are never both } 1 @@ if xy = 1, then x ⊕ y = 0
xy + (x \oplus y)c = xy \lor (x \oplus y)c @@ the or-as-greater technique with no overlap (39a2)
\htmlClass{new}{x + y + c = 2\,\big(xy \lor (x \oplus y)c\big) + (x \oplus y \oplus c)} \quad \blacksquare @@ and xy ∨ (x ⊕ y)c is 1 exactly when at least two of the three are 1

Two names keep the rest short. In each place k, gₖ = xₖyₖ says the place makes a carry by itself, and pₖ = xₖ ⊕ yₖ says it passes one along. The carry out is then cₖ₊₁ = gₖ ∨ (pₖ ∧ cₖ), and the sum bit is pₖ ⊕ cₖ.

39i2The carry chain

Chain n places, the carry out of each into the next, c₀ = 0. Every gate takes a delay τ to answer. The carry rule cₖ₊₁ = gₖ ∨ (pₖ ∧ cₖ) is a both then an either: two delays from cₖ to cₖ₊₁.

Carry-delay technique: in a chain of n places the last carry may wait 2nτ, and it does when every place passes and none makes.

\text{all } g_k, p_k \text{ ready after a few delays} @@ each place sees only its own two bits
c_{k+1} \text{ ready } 2\tau \text{ after } c_k @@ a both, then an either
c_n \text{ ready } 2n\tau \text{ after } c_0 @@ induction on k (4c)
x = 0111\ldots1,\ y = 0\ldots01:\ \ p_k = 1 \text{ above place } 0 @@ the carry made in place 0 must pass through every place
\htmlClass{new}{n = 64,\ \tau = 15 \text{ ps}:\ 2 \cdot 64 \cdot 15 = 1920 \text{ ps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ about 1.9 ns

39h3 says the clock may not rise before every path has settled. An adder that may need 1.9 ns caps the clock near 520 million rises a second, whatever else the machine could do. The carry is the slow part of the sum, and it is slow only because each place waits for the one below it.

39i3A carry tree

A block of places, taken as one, also makes or passes a carry. Two blocks side by side, the higher with (g, p) and the lower with (g′, p′), make one block with (g, p) ∘ (g′, p′) = (g ∨ (p ∧ g′), p ∧ p′): the pair makes a carry if the higher makes one, or passes one the lower made; it passes if both pass.

Carry-tree technique: the combining of (make, pass) pairs is grouping-free, so every carry of an n-place adder can be found in about log₂ n levels of combining, each two gates deep.

\big((g_1, p_1) \circ (g_2, p_2)\big) \circ (g_3, p_3) = (g_1 \lor p_1 g_2 \lor p_1 p_2 g_3,\ p_1 p_2 p_3) @@ combine twice, spreading and over or (1f)
(g_1, p_1) \circ \big((g_2, p_2) \circ (g_3, p_3)\big) = (g_1 \lor p_1 (g_2 \lor p_2 g_3),\ p_1 p_2 p_3) @@ the other grouping
\text{the two are one pair} @@ spreading and over or, again
\text{pair neighbours, then neighbouring pairs, and so on} @@ grouping does not matter, so any tree gives the same block pair
\htmlClass{new}{\lceil \log_2 n \rceil \text{ levels};\ n = 64:\ 6 \text{ levels},\ \text{about } 2 \cdot 6 \cdot 15 = 180 \text{ ps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 64 halves to 1 in six steps (17d)
  
\text{places } 3, 2, 1, 0:\ \ g = 0, 0, 1, 0,\ \ p = 1, 1, 0, 1 @@ g = xy, p = x ⊕ y, place by place
c_1 = 0,\ c_2 = g_1 = 1,\ c_3 = p_2 c_2 = 1,\ c_4 = p_3 c_3 = 1 @@ the carry rule, each read from the blocks below
\text{sum bits } p_k \oplus c_k = 0, 0, 0, 1 \text{ for places } 3, 2, 1, 0 @@ the three-bit-sum technique
\htmlClass{new}{1011 + 0110 = 10001:\ \ 11 + 6 = 17} \quad \blacksquare @@ c₄ is the fifth bit

The ripple chain does about n combinings in a row; the tree does more combinings in all, about n log₂ n in the plainest layout, but no path is longer than about log₂ n of them. Spending more gates to wait less is the trade every fast circuit makes.

39i4Subtracting, and the wrap

A row of n catches holds 0 to 2ⁿ − 1. Let the row stand for x when x ≥ 0 and for 2ⁿ + x when x < 0, for x from −2ⁿ⁻¹ to 2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1. Then adding in the row, and dropping the carry out of the top place, is adding numbers, as long as the answer stays in range.

Twos-complement technique: in a row of n places, −x is written as 2ⁿ − x, which is every bit of x turned over, plus 1; so x − y is x plus that, with the top carry dropped.

\text{turning every bit of } y \text{ over gives } (2^n - 1) - y @@ 1 − bit, place by place (39a2); the places of 2ⁿ − 1 are all 1
2^n - y = \big((2^n - 1) - y\big) + 1 @@ add 1
n = 8:\ \ 9 = 00001001 \to 11110110 + 1 = 11110111 = 247 = 256 - 9 @@ worked
5 + 247 = 252 = 11111100 \to 252 - 256 = -4 @@ the top bit 1 marks a negative; 5 − 9 = −4
\htmlClass{new}{n = 8:\ \ 127 + 1 = 10000000 \to -128} \quad \blacksquare @@ the answer 128 is out of range: it wraps round

The wrap is the price of a fixed row. A count of 127 in an 8-place row, plus one, reads −128, and nothing in the gates objects. Programs have crashed rockets and frozen games on exactly this line. The catch is that it can be seen: the wrap happens exactly when two numbers of the same sign give a sum of the other sign.

The accepted names

The three-bit adder is a full adder, and the chain of them a ripple-carry adder. g and p are generate and propagate; the combining of pairs is the carry operator, and its tree is a carry-lookahead or parallel prefix adder, of which the Kogge–Stone and Brent–Kung layouts are the best known. −x as 2ⁿ − x is two’s complement, and the wrap is integer overflow. The sign test is the overflow flag.

Who said it first

Carry look-ahead was worked out for valve and relay machines in the 1950s, by Weinberger and Smith (1956) among others. Peter Kogge and Harold Stone gave the log-depth prefix tree in 1973, and Richard Brent and H. T. Kung a thriftier one in 1982. Two’s complement was used on the EDSAC (1949) and was proposed for machines by John von Neumann in 1945. The first flight of Ariane 5 (1996) was lost when a 64-bit number was squeezed into 16 places and overflowed.

A proof that is not allowed

“The carry tree finds every carry in log₂ n levels, so adding n-place numbers takes log₂ n steps in all.” Not allowed: it takes about log₂ n levels of waiting, and many gates at each level. Time and gates are different counts, and 39k counts steps of a machine that does one thing at a time.
