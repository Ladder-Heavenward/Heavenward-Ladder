Rung 4 · theory

Operating over a series

Rung 3 ended with a set that never runs out of next ones, a set that holds the whole run from 0 but may hold other things as well. This rung keeps the run and throws the rest away. What is left is the set of the counting numbers: an object, whose members are exactly the entries of the run. Because nothing spare is in it, a principle that every mathematician leans on turns out to be a technique about it. Whatever is so of 0, and passes from each number to the next one, is so of every number. The same fact lets you define things by repeating a step. With that the rung builds adding, multiplying and powers out of the next-one gesture alone. And it proves, with a move to each line, that one and three make four.

And you already know what a number is for. Seven is what every collection of seven things shares: the seven is kept in, and the apples and the apricots are kept out. The ladder gives each point of the run the name you already use for it. Since the run comes in order, the numbers snap together in that order, simple as that. The only hard part is keeping the names straight.

The rung has two goals. The first clears the foreclosure on arguments. 4a builds the proof itself at last, and the counting numbers, induction and defining by repeating, which a proof needs in order to be counted and described, are built right after it. The second teaches a different kind of argument: the equation. Counting becomes an operation, adding is defined by repeating it, and an argument becomes a chain of samenesses, each line swapped from the one before. The second goal is written in the column the first one builds.

Those are two kinds of question, and it pays to tell them apart. A proof answers questions about the arrangement, and its answer is true, false or nothing. An equation answers questions within the series: is this the same as that, or more, or less? When a question asks for true or false, reach for a proof. When it asks for an equality or an inequality, reach for an equation. And since time is a series, one moment after another, questions about how things change with time are usually questions within the series.

Keep exactly what every inductive set holds and call it the counting numbers. Since it holds nothing spare, a property that starts at 0 and passes along each step covers all of it. A definition that starts at 0 and says how to take each step defines exactly one thing for all of it. And adding, multiplying and powers are each the operation below them, repeated.

The foreclosure completes

Rungs 0 to 3 used the proof before it was built. This rung builds it first, at 4a, and that foreclosure completes. The proof needs three parts the ladder has not built yet: the counting numbers to number its lines, induction to say something about every proof at once, and functions to keep its lines in order. 4a holds each in trust, in plain words, and 4b to 4d earn them. From 4a on, the questions ask for proofs. Until then some questions ask for an argument before every piece of it is built.

What it means to say counting is an object

Counting is going along the run: start at 0, take the next one, and the next. Rung 3 showed that this never stops and never returns where it has been, and it gave the earliest entries their names, 0 to 4. But each of those facts was about each entry in its turn. “Every entry of the run” was not yet something a proof could say. The techs of every and some speak about the members of a set, and the run was not yet a set.

By rung 3's axiom, some set holds every entry. Nothing so far says that some set holds only the entries. An inductive set may carry cargo, another chain of sets that starts somewhere off the run and is dragged along by the step part. Different inductive sets may carry different cargo. So “a member of an inductive set” is not yet “an entry of the run”.

To say counting is an object is to say that the entries of the run, and nothing else, are the members of a set. 4b builds it by sifting, from any inductive set, the things that are in every inductive set (4b3): cargo fails that test and the entries pass it. It names what comes out ℕ (4b5), and proves the fact rung 3 promised, that different sets have different next ones (4b13).

Here the ladder owns up to what it has been doing all along. Every name you have used was quietly a statement: on 0b, naming a thing came with the statement that it is itself. A set, on rung 2, was a name made of other names. And counting, now, is an object: a name with the motion kept in. Rung 3’s next-one technique is packed into one name, and unpacking the name runs the motion again. Plain speech does this too. “Mike put up a picture” unpacks to “Mike hammered a nail into a wall, to bear a wire, to bear a photo”: a packed motion, opened into statements with new names in their gaps. The difference is that speech could have unpacked to a hook or a strip of tape, so the nail is a new claim riding along. A name on the ladder unpacks to exactly what was packed into it, and nothing more. So there were only ever three kinds of thing: names, statements with gaps for names and other statements, and the moves that happen while a statement is carried out. From here on, statements go into statements, by rung 2’s collecting and rung 3’s repeating, and what comes out is statements again.

Even a single word is a statement. “Runner” says: someone is a member of the things that move, sifted for the things that run. So from here on the ladder describes everything it builds as a statement. A function, a formula, an operator, a point, a group: each is a statement that names, or a statement that does, and the pages say so.

One founder of the subject built the counting numbers in just this way. From a system and a step, he took the part common to every system that holds the start and is closed under the step. Another, a little earlier, had defined what it is for one thing to follow another in a series by the same device.

What you can do because you know it: prove things of every number

Here is the payoff. Suppose a property is so of 0, and so of each number's next one whenever it is so of the number. The numbers that have it form an inductive set, and ℕ is a part of every inductive set, so every number has it. That is the principle of induction, and on this ladder it is a short technique, proved on 4c3.

The base is the push on a row of dominoes and the step is their spacing (4c6). A proof that leaves out the push, or has a gap in the spacing, proves nothing past the gap, and 4c has a gallery of such proofs.

Early in the century before this one, a great mathematician argued that reasoning by recurrence packs an endless series of syllogisms into a statement. No amount of logic could justify it: it was, he thought, a power the mind has, laid down before any proof. Another, writing axioms for the numbers, listed induction among them rather than proving it.

On this ladder you do not have to hold the principle in trust, though something is held. The endless run of syllogisms is settled in advance, and for good, by rung 3's axiom and by 4b's sift. Leave the cargo in and induction fails: a property can be true at 0, pass along every step, and still be false of the cargo. Sifting the cargo out is exactly what makes “every number” mean “every entry you can reach from 0 by steps”.

That is a first handful. What you will build with counting, the ladder can only guess at; so far it has seen the tip of it.

Defining by repeating

Induction proves things about every number. Its twin defines things for every number. Say what something is at 0, and say how to get it at the next one from what it is at this one. Is that enough to fix exactly one thing at every number? It needs a proof, because a definition names a thing only when exactly one thing fits it. 4d says what is being defined, a function (4d2), and proves that a start and a step fix exactly one function on the numbers (4d8). This is the tool rung 3 asked for when it wanted “the layer for every entry” as an object, and 4d is honest about what it still needs for that. Defining by a start and a step has the accepted name recursive definition, from the Latin recurrere, “to run back”.

The ladder's earliest arithmetic

With that tool, adding is a definition. Adding 0 to a number changes nothing; adding the next one after b is taking the next one after what adding b gave. 4f makes that a pair of lines (4f2), proves that one and three make four, a move to each line (4f4), and then proves by induction the techniques you were never asked to prove before: the order and the brackets of a sum do not matter, and a number added to both sides can be taken away again.

A writer of the century before last seems to have been the earliest to define adding this way, by its value at the start and its step, and to prove the techniques of arithmetic from that definition by induction. A later author wrote a whole book on the numbers in this style. He told his student readers to forget what they had learned of arithmetic at school, since they had not really learned it.

4g climbs further: multiplying is adding repeated, and raising to a power is multiplying repeated, each operation starting at the do-nothing of the one below (4g5); their techniques come out by induction as before. 4h adds order: one number is less than another when it is a member of it (4h1), any two numbers compare, a settled set of numbers with a member has a least one, and induction comes in a strong form that leans on every earlier case at once (4h12).

The rest of the rung

The rung's later part takes these tools and makes the hands fast. 4i gives names to the numbers past four, and explains how a row of digits names any number. It writes numbers with the digits 0 and 1 alone as well as with the usual digits. And it settles which operation is done before which when a line has no brackets. 4j builds dividing exactly and dividing with a remainder, the words even and odd, and the primes: the numbers above one whose only exact divisors are one and themselves. It proves the endless-primes technique: for any primes you list there is a prime not on the list. It proves that a number multiplied by itself is never two times another number other than 0 multiplied by itself, a technique rung 5 builds on. And it proves that each number above one splits into primes in exactly one way. 4k counts. It says what it means for a collection to have three members: matching it, member for member, with the set named 3. It counts the ways of choosing, and builds the table of choices and the binomial technique. It brings in a mark for adding up a list. And it proves that adding up the numbers from one to any number gives that number times the next one, divided exactly by two, with the division 4j made exact. 4l takes a recipe for answers apart: a state, a start, a stop test, a move and an output. It proves a recipe stops when some count falls on every move, and that its answer is right when some statement is kept by every move. 4·comp builds circuits from rung 1's tables and an adder from circuits. It shows that the marks 0 and 1 of rung 1's tables and the sets named 0 and 1 agree.

What you can do because you know this

At the end of the rung's opening part you can speak of every number at once, as the members of a set. You can prove a statement for all of them with a pair of short pieces of work, a base and a step. You can define a function on the numbers by a start and a step, and know that exactly one function fits. You can add, multiply and raise to powers, and justify each move of a calculation by a definition or a technique you proved. You can compare numbers, find the least member of a set of them, and lean on every earlier case in a proof. And you know where each of these things came from: a set sifted, with rung 2's tools, from an inductive set, and by rung 3's axiom there is one.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 4k carries a green box. Its box is counting. When coins or leaves fall at random, the shape of the pile is what you can say, and the spread is part of the answer. 4l carries 2e’s loose, then locked again: a recipe gathered by habit is locked by a measure and an invariant, and the measure prices the lock in moves.

Who said it first

The founder who built the numbers as the part common to every closed system is Richard Dedekind; the one a little earlier, with following in a series, is Gottlob Frege. The mathematician who took induction to be a power of the mind is Henri Poincaré; the one who listed it as an axiom is Giuseppe Peano. The writer who first defined adding by a start and a step is Hermann Grassmann; the later author of the whole book is Edmund Landau. The endless-primes technique is Euclid’s.

Dedekind built the counting numbers in just this way: from a system and a step, he took the part common to every system that holds the start and is closed under the step, and called it the chain of the start. Frege, a little earlier, had defined what it is for one thing to follow another in a series by the same device: belonging to everything that contains the earlier thing's successors and is closed under succession.

Giuseppe Peano, writing his axioms for the numbers, listed induction among them rather than proving it.

Accepted names

The counting numbers are the natural numbers. Defining by repeating is definition by recursion, or recursive definition. The techniques 4b and 4c prove, written as axioms, are the Peano axioms.
