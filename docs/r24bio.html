How Does a Cell Keep Time?

How does a cell keep time? A bean plant lifts its leaves by day and folds them at night. Shut it in a dark cupboard, and it goes on lifting and folding for days, with nothing outside to tell it the hour. People sent to live for weeks in caves with no clocks still sleep and wake on a cycle of about a day. A single cell has no gears and no pendulum. It has only molecules that are made, that stick to one another, and that wear out. What makes the question hard is that each of those is a slow, smooth change, and a clock needs something that turns over and over. This page shows, with rung twenty-four’s rules on rates and their states that hold, how sticking together can make a sharp switch, how two genes can make a memory with two settings, how three genes in a ring can make a beat, and how anyone could tell that the beat comes from inside.

When a protein must catch several copies of a signal at once, the share of it caught rises slowly, then suddenly, then levels off: a switch. Two genes, each making a protein that shuts the other off, have two states that hold, one with the first gene on and one with the second, and a tipping point between them that does not hold. Three genes in a ring, each shutting off the next, have only one state left alone, and when the switches are sharp enough it pushes the cell away, so the genes take turns, on and off, for as long as the cell lives. A clock that runs a little slow or fast when cut off from daylight, and drifts steadily against the sun, must be keeping its own time.

24bio1Catching Several at Once

Many proteins work only after catching some signal molecule, present at amount x. Suppose a protein catches n copies at once, or none. Let θ be the share of the protein holding them. Free protein catches at a rate in proportion to xⁿ; held protein lets go at a steady rate. As a rule on rates, θ′ = pxⁿ(1 − θ) − qθ.

\theta' = p\,x^n(1 - \theta) - q\,\theta @@ caught, less let go; x held steady
F(\theta^*) = 0 \iff \theta^* = \tfrac{p x^n}{p x^n + q} = \tfrac{x^n}{K^n + x^n},\ \ K^n = \tfrac{q}{p} @@ the state left alone
F'(\theta) = -(p\,x^n + q) \lt 0 @@ the state holds (24k)
\theta - \theta^* = (\theta_0 - \theta^*)\,e^{-(p x^n + q)t} @@ the own-rate technique (24a), on the gap
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \to \tfrac{x^n}{K^n + x^n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ whatever the start

Binding technique: a protein that catches n copies at once ends up with the share xⁿ/(Kⁿ + xⁿ) caught, and half caught when x = K. With n = 1 the share climbs gently. With n = 4 it hardly moves below K, and is nearly full a little above it.

24bio2How Steep the Switch Is

Blood is the best-known case. Each haemoglobin molecule carries oxygen to the body. Fitted to measured binding, its curve has K ≈ 26.8 in the units of oxygen pressure, mmHg, and an n of about 2.8. The fitted n is not a whole number, because the four seats do not all fill at once; it measures how steep the curve is. It is a law found by induction under the case with predictable variation.

\tfrac{d}{dx}\tfrac{x^n}{K^n + x^n} = \tfrac{n\,K^n x^{n-1}}{(K^n + x^n)^2} @@ the quotient rule
\text{at } x = K:\ \ \tfrac{n\,K^{2n-1}}{4K^{2n}} = \tfrac{n}{4K} @@ steepest climb grows with n
\theta = 0.1 \iff x = K\,9^{-1/n};\ \ \theta = 0.9 \iff x = K\,9^{1/n} @@ solve for x
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{x_{90}}{x_{10}} = 81^{1/n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stretch of signal that throws the switch

Steepness technique: to go from 10% caught to 90% caught takes 81 times as much signal when n = 1, and only 81^(1/n) times as much when n copies are caught at once. For haemoglobin that is about 4.8 times. In the lungs, at 100 mmHg, the curve gives 98% loaded. In a hard-working muscle, at 20 mmHg, it gives 31%. So 67% of the load is handed over. With n = 1 and the same K, the shares would be 79% and 43%, and only 36% would be handed over.

24bio3Two Genes That Hold Each Other Down

A gene makes a protein at a top rate α (little alpha) when it is free. A second protein, at amount v, sits on the gene and stops it, catching it n at a time. By the binding technique, measured in units of K, the gene is free for the share 1/(1 + vⁿ) of the time. Proteins wear out at a rate in proportion to their amount. So u′ = α/(1 + vⁿ) − u. Now let v’s gene be stopped by u in the same way: v′ = α/(1 + uⁿ) − v. Write f(x) = α/(1 + xⁿ).

Two rules on rates at once are past rung 24’s one-variable tools. Here is a guess, a relay reading: let u settle with v held, then v with u held, and so on. Each settles at once by the binding technique, so the relay runs uₖ₊₁ = f(f(uₖ)), a rule fed its own answers (22h).

u = f(v),\ \ v = f(u) @@ a state left alone by both rules
u(1 + v^2) = v(1 + u^2) \iff (u - v)(1 - uv) = 0 @@ both equal α; take one from the other
uv = 1 \Rightarrow u + v = u + v\cdot uv = u(1 + v^2) = \alpha @@ the second case
u,\ v = \tfrac{\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - 4}}{2},\ \ \text{for } \alpha \gt 2 @@ two numbers with sum α and product 1
\big(f\circ f\big)'(u) = f'(v)\,f'(u) = \tfrac{4}{\alpha^2} \lt 1 @@ f′(x) = −2αx/(1 + x²)², and 1 + u² = α/v
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two states hold: } u \text{ high, } v \text{ low, and } u \text{ low, } v \text{ high}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pull-together technique (22h)

Switch technique: two genes that hold each other down, with α > 2 and n = 2, have two states that hold, one with each gene on. The third state, u = v = L with L(1 + L²) = α, has relay slope f′(L)² = (2(1 − L/α))², which is above 1 for α > 2: it tips. With α = 4 the held states are 2 ± √3, about 3.73 and 0.27. A pulse of a chemical that pulls one protein off its gene flips the switch, and it stays flipped. The relay reading is a guess. For this pair, the full two-rule check, held in trust for higher up the ladder, gives the same answer.

24bio4Three Genes in a Ring

Now three genes in a ring: the first stops the second, the second stops the third, the third stops the first. The relay reading runs once round the ring with h = f∘f∘f.

f' \lt 0 \Rightarrow h' = f'(f(f(x)))\,f'(f(x))\,f'(x) \lt 0 @@ three negative slopes: h falls (14d)
h(0) \gt 0,\ \ h(x) - x \lt 0 \text{ for large } x @@ h stays between 0 and α
\text{exactly one } L \text{ with } h(L) = L,\ \text{and } f(L) = L @@ in-between values (12a); h(x) − x falls; L = f(L) is one such point
f'(L) = -\tfrac{\alpha\,n\,L^{n-1}}{(1 + L^n)^2} = -n\big(1 - \tfrac{L}{\alpha}\big) @@ use 1 + Lⁿ = α/L
h'(L) = f'(L)^3 @@ the chain rule
\htmlClass{new}{n\big(1 - \tfrac{L}{\alpha}\big) \gt 1 \Rightarrow |h'(L)| \gt 1:\ L \text{ pushes; nothing else is left alone}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 22h: the run cannot settle

Ring technique: three genes in a ring that stop one another have one state left alone, and when the switches are steep enough it pushes, so the genes take turns for ever. Since 1 − L/α < 1, n = 1 never ticks: a clock of this kind needs genes caught several copies at a time. The relay reading asks for n(1 − L/α) > 1. The two-rule check with the rules on rates, held in trust, asks for more: n(1 − L/α) > 2. With α = 10 and n = 3, L ≈ 1.70 and n(1 − L/α) ≈ 2.49, so it passes both. A ring of this kind was built in a bacterium in 2000. Its genes took turns about every 150 minutes, slower than the cells divided.

24bio5Is the Beat Inside?

Plants, flies, mice and people keep a cycle of about a day. Is it a clock inside, or a reply to something outside: light, warmth, the turning earth? A reply would keep exactly 24 hours, in step with what it replies to. Cut off from daylight, a clock inside would keep its own period, a little off 24 hours, and drift against the sun.

\text{own period } P,\ \text{days of } 24 \text{ h} @@ cut off from daylight
\text{after } m \text{ days: } m(P - 24) \text{ hours out of step} @@ each cycle starts P − 24 h later than the last
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a steady drift } P - 24 \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{the period is not the sun’s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a reply to the sun would keep 24 h

Own-period technique: a cycle that drifts steadily against the sun, when cut off from daylight, keeps a period of its own. People living without clocks in dim, steady light kept a period averaging about 24.2 hours, drifting about 11 minutes a day. In 2005 three proteins from a blue-green bacterium, mixed in a test tube with fuel, kept a cycle of about a day with no genes and no light at all.

The accepted names

Catching several copies at once is cooperative binding; xⁿ/(Kⁿ + xⁿ) is the Hill equation, n the Hill coefficient and K the half-saturation constant. Haemoglobin’s curve is the oxygen dissociation curve, and its K the P₅₀. A protein that stops a gene is a repressor. The two-gene switch is the genetic toggle switch, and its flipping is bistability; the three-gene ring is the repressilator. A cycle of about a day is a circadian rhythm; its period cut off from daylight is the free-running period, and the outside signal that resets it a zeitgeber. The test-tube clock is the KaiABC oscillator.

Who said it first

Archibald Vivian Hill fitted the curve to haemoglobin in 1910. Timothy Gardner, Charles Cantor and James Collins built the toggle switch, and Michael Elowitz and Stanislas Leibler the repressilator; both appeared in the same issue of Nature, in January 2000. Jean-Jacques d’Ortous de Mairan kept a mimosa in a dark cupboard in 1729. Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer found the first clock gene, in flies, in 1971; Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young shared the Nobel prize for the clock’s gears in 2017. Charles Czeisler’s group measured the human period near 24.2 hours (1999). Masato Nakajima, Takao Kondo and colleagues built the test-tube clock (2005). In 24·bio.7.5, Frank A. Brown Jr. argued for an outside signal through the 1950s and 1960s; Colin Pittendrigh and Erwin Bünning argued for a clock inside.
