27i · technique

Vectors by hand

Rung eighteen measured arrows in the plane: their lengths, and when two of them meet at a right angle. Rung twenty one turned the meeting of two arrows into an angle with a cosine. This rung has shown that lists of any number of numbers are arrows too. So the same tools now work in space, and in spaces with more directions than anyone can picture: the length of an arrow, the angle between two, and how much of one arrow points along another. Space has one tool the plane lacks. Two arrows in space have a third arrow standing at right angles to both, as long as the area they span. It comes straight out of the determinant. And an arrow that moves with time has a slope and an area, worked one place at a time.

The dot of two arrows gives the cosine of the angle between them, in any number of directions. Arrows at right angles to each other read off any arrow by dots alone. In space, the determinant gives an arrow at right angles to two others, as long as the area they span. A moving arrow is worked one place at a time.

27i1Lengths and angles in any number of directions

Stands on 27h2 (the two-slot technique), 18a3 (the dot technique), 18b1 (the dot-bound technique), 21m (the arc-is-arccos technique) and 21·cb18 (the city-distance technique).

For lists u and v of n numbers, the dot u · v = u₁v₁ + ⋯ + uₙvₙ is 27h2’s two-slot reading with G = I. The length of u is |u| = √(u · u). For n = 2 and n = 3 these are 18a’s dot and 18a’s distance. The dot technique of 18a3 holds for every n, place by place: u · v = v · u, u · (v + w) = u · v + u · w, (tu) · v = t(u · v), and u · u = |u|².

Angle-between technique: for arrows u, v ≠ 0 of lists of n numbers, u · v/(|u| |v|) lies between −1 and 1; so exactly one θ between 0 and π has cos θ = u · v/(|u| |v|), and with it |u − v|² = |u|² + |v|² − 2|u| |v| cos θ. That θ is the angle between u and v. They are at right angles, u ⊥ v, when u · v = 0.

|u\cdot v| \le |u|\,|v| @@ the dot-bound technique (18b1); its proof uses only the dot technique, which holds place by place for every n
-1 \le \tfrac{u\cdot v}{|u|\,|v|} \le 1 @@ u, v ≠ 0, so |u| |v| > 0 (distance-laws, 18a2); divide
\cos\theta = \tfrac{u\cdot v}{|u|\,|v|} \text{ for exactly one } \theta \in [0, \pi] @@ the arc-is-arccos technique (21m)
|u - v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 - 2\,u\cdot v @@ the dot technique, as in the right-triangle technique (18a4)
\htmlClass{new}{|u - v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 - 2|u|\,|v|\cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ put in u · v = |u| |v| cos θ
  
u = (1, 2, 2),\ v = (2, -1, 2):\ \ u\cdot v = 2 - 2 + 4 = 4,\ \ |u| = |v| = 3 @@ place by place
\cos\theta = \tfrac49,\ \ \theta \approx 63.6^\circ @@ the angle-between technique
\text{Vancouver } (49.28^\circ\text{N}, 123.12^\circ\text{W}),\ \text{San Francisco } (37.77^\circ\text{N}, 122.42^\circ\text{W}) @@ each a point on the sphere of radius 1 by the two-turns technique (21·cb18)
\cos\gamma = u\cdot v \approx 0.97985,\ \ \gamma \approx 11.52^\circ @@ two arrows of length 1: the dot is the cosine
\htmlClass{new}{6371\,\text{km}\times 0.2011 \approx 1281\,\text{km}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the arc is the radius times the angle (21a); this is the city-distance technique, read as one dot

The proof never drew a picture. In lists of four numbers, or forty, there is nothing to draw, and the angle is still exactly one number, because the dot-bound technique holds there too.

27i2Reading off along right angles

Stands on 27i1, 27b3 (the grow-to-basis technique) and 18a3.

Read-off technique: if d₁, …, dₙ are arrows of length 1 in lists of n numbers, each at right angles to the others, they are a basis, and every arrow u is (u · d₁)d₁ + ⋯ + (u · dₙ)dₙ. The number u · dⱼ is the component of u along dⱼ.

t_1d_1 + \cdots + t_nd_n = 0 \Rightarrow t_k = (t_1d_1 + \cdots + t_nd_n)\cdot d_k = 0 @@ dot with dₖ: dⱼ · dₖ = 0 for j ≠ k, and dₖ · dₖ = 1
d_1, \ldots, d_n \text{ are free, so a basis} @@ n free arrows in a space of dimension n (the grow-to-basis technique, 27b3)
u = c_1d_1 + \cdots + c_nd_n @@ they build every arrow
\htmlClass{new}{u\cdot d_k = c_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ dot with dₖ, as in the first line
  
d_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1),\ d_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1, 0),\ d_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, 1, -2) @@ each of length 1; the three dots between them are 0
u = (3, 1, 2):\ \ u\cdot d_1 = 2\sqrt3,\ \ u\cdot d_2 = \sqrt2,\ \ u\cdot d_3 = 0 @@ three dots, no samenesses to solve
\htmlClass{new}{u = 2\sqrt3\,d_1 + \sqrt2\,d_2 = (2, 2, 2) + (1, -1, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the read-off technique; check: the sum is (3, 1, 2)

With any other basis, finding the numbers of a mix means solving samenesses, the work of 27j. With arrows at right angles, each number is one dot. That is why measuring directions are chosen at right angles whenever they can be. The part (u · d)d alone is the shadow of u on the line along d, and u − (u · d)d is at right angles to d.

27i3An arrow at right angles to two

Stands on 27e2 (the three-rules technique), 27e1 (the column-slide technique), 27e4 (the nonzero-det technique), 27h1 (readings) and 27i1.

Fix arrows u and v of space. The number det[u v w], with u, v, w as the columns, is straight in w (27e2), so it is a reading of w; and a reading of lists is a row (27h1), so it is a dot with one fixed arrow. That arrow is the cross of u and v, u × v: the one arrow with (u × v) · w = det[u v w] for every w.

Cross technique: u × v = (u₂v₃ − u₃v₂, u₃v₁ − u₁v₃, u₁v₂ − u₂v₁). It is at right angles to u and to v; v × u = −(u × v); |u × v| = |u| |v| sin θ, the area of the parallelogram on u and v; and u × v = 0 exactly when u and v are not free.

(u\times v)_i = (u\times v)\cdot e_i = \det[u\ v\ e_i] @@ the definition with w = eᵢ; the three-rules formula (27e2) gives the three places
(u\times v)\cdot u = \det[u\ v\ u] = 0,\ \ (u\times v)\cdot v = 0 @@ flat: two equal columns
\det[v\ u\ w] = -\det[u\ v\ w] \Rightarrow v\times u = -(u\times v) @@ a swap changes the sign (the column-slide technique, 27e1)
|u\times v|^2 = |u|^2|v|^2 - (u\cdot v)^2 = |u|^2|v|^2(1 - \cos^2\theta) @@ multiply out both sides place by place; the angle-between technique (27i1)
|u\times v| = |u|\,|v|\sin\theta @@ sin θ ≥ 0 for θ in [0, π], and sin² + cos² = 1 (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{u\times v = 0 \iff u, v \text{ not free}} \quad \blacksquare @@ if free, grow to a basis u, v, w: det ≠ 0 (27e4); if not, every det[u v w] has a spare column and is 0
  
(1, 2, 0)\times(0, 1, 3) = (2\cdot3 - 0\cdot1,\ 0\cdot0 - 1\cdot3,\ 1\cdot1 - 2\cdot0) @@ the cross technique, place by place
= (6, -3, 1) @@ check: (6, −3, 1) · (1, 2, 0) = 0 and (6, −3, 1) · (0, 1, 3) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = \sqrt{36 + 9 + 1} = \sqrt{46} \approx 6.78} \quad \blacksquare @@ and |u|²|v|² − (u · v)² = 5 · 10 − 4 = 46

The cross is no tidy product. It exists only in space, it changes sign when turned about, and it is not grouping-free: (e₁ × e₁) × e₂ = 0, while e₁ × (e₁ × e₂) = e₁ × e₃ = −e₂. Its order matters too: e₁ × e₂ = e₃, so u, v, u × v stand the way e₁, e₂, e₃ do, with det[u v u × v] = |u × v|² > 0 when u, v are free.

27i4Arrows that move

Stands on 18i (the path-slope, speed and distance-travelled techniques), 13c (the product technique for slopes), 13d (the slopes-add technique), 16c (the area-is-a-change technique) and 27i3.

A moving arrow r(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t)) is three functions of one time, as 18i had two. Its slope r′(t) = (x′(t), y′(t), z′(t)) and its area ∫ r(t) dt are taken place by place. The speed is |r′(t)|, and the distance travelled from a to b is ∫ from a to b of |r′(t)| dt, by 18i with one more place.

Moving-dot technique: if u(t) and v(t) have slopes, then (u · v)′ = u′ · v + u · v′ and (u × v)′ = u′ × v + u × v′. So an arrow of fixed length moves at right angles to itself: if |r(t)| is the same for every t, then r(t) · r′(t) = 0.

(u\cdot v)' = (u_1v_1)' + (u_2v_2)' + (u_3v_3)' @@ slopes add (13d)
= (u_1'v_1 + u_1v_1') + (u_2'v_2 + u_2v_2') + (u_3'v_3 + u_3v_3') @@ the product technique (13c), place by place
= u'\cdot v + u\cdot v' @@ gather; the cross is the same, each of its places a sum of products
r\cdot r = |r|^2 \text{ fixed} \Rightarrow 0 = (r\cdot r)' = 2\,r\cdot r' @@ a fixed number has slope 0; the dot turns about
\htmlClass{new}{r(t)\cdot r'(t) = 0} \quad \blacksquare
  
r(t) = (3\cos t,\ 3\sin t,\ 4t) @@ round a circle of radius 3, climbing 4 for each unit of t
r'(t) = (-3\sin t,\ 3\cos t,\ 4) @@ place by place (21g)
|r'(t)| = \sqrt{9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t + 16} = 5 @@ sin² + cos² = 1 (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one turn: } \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} 5\,dt = 10\pi \approx 31.4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the distance-travelled technique (18i)

Slopes of moving arrows are where arrows meet motion. Velocity is r′, a moving arrow; acceleration is r″. A stone on a string, going round at a steady speed, has a velocity of fixed length; by the moving-dot technique its acceleration is at right angles to its velocity, pointing across its path, never along it.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

An arrow of lists of n numbers is a vector of ℝⁿ, and u · v is the dot product (or inner product, or scalar product). Its length |u| is the norm (or magnitude); the dot-bound technique in ℝⁿ is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Arrows of length 1, each at right angles to the others, are an orthonormal basis, and u · d is the component (or scalar projection) of u along d; (u · d)d is the projection. The cross is the cross product (or vector product), and det[u v w] = (u × v) · w is the scalar triple product. The formula |u × v|² = |u|²|v|² − (u · v)² is Lagrange’s identity. The way u, v, u × v stand is the right-hand rule. A moving arrow is a vector-valued function; r′ is velocity and r″ acceleration. A carbon atom’s bond angle, about 109.5°, is the tetrahedral angle.

Who said it first

Hermann Grassmann worked with products of arrows in any number of directions in 1844. William Rowan Hamilton’s quaternions of 1843 carried a dot and a cross inside one product, and Peter Guthrie Tait championed them. Josiah Willard Gibbs, in notes printed for his Yale students from 1881, and Oliver Heaviside, in his electrical papers, split the quaternion product into the dot and the cross, and wrote arrows as we do now. The quarrel between Tait and the two of them ran through the journal Nature in the early 1890s. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the dot-bound in ℝⁿ for sums in 1821; Hermann Schwarz proved it for integrals in 1888.
