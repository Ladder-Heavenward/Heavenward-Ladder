29·phys · physics

Does time run the same for everyone?

Does time run the same for everyone? For most of history the answer looked too plain to ask. A second in Seattle is a second in Tokyo, on a train or on a ship, and two good clocks set together stay together however they are carried. Then light was measured with great care, from a laboratory riding on the earth as it sped round the sun, and it came out at the same speed whichever way it went. If that is true for every steady traveller, something else has to give, and what gives is time itself. A moving clock ticks slower, seen from the ground, and a moving rod is shorter. What makes the question hard is that each traveller is right about her own clock, and the two accounts must still fit together into one world. This page fits them together with rung twenty-nine’s ruler. Space and time share one ruler, with a minus sign in it, and the change from one traveller to another is a turn that mixes space with time. Speeds then add as turns do, and never pass the speed of light. Mass turns out to be energy, a great deal of it. And light, bounced off a single electron, comes back changed by exactly the amount this geometry says.

Bounce light up and down between two mirrors and count the trips as ticks. Carried past you, the light runs on a slant, so each trip is longer, and at the same speed it takes longer: the moving clock is slow by a factor that grows without bound as its speed nears the speed of light. Write each event by its time and its place. The square of the time, scaled by the speed of light, less the square of the place, comes out the same for every steady traveller. So space and time have one ruler, and passing from one traveller to another is a kind of turn, built from the growing and shrinking curves of rung seventeen instead of the circle’s. Turns add, so speeds add the way those turns do, and two speeds below light’s never make one above it. Measure a body by its own clock and its energy and momentum form one arrow of fixed ruler length. At rest that arrow still has length: the energy a body carries just by having mass. Light has an arrow of length zero, and when it strikes an electron the two arrows are kept in total, which fixes how much longer the light’s waves come out.

29phys1A clock made of light

Stands on 5i (the metre set by light), 18a (the right-triangle technique), 23h (the binomial-sum technique) and 17j (growth and decay).

Two measured laws carry this page. First: the rules of physics read the same to every observer moving steadily, without speeding up or turning. Second: every such observer measures light in empty space at the same speed c, whichever way it goes and however its source moves. 5i fixed c = 299 792 458 m/s exactly, by setting the metre from it.

Build a clock from light. Two mirrors face each other a distance d apart, and light runs from one to the other and back: one tick. Carry the clock past the ground at speed v, the mirrors across the direction of travel.

\text{at rest: one tick } = \tfrac{2d}{c} @@ up to the far mirror and back, at speed c
\big(\tfrac{cT}{2}\big)^2 = d^2 + \big(\tfrac{vT}{2}\big)^2 @@ seen from the ground the light runs on a slant, still at c (the second law); the right-triangle technique (18a)
T^2\,(c^2 - v^2) = 4d^2 @@ multiply by 4 and gather T²
\htmlClass{new}{T = \gamma\,\tfrac{2d}{c},\ \ \gamma = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ γ ≥ 1, so the moving tick is longer

Light-clock technique: a clock moving at speed v ticks slower, seen from the ground, by the factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Every other clock riding with it must keep the same pace, or the rider could tell she is moving by comparing them, against the first law. So it is time itself that runs slow, not one kind of clock. For slow speeds, the binomial-sum technique (23h) with x = −v²/c² and power −½ gives γ = 1 + v²/(2c²) + 3v⁴/(8c⁴) + ⋯, so the slowing is about v²/(2c²) of each second.

Muons are made by cosmic rays high in the air and live, at rest, 2.197 microseconds on average; the share still alive after a time t is e^(−t/2.197 μs) (17j). Count those arriving at 0.995c on the summit of Mount Rainier, 4392 m up, and again at sea level in Seattle. The fall takes 14.7 microseconds. With no slowing, only 0.12% would survive it. But γ ≈ 10, so each muon’s own clock ticks only 1.47 microseconds on the way, and 51% survive. Counts of this kind, made between a mountain top and sea level, find the slowed figure.

29phys2One ruler for space and time

Stands on 29·phys.1, 29d (the ruler; the ruler-change technique), 27d (grids multiply) and 17e (exp; adding becomes multiplying).

Name an event by when and where: (ct, x), with time scaled by c so that both are lengths, and one direction of space enough for now. A flash at the event (0, 0) reaches x = ±ct, where c²t² − x² = 0, and by the second law every observer finds the same. Measure events by a ruler, as 29d4 measured a plane with a ruler that no surface around it supplies: ds² = c²dt² − dx², with ruler grid g = (1, 0; 0, −1). The minus sign is new: this ruler gives some pairs of events a negative square. From here on, the events with this ruler are spacetime.

An observer moving at v along x labels events (ct′, x′). The change must send straight lines to straight lines (steady motion to steady motion), so it is a grid. Write β = v/c and take the grid J = γ(1, −β; −β, 1).

J = \gamma\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\beta \\ -\beta & 1 \end{pmatrix}:\ \ x' = \gamma\,(x - vt),\ \ ct' = \gamma\,(ct - \beta x) @@ the change to the moving observer
x' = 0 \iff x = vt @@ her own place moves at v, seen from the ground
J^{T}gJ = \gamma^2\begin{pmatrix} 1 - \beta^2 & -\beta + \beta \\ -\beta + \beta & -(1 - \beta^2) \end{pmatrix} @@ multiply the grids (27d)
J^{T}gJ = g @@ γ²(1 − β²) = 1
\htmlClass{new}{c^2t'^2 - x'^2 = c^2t^2 - x^2 \text{ for every event}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ruler-change technique (29d): the grid is unchanged

Kept-interval technique: the change to an observer moving at v keeps c²t² − x², so every steady observer reads the same ruler of spacetime, ds² = c²dt² − dx². A rod of length L at rest in her frame has ends at x′ = 0 and x′ = L at every t′. From the ground, at one instant t, its ends sit at x = vt and x = vt + L/γ: the moving rod is shorter by γ.

Now write J as a turn. Let cosh u = (eᵘ + e⁻ᵘ)/2 and sinh u = (eᵘ − e⁻ᵘ)/2 (17e), and tanh u = sinh u/cosh u.

\cosh^2 u - \sinh^2 u = 1 @@ multiply out: the cross terms are ½ and −½
\beta = \tanh u \Rightarrow \cosh u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} = \gamma,\ \ \sinh u = \gamma\beta @@ divide the line above by cosh² u; cosh u > 0
J_u = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh u & -\sinh u \\ -\sinh u & \cosh u \end{pmatrix} @@ J, with γ and γβ put in
\cosh(u + w) = \cosh u\cosh w + \sinh u\sinh w,\ \ \sinh(u + w) = \sinh u\cosh w + \cosh u\sinh w @@ multiply out with e^(u + w) = eᵘ e^w (17e)
\htmlClass{new}{J_u\,J_w = J_{u + w}} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply the grids (27d), and read the line above

Mixing-turn technique: the change to an observer moving at v is a turn of spacetime by u, where tanh u = v/c, and two such turns add their u. A turn of the plane (21) keeps x² + y² and is built from cos and sin. This one keeps c²t² − x² and is built from cosh and sinh. At v = 0.6c, tanh u = 0.6 gives eᵘ = 2, so u = ln 2.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a measurement picks the geometry

Logic alone does not choose the ruler of spacetime. Before 1905 the change of observer was taken to be x′ = x − vt and t′ = t. It keeps every time between events, and every length. Nothing in it is contradictory. It just does not keep c: in it, light chased by a traveller is slower for her. A ruler c²dt² + dx², with a plus sign, is just as free of contradiction. Its changes would be ordinary turns, and enough speed would turn a clock’s time into a direction of space.

The ruler of spacetime is chosen by measurement, so name the measurement, and how small a departure from the ruler it could have seen.

Which ruler the world has was settled by measuring: light timed both ways along the earth’s path round the sun, muons counted on mountains, clocks flown in jets. Each measurement has a smallest departure it could have seen. Within those limits the minus sign holds. Rung 29 built geometries with every kind of ruler, flat, round and saddle-shaped. Measurement picks among them, the way a guess about the world is tested against cases.

A clock is flown from Seattle to Tokyo and back, about 15 300 km each way at 250 m/s, and compared with a twin left at home. By the light-clock technique alone, how far behind should it come back? What else might move its reading, before you trust the comparison?

29phys3Speeds add as turns

Stands on 29·phys.2 and 17e (exp).

An observer moves at v₁ along x. A body moves at v₂ along x, as she measures it. How fast does it move, seen from the ground? Before 1905 the answer was v₁ + v₂.

v_1 = c\tanh u,\ \ v_2 = c\tanh w @@ the mixing-turn technique: each speed is a turn
J_u\,J_w = J_{u + w} @@ the body’s speed from the ground is c tanh(u + w)
\tanh(u + w) = \tfrac{\sinh u\cosh w + \cosh u\sinh w}{\cosh u\cosh w + \sinh u\sinh w} = \tfrac{\tanh u + \tanh w}{1 + \tanh u\tanh w} @@ divide above and below by cosh u cosh w
\htmlClass{new}{v = \tfrac{v_1 + v_2}{1 + v_1v_2/c^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and |tanh u| < 1 for every u, so |v| < c

Speeds-add technique: two speeds along one line combine to (v₁ + v₂)/(1 + v₁v₂/c²), which never reaches c however many are added. Two speeds of 0.6c give 1.2c/1.36 ≈ 0.882c. As turns: ln 2 + ln 2 = ln 4, and tanh(ln 4) = (4 − ¼)/(4 + ¼) = 15/17 ≈ 0.882. Put v₂ = c: the result is c, whatever v₁ is, as the second law asks. For everyday speeds the bottom is 1 plus a few parts in a trillion, and v₁ + v₂ is right to every place a speedometer shows.

29phys4Mass is energy

Stands on 29·phys.2, 14·phys (momentum and kinetic energy kept) and 23h (the binomial-sum technique).

Measure a moving body by its own clock, τ. In a short time dt it moves dx = v dt, and its own clock reads the ruler: c²dτ² = c²dt² − dx², so dτ = dt/γ, as the light clock found. The body’s arrow through spacetime is (c dt/dτ, dx/dτ).

\big(c\tfrac{dt}{d\tau},\ \tfrac{dx}{d\tau}\big) = (\gamma c,\ \gamma v) @@ dt/dτ = γ
(\gamma c)^2 - (\gamma v)^2 = \gamma^2c^2\,(1 - \beta^2) = c^2 @@ its ruler length is c, for every body at every speed
\big(\tfrac Ec,\ p\big) = m\,(\gamma c,\ \gamma v),\ \ \tfrac{E^2}{c^2} - p^2 = m^2c^2 @@ times the mass: the energy-momentum arrow
\gamma mc^2 = mc^2\big(1 + \tfrac12\beta^2 + \tfrac38\beta^4 + \cdots\big) = mc^2 + \tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac38\tfrac{mv^4}{c^2} + \cdots @@ the binomial-sum technique (23h), x = −β², power −½
\htmlClass{new}{E = mc^2 \text{ at rest}} \quad \blacksquare @@ v = 0: γ = 1; the next term is 14·phys’s kinetic energy

A measured law takes the place of 14·phys’s: in every collision, seen by every steady observer, the total of the bodies’ arrows (E/c, p) is kept. Total momentum m v alone cannot be kept by every observer, since speeds no longer simply add. Its replacement γmv can. Mass-energy technique: a body of mass m carries energy γmc², which at rest is mc², and whose next term is the kinetic energy ½mv².

Since energy is kept in total, mass can turn into other energy. One kilogram is 9 × 10¹⁶ J. The two reactors at Diablo Canyon, on the coast of San Luis Obispo County, make about 6800 MW of heat. Each day that is 5.9 × 10¹⁴ J, and the fuel ends the day lighter by about 6.6 g. Up the coast, the linear accelerator at Menlo Park has driven electrons to 50 GeV of energy. An electron’s mc² is 0.511 MeV, so γ ≈ 98 000.

29phys5Light bounced off an electron

Stands on 29·phys.4, 17·phys (light in lumps, E = hf = hc/λ) and 21 (cos).

A lump of light has no mass, so its arrow has ruler length 0: E/c = |p|, and its momentum is E/c = h/λ. Write the ruler’s dot for arrows A = (a₀, a₁, a₂, a₃) and B the same way: A · B = a₀b₀ − a₁b₁ − a₂b₂ − a₃b₃. Then the arrow of a body of mass m has P · P = m²c². Light with energy E strikes an electron at rest and leaves with energy E′, turned by θ.

P + Q = P' + Q' @@ light P, electron Q = (mc, 0, 0, 0) at rest; the measured law keeps the total
Q'\cdot Q' = (P + Q - P')\cdot(P + Q - P') @@ Q′ = P + Q − P′
m^2c^2 = 0 + m^2c^2 + 0 + 2P\cdot Q - 2P\cdot P' - 2Q\cdot P' @@ multiply out; light has P · P = 0
P\cdot Q = Emc/c,\ \ Q\cdot P' = E'mc/c,\ \ P\cdot P' = \tfrac{EE'}{c^2}(1 - \cos\theta) @@ the ruler’s dot, with |p| = E/c for each lump
\tfrac{1}{E'} - \tfrac{1}{E} = \tfrac{1 - \cos\theta}{mc^2} @@ divide by 2EE′m
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda' - \lambda = \tfrac{h}{mc}\,(1 - \cos\theta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ E = hc/λ (17·phys); multiply by hc

Scattered-light technique: light scattered by a free electron through a turn θ comes out longer in wavelength by (h/mc)(1 − cos θ), about 2.43 trillionths of a metre at a right angle, whatever the light was. X-rays of wavelength 71.1 pm, turned through a right angle, come out at 73.5 pm, and the electron flies off with the missing 0.58 keV. Green light at 500 nm gets the same 2.43 pm: a change of five parts in a million, which is why it was first seen with X-rays.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The first law is the principle of relativity, the second the constancy of the speed of light; with them the theory is special relativity. γ is the Lorentz factor; a moving clock’s slowing is time dilation, a moving rod’s shortening length contraction. c²t² − x² is the spacetime interval, ds² = c²dt² − dx² − dy² − dz² the Minkowski metric, and spacetime with it Minkowski space. The change J is a Lorentz transformation (a boost), and u its rapidity. τ is proper time, (γc, γv) the four-velocity, and (E/c, p) the four-momentum; E = mc² is mass–energy equivalence. Light bounced off an electron is Compton scattering, and h/(mc) the Compton wavelength.

Who said it first

Albert Michelson and Edward Morley timed light along and across the earth’s path in 1887 and found no difference. George FitzGerald (1889) and Hendrik Lorentz (1892) proposed that bodies moving through the ether shrink; Lorentz wrote the change J in 1904, and Henri Poincaré showed in 1905 that such changes form a group. Albert Einstein derived them from the two laws in 1905, with no ether, and later that year showed that a body giving off energy E loses mass E/c². Hermann Minkowski joined space and time into one geometry in 1908. Arthur Compton measured the lengthened X-rays in 1923. Bruno Rossi and David Hall counted muons on mountains in Colorado in 1941; David Frisch and James Smith counted 563 an hour on Mount Washington in New Hampshire and 408 at sea level in 1963, where unslowed clocks predicted about 27. Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew cesium clocks round the world in 1971. In 29·phys.7.5, Lorentz kept the ether to the end of his life; Einstein held that it did no work, and the dispute became a question about what physics should count as real.
