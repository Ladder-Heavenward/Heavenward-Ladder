22·econ · economics

How far does one dollar of spending go?

How far does one dollar of spending go? When a mill in Port Angeles pays its crews, the wages do not stop there. A sawyer buys groceries. The grocer pays a clerk. The clerk pays rent, and the landlord hires a roofer. The same dollar keeps moving from hand to hand. In the early 1930s, with about a quarter of American workers out of a job, people asked whether a government that spends one dollar starts more than one dollar of spending. What makes it hard is that the rounds never end. Each one is smaller than the last, but there is no last one. This page shows, with 15i’s endless ratio sums and 22e’s geometric yardstick, what the whole chain of rounds adds up to, where the money goes when it stops moving, and what happens when the share spent changes from round to round.

Each person who receives a dollar spends part of it nearby. The rest leaks away, into savings, into taxes, or into goods made far off. If the part spent is the same at every round, the rounds form an endless ratio sum, and its total is one over the part that leaks. Every cent of the first dollar ends in some leak, so the leaks add up to exactly the dollar. If the part spent changes from round to round but stays under a ceiling, the geometric yardstick still caps the total.

22econ1Round after round

Stands on 15i (finite-ratio-sum, endless-ratio-sum) and 22d (a sum is where its partial sums settle).

A dollar is spent at a shop. The shop’s owners and workers receive it. Suppose each person who receives money spends a fixed share c of it nearby, with 0 ≤ c < 1. Round 0 is the first dollar. Round 1 is c, round 2 is c², and round k is cᵏ. The spending after n rounds is a partial sum, and the whole chain is the endless sum (22d).

s_n = 1 + c + c^2 + \cdots + c^{n-1} = \tfrac{1 - c^n}{1 - c} @@ finite-ratio-sum (15i)
c^n \to 0 @@ 0 ≤ c < 1: small-powers-vanish (15i)
\htmlClass{new}{1 + c + c^2 + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{1 - c}} \quad \blacksquare @@ endless-ratio-sum (15i): the total is where the sₙ settle (22d)

Round technique: if every round spends the same share c of the round before, one first dollar becomes 1/(1 − c) dollars of spending in all. Its accepted name is the multiplier.

1,\ 0.8,\ 0.64,\ 0.512,\ 0.4096,\ \ldots @@ each round 0.8 of the last
s_5 = \tfrac{1 - 0.8^5}{0.2} = 5\,(1 - 0.32768) = 3.3616 @@ after five rounds, two thirds of the way
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{1 - 0.8} = 5} @@ the whole chain

22econ2Where the dollar leaks

Stands on 22·econ.1 and 15·econ (the ledger).

The share not spent nearby leaks out of the chain. Of each dollar received, a share s is saved, a share t goes in taxes, and a share m buys goods made far away. What is left is spent nearby. Write L = s + t + m for the whole leak.

c = 1 - L @@ every cent is spent nearby, or leaks
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{1 - c} = \tfrac{1}{L}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the round technique

Leak technique: the total is one over the share that leaks at each round. A small town leaks more than a country, because more of what it buys is made somewhere else. Suppose a household in Forks, on the Olympic Peninsula, saves 0.05 of each new dollar, pays 0.15 in taxes, and spends 0.50 on goods made outside the town. Then L = 0.70, and the town sees 1/0.7 ≈ 1.43 dollars of spending from each dollar. For the United States as a whole, rough shares are s = 0.05, t = 0.25 and m = 0.15. Then L = 0.45, and the total is about 2.2.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

15·econ kept a loan’s ledger: every line paid for, and the books closing at 0. Keep the dollar’s ledger the same way. At round k, cᵏ is received and L cᵏ leaks. The leaks over all rounds add to L(1 + c + c² + ⋯) = L/(1 − c) = L/L = 1.

Every cent of the first dollar ends, in full, in some leak.

So the leaks split the dollar. A share s/L of it ends in savings, t/L in taxes, and m/L in far-off tills. The 5 dollars of spending that c = 0.8 gives are not 5 dollars of new money. They are one dollar, changing hands five times on average.

In Forks, how much of the first dollar ends in taxes? If the tax share rose to 0.25, would the town’s tax office end up with more of that dollar, or less?

22econ3Shares that change

Stands on 22·econ.1, 22e (compare with a geometric sum) and 22a (a climb under a ceiling arrives).

Real rounds differ. The shop owner may save more than the clerk, and the clerk more than the roofer. Let each round be a share cₖ of the round before, with every cₖ between b and c, where 0 ≤ b ≤ c < 1. Round k is then aₖ = c₀c₁⋯cₖ₋₁, a product of k shares.

b^k \le a_k \le c^k @@ k factors, each between b and c, all ≥ 0
a_0 + a_1 + \cdots + a_{n-1} \le \tfrac{1}{1 - c} @@ terms never negative: the partial sums rise, under the yardstick’s total
\text{the partial sums settle} @@ a climb under a ceiling arrives (22a)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{1 - b} \le \textstyle\sum_k a_k \le \tfrac{1}{1 - c}} \quad \blacksquare @@ compare with a geometric sum, on both sides (22e)

Between-shares technique: if every round spends between b and c of the round before, the total lies between 1/(1 − b) and 1/(1 − c). Shares anywhere from 0.5 to 0.7 give a total between 2 and about 3.33. The bracket is as wide as the guesses about the shares.

22econ4A tax cut starts one round later

Stands on 22·econ.1 and 15i (sums-are-linear).

A government can spend a dollar itself, or cut a household’s taxes by a dollar. The spent dollar is round 0, at full size. The tax cut hands the household a dollar, and it spends only c of it. So the tax cut’s chain starts at c. A tax raised to pay for spending runs the same chain backwards.

\text{spend a dollar}:\ 1 + c + c^2 + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{1 - c} @@ the round technique
\text{cut taxes by a dollar}:\ c + c^2 + \cdots = \tfrac{c}{1 - c} @@ the same chain without round 0
\text{spend a dollar raised by a dollar of tax}:\ \tfrac{1}{1 - c} - \tfrac{c}{1 - c} @@ the tax takes away a tax cut’s chain (sums-are-linear)
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac{1 - c}{1 - c} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ whatever c is

Balanced technique: a dollar spent and paid for by a dollar of tax adds exactly one dollar of spending, whatever share is spent. Only round 0 survives. Every later round of the spending is cancelled by a round the tax took away.

The accepted names

The total 1/(1 − c) is the multiplier, and c is the marginal propensity to consume. The shares s, t and m are the marginal propensities to save, to pay in tax and to import; together they are leakages. The first dollar spent is an injection. The balanced technique is the balanced-budget multiplier. A multiplier for one town or county is a local multiplier. When a government’s borrowing takes the place of private spending, that is crowding out.

Who said it first

Richard Kahn (1931) counted the jobs that one new public-works job starts, round after round, as a ratio sum. John Maynard Keynes made it a multiplier of spending in the General Theory (1936). Trygve Haavelmo (1945) found the balanced-budget multiplier of 1. In 22·econ.6.5, the British Treasury’s view of 1929 held that public spending only moves money that would have been spent anyway; Kahn and Keynes answered it, and Robert Barro (1974) reopened the question with households who save for the taxes to come. Measured national multipliers vary widely, mostly between about one half and two. Enrico Moretti (2010) found that each new high-tech job in an American city brings about five more local jobs.
