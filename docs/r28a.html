28a · proof

Two Knobs, Keep One Still

Most things you measure depend on more than one input. The pressure in a tyre depends on how hot it is and how much air is in it. The height of a hillside depends on how far east you stand and how far north. This step asks what a slope means when there are two knobs to turn. The first answer is simple: hold one knob still, turn the other, and take the slope of what you get. Then the step finds out that this simple answer is not enough. A function can have a slope along every knob at a point and still jump there.

A function of two inputs has a slope along each input: hold the other still and take the slope. Those slopes can all exist at a point where the function jumps.

28a1Near, in Every Direction

With one input, x is near w when |x − w| is small. With two inputs, the point x = (x₁, x₂) is near w = (w₁, w₂) when the length |x − w| is small, the length of 27i. The same holds for lists of n numbers.

So f, from lists of n numbers to numbers, heads to L at w when for every ε > 0 there is a δ > 0 with |f(x) − L| < ε whenever 0 < |x − w| < δ. And f is continuous at w when it heads to f(w) there. Nothing in rung 10’s proofs used more than the triangle technique and the size of a gap, so its limit techniques carry over word for word.

Each-place technique: each place xᵢ is a continuous function of the point x, and so is every sum, product and quotient (where the bottom is not 0) of continuous functions.

|x_i - w_i| \le |x - w| @@ |x − w|² is a sum of squares, one of them (xᵢ − wᵢ)²
|x - w| < \varepsilon \Rightarrow |x_i - w_i| < \varepsilon @@ the line above, with δ = ε
x \mapsto x_i \text{ is continuous} @@ continuity at a, for every a
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sums, products, quotients of continuous functions are continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the limits-add, limits-multiply and limits-divide techniques (10c), whose proofs use only gaps

28a2One Knob at a Time

Hold every input but one still. What is left is a function of one input, and rung 13 can take its slope. For f(x, y), the slope along x at (w₁, w₂) is the slope at w₁ of x ↦ f(x, w₂). Write it ∂f/∂x (w₁, w₂), or fx(w₁, w₂). The slope along y is the slope at w₂ of y ↦ f(w₁, y). These are partial slopes. Accepted name: partial derivatives.

One-knob technique: to find a partial slope, treat every other input as a fixed number and use the slope techniques of rung 13.

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(w_1,w_2) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(w_1+h,w_2) - f(w_1,w_2)}{h} @@ the slope at w₁ of x ↦ f(x, w₂) (13a)
x \mapsto f(x,b) \text{ is a function of one input, with } b \text{ fixed} @@ b is a number, not a variable
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every technique of rung 13 applies, with } b \text{ as a constant}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the constant-has-slope-0, product and power techniques (13c, 13d) see b as a number

So for f(x, y) = x²y + y³, fx = 2xy and fy = x² + 3y². The first treats y like 5; the second treats x like 5.

28a3The Knobs Are Not Enough

With one input, a slope at w forces continuity at w (13b). With two inputs, a slope along each knob does not.

Knobs-miss-jumps technique: a function can have both partial slopes at a point and still not be continuous there.

f(x,y) = \frac{xy}{x^2+y^2},\ \ f(0,0) = 0 @@ the example
f(x,0) = 0,\ \ f(0,y) = 0 \text{ for every } x, y @@ the top is 0 on both axes
f_x(0,0) = 0,\ \ f_y(0,0) = 0 @@ each knob alone sees a flat line (13a)
f(t,t) = \frac{t^2}{2t^2} = \tfrac12 \text{ for every } t \ne 0 @@ along the line y = x
(t,t) \to (0,0) \text{ but } f(t,t) \not\to 0 @@ the points heading in stay at height ½
\htmlClass{new}{f \text{ is not continuous at } (0,0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ “continuous” (28a1), denied

The two knobs look only along two lines through the point. The jump hides on every other line. So a slope that speaks for every direction must be something more than a list of partial slopes. That is the next step.

The accepted names

A partial slope is a partial derivative, written ∂f/∂x or fx. The sign ∂ is a rounded d, kept apart from the d of a function of one input. Lists of n numbers with the length of 27i make up Euclidean n-space, ℝⁿ.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler and Alexis Fontaine des Bertins used partial derivatives in the 1730s, writing them in ways close to today’s. The rounded ∂ was used by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1786 and made standard by Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi in 1841. The example xy/(x² + y²) is in Giuseppe Peano’s edition of Angelo Genocchi’s calculus of 1884, which corrected several such mistakes in the textbooks of the day.
