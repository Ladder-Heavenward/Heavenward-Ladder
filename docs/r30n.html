30n · technique

Random Walks

Toss a coin, and take a step forward for heads and a step back for tails. Do it again, and again. Where do you end up? On average nowhere, since forward and back are equally likely. Yet you are seldom exactly where you began, and the longer you go on the farther you tend to be. A molecule of oxygen in the water around a cell does much the same thing, knocked one way and then another by its neighbours, many billions of times a second. Back on rung fourteen, the biology annex took from the laboratory a law for how far such molecules spread in a given time, and held it in trust. This step earns it. The spread of a walk of independent steps grows like the square root of the number of steps, not like the number itself, and that is the whole reason why spreading is fast over the width of a cell and hopelessly slow over the length of an arm. Then the fair games of this rung find how long a walk takes to leave a stretch, and prove that a walk on a line always comes home, though on average it takes for ever to do so.

A walk of independent steps, each averaging zero, ends on average where it began, but its spread width grows like the square root of the number of steps. Steps taken at a steady pace give the spreading law of the biology annex on rung fourteen. A fair walk between two walls takes on average the product of its distances to them to leave, and on a line it always comes back.

30n1How Far a Walk Gets

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent chance variables, the steps, each with average 0 and spread s². The walk after n steps is Sₙ = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ, with S₀ = 0. The fair coin walk has every step +1 or −1, each with chance ½; then s = 1.

Root-n technique: a walk of n independent steps, each with average 0 and spread width s, ends on average at 0, with average square distance n s² and spread width s√n.

E S_n = E X_1 + \cdots + E X_n = 0 @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
\text{Var}\,S_n = \text{Var}\,X_1 + \cdots + \text{Var}\,X_n = n s^2 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3)
E S_n^2 = \text{Var}\,S_n + (E S_n)^2 = n s^2 @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2): Var Y = E Y² − (E Y)²
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread width of } S_n = s\sqrt{n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the root of the spread

Back-at-start technique: the fair coin walk is back at 0 after 2n steps with chance C(2n, n)/4ⁿ, and this chance divided by 1/√(πn) heads to 1 as n grows.

2^{2n} = 4^n \text{ equally likely lists of steps} @@ each step ±1 with chance ½, independently: every list has chance 1/4ⁿ, and the equal-cases technique (30l1) counts
S_{2n} = 0 \iff \text{exactly } n \text{ of the } 2n \text{ steps are } +1 @@ n up and n down
P(S_{2n} = 0) = \tbinom{2n}{n}\big/4^n @@ the choosing-counts-parts technique (4k13)
\tbinom{2n}{n} = \tfrac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2},\ \ \tfrac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2} \Big/ \tfrac{\sqrt{4\pi n}\,(2n/e)^{2n}}{2\pi n\,(n/e)^{2n}} \to 1 @@ the size-of-factorials technique (23k4), three times
\htmlClass{new}{\tbinom{2n}{n}\big/4^n \text{ against } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}:\ \text{ratio heads to } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ √(4πn)(2n/e)²ⁿ / (2πn (n/e)²ⁿ) = 4ⁿ/√(πn)
  
S_{100} = \text{heads} - \text{tails} @@ 100 tosses of a fair coin as a walk
\text{spread width } \sqrt{100} = 10 @@ the root-n technique, s = 1
P(|S_{100}| \ge 30) \le \tfrac{1}{3^2} = \tfrac19 @@ the spread-bound technique (30h1), k = 3
P(S_{100} = 0) = \tbinom{100}{50}\big/2^{100} \approx 0.0796,\ \ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{50\pi}} \approx 0.0798 @@ the back-at-start technique, n = 50
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a typical gap of } 10,\ \text{exact balance about 1 time in 13}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the walk is seldom far, and seldom exactly home

30n2From Steps to Spreading

Let a walker step a length h, one way or the other with chance ½, once every τ seconds. In time t it takes n = t/τ steps (take t a whole number of steps).

Spread-in-time technique: such a walk has, after time t, average square distance 2Dt along its line, where D = h²/(2τ). Three walks at once, one along each of three square directions and independent, have average square distance 6Dt from the start.

S = h\,(X_1 + \cdots + X_n),\ \ n = \tfrac{t}{\tau} @@ X_i = ±1, each with chance ½
E S^2 = h^2 n = \tfrac{h^2}{\tau}\,t @@ the root-n technique (30n1): s = h
\htmlClass{new}{E S^2 = 2Dt,\ \ D = \tfrac{h^2}{2\tau}} @@ name the constant
r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 @@ the square length of the vector from the start, along three square directions (27i)
E\,r^2 = 2Dt + 2Dt + 2Dt = 6Dt \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)

This is 14·bio’s law x² = 2Dt, with its typical distance x read as the spread width √(2Dt). There it was a premise from the laboratory, held in trust; here it is proved from independent steps, and that flag on 14·bio closes. What the laboratory still supplies is D, and the claim that a molecule’s motion is such a walk. The two pieces of the walk, h and τ, enter only through D: a measured spread fixes h²/τ and nothing more.

The central limit technique (30j3) says more. For many steps, S / (h√n) lands in chance like the standard bell (30m3). By the stretched-density technique (30m3), S lands like the bell of average 0 and spread width √(2Dt), whose density is e^(−x²/(4Dt)) / √(4πDt). A drop of dye spreads into this shape, ever lower and wider.

D \approx 2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s} @@ oxygen in water near 20 °C, the value 14·bio used
t = \tfrac{x^2}{2D} = \tfrac{(10^{-5})^2}{4 \times 10^{-9}} = 0.025\ \text{s} @@ the spread-in-time technique, x = 10 µm
P(|S| \le 10\ \mu\text{m at } t = 0.025\ \text{s}) \approx P(|Z| \le 1) \approx 0.68 @@ the central limit technique, with the bell chances of 30m3
h = 10^{-10}\ \text{m}:\ \ \tau = \tfrac{h^2}{2D} = 2.5 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{s} @@ a toy molecule stepping a tenth of a nanometre
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 4 \times 10^{11} \text{ steps a second, and a hundredth of a millimetre in a fortieth of a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ fast over a cell, slow over an arm

30n3How Long Until It Leaves, and Coming Home

Start the fair coin walk at k, with 0 < k < N, and stop it at T, the first time it is at 0 or at N. 30k4’s ruin-chance technique gives the chance k/N that it reaches N first. How long does it take?

Exit-time technique: the fair coin walk started at k, with 0 ≤ k ≤ N, reaches 0 or N after T steps, where T is finite with chance 1 and E T = k(N − k).

M_n = S_n^2 - n \text{ is a fair game} @@ given the past, E[(S + X)² − (n + 1)] = S² + 2S · 0 + 1 − n − 1 = S² − n, with X the next step (30k1)
E\,S_{T\wedge n}^2 = k^2 + E(T \wedge n) @@ T ∧ n, the lesser of T and n, is a bounded stopping technique: the stopping-keeps technique (30k2)
E(T \wedge n) \le N^2 \text{ for every } n,\ \text{so } E T \le N^2 @@ 0 ≤ S ≤ N until T; then the rising-limit technique (30d1), as T ∧ n rises to T
T \text{ is finite with chance } 1 @@ an average that is finite: T = ∞ only on a set of chance 0
E\,S_T^2 = k^2 + E T @@ S²_{T∧n} heads to S²_T, fenced by N²: the fenced-limit technique (30d3)
E\,S_T^2 = N^2\cdot\tfrac{k}{N} + 0 = kN @@ the ruin-chance technique (30k4)
\htmlClass{new}{E T = kN - k^2 = k(N - k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines above

Back to steps of length h every τ seconds. A walker x from one wall of a gap of width ℓ starts at k = x/h, with N = ℓ/h, and leaves on average after k(N − k)τ = x(ℓ − x) τ/h² = x(ℓ − x)/(2D) seconds. From the middle of a sheet of thickness 2L that is L²/(2D): 14·bio’s time for oxygen to arrive at the middle of a sheet, t = L²/(2D), now proved as an average time.

Comes-back technique: the fair coin walk started at 0 comes back to 0 with chance 1; yet the average time it takes to come back is endless.

\text{after one step the walk is at } 1 \text{ or } -1 @@ the two sides are mirror images: take 1
\text{never back} \subseteq \{\text{reaches } N \text{ before } 0\},\ \text{for every } N @@ the walk moves by 1, and leaves [0, N] with chance 1 (the exit-time technique)
P(\text{never back}) \le \tfrac{1}{N} \text{ for every } N @@ the ruin-chance technique (30k4), from k = 1
P(\text{never back}) = 0 @@ a number from 0 that is below every 1/N is 0
\text{time to come back from } 1 \ \ge\ T_N,\ \ E\,T_N = 1\cdot(N - 1) @@ T_N the time to leave [0, N]: the walk must leave it first; the exit-time technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{back with chance } 1;\ \ \text{average time} \ge N \text{ for every } N} \quad \blacksquare @@ one step, then at least N − 1 more on average

The accepted names

The fair coin walk is the simple symmetric random walk; a walk with a lean is a biased random walk. The root-n technique is the square-root law for the spread of a sum. The average square distance is the mean squared displacement, D the diffusion coefficient, and 2Dt and 6Dt the Einstein relation in one and three dimensions. The bell spreading with width √(2Dt) is the heat kernel, the solution of the diffusion equation that starts from one point; the walk in the limit of small steps is Brownian motion (the Wiener process). The exit-time technique gives the expected duration of the gambler's ruin game, an expected exit time or first passage time. A walk that comes back with chance 1 is recurrent, one that need not is transient. The comes-back technique and 30n.5.2 say the walks on the line and on the plane are recurrent; on the grid of space they are not.

Who said it first

Karl Pearson named the random walk in a letter to Nature in 1905, asking where a walker who takes steps in random directions ends up; Lord Rayleigh answered that he had solved it in 1880 for sound. Louis Bachelier used the walk for prices on the Paris exchange in his thesis of 1900. Albert Einstein derived x² = 2Dt for grains in water in 1905, and Marian Smoluchowski did so independently in 1906. Jean Perrin measured the spread of grains of gamboge resin from 1908, found Avogadro's number from it, and won the Nobel Prize in 1926. Wilhelm Ostwald, who with Ernst Mach had doubted atoms, conceded in the preface of a book of 1908 or 1909 that their existence was proved. The duration of play in the ruin game goes back to Abraham de Moivre (1711) and Jacob Bernoulli. George Pólya proved in 1921 that a walk on the grid returns with chance 1 on the line and in the plane, and not in space. Norbert Wiener built Brownian motion as a chance space in 1923.
