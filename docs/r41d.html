41d · proof

Flat drawings

Three houses and three wells: can a path run from every house to every well, with no two paths crossing? Puzzle books have asked it for more than a century, and every attempt ends with one path short. Rung 34 counted corners, edges and faces of a drawing on a ball and found the count always comes to two. That count, read for a graph drawn on a flat sheet, is a ceiling on how many links a flat drawing can hold, and the three houses and three wells have one link too many for it. So does the graph of five dots all joined. The same ceiling says that every flat drawing has a dot with at most five links, and that is enough to colour the dots of any flat drawing with five colours so that joined dots differ, which is the map colourer’s question in disguise. Four colours also suffice. That was guessed in 1852 and proved only in 1976, with a computer, and the step does not prove it: 41·now asks what that proof is.

A drawing of a graph in one piece on a flat sheet has dots minus links plus faces equal to two. Each face is rimmed by at least three links, so a flat graph with V dots has at most 3V − 6 links, and at most 2V − 4 with no triangle. So five dots all joined, and three houses joined to three wells, cannot be drawn flat. Every flat graph has a dot with at most five links, and five colours suffice.

41d1Drawn without crossing

A graph is flat when it can be drawn on a flat sheet with each link a curve between its two dots, and no two curves meeting except at a shared dot. The curves cut the sheet into faces, one of them the outside, which goes on without end.

34f’s sphere-drawing technique said that a drawing on the sphere in one piece, with at least one corner, has V − E + F = 2. A flat drawing becomes a drawing on the sphere by wrapping the sheet round it and closing it at one point, which lies in the outside face; and a drawing on the sphere becomes flat by opening the sphere at a point in any face. The faces correspond, the outside to the face round the point. Held in trust, as rung 34 held it: that a closed curve drawn without crossing itself splits the sheet into exactly two parts, an inside and an outside.

Flat-count technique: a flat drawing of a graph in one piece has V − E + F = 2, counting the outside face. One graph has many flat drawings, and the faces move about between them; the count of faces does not, since F = 2 − V + E.

41d2Too many links

Walk round the rim of a face and count the links used, counting a link twice if the face lies on both its sides. Every link is counted twice over all the faces: once for each side.

Flat-ceiling technique: a flat graph with V ≥ 3 dots has at most 3V − 6 links; if it has no triangle, at most 2V − 4.

\text{a flat graph in one piece, } V \ge 3 @@ a graph not in one piece gains links until it is, staying flat
\textstyle\sum_{\text{faces}} (\text{links on the rim}) = 2E @@ each link counted once from each side
\text{each rim uses at least 3 links, so } 3F \le 2E @@ two links bound no face when V ≥ 3
2 = V - E + F \le V - E + \tfrac{2}{3}E @@ the flat-count technique
\htmlClass{new}{E \le 3V - 6;\ \ \text{with no triangle each rim uses at least 4, so } E \le 2V - 4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines with 4F ≤ 2E
  
K_5:\ V = 5,\ E = 10 > 3 \cdot 5 - 6 = 9 @@ five dots, all joined
K_{3,3}:\ V = 6,\ E = 9 > 2 \cdot 6 - 4 = 8 @@ three houses, three wells; two-sided, so no triangle (41a3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither is flat}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the flat-ceiling technique

These two are the only obstacles. A graph is flat exactly when no part of it is K₅ or K₃,₃ with some links replaced by strings of links through new dots. That was proved in 1930, and the proof is longer than this step: it is stated, and used nowhere.

41d3A dot with few links

Five-or-fewer technique: every flat graph has a dot with at most five links.

V \le 2:\ \text{every dot has at most one link} @@ the small cases
\textstyle\sum_v d(v) = 2E \le 6V - 12 @@ the ends-count and flat-ceiling techniques
\text{if every } d(v) \ge 6 \text{, then } \textstyle\sum_v d(v) \ge 6V @@ suppose
\htmlClass{new}{\bot:\ 6V \le 6V - 12;\ \text{so some dot has } d(v) \le 5} \quad \blacksquare @@

The average count is below 6. A flat graph is thin on average, however it is drawn: the seams of a football meet three at each corner, and it always has exactly 12 pentagons; 41d.6.4 asks why.

41d4Five colours

A colouring of a graph with k colours gives each dot one of k colours so that joined dots differ. A map whose countries are joined when they share a stretch of border becomes a flat graph, a dot in each country and a link across each shared border. So colouring a map is colouring a flat graph.

Six colours are easy, by induction on V: take a dot with at most five links, colour the rest, and one of six colours is free for it. Five need one more move. In a colouring, take two colours, say 1 and 3, and look at the dots of those two colours only, with the links between them. Swapping 1 and 3 in one piece of that two-colour graph leaves a colouring, since inside the piece the two colours trade places, and a link from the piece to a dot outside it goes to a dot of another colour: a link to a dot of colour 1 or 3 would put that dot in the piece. Call that a two-colour swap.

Five-colour technique: every flat graph can be coloured with five colours.

v \text{ with } d(v) \le 5;\ \ G - v \text{ coloured with } 1, \dots, 5 @@ five-or-fewer; induction on V (4c)
\text{the neighbours use four colours or fewer: give } v \text{ the fifth} @@ done
\text{else } v_1, \dots, v_5 \text{ in order round } v \text{, coloured } 1, \dots, 5 @@ the order of the curves leaving v in the drawing
v_3 \text{ not in the piece of colours 1 and 3 that holds } v_1:\ \text{swap there; } 1 \text{ is free for } v @@ v₁ turns 3; v₃ keeps 3
\text{else a path } v_1 \cdots v_3 \text{ in colours 1 and 3, and } v \text{, close a cycle with } v_2 \text{ inside and } v_4 \text{ outside} @@ the closed curve splits the sheet (held in trust, 41d1)
\text{the piece of colours 2 and 4 that holds } v_2 \text{ cannot reach } v_4 @@ to cross the cycle it would meet a dot of colour 1 or 3, or v
\htmlClass{new}{\text{swap 2 and 4 there; } 2 \text{ is free for } v} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two-colour swap

With four colours the same move fails: at a dot with five neighbours using four colours, one swap can undo the other. A proof of 1879 made that mistake, and stood for eleven years. 41·now takes up the four colours.

The accepted names

Flat graphs are planar graphs, and a flat drawing is a plane embedding. The flat-count technique is Euler's formula for plane graphs; the flat-ceiling technique is its corollary on edges. The characterization by K₅ and K₃,₃ is Kuratowski's theorem (1930; Wagner's theorem, 1937, says the same with minors). A two-colour swap is a Kempe chain interchange. The five-colour technique is Heawood's five colour theorem; the four colour theorem is the claim for four. That a closed curve splits the plane in two is the Jordan curve theorem.

Who said it first

Francis Guthrie guessed in 1852 that four colours suffice, colouring a map of the counties of England. Alfred Kempe published a proof in 1879; Percy Heawood found its flaw in 1890 and proved five colours with Kempe's own swap. Kazimierz Kuratowski found the two obstacles in 1930. Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved four colours in 1976.

A proof that is not allowed

“A map that needs five colours must have five countries each touching the other four. That would be K₅ drawn flat, and K₅ is not flat. So four colours suffice.” Not allowed: a graph can need many colours without holding five dots all joined. Needing five colours and holding K₅ are different, and the step from one to the other is the whole difficulty.
