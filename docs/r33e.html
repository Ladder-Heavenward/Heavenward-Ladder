33e · proof

Characteristics and Shocks

A patch of muddy water from a landslide rides down a river at the river’s speed. Nothing spreads it, nothing smooths it; it is simply carried. The rule for that has only first slopes, one in time and one in space, and it is the plainest rule of the rung. Its answers are found by riding along with the water: on each riding path the muddiness does not change. When the speed of the riding paths is fixed, every shape is carried unchanged. When the speed depends on the thing being carried, as it does for cars on a highway, where a crowded stretch moves slower than an empty one, the paths can run into each other. Faster water behind slower water catches up, the slope of the shape grows steeper and steeper, and at a time the step computes exactly, it becomes a jump. After that the rule in slopes means nothing at the jump. The books still have to be kept, and keeping them fixes how fast the jump moves.

A first-slope rule is solved by riding along paths on which the answer does not change. When the riding speed depends on the answer, paths cross, the answer steepens into a jump at a time fixed by the steepest fall of the start, and the books fix the speed of the jump.

33e1Carried Along

Let u(x, t) be how muddy a river is at the point x, a distance downstream, at time t. The river flows at the speed c everywhere. Water does not mix in this model: each bit of water keeps its mud. So along a bit of water, at x = x₀ + ct, u does not change.

Carried-along technique: the answers of u_t + c u_x = 0 with a continuous slope are exactly u = f(x − ct), the start f carried at the speed c.

\tfrac{d}{dt}\,u(x_0 + ct,\ t) = c\,u_x + u_t @@ the roads-add technique (28c), along the road x = x₀ + ct
\tfrac{d}{dt}\,u(x_0 + ct,\ t) = 0 @@ the rule
u(x_0 + ct,\ t) = u(x_0, 0) = f(x_0) @@ zero slope means flat (14d)
\htmlClass{new}{u(x, t) = f(x - ct)} \quad \blacksquare @@ every (x, t) lies on the road with x₀ = x − ct; and f(x − ct) solves the rule, by the chain rule

A rule with only first slopes is first order, as on 24a1, and this one is the carrying rule. Mud from a slide entering the Thompson River, with the river at 2 m/s, reaches a gauge 36 km downstream 5 hours later, the same shape it started. Real mud spreads a little as well; the heat rule, added on, does that.

33e2Riding Paths

Now let the speed vary from place to place and time to time: u_t + a(x, t) u_x = 0, with a tame (24d). A riding path is a solution of the rate rule x′(t) = a(x, t). By the existence technique (24d), exactly one riding path passes through each point, near it. Their accepted name is characteristics.

Riding-path technique: an answer of u_t + a(x, t)u_x = 0 with a continuous slope does not change along any riding path; so u at (x, t) is the start at the point where the riding path through (x, t) began.

x^{\prime}(t) = a(x(t), t) @@ a riding path
\tfrac{d}{dt}\,u(x(t), t) = u_x\,x^{\prime}(t) + u_t = a\,u_x + u_t @@ roads add (28c)
\tfrac{d}{dt}\,u(x(t), t) = 0 @@ the rule
\htmlClass{new}{u(x(t), t) = u(x(0), 0) = f(x(0))} \quad \blacksquare @@ zero slope means flat (14d)

One example first. With a(x, t) = x, the riding paths solve x′ = x, so x = x₀eᵗ (24a), and the path through (x, t) began at x₀ = xe^(−t). So u = f(xe^(−t)). Check: u_t = −xe^(−t) f′ and u_x = e^(−t) f′, so u_t + xu_x = 0. The start is carried and stretched, faster the further out it sits.

33e3Paths That Cross

Now let the speed be the answer itself: u_t + u u_x = 0, started at u(x, 0) = f(x), with f continuous, bounded and with a continuous bounded slope. A riding path has x′ = u, and u does not change along it, so it is a straight line, x = x₀ + f(x₀)t, carrying the value f(x₀). Where f falls, fast paths start behind slow ones.

Breaking-time technique: if −f′ has greatest value m > 0, the answer of u_t + uu_x = 0 from f has a continuous slope for 0 ≤ t < 1/m and no longer; its slope heads to −∞ as t heads to 1/m.

x = x_0 + f(x_0)\,t,\ \ u = f(x_0) @@ the riding-path technique: u is constant on the path, so the path is straight
\tfrac{\partial x}{\partial x_0} = 1 + f^{\prime}(x_0)\,t \ge 1 - mt > 0 \text{ for } t < 1/m @@ so x₀ ↦ x is increasing, and with f bounded it passes every bound both ways
\text{each } x \text{ comes from exactly one } x_0 @@ no gaps (12e) and increasing
u_x = f^{\prime}(x_0)\,\tfrac{\partial x_0}{\partial x} = \tfrac{f^{\prime}(x_0)}{1 + f^{\prime}(x_0)\,t} @@ the chain rule, and the slope of an inverse (13l)
\htmlClass{new}{f^{\prime}(x_0) = -m \Rightarrow u_x = \tfrac{-m}{1 - mt} \to -\infty \text{ as } t \to 1/m} \quad \blacksquare @@ on the path from the steepest fall; past 1/m two paths reach one point with two values

One example first. Start at f(x) = e^(−x²). Its steepest fall is at x = 1/√2, where −f′ = √2 e^(−1/2) ≈ 0.858. So the answer breaks at t = 1/0.858 ≈ 1.17. Before that, the front of the bump steepens; at 1.17 it is upright; after, the slope rule has no answer.

33e4The Jump Keeps the Books

The rule u_t + uu_x = 0 is also u_t + (u²/2)_x = 0. In that form it keeps books: on any stretch [α, β], the slope of ∫u is the flow q(u) = u²/2 in at α less the flow out at β. Books make sense where slopes do not. Let the answer jump along a path x = s(t), with u_L just left of the jump and u_R just right, and smooth on each side.

Jump-speed technique: if a rule keeps the books with flow q(u), a jump from u_L to u_R moves at the speed s′ = (q(u_L) − q(u_R)) / (u_L − u_R).

\tfrac{d}{dt}\textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta u\,dx = q(u(\alpha, t)) - q(u(\beta, t)) @@ the books, with α < s(t) < β
\tfrac{d}{dt}\textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta u = u_L\,s^{\prime} + \int_\alpha^{s} u_t - u_R\,s^{\prime} + \int_{s}^\beta u_t @@ split at s(t); the moving-end technique (16b) on each piece
\textstyle\int_\alpha^{s} u_t = q(u(\alpha, t)) - q(u_L),\ \ \int_{s}^\beta u_t = q(u_R) - q(u(\beta, t)) @@ on each side the slope rule holds: u_t = −q(u)_x, then 16c
(u_L - u_R)\,s^{\prime} = q(u_L) - q(u_R) @@ the two lines agree, so the rest cancels
\htmlClass{new}{s^{\prime} = \tfrac{q(u_L) - q(u_R)}{u_L - u_R}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for q = u²/2 this is (u_L + u_R)/2, the average of the two sides

Its accepted name is a shock. Cars on a highway keep the same books. Let ρ be cars per kilometre of lane, and suppose drivers go at v = v_max(1 − ρ/ρ_max), so the flow is q = ρv. For Interstate 5 through Seattle take v_max = 100 km/h and ρ_max = 150 cars a km. Cars at ρ = 30, doing 80 km/h, run into a stopped queue at ρ = 150. The back of the queue is a shock with speed 100 × (1 − 180/150) = −20 km/h. It moves backward, toward the oncoming cars, at 20 km/h, though every car in it moves forward or not at all.

The accepted names

u_t + cu_x = 0 is the transport, or advection, equation. The riding paths are characteristic curves, and the riding-path technique is the method of characteristics. u_t + uu_x = 0 is the inviscid Burgers equation. The books form of a rule is its conservation form, or weak form, and q is the flux. The jump-speed technique is the Rankine–Hugoniot condition. The rule that keeps jumps whose riding paths run into them is the Lax entropy condition, and the fan u = x/t is a rarefaction wave. The traffic rule is the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards model.

Who said it first

Bernhard Riemann studied waves in a gas that steepen into shocks in 1860. William Rankine (1870) and Pierre-Henri Hugoniot (1887) found the speed of a shock from the books of mass, momentum and energy. Harry Bateman wrote the equation in 1915, and Johannes Burgers studied it from 1948 as a model of turbulence. Michael Lighthill and Gerald Whitham (1955) and Paul Richards (1956) made traffic a rule of this kind. Peter Lax gave the entropy condition in 1957.

A proof that is not allowed

“u_t + uu_x = 0 and u_t + (u²/2)_x = 0 are the same rule, so any form of it gives the same jumps.” Not allowed: they are the same where u has a slope. Multiply the rule by u and it reads (u²/2)_t + (u³/3)_x = 0, true wherever slopes exist, and it keeps different books, so its jump from 1 to 0 moves at speed ⅔, not ½. The rule alone does not fix the jump. The books do, and choosing which books is choosing what is kept.
