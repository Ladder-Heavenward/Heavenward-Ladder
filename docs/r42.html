Arithmetic Works on a Clock

A clock adds without ever passing twelve: nine o’clock and five hours make two. Rung 4 divided with remainders, and rung 5 sorted things into classes that count as the same. This rung puts the two together. Two whole numbers count as the same on the clock of n when n divides their difference, and then sums and products of the same things are the same: the whole of arithmetic carries over to the clock. Then the rung asks what is new there. On some clocks every hour but zero can be undone by multiplying, exactly when the size of the clock is prime, and those clocks behave like the fractions. Several clocks can be read at once, and a number is fixed by its readings. Powers on a clock go round in cycles, of a length that divides a count fixed by the clock; on a prime clock some hand reaches every hour; half the hours are squares, and a hidden symmetry links which primes are squares on each other’s clocks. Last, the rung counts the primes themselves: there are endlessly many, they thin out like one over the log, and each clock shares them out fairly. The later steps do all this by hand and put it to work: fast powers, a lock with a key anyone may print, quick tests for being prime, best fractions, and secrets agreed in public.

Numbers that differ by a multiple of n are one hour of the clock of n, and plus and times carry over. An hour can be undone exactly when it shares nothing with n, so prime clocks undo everything but zero. Coprime clocks read together are one clock. Powers cycle with a length that divides the count of units; prime clocks have a hand that reaches every hour; squares obey a swap law between two primes. The primes are endless, thin like 1/ln x, and shared fairly among the hours.

What it means to say it

To say that arithmetic works on a clock is to say that the whole numbers, taken only by their remainders on dividing by a fixed n, can still be added and multiplied, with every rule of plus and times that holds for whole numbers still holding; and that this smaller arithmetic has laws of its own, about undoing, powers, squares and primes, that come back to tell us about the whole numbers themselves.

On the clock of n, sums and products carry over and obey every rule of plus and times; an hour can be undone exactly when it shares nothing with n, so a prime clock is a field; clocks of sizes that share nothing read together are one clock; every unit to the power of the count of units is 1, and a prime clock has a hand that reaches every unit; squares on prime clocks obey the square-swap technique; and the primes are endless, with Σ 1/p passing every bound and ζ a product over them. The rung proves it, and holds in trust where it says so the prime count and the fair sharing of primes among the hours.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Where it comes from

Remainders are as old as calendars, and a puzzle of soldiers counted off in threes, fives and sevens is in a Chinese book of the third to fifth century. In 1640 a lawyer found the little technique in letters to friends; in the next century it was proved and extended to every clock with the count of units, and by 1801 the full hand was proved to exist on every prime clock. In 1796, at eighteen, a student proved the swap law for squares, and in 1801 he published the book that gave the mark ≡ and the word modulus. In 1737 the product over primes was found, in 1837 endlessly many primes proved in each unit hour, and in 1896 the prime count. In 1976 and 1977 the clock arithmetic of this rung became the lock of every web page. The names are in the cellars of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things.

First, two whole numbers are the same hour of the clock of n when n divides their difference; there are n hours, and sums and products of the same hours are the same hours, so every rule of plus and times carries over.

Second, an hour has an undo exactly when it shares nothing with n, by writing the gcd as a sum. The hours with an undo, the units, form a group under times, and on a prime clock every hour but zero is a unit: a prime clock is a field.

Third, if m and n share nothing, an hour of the clock of mn is the same as a pair of hours, one on each clock, and sums and products are read clock by clock.

Fourth, a unit raised to the count of units is 1; on a prime clock, a^(p − 1) is 1, and some hand reaches every unit by its powers.

Fifth, on the clock of an odd prime half the units are squares, the square test tells which, and two odd primes are squares on each other’s clocks in the same way, unless both are 3 more than a multiple of 4.

Sixth, there are endlessly many primes, so many that the sum of their reciprocals passes every bound, and ζ is the product over them; π(x) is fenced below by a fixed share of x/ln x. That π(x) is near x/ln x, and that each unit hour of a clock gets its fair share of primes, are held in trust.

What you can do because you know it

You can do arithmetic on any clock, find an undo or say why there is none, and solve several remainders at once. You can take any power on a clock by hand, find orders and full hands, and decide whether a number is a square on a prime clock without listing squares. You can say why the primes are endless and how thinly they lie, and what is proved about them and what is only held in trust.

And you can follow the arithmetic of a lock that keeps the web, of a test that finds primes of three hundred digits in a blink, and of a crystal whose electrons sit on a clock.
