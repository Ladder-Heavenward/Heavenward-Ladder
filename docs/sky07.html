What Happens When a Material Is One Atom Thick?

What happens when a material is one atom thick? The grey of a pencil line is graphite, stacks of flat sheets of carbon held together so weakly that they slide off onto the paper. For most of the last century the single sheet was a thing of calculations only: a crystal one atom thick, it was argued, would be shaken apart by its own warmth or curl into a ball. In 2004 a group in Manchester pressed sticky tape onto graphite, peeled it again and again, laid the flakes on a polished slice of silicon, and found sheets one atom thick lying flat and whole. The sheet is the stiffest and strongest material ever measured for its weight, and the electrons in it move as if they had no mass at all, because of nothing more than the shape of a honeycomb. Then in 2018 a group in Cambridge, Massachusetts, laid one sheet on another, turned the top one by about one degree, and the pair carried current with no loss when cooled, a thing neither sheet does alone. This page builds the honeycomb, finds its bands with the tools of the rungs on metals and molecules, finds the point where they touch and the cone around it, weighs and pulls a sheet with real numbers, and then twists two sheets and counts the long pattern they make. Why the twisted pair carries current with no loss is still argued.

The honeycomb is a lattice of cells with two atoms each, its cell spanned by two arrows of length √3 times the bond. An electron hopping between neighbours has the two bands ±t|1 + e^(ik·a₁) + e^(ik·a₂)|, which touch exactly at the corners of the pattern of waves, where three unit turnings a third of a turn apart add to 0. Near a corner the levels form a cone, E = ±ħv|q| with v = 3td/(2ħ), about 10⁶ m/s, the rule of a particle with no mass. One electron per carbon fills the lower band to the tip. Two sheets turned by θ beat into a pattern of period a/(2 sin(θ/2)), 12.8 nm at 1.1°, and four electrons per cell of that pattern fill its flattest bands.

sky7.1The Honeycomb Cell

Each carbon in the sheet joins three others at 120°, d = 0.142 nm away. The honeycomb is not itself a lattice in the sense of 43·phys: the neighbours of one atom point up, the neighbours of the next point down, so one step along a bond does not carry the sheet onto itself. Two kinds of site, call them A and B, take turns. The A sites alone form a lattice of triangles, and so do the B sites; each cell of it holds one A and one B.

Honeycomb-cell technique: the honeycomb with bond d is a lattice spanned by a₁ = (a/2, √3a/2) and a₂ = (−a/2, √3a/2) with a = √3 d, each cell holding two atoms, and each cell has area (3√3/2)d².

\text{A to its three B neighbours: } \delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3,\ |\delta_i| = d,\ 120^\circ \text{ apart} @@ the bonds
a_1 = \delta_1 - \delta_3,\ \ a_2 = \delta_2 - \delta_3,\ \ |a_i|^2 = d^2 + d^2 - 2d^2\cos 120^\circ = 3d^2 @@ A to A, across one B
\text{area} = |\det(a_1\ a_2)| = \tfrac{a}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3 a}{2} + \tfrac{a}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3 a}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a^2 @@ 27e: the determinant is the area of the cell
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 3d^2 = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}d^2 = 0.0524 \text{ nm}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ with d = 0.142 nm, a = 0.246 nm
  
2 / (5.24 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{m}^2) = 3.82 \times 10^{19} \text{ atoms per m}^2 @@ two atoms a cell
3.82 \times 10^{19} \times 12.011 \times 1.661 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} @@ 5·chem: a carbon atom weighs 12.011 mass units
\htmlClass{new}{7.6 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{kg} = 0.76 \text{ mg per m}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ a square metre weighs less than a grain of salt

Turning the sheet by a third of a turn about any atom carries it onto itself, and so does the flip that swaps the two kinds of site through the middle of a bond. These moves are the sheet’s group in the sense of 19a, and the next two footings spend them.

sky7.2Three Neighbours Make Two Bands

43·phys let an electron hop between two kinds of site taking turns along a row, and 44·chem read a molecule’s levels off its grid of links. The sheet is a molecule with endlessly many atoms, and its grid of links has a repeat in two directions. Wrap the sheet round in both directions, as copper was wrapped, so a wave is labelled by an arrow k, and on each cell put an amount α e^(ik·R) at its A site and β e^(ik·R) at its B site, R the corner of the cell. Every A sees its three B neighbours, each hop costing −t, and every site has the same energy, set at 0.

Three-neighbour technique: an electron hopping by t between neighbours of the honeycomb has, for each wave k, the two levels E = ±t|f(k)| with f(k) = 1 + e^(ik·a₁) + e^(ik·a₂).

E\alpha = -t\,(1 + e^{-ik\cdot a_1} + e^{-ik\cdot a_2})\,\beta = -t\,\overline{f(k)}\,\beta @@ the rule at an A site: its B neighbours lie in its own cell and in the cells one step back along a₁ and a₂
E\beta = -t\,f(k)\,\alpha @@ the rule at a B site: its neighbours one step forward
E^2\alpha\beta = t^2|f(k)|^2\alpha\beta @@ multiply the two, f times its flip
\htmlClass{new}{E = \pm t\,|f(k)|} \quad \blacksquare @@ α, β not both 0, so neither is 0 unless E = 0 = f

At k = 0 the three hops agree, f = 3, and the levels are ±3t: the bottom of the lower band and the top of the upper. A band is 6t wide. Each carbon gives one electron to the sheet, two kinds of site give two bands, and by the two spins of 38·phys the two bands hold four electrons a cell. The sheet has two. The lower band is exactly full, and nothing yet says whether it touches the upper one.

sky7.3Where the Bands Touch

The bands touch where f(k) = 0, and the electrons at the top of the full band sit exactly there. f is a sum of three turnings of length 1.

Touching-corner technique: three turnings of length 1 add to 0 exactly when they are a third of a turn apart; so f(k) = 0 exactly when k·a₁ and k·a₂ are 2π/3 and −2π/3 or the other way round, at the corners of the six-sided pattern of waves, and only two of the six corners are different waves.

1 + u + v = 0,\ |u| = |v| = 1 \Rightarrow |1 + u| = |v| = 1 @@ move v across
|1 + u|^2 = 2 + 2\cos\theta_u = 1 \Rightarrow \cos\theta_u = -\tfrac12 @@ 21c, 25d
u = \omega \text{ or } \bar\omega,\ \ \omega = e^{2\pi i/3},\ \ v = -1 - u = \bar\omega \text{ or } \omega @@ and 1 + ω + ω̄ = 0 checks: the roots of 1 on the clock of 3 (43·phys.1)
K = \big(\tfrac{4\pi}{3a}, 0\big):\ \ K\cdot a_1 = \tfrac{2\pi}{3},\ K\cdot a_2 = -\tfrac{2\pi}{3} @@ one corner
\htmlClass{new}{f(K) = 1 + \omega + \bar\omega = 0,\ \ f(-K) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other four corners differ from K or −K by a wave that is 1 at every cell

So the full band and the empty one meet at two points, K and −K, and the top of the electrons sits exactly on them. The sheet is not a metal with a sea of free levels, and not an insulator with a gap: its gap is exactly zero. The zero is held in place by the moves of the last footing. The third of a turn sends the three hops into one another, so at the corner, where it is a symmetry of the wave too, their sum must be a sum of the three roots of 1. Spoil the swap of A and B and the zero goes: boron nitride is the same honeycomb with boron on A and nitrogen on B, and by the two-site-gap technique the difference of the two sites opens a gap, about 6 eV. Boron nitride is clear, and it insulates.

sky7.4A Cone With No Mass

Near the bottom of a band, 43·phys found a parabola, an electron moving as a free one with some mass. Near a corner of the honeycomb it is different. Write k = K + q with q small and use e^x ≈ 1 + x.

Massless-cone technique: near each corner, the two levels of the sheet are E = ±ħv|q| with v = 3td/(2ħ), the same in every direction of q.

f(K + q) = 1 + \omega e^{iq\cdot a_1} + \bar\omega e^{iq\cdot a_2} \approx i\,(\omega\, q\cdot a_1 + \bar\omega\, q\cdot a_2) @@ 1 + ω + ω̄ = 0 takes the first terms away
q = (q_x, 0):\ \ i(\omega - \bar\omega)\tfrac{a}{2}q_x = i \cdot i\sqrt3 \cdot \tfrac{a}{2}q_x,\ \ |f| = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a\,|q_x| @@ q·a₁ = aq_x/2, q·a₂ = −aq_x/2
q = (0, q_y):\ \ i(\omega + \bar\omega)\tfrac{\sqrt3 a}{2}q_y,\ \ |f| = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a\,|q_y| @@ ω + ω̄ = −1; the same size
\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a = \tfrac{3}{2}d @@ a = √3 d; any direction gives the same, as a check with both parts shows
\htmlClass{new}{E = \pm t\,\tfrac{3d}{2}|q| = \pm\hbar v|q|,\ \ v = \tfrac{3td}{2\hbar}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three-neighbour technique
  
t \approx 2.8 \text{ eV},\ \ d = 0.142 \text{ nm},\ \ \hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16} \text{ eV s} @@ t measured, held in trust
v = \tfrac{3 \times 2.8 \times 0.142 \times 10^{-9}}{2 \times 6.582 \times 10^{-16}} @@ the massless-cone technique
\htmlClass{new}{v \approx 9.1 \times 10^5 \text{ m/s} \approx c/330} \quad \blacksquare @@ measured: about 1.0 × 10⁶ m/s

E = ħv|q| is the rule of 29·phys for a particle of mass 0, E = pc, with v in place of c. An electron with a mass speeds up as it is given energy; one on the cone moves at v whatever its energy, as light does. Nothing about the electron itself has changed. It is the honeycomb, and the zero held by its moves, that makes the electron act as if it had no mass. The cone was calculated in 1947 for graphite, and measured in a sheet in 2005, by a pattern of levels in a magnet that only a cone gives.

sky7.5Pulled, and Filled

In 2008 a group in New York pressed a diamond tip into sheets laid over holes a micrometre across and pulled until they broke. A sheet one metre wide held 42 newtons before it tore, and stretched with a stiffness of 340 newtons per metre. Spread over the sheet’s thickness in graphite, 0.335 nm, that is a stiffness of 1.0 × 10¹² pascals and a strength of 1.3 × 10¹¹: steel of the best kind breaks near 2 × 10⁹. Strength over density, in newton metres per kilogram, is 5.5 × 10⁷ for the sheet and 2.5 × 10⁵ for that steel: for its weight the sheet is about 200 times as strong. A cat of 4 kg pulls with 39 newtons, so a strip one metre wide and weighing 0.76 mg would hold it, if it were perfect and pulled straight. Real sheets of that size are not perfect: a single missing row of atoms starts a tear.

The electrons are counted as copper’s were. Each wave q near a corner takes four electrons, two spins at each of the two corners.

Cone-filling technique: n extra electrons per unit area, put into the upper cone, fill it to |q| = q_F with q_F² = πn, and the top level is ħvq_F.

\text{one } q \text{ per box of area } (2\pi/L)^2 \text{ on a sheet } L \times L @@ the wrap-round technique, in two directions
4 \cdot \pi q_F^2 / (2\pi/L)^2 = nL^2 @@ two spins, two corners; the filled q form a disc, as copper’s formed a ball
\htmlClass{new}{q_F^2 = \pi n,\ \ E_F = \hbar v q_F} \quad \blacksquare @@ the L² cancels
  
\text{charge per volt per m}^2 = \kappa\varepsilon_0/d = 3.9 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}/(300 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.15 \times 10^{-4} @@ 28·phys: the sheet is one plate, silicon the other, 300 nm of oxide between
n = 1.15 \times 10^{-4}/1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 7.2 \times 10^{14} \text{ per m}^2 \text{ per volt} @@ so 14 V puts in n = 10¹⁶
q_F = \sqrt{\pi \times 10^{16}} = 1.77 \times 10^8 \text{ per m},\ \ E_F = 0.12 \text{ eV} @@ the cone-filling technique, v = 10⁶ m/s
\sigma = ne\mu = 10^{16} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 1\ \text{m}^2/\text{V s} = 1.6 \times 10^{-3},\ \ \text{a square of it: } 625 \text{ ohms} @@ μ, the drift speed per field, measured on oxide
\htmlClass{new}{\text{run between knocks} = \hbar q_F\mu/e = 1.17 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 43·phys’s drift-current technique with ħq_F in place of mv: 117 nm, three times copper’s 39

A voltage on the silicon below sets how many electrons the sheet holds, and turned the other way it takes them out, so the same sheet can be filled with electrons or emptied into holes by a knob. That knob is what every later experiment on this page spends. Hung free of the oxide and cooled, sheets have shown runs of micrometres.

sky7.6Two Sheets, Turned

Lay one sheet on another and turn the top one by a small angle θ. Where the atoms of the two sit over each other the pair looks like graphite; a few nanometres away they sit over the gaps; further on they line up again. The long pattern is the pair’s beat. 32·phys heard two notes of nearly the same frequency swell and fade at the difference of the frequencies. Rows of atoms are waves in space, and here the two waves have the same spacing and differ in direction.

Twist-beat technique: two equal lattices of triangles with side a, one turned by θ against the other, make a pattern of triangles with side L = a/(2 sin(θ/2)).

\text{rows of atoms } s = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}a \text{ apart: a wave arrow } G,\ |G| = 2\pi/s @@ the rows of one sheet, read as e^(iG·x)
G' = G \text{ turned by } \theta,\ \ |G - G'| = 2|G|\sin(\theta/2) @@ two arrows of one length, θ apart: an isosceles triangle
e^{iG\cdot x} + e^{iG'\cdot x} \text{ swells and fades with the arrow } G - G' @@ the beat technique, in space
\text{beat rows } \tfrac{2\pi}{|G - G'|} = \tfrac{s}{2\sin(\theta/2)} \text{ apart, for each of the three row directions} @@ the three beats are three rows of a new lattice of triangles
\htmlClass{new}{L = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\cdot\tfrac{s}{2\sin(\theta/2)} = \tfrac{a}{2\sin(\theta/2)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ rows of a lattice of side L are (√3/2)L apart
  
L = \tfrac{0.246}{2\sin(0.55^\circ)} = \tfrac{0.246}{0.01920} = 12.8 \text{ nm} @@ fifty times the cell
\tfrac{\text{area of a big cell}}{\text{area of a small cell}} = (L/a)^2 = 2707 @@ both cells are triangles of the same shape
\htmlClass{new}{\text{atoms in one big cell} = 4 \times 2707 \approx 10\,800} \quad \blacksquare @@ two sheets, two atoms per small cell

The beat is not a new lattice of atoms. No atom moves; it is a pattern in where the atoms of one sheet sit against the other, and it has its own wrap-round, its own waves and its own bands. A cell of ten thousand atoms gives an electron room to make a wave of its own size, and the twist sets the size: at 2° the big cell is 7.0 nm, at 5° only 2.8 nm.

sky7.7The Magic Angle

An electron hops between the sheets as well as within one, with an energy w ≈ 0.11 eV, held in trust from measurement. Inside one big cell, an electron on one sheet’s cone that crosses the cell gains about ħv times the size k_θ of the beat arrow in energy, the cones of the two sheets sitting k_θ = 2|K| sin(θ/2) apart. A calculation of 2011, held in trust here, found that the hop between the sheets slows the cone, by the factor (1 − 3α²)/(1 + 6α²) with α = w/(ħvk_θ). At α² = 1/3 the speed is 0: the lowest bands of the pair go flat.

\alpha = 1/\sqrt3:\ \ \hbar v k_\theta = \sqrt3\,w = 0.19 \text{ eV} @@ the slowing factor is 0
k_\theta = \tfrac{0.19}{0.596 \text{ eV nm}} = 0.320 \text{ per nm} @@ ħv with v = 9.1 × 10⁵ m/s
\sin(\theta/2) = \tfrac{k_\theta}{2|K|} = \tfrac{0.320}{2 \times 17.03} = 0.00939 @@ |K| = 4π/(3a) = 17.03 per nm
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \approx 1.08^\circ} @@ the first magic angle, measured near 1.1°

Moiré-filling technique: the flat bands at the magic angle hold four electrons per big cell for each of them, so filling one takes n_s = 4/((√3/2)L²) electrons per unit area, and half filling it takes two per cell. It is the cone-filling technique’s count, two spins times two corners, with the big cell in place of the area of the sheet: each band has one wave per big cell, so four places per big cell.

(\sqrt3/2)L^2 = 0.866 \times 12.8^2 = 142 \text{ nm}^2 @@ the big cell
n_s = 4/142 \text{ nm}^2 = 2.8 \times 10^{16} \text{ per m}^2 @@ one flat band full
\htmlClass{new}{n_s/2 = 1.4 \times 10^{16}:\ 20 \text{ V on 300 nm of oxide}} \quad \blacksquare @@ sky7.5: 7.2 × 10¹⁴ per volt

Why flat matters. When a band is flat the electrons in it barely move, so there is almost no energy of motion to spend, and their push on each other decides everything. Two electrons a big cell apart push with 1.44 eV nm/12.8 nm ≈ 0.11 eV, cut to roughly 0.03 by the materials around them (34·phys’s fields in matter), while the flat bands are about 0.01 eV wide. In 2018 a group in Cambridge, Massachusetts, turned the voltage knob through these bands at 1.1°. At two electrons, or two holes, per big cell the pair stopped conducting. Filled a little further, below 1.7 kelvins, it carried current with no loss, as sky06’s superconductors do, with about one extra electron for every five thousand carbon atoms.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞7.oq.1What makes the electrons of the twisted pair go round in pairs? In the metals of sky06 the shaking of the lattice glues the pairs, and that glue can be computed. Here the band is so flat that the electrons’ own push on each other is larger than their energy of motion, and many think that push, not the shaking, does it. Measurements of how the loss-free state answers a magnet, a temperature and the knob have been read both ways. A clean test is hard because the same voltage that fills the band also changes how strongly the electrons are screened.
∞7.oq.2Is the state that stops conducting at two electrons per big cell the parent of the loss-free state, or its rival? In the copper oxides of sky06 the insulator and the superconductor sit side by side, and the same was assumed here at first. In 2019 and 2020 groups put a metal sheet a few nanometres away to screen the push, and in some samples the insulator vanished while the loss-free state stayed. Whether that settles it, or the two are two faces of one state, is argued.
∞7.oq.3Why do no two twisted samples behave quite alike? The magic angle is 1.1° within about a tenth of a degree, and a sample twisted by 1.05° in one corner and 1.15° in another is two materials. Maps of real samples show the angle wandering by that much over a few micrometres. Can a twist be set and held across a whole chip, and if not, what does an average over wandering angles measure?
∞7.oq.4Can a perfect sheet be made by the square metre? A flake peeled with tape is nearly perfect and smaller than a hair’s width. Sheets grown on copper foil reach metres but are patchworks of grains, joined along lines of misplaced rings that weaken them and knock their electrons. Most of what has been sold as graphene powder is thicker stacks. Whether a cheap route to large perfect sheets exists, or the remarkable numbers of this page belong only to small flakes, is open.
∞7.oq.5How many states of matter can stacking and twisting make? Since 2023 groups have reported charges that act as fractions of an electron, with no magnet, in twisted sheets of other layered materials and in five sheets of graphene stacked in a particular order. Whether these reports hold up, how they are related to the twisted pair, and whether a sheet can be designed for a chosen state before it is made, are questions of the last three years.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The single sheet was a calculation first. In 1947 its bands were worked out as a step toward graphite, cone and all, and for half a century the sheet was the starting point of sums about graphite and, after 1991, about carbon tubes. Few thought it could exist alone. A line of argument from the 1930s held that a crystal in two directions would be shaken out of order by its own warmth, and a technique of 1966 made a version of it exact. Thin flakes of graphite a few layers thick had been made and measured in 1962, and again in the 1990s, but nobody had shown a sheet of one layer and measured its electrons.

In 2004 a group in Manchester did, with tape, and the claim was checked fast because anyone could repeat it: the flakes are visible under an ordinary microscope on silicon with exactly 300 nm of oxide, which makes a single layer change the colour of reflected light enough to see. In 2005 the Manchester group and a group in New York found, in a magnet, the pattern of levels that only a cone gives. The argument from the 1930s was not wrong; it speaks of a sheet of endless size, and a real sheet is held flat on a surface or ripples a little in three directions to keep its order. The physics prize of the Swedish academy followed in 2010, six years after the first paper, fast by that prize’s standards.

The prize was contested. A group in Atlanta had grown carbon layers on silicon carbide since the early 2000s and measured electrons in them in 2004, and its leader wrote to the prize committee that its account of the history was wrong in places. Others pointed to the 1962 flakes and the 1990s work. What the Manchester work clearly added was the isolated single sheet on a chip and the electrical measurement of it; who should share credit for the field is still told differently in different places.

Money followed. In 2013 the European Union launched a programme of about one billion euros over ten years, and Manchester opened a national institute for the material in 2015. Promises of flexible screens, faster chips and stronger everything were made in that decade, and most have not arrived: a sheet with zero gap does not switch off, so it cannot replace silicon in a chip’s switches, and large sheets are not perfect. A study of 2018 tested powders sold by dozens of makers and found most were mostly graphite, with little true single-layer material. Real uses so far are quieter: films that spread heat in phones, additives in composites and coatings, and sensors.

The twist began as a calculation too. In 2011 a group in Texas showed that two sheets turned by about 1.1° should have flat bands, and for years almost no one could test it, since sheets laid by hand settle back into line. In 2018 the group in Cambridge, Massachusetts, tore one flake in two, picked up one half, turned the stage by just over a degree and laid it on the other half, and found both the insulator and the loss-free state. Groups in New York and Santa Barbara, working together, and in Barcelona reproduced the loss-free state within a year, at somewhat different fillings and temperatures, and the word twistronics, coined a year before, caught on. Reproductions also showed how sensitive the samples are: the same recipe gives a superconductor in one sample and not the next.

What is argued now. The mechanism of the pairs is open (see the first open question). Measurements of how stiff the loss-free state is, published in 2025, came out larger than the usual rules for a band so flat would allow, and were read as evidence that the shape of the electrons’ waves, not only the flatness, sets it; how far that reading goes is debated. A second argument is about where the field’s effort should go: twisting is a slow craft done one sample at a time, and some groups have turned to stacks that need no twist at all, such as several sheets of graphene in a fixed order, which since 2021 have shown loss-free states and other surprises of their own.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The sheet is graphene, and a sheet of boron nitride in the same honeycomb is hexagonal boron nitride, hBN. The A and B sites are the two sublattices of the honeycomb, and the lattice of triangles is the triangular, or hexagonal, Bravais lattice. The three-neighbour technique is the tight-binding model of graphene, with t the nearest-neighbour hopping integral, near 2.7 to 3.0 eV in different fits. The six-sided pattern of waves is the first Brillouin zone, and K and −K (often K and K′) are the valleys. The cone is the Dirac cone, its tip the Dirac point, v the Fermi velocity, and the electrons near it are called massless Dirac fermions; the zero gap makes graphene a semimetal. The cone-filling technique gives n = k_F²/π with degeneracy 4. μ is the mobility, and the pattern of levels in a magnet that showed the cone in 2005 is the half-integer quantum Hall effect. The stiffness is Young’s modulus. The long pattern is a moiré pattern, the twist-beat technique gives the moiré period, and the flat bands come from the Bistritzer–MacDonald continuum model; the magic angle is about 1.1°. The state that stops conducting at half filling is the correlated insulator, and the pair is magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene, MATBG. The 1930s argument is due to Peierls and Landau, the 1966 technique is the Mermin–Wagner theorem, and the stiffness of the loss-free state is its superfluid stiffness, with a quantum-geometric contribution.

Who said it first

Philip Wallace computed the bands of a single sheet of graphite in 1947. Hanns-Peter Boehm and coworkers reported very thin graphite flakes in 1962 and later proposed the name graphene. Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov, with coworkers in Manchester and Chernogolovka, isolated single sheets with tape in 2004; their group and Philip Kim’s group at Columbia measured the half-integer quantum Hall effect in 2005; Geim and Novoselov shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010. Walt de Heer’s group at Georgia Tech grew graphene on silicon carbide and wrote the 2010 letter to the Nobel committee. Changgu Lee, Xiaoding Wei, Jeffrey Kysar and James Hone measured the strength in 2008. Rafi Bistritzer and Allan MacDonald predicted the magic angle in 2011. Yuan Cao, Pablo Jarillo-Herrero and coworkers at MIT reported the correlated insulator and superconductivity in 2018, with the tear-and-stack method developed with Kim’s and Emanuel Tutuc’s groups; Cory Dean’s and Andrea Young’s groups (Columbia, Santa Barbara) and Dmitri Efetov’s group (ICFO, Barcelona) reported further superconducting states in 2019. The 2018 survey of commercial graphene was by Alan Kauling, Antonio Castro Neto and coworkers in Singapore.

A proof that is not allowed

“The sheet’s band holds two electrons a cell and the sheet gives two, so it is an insulator, like silicon.” Not allowed: a full band makes an insulator only if a gap separates it from the next, and 43·phys’s magnesium already showed that bands can meet. Here they meet at exactly two points, and the touching-corner technique, not the count, decides it.
