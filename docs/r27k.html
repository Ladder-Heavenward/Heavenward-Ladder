27k · technique

Determinants by Hand

The determinant was found as a sum over every shuffle, one term for each way of picking an entry from every column. For a grid of ten rows that is more than three million terms, each a product of ten numbers. Nobody works it out that way. The row moves that solved systems also find determinants: a swap changes the sign, adding a stretch of one row to another changes nothing, and once the grid is a staircase the determinant is the product down its middle. A row or a column with many zeros gives another road, through the smaller determinants of the stand-in grid. And when a system has exactly one answer, each unknown is one determinant divided by another, a formula that shows how the answer moves when the numbers do.

Bring a grid to a staircase by row moves, counting the swaps, and its determinant is the product down the middle, with a sign for each swap. Or expand along any row or column through smaller determinants. When the determinant is not zero, each unknown of a system is a ratio of two determinants.

27k1Determinants Down the Stairs

Stair-det technique: if every entry below the middle of a square grid is 0, its determinant is the product of the entries down the middle. So if row moves with s swaps, and otherwise only adding stretches of rows, bring A to a staircase, det A is (−1)ˢ times the product down the middle of the staircase.

\det A = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}\sigma\ a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n} @@ the three-conditions technique (27e2)
a_{\sigma(j)j} = 0 \text{ whenever } \sigma(j) > j @@ that entry is below the middle
\text{a term that is not } 0 \text{ has } \sigma(j) \le j \text{ for every } j \Rightarrow \sigma(1) = 1,\ \sigma(2) = 2,\ \ldots @@ σ(j) ≤ j, and 1, …, j − 1 are already taken; induction on j
\det A = a_{11}a_{22}\cdots a_{nn} @@ only doing nothing is left, and it is even (19e)
\text{a row swap changes the sign; adding a stretch of a row changes nothing} @@ the column-slide technique (27e1), carried to rows by the side-turn technique (27g1)
\htmlClass{new}{\det A = (-1)^s \times \text{the product down the middle of the staircase}} \quad \blacksquare
  
A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\2&5&1&3\\0&3&4&2\\1&2&2&6\end{pmatrix} @@ given
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\0&1&1&1\\0&3&4&2\\0&0&2&5\end{pmatrix} @@ add −2 times row 1 to row 2, and −1 times row 1 to row 4: det unchanged
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\0&1&1&1\\0&0&1&-1\\0&0&2&5\end{pmatrix} @@ add −3 times row 2 to row 3
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&1\\0&1&1&1\\0&0&1&-1\\0&0&0&7\end{pmatrix} @@ add −2 times row 3 to row 4; no swaps, s = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\det A = 1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot7 = 7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stair-det technique

The formula over shuffles has n! terms. For n = 10 that is 3 628 800 terms of 9 multiplications each, about 33 million. Down the stairs it is the elimination of 27j3 without a right side, a few hundred. For n = 20 the shuffles number about 2.4 × 10¹⁸; the stairs take about three thousand.

27k2Along Any Row or Column

Expansion technique: for any column j, det A = a₁ⱼC₁ⱼ + ⋯ + aₙⱼCₙⱼ; and for any row i, det A = aᵢ₁Cᵢ₁ + ⋯ + aᵢₙCᵢₙ, where Cᵢⱼ is the stand-in for place i, j: (−1)^(i + j) times the det of A with row i and column j struck. Choose the row or column with the most zeros.

\det A = \textstyle\sum_i a_{ij}\,\det(A \text{ with column } j \text{ replaced by } e_i) @@ the first line of the stand-in technique (27g2): straight in column j
\det(A \text{ with column } j \text{ replaced by } e_i) = C_{ij} @@ the second line of the stand-in technique (27g2)
\det A = \textstyle\sum_i a_{ij}C_{ij} @@ down column j
\text{striking row } j, \text{ column } i \text{ of } A^{\mathsf T} \text{ leaves the side-turn of what striking row } i, \text{ column } j \text{ of } A \text{ leaves} @@ (Aᵀ)ⱼᵢ = aᵢⱼ
\htmlClass{new}{\det A = \det A^{\mathsf T} = \textstyle\sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the side-turn technique (27g1), twice: down column i of Aᵀ is along row i of A
  
\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\3&0&4\\1&5&2\end{pmatrix} = 5\cdot(-1)^{3+2}\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\3&4\end{pmatrix} @@ down column 2: only a₃₂ = 5 is not 0
= -5\,(8 - 3) = -25 @@ the 2 × 2 det, ad − bc (27e2)
\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{pmatrix} = a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg) @@ along row 1, signs +, −, +
\htmlClass{new}{= aei + bfg + cdh - ceg - afh - bdi} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out: six terms, one for each shuffle of three (19e)

Signs run like a chessboard, + in the top left corner. A grid of four rows has 24 terms, and the six-term pattern of diagonals does not stretch to it: copied to four rows it finds only 8.

27k3Answers as Ratios of Determinants

Det-ratio technique: if det A ≠ 0, then Ax = b has exactly one answer, and xⱼ = det Aⱼ / det A, where Aⱼ is A with column j replaced by b.

A \text{ undoable, so } x = A^{-1}b \text{ is the one answer} @@ the nonzero-det technique (27e4)
b = Ax = x_1(\text{col } 1) + \cdots + x_n(\text{col } n) @@ Ax is the mix of the columns with the numbers of x (27d1)
\det A_j = \textstyle\sum_k x_k \det(A \text{ with column } j \text{ replaced by column } k) @@ straight in column j (27e1)
\text{for } k \ne j \text{ that grid has column } k \text{ twice} @@ flat: its det is 0
\htmlClass{new}{\det A_j = x_j\det A,\ \ x_j = \tfrac{\det A_j}{\det A}} \quad \blacksquare @@ only k = j is left; divide by det A ≠ 0
  
2x + y = 5,\ \ x + 3y = 10 @@ given
\det A = 2\cdot3 - 1\cdot1 = 5 @@ not 0: exactly one answer
x = \tfrac{1}{5}\det\begin{pmatrix}5&1\\10&3\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{15 - 10}{5} = 1 @@ column 1 replaced by b
\htmlClass{new}{y = \tfrac15\det\begin{pmatrix}2&5\\1&10\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{20 - 5}{5} = 3} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 2 + 3 = 5, 1 + 9 = 10

For many unknowns the ratios are slow: n + 1 determinants where one elimination will do. Their worth is the formula. Every answer of a system with whole numbers is a fraction with det A below. And the undo of a 2 × 2 grid, by the stand-in technique, is the grid with rows (d, −b), (−c, a), divided by ad − bc.

27k4Determinants of Blocks

Many grids come in blocks: a square grid A in the top left corner, a square grid D in the bottom right, a block B above D, and only zeros below A.

Block-det technique: if M has the square blocks A and D down its middle and only zeros below A, then det M = det A · det D, whatever the block B above D holds.

M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ 0 & D\end{pmatrix} @@ A is r × r and D is s × s; the 0 block is s × r
\text{rows } 1 \text{ to } r:\ A \to A_1 \text{, a staircase} @@ by p swaps and adding stretches of rows; B becomes some B₁
\text{rows } r + 1 \text{ to } r + s:\ D \to D_1 @@ a staircase, by q swaps and adding stretches; the 0 block stays 0
\begin{pmatrix}A_1 & B_1\\ 0 & D_1\end{pmatrix} \text{ is a staircase} @@ every entry below its middle is 0
\det M = (-1)^{p+q}\,\Pi(A_1)\,\Pi(D_1) @@ the stair-det technique (27k1); Π is the product down the middle
\htmlClass{new}{\det M = \det A \cdot \det D} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stair-det technique again, for A and for D

The grid with rows (1, 2, 5, 6), (3, 4, 7, 8), (0, 0, 2, 1), (0, 0, 1, 1) has determinant (4 − 6)(2 − 1) = −2, and the 5, 6, 7, 8 never enter. By the side-turn technique the same holds with the zeros above D. And with more blocks down the middle, the determinant is the product of all their determinants.

When all four blocks are full, there is still a formula as long as A can be undone.

L = \begin{pmatrix}I & 0\\ CA^{-1} & I\end{pmatrix},\ \ \det L = 1 @@ its side-turn is a staircase with 1 down its middle (27g1, 27k1)
U = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ 0 & D - CA^{-1}B\end{pmatrix} @@ four r × r blocks
LU = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{pmatrix} @@ blocks multiply as entries do (27d2): CA⁻¹A = C, and CA⁻¹B + D − CA⁻¹B = D
\det(LU) = \det A \cdot \det(D - CA^{-1}B) @@ the det-product technique (27e3) and the block-det technique
\htmlClass{new}{= \det(AD - CB) \text{ when } AC = CA} \quad \blacksquare @@ det-product: A(D − CA⁻¹B) = AD − ACA⁻¹B, and ACA⁻¹ = CAA⁻¹ = C

Four-blocks technique: if A, B, C, D are r × r and A is undoable, the grid with rows of blocks (A, B), (C, D) has determinant det A · det(D − CA⁻¹B); and that is det(AD − CB) when AC = CA.

The 2 × 2 formula ad − bc does not carry over to blocks as det A · det D − det B · det C. Put the 2 × 2 grids 0, I, I, 0 in the four places. The grid swaps e₁ with e₃ and e₂ with e₄: two swaps, so its determinant is 1. The copied formula gives 0 − 1 = −1.

27k5A Staircase in Some Basis

Not every grid can be straightened out: the slide with rows (1, 1), (0, 1) cannot (27f3). But the slide is already a staircase. Over ℂ every grid can be brought that far.

Staircase-basis technique: for every n × n grid A with entries in ℂ there is an undoable P with P⁻¹AP a staircase. The stretches of A stand down its middle, each as often as its factor appears in det(A − λI).

Av_1 = \lambda_1v_1,\ v_1 \neq 0 @@ det(A − λI) has a root in ℂ (25d); the stretch-test technique (27f1)
P_1 = (v_1\ v_2\ \cdots\ v_n) \text{, undoable} @@ grow v₁ to a basis (27b3) and write it down as columns
P_1^{-1}AP_1e_1 = P_1^{-1}\lambda_1v_1 = \lambda_1e_1 @@ P₁e₁ = v₁
P_1^{-1}AP_1 = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1 & *\\ 0 & A_1\end{pmatrix} @@ its first column is λ₁e₁; A₁ is (n − 1) × (n − 1)
Q^{-1}A_1Q \text{ is a staircase} @@ the induction hypothesis, for size n − 1 (4f); size 1 is the base
P = P_1\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & Q\end{pmatrix}:\ P^{-1}AP = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1 & *\\ 0 & Q^{-1}A_1Q\end{pmatrix} @@ multiply the blocks (27d2)
\htmlClass{new}{P^{-1}AP = S \text{, a staircase}} \quad \blacksquare @@ zeros below the middle

The middle of S holds the stretches. By the det-product technique det(P⁻¹(A − λI)P) = det(A − λI), and P⁻¹(A − λI)P = S − λI is a staircase, so det(A − λI) = (s₁₁ − λ)⋯(sₙₙ − λ) by the stair-det technique. A real grid may have no real staircase: the quarter turn has no real stretch at all.

Stretches-of-f technique: if f is a polynomial and the stretches of A are λ₁, …, λₙ, each as often as its factor appears, then the stretches of f(A) are f(λ₁), …, f(λₙ).

(ST)_{ij} = \textstyle\sum_k s_{ik}t_{kj},\ \ s_{ik} = 0 \text{ for } k \lt i,\ t_{kj} = 0 \text{ for } k \gt j @@ S and T staircases
i \gt j:\ (ST)_{ij} = 0;\ \ (ST)_{ii} = s_{ii}t_{ii} @@ a term that is not 0 needs i ≤ k ≤ j
S^k \text{ has } \lambda_i^k \text{ down its middle} @@ induction on k (4f); sums of staircases add their middles
f(S) \text{ is a staircase, } f(\lambda_i) \text{ down its middle} @@ a polynomial is a sum of stretched powers
f(A) = Pf(S)P^{-1} @@ Aᵏ = PSᵏP⁻¹: the P⁻¹P in the middle cancel
\htmlClass{new}{\det(f(A) - \lambda I) = \textstyle\prod_i\big(f(\lambda_i) - \lambda\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ as for A and S above

The grid with rows (4, 1), (2, 3) stretches (1, 1) by 5 and (1, −2) by 2. So its square has the stretches 25 and 4, with no squaring of the grid. And a grid whose stretches are all 0 has a staircase with zeros down the middle, which fades out (27g5).

27k6The Size Reached, From Smaller Determinants

A square grid reaches everything exactly when its determinant is not 0 (27e4). For a grid of any shape, strike out some rows and some columns until a square k × k grid is left, and take its determinant. Those smaller determinants tell more.

Small-dets technique: the dimension of what a grid A reaches is the largest k for which some k × k grid left by striking rows and columns of A has a determinant that is not 0.

r \text{ columns of } A \text{ are free} @@ r is the dimension reached; drop spare columns (27b1). Call the grid of those columns B
r \text{ rows of } B \text{ are free} @@ the rows of B build dimension r too (27j5); drop spare rows
C = \text{those rows of } B:\ \det C \neq 0 @@ r × r with free rows: it reaches everything (27j5), so it is undoable (27c2, 27d3, 27e4)
\det C' \neq 0,\ C' \text{ a } k \times k \text{ grid inside } A @@ now the other way
\text{the } k \text{ columns of } C' \text{ are free} @@ the nonzero-det technique (27e4)
\text{the } k \text{ columns of } A \text{ holding them are free} @@ a mix of the long columns that is 0 is 0 in the kept rows too
\htmlClass{new}{k \le r \text{, and } k = r \text{ happens}} \quad \blacksquare @@ free vectors in a space of dimension r (27b2)

The grid with rows (1, 2, 3), (2, 4, 6), (1, 0, 1) has determinant 0, since row 2 is twice row 1. Strike row 2 and column 2: rows (1, 3), (1, 1) are left, with determinant −2. So the grid reaches a plane.

For finding r, row moves are far quicker. The worth of the technique is that each small determinant is a polynomial in the entries. Nudge the entries a little and a determinant that was not 0 stays not 0. So a small nudge can raise what a grid reaches, and can never lower it.

27k7Determinants With Letters Inside

The own-polynomial technique (27g3) took the determinant and the stand-in grid of A − λI, a grid whose entries hold a letter. Every technique for determinants was proved for grids of numbers. Do they hold for such a grid?

Letters-inside technique: the shuffle formula, det(AB) = det A · det B, and SA = (det A) I hold for square grids whose entries are polynomials, and for entries of any kind that add and multiply by the nine techniques (5d) with products that swap. Such a grid has an undo of its own kind exactly when its determinant has a reciprocal of that kind.

\det A = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}\sigma\ a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n} @@ only adding and multiplying: it has a meaning for entries of any such kind
F = \det(AB) - \det A\cdot\det B @@ a polynomial in the 2n² entries, taken as unknowns, with whole numbers in front
F = 0 \text{ whenever numbers are put in} @@ the det-product technique (27e3)
\text{one unknown: } F \text{ is the zero polynomial} @@ a polynomial of degree d ≥ 1 has at most d roots (25d)
\text{many unknowns: the same} @@ induction on the number of unknowns (4f): hold all but one, and each number in front is 0 at every choice of the rest
\htmlClass{new}{F = 0 \text{ as a polynomial}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so det(AB) = det A · det B with the entries as unknowns; and the same proof for SA = (det A) I, entry by entry

A sameness between polynomials with whole numbers in front stays true whatever is put in for the unknowns, as long as what is put in adds and multiplies by the nine techniques and its products swap. Only those techniques are used in multiplying out. So the two formulas hold for entries that are polynomials in λ, which is what 27g3 took on trust.

For the undo: if AB = I, then det A · det B = 1, so det A has a reciprocal. And if det A has a reciprocal, S times that reciprocal is the undo. Among the whole numbers only 1 and −1 have reciprocals. So a grid of whole numbers has an undo of whole numbers exactly when its determinant is 1 or −1. Rows (2, 1), (5, 3) have determinant 1, and the undo has rows (3, −1), (−5, 2). Rows (2, 0), (0, 1) have determinant 2, and the undo needs ½.

\det A = \textstyle\sum_j a_{1j}\det(\text{strike row } 1,\ \text{column } j)\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> the stand-ins carry the signs (−1)^(1 + j)
x_j = \det A_j/\det A \text{ when } \det A = 0\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-a">out of arrangement</span> there is no ratio; the system has no answer or many (27e4, 27j2)

The accepted names

Finding a determinant down the stairs is computing it by Gaussian elimination, or by LU decomposition; a grid with zeros below its middle is upper triangular. Expanding along a row or column is cofactor expansion, or Laplace expansion, and the six-term rule for 3 × 3 grids is the rule of Sarrus. The det-ratio technique is Cramer’s rule. The determinant in 27k.4.4 is the Vandermonde determinant. A grid with a block of zeros below its middle blocks is block triangular, and D − CA⁻¹B is the Schur complement of A. Bringing a grid to a staircase in some basis is triangularisation; with a basis of unit vectors square to each other it is the Schur form. The stretches-of-f technique is the spectral mapping property for polynomials. A determinant of a k × k grid inside A is a minor of A, and this count is the determinantal rank. Entries that add and multiply by the nine techniques with products that swap form a commutative ring (rung 44); a member with a reciprocal is a unit, and a whole-number grid with determinant 1 or −1 is unimodular.

Who said it first

Gabriel Cramer published the ratio rule in 1750, in a book on curves through given points; Colin Maclaurin had used it for two and three unknowns in a treatise printed in 1748. Pierre-Simon Laplace gave the expansion by smaller determinants in 1772. Pierre Frédéric Sarrus taught the six-term rule in the 1830s. Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde studied determinants as objects in their own right in 1772, though the determinant that bears his name does not appear in his papers.
