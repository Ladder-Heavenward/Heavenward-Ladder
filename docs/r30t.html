30t · technique

The Best Test

The step before this one built tests the way people first built them, by picking a sensible number from the data and asking how surprising it would be if the claim were true. Sensible is not the same as best. With the same data, and the same chance of wrongly rejecting a true claim, one test can catch a false claim far more often than another. This step finds the test that catches it most often. The answer is to set the claim beside its rival and ask, for the data in hand, how many times more likely the rival makes them than the claim. That ratio is rarely large when the claim is true, whatever the data. Rejecting the claim when the ratio is large is the best test there is, and the proof takes four lines. Then the step asks how much of the data a test really needs. Often one number, a count or an average, carries everything the data say about the unknown, and the rest can be thrown away without losing anything.

Weigh a claim against its rival by the ratio of the chances they give the data seen. If the claim is true, the ratio reaches twenty at most one time in twenty, and the same holds for every size. Rejecting the claim when the ratio is large beats every other test at the same level. A summary that the chances depend on only through it loses nothing, and every best test uses only it.

30t1Two Stories, One Ratio

A test of 30s tries a claim, H₀. Here the claim is set against one rival, H₁, what is believed instead if the claim falls. In this step each of them names one chance pattern in full: under H₀ the data x have chance (or density) f₀(x), under H₁ they have f₁(x). 14·stat and 30r read the chance of exactly what was seen, as a function of the knob, as the likelihood. Here the knob has only two settings, and the natural thing to compare is their ratio.

The likelihood ratio of the data is Λ(x) = f₁(x)/f₀(x), wherever f₀(x) > 0. When the data are n readings, independent under each story, each story’s chance is a product, so Λ is a product of one ratio per reading, and log Λ is a sum (17c). Large Λ means the data fit H₁ better than H₀. The worry is that large ratios might turn up often by luck even when H₀ is true. They cannot.

Ratio-bound technique: if H₀ is true, the chance that the likelihood ratio reaches k is at most 1/k, for every k > 0.

\Lambda(x) = f_1(x)/f_0(x) \ge 0 \text{ where } f_0(x) > 0 @@ the likelihood ratio, a chance variable under H₀
E_0[\Lambda] = \textstyle\int_{f_0 > 0} \tfrac{f_1}{f_0}\,f_0 = \int_{f_0 > 0} f_1 @@ the average as an integral (30g), by a density (30m); a sum when the data are counts
\textstyle\int_{f_0 > 0} f_1 \le \int f_1 = 1 @@ f₁ is a chance pattern: never negative, total 1
P_0(\Lambda \ge k) \le E_0[\Lambda]/k @@ the stray-bound technique (30h), since Λ ≥ 0
\htmlClass{new}{P_0(\Lambda \ge k) \le 1/k} \quad \blacksquare @@ E₀[Λ] ≤ 1
  
H_0:\ \theta = 0.5,\ \ H_1:\ \theta = 0.7;\ \ 13 \text{ of } 20 \text{ fish clipped} @@ θ the share of hatchery fish, marked by a clipped fin, in a run of coho counted at the fish ladder
\Lambda = \big(\tfrac{0.7}{0.5}\big)^{13}\big(\tfrac{0.3}{0.5}\big)^{7} @@ one ratio per fish, multiplied (30f, independent fish)
\log\Lambda = 13\log 1.4 + 7\log 0.6 \approx 4.374 - 3.576 = 0.798 @@ the log-of-a-product technique (17c)
\Lambda \approx 2.22 @@ the data fit 0.7 a little more than twice as well as 0.5
\htmlClass{new}{P_0(\Lambda \ge 2.22) \le 0.45} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ratio-bound technique: a ratio this size is common under H₀

A ratio of 2 is weak. The ratio-bound technique says what a strong one means: a ratio of 20 turns up less than one time in twenty when H₀ is true, and a ratio of 1000 less than one time in a thousand, whatever the claim and rival are and however the data were made. Notice what the ratio does not say. It does not give the chance that H₁ is true. By the odds-update technique of 30l, the ratio multiplies the odds of H₁ held before the data, and those odds have to come from somewhere: that is 30u.

30t2The Most Powerful Test

A test is a technique φ that looks at the data and rejects H₀ (φ(x) = 1) or keeps it (φ(x) = 0). As in 30s, its chance of an error of the first kind is P₀(φ = 1), and its power is P₁(φ = 1), one less the chance of an error of the second kind. Fix the level α in advance. Among all tests whose chance of an error of the first kind is at most α, which has the greatest power? This φ (little phi) is not 2e's condition.

Best-test technique: let φ* reject H₀ when Λ(x) > k and keep it when Λ(x) < k, for some k ≥ 0, and let α = P₀(φ* = 1). Then every test φ with P₀(φ = 1) ≤ α has P₁(φ = 1) ≤ P₁(φ* = 1). Where Λ(x) = k exactly, φ* may do either; for readings with a density this has chance 0.

(\varphi^*(x) - \varphi(x))(f_1(x) - k f_0(x)) \ge 0 \text{ for every } x @@ where f₁ > kf₀, φ* = 1 ≥ φ; where f₁ < kf₀, φ* = 0 ≤ φ; where equal, 0
\textstyle\int (\varphi^* - \varphi)(f_1 - k f_0) \ge 0 @@ the integral of a function never negative (30c)
\textstyle\int \varphi^* f_1 - \int \varphi f_1 \ge k\big(\int \varphi^* f_0 - \int \varphi f_0\big) @@ the average-adds technique (30g), integrals split
k\big(P_0(\varphi^* = 1) - P_0(\varphi = 1)\big) = k\big(\alpha - P_0(\varphi = 1)\big) \ge 0 @@ ∫φf₀ is P₀(φ = 1); k ≥ 0
\htmlClass{new}{P_1(\varphi^* = 1) \ge P_1(\varphi = 1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∫φf₁ is P₁(φ = 1)
  
X_1, \dots, X_{25} \text{ bell-shaped, spread width } 25\text{ g};\ \ H_0:\ \mu = 200,\ \ H_1:\ \mu = 210 @@ a packer’s claim about the average weight of its Honeycrisp apples, tested against a heavier story
\log\Lambda = \tfrac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sigma^2}\textstyle\sum x_i - \tfrac{n(\mu_1^2 - \mu_0^2)}{2\sigma^2} @@ the log of a product of bell densities (30j); the squares in xᵢ cancel
\Lambda > k \iff \bar x > c @@ μ₁ > μ₀, so log Λ climbs with x̄
P_0(\bar x > c) = 0.05:\ \ c = 200 + 1.645 \cdot \tfrac{25}{\sqrt{25}} \approx 208.2 @@ under H₀, x̄ is a bell (30j, average-functions multiply) of spread width σ/√n (30q1)
P_1(\bar x > 208.2) = P\big(Z > \tfrac{208.2 - 210}{5}\big) \approx 0.64 @@ the power, Z a standard bell
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same test is best against every } \mu_1 > 200} \quad \blacksquare @@ c does not depend on μ₁

The last line is worth a second look. The best test of 200 against 210 never used the number 210, only that it was bigger. So one test, reject when x̄ > 208.2, is the best against every story above 200 at once. It is the one-sided form of the bell test of 30s, and now it is proved best rather than chosen for being sensible. It is not best against a story below 200: there the best test rejects when x̄ is small.

30t3Summaries That Lose Nothing

Twenty fish come with an order: clipped, clipped, wild, clipped, and so on. Does the order say anything about the share θ? It cannot, if the fish are independent with the same chance. Only the count seems to matter. Call a summary T of the data a full summary when, once T(x) = t is known, the given chance of the data is the same under every θ (30f). Then the rest of the data, beyond T, is a draw that θ has no say in.

Summary-factor technique: for data with chances f(x; θ), T is a full summary exactly when f(x; θ) = g(T(x), θ) · h(x), with g depending on the data only through T and h not depending on θ.

P_\theta(X = x \mid T = t) = \tfrac{f(x;\theta)}{\sum_{T(y) = t} f(y;\theta)} \text{ for } T(x) = t @@ the given-chance technique (30f): X = x already makes T = t
\text{if } f = g(T, \theta)\,h: \ \ \tfrac{g(t,\theta)\,h(x)}{g(t,\theta)\sum_{T(y)=t} h(y)} = \tfrac{h(x)}{\sum_{T(y)=t} h(y)} @@ g(t, θ) is the same for every y with T(y) = t, and cancels
\text{so } T \text{ is a full summary} @@ the given chance is free of θ
\text{if } T \text{ is full: } f(x;\theta) = P_\theta(T = t) \cdot P(X = x \mid T = t) @@ the given-chance technique, turned round; the second factor is free of θ
\htmlClass{new}{f(x;\theta) = g(T(x),\theta)\,h(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ g(t, θ) = P_θ(T = t), h(x) = P(X = x | T = T(x))
  
f(x;\mu) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\textstyle\sum (x_i - \mu)^2\big) @@ n independent bell readings, σ known, μ unknown
\textstyle\sum (x_i - \mu)^2 = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2 + n(\bar x - \mu)^2 @@ the squared-misses technique (8·stat)
f = \underbrace{e^{-n(\bar x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}_{g(\bar x,\,\mu)} \cdot \underbrace{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} e^{-\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}_{h(x)} @@ the exp of a sum is a product
\htmlClass{new}{\bar x \text{ is a full summary for } \mu} \quad \blacksquare @@ the summary-factor technique, with densities in place of chances

Now look back. The likelihood ratio is g(T, θ₁)/g(T, θ₀): h cancels. So the best test uses the data only through a full summary. The best guess of 30r makes g(T, θ) greatest, so it too depends only on T. A full summary is not a loss of information but a proof that the rest was never information about θ at all. It is information about the model, though: whether the fish really are independent can only be checked from the order that the count throws away.

30t.4.5seamIn 1925 a statistician at a farm research station north of London printed tables for testing, with one in twenty as a handy line, and read a small chance of the data under H₀ as evidence against it, to be weighed with everything else known. In 1933 two others, one in London and one in Warsaw, recast a test as a technique for deciding: fix the false alarm chance in advance, then take the test of greatest power, and they proved the best-test technique. The first answered, for thirty years, that science is not a factory, and that a scientist judging one experiment is not choosing a technique for a thousand.

The accepted names

H₀ is the null hypothesis and H₁ the alternative; a claim or rival that names one chance pattern in full is a simple hypothesis. Λ is the likelihood ratio; the ratio-bound technique is sometimes called the universal bound. A test is φ, a test function; φ* is the likelihood ratio test, and the best-test technique is the Neyman–Pearson lemma. A test that is best against every story of a family at once is uniformly most powerful; it exists when the ratio climbs with one number, a monotone likelihood ratio. A full summary is a sufficient statistic, and the summary-factor technique is the factorization theorem (Fisher–Neyman). The ratio with the most likely parameter put in, as in 30t.4.4, is the generalized likelihood ratio; for large samples 2 log Λ is spread like χ² (30s), Wilks’s theorem, whose proof waits much higher up the ladder.

Who said it first

Ronald Fisher defined sufficiency in 1922, in the paper that also named likelihood, and gave the factorization; Jerzy Neyman proved it in general in 1935. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson proved the lemma in 1933. Samuel Wilks found the large-sample law of the ratio in 1938. Richard Royall stressed the ratio-bound in 1997 as the reason a likelihood ratio can be read as evidence on its own.

In 30t.4.5, the statistician of 1925 is Ronald Fisher, at Rothamsted, in Statistical Methods for Research Workers; the two of 1933 are Egon Pearson, at University College London, and Jerzy Neyman, then in Warsaw, who joined him there in 1934. Fisher’s sharpest reply was his paper of 1955, Statistical methods and scientific induction.
