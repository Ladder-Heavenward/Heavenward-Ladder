Why Is Changing an Orbit’s Plane So Costly?

Why is changing an orbit’s plane so costly? A satellite in a low orbit can climb thousands of kilometres for less fuel than it takes to tilt its orbit by a few degrees. Launch sites near the equator charge more, and are worth it. This page sets out an orbit’s plane and tilt, finds the cost of turning a speed through an angle with 21’s sines, prices a 10° turn in low orbit, finds why a turn is best done where the craft is slow, and why rockets launch east from near the equator.

To turn a speed v through an angle Δ without changing its size, the push is the third side of an isosceles triangle: 2v sin(Δ/2). It grows with v, so turn where the craft is slowest, and combine the turn with another push to save more. A rocket launched due east from latitude λ starts in a plane tilted λ, with the earth’s own spin as a free push.

25nav1An Orbit’s Plane and Its Tilt

Terry plans satellite launches. An orbit about the earth lies in a flat plane through the earth’s centre (18·phys). Its tilt is the angle between that plane and the equator’s.

From here on, on this page, a craft launched due east from latitude λ starts in an orbit tilted λ, since its plane must pass through both the earth’s centre and the launch site. A satellite meant to hang over the equator needs tilt 0.

25nav2Turning a Speed Costs Twice the Speed Times the Sine of Half the Angle

To change the plane, the craft must turn its speed arrow through the angle Δ between the planes, keeping its size v. The push is the arrow from the old speed to the new (21·phys).

\text{old and new speed: two sides of length } v, \text{ angle } \Delta \text{ between} @@ an isosceles triangle
\text{split it in half: each half has } v\sin(\Delta/2) \text{ opposite } \Delta/2 @@ sin as a side (21h)
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta v = 2v\sin\dfrac{\Delta}{2}} @@ the third side

Plane-turn technique: turning a craft’s speed v through Δ costs a push of 2v sin(Δ/2), so the cost grows with the speed at which the turn is made.

25nav3At Low Orbit, a 10° Turn Costs About 1.3 km/s

\Delta = 10°:\ 2 \times 7.7 \times \sin 5° \approx 1.34 \text{ km/s} @@ 
\Delta = 28.5°:\ 2 \times 7.7 \times \sin 14.25° \approx 3.79 \text{ km/s} @@ about half of what it took to reach orbit
\htmlClass{new}{\text{climbing from 300 km to 1\,000 km costs about } 0.4 \text{ km/s}} @@ two pushes, as 21·nav2’s transfer

A tilt of 10° costs over three times what climbing 700 km does. That is why satellites are launched as near as possible to the plane they will work in.

25nav4Turn Where the Craft Is Slow

A satellite bound for the equator’s plane at 35,786 km goes first onto a long ellipse, from low orbit up to that height. At the far end of the ellipse it moves only about 1.6 km/s; there it must speed up to 3.07 km/s anyway. Turning and speeding up in one push makes one triangle, with the cosine-side technique (21h).

\text{separately: speed up } 1.47 + \text{turn } 2 \times 3.07 \times \sin 14.25° \approx 1.47 + 1.51 = 2.98 \text{ km/s} @@ 
\text{together: } \sqrt{1.6^2 + 3.07^2 - 2 \times 1.6 \times 3.07\cos 28.5°} \approx 1.83 \text{ km/s} @@ the cosine-side technique (21h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{from a site at } 5°:\ \sqrt{1.6^2 + 3.07^2 - 2 \times 1.6 \times 3.07\cos 5°} \approx 1.48 \text{ km/s}} @@

Make the turn where the craft is slowest, and fold it into a push that must be made anyway; the triangle’s third side is shorter than the two pushes added. Flight controllers plan every such push this way, and the craft’s tracking afterwards shows it in the new plane at the new speed.

25nav5Launch East, Near the Equator

The ground turns east under a rocket before it lifts off. At latitude λ the ground moves 465 cos λ metres a second (21b). Launching east takes that speed for free, and a site near the equator both gets the most of it and starts at the smallest tilt.

site latitudefree speed easttilt to remove for the equator’s planelast push at the far end
5°463 m/s5°about 1.48 km/s
28.5°409 m/s28.5°about 1.83 km/s

A launch site near the equator saves fuel twice: once from the ground’s faster spin, and again from the smaller tilt to remove. A satellite operator choosing a launch weighs this saving, which can mean years more fuel for keeping station, against the launch price.

Accepted names

The tilt is the orbit’s inclination; a turn alone is a simple plane change, and one folded into a speed change is a combined plane change. The long ellipse is a geostationary transfer orbit, and its far end the apogee.

Who said it first

The plane-change push is worked out in every first course on orbits. The European launch site at Kourou in French Guiana lies at about 5° north; Cape Canaveral in Florida at about 28.5° north.
