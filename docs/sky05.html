Why Does a Crystal Hold Heat the Way It Does?

Why does a crystal hold heat the way it does? In 1819 two chemists in Paris found that a mole of almost any solid element takes the same heat to warm by one degree, about twenty five joules. Rung thirty found the reason: each atom sits on three springs, and each spring takes the same share of the warmth. But diamond takes a quarter of that, and every solid takes less and less as it is cooled, until near the coldest temperatures the heat falls by a factor of eight each time the temperature is halved. A single frozen spring, as rung thirty froze nitrogen, falls far faster than that. The springs of a crystal are not separate. They are joined, atom to atom, and what shakes is not one atom but a wave running through all of them. This page builds those waves on a ring of atoms, finds the shortest one a lattice allows, counts the steps of energy each wave takes, and from the count gets the fall by eight, the level at the top, and the reason diamond and lead sit at opposite ends.

Atoms of mass m joined by springs of stiffness κ, a distance a apart, carry waves with ω = 2√(κ/m) |sin(ka/2)|. No wave is shorter than 2a; long waves run at the speed of sound v = a√(κ/m). Each wave is a spring, so its energy comes in steps ħω, and at temperature T it holds on average 1/(e^(ħω/kT) − 1) steps. Slow waves never freeze, and their share of all the waves is (T/θ)³. The exact count gives a heat of (12π⁴/5)R(T/θ)³ when cold and 3R when warm, with θ = (ħv/k)(6π²n)^(1/3) read from the speed of sound: about 2230 K for diamond and about 100 K for lead.

sky5.1Atoms on Springs

Take a ring of N atoms, each of mass m, a distance a apart, each joined to its two neighbours by a spring of stiffness κ. Let xⱼ be how far atom j has moved from its place. The spring on its right pulls with κ(xⱼ₊₁ − xⱼ) and the spring on its left with −κ(xⱼ − xⱼ₋₁), so m xⱼ″ = −(Kx)ⱼ with (Kx)ⱼ = κ(2xⱼ − xⱼ₊₁ − xⱼ₋₁). That rule is 42·phys.2’s hopping rule with E₀ = 2κ and t = κ, and the same at every site.

Chain-wave technique: a ring of N atoms of mass m joined by springs κ moves as a sum of N steady swings, one for each hour m of the clock of N, in which atom j moves as cos(2πmj/N) times a swing of turn rate ω_m = 2√(κ/m) |sin(πm/N)|.

K = 2\kappa I - \kappa(S + S^{-1}) @@ the slide S of 42·phys.1, one site each way
K c = \kappa\,(2 - z - z^{-1})\,c \text{ for } c_j = z^{j},\ z = e^{2\pi i m/N} @@ the wrap-round technique: c is stretched by S and by S⁻¹
2 - 2\cos(2\pi m/N) = 4\sin^2(\pi m/N) @@ a turning plus its mirror, then the half-angle (21)
m\,x'' = -Kx \text{ with } N \text{ free arrows, stretches } 4\kappa\sin^2(\pi m/N) @@ the normal-mode technique (27·then.1); the hours m and −m pair into real waves
\htmlClass{new}{\omega_m = 2\sqrt{\kappa/m}\,\lvert\sin(\pi m/N)\rvert} \quad \blacksquare @@ one steady swing per hour

On a ring of six, in units of √(κ/m), the six turn rates are 0, 1, 1, √3, √3 and 2. The rate 0 is the whole ring sliding round, which costs nothing. The rate 2 is each atom moving against both its neighbours. Nothing in between is allowed, and nothing above.

sky5.2The Shortest Wave

In length units the wave of hour m has k = 2πm/(Na), and 42·phys.1 found that k and k + 2π/a are one wave at the atoms. So every wave has a k between −π/a and π/a, and ω(k) = 2√(κ/m) |sin(ka/2)|.

Shortest-wave technique: a lattice of spacing a carries no wave shorter than 2a. Long waves run at v = a√(κ/m), whatever their length; the top turn rate is 2v/a, and the shortest wave does not run at all.

\lvert k\rvert \le \pi/a \Rightarrow \lambda = 2\pi/\lvert k\rvert \ge 2a @@ at k = π/a each atom moves against its neighbours
\sin(ka/2) \approx ka/2 \text{ for small } k \Rightarrow \omega \approx a\sqrt{\kappa/m}\,\lvert k\rvert @@ the slope of sin at 0 is 1 (21)
\omega_{\text{top}} = 2\sqrt{\kappa/m} = 2v/a @@ at k = π/a, where sin is 1
\tfrac{d\omega}{dk} = a\sqrt{\kappa/m}\,\cos(ka/2) = 0 \text{ at } k = \pi/a @@ the group speed of 32·phys
\htmlClass{new}{v = a\sqrt{\kappa/m},\ \ f_{\text{top}} = v/(\pi a)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ω = 2πf

Diamond: carbon atoms 0.154 nm apart, of mass 1.99 × 10⁻²⁶ kg, and sound runs through it at about 18 km/s. Then κ = mv²/a² ≈ 270 newtons per metre and f_top = v/(πa) ≈ 37 THz. Lead: atoms 0.350 nm apart, 3.44 × 10⁻²⁵ kg, sound at about 2.16 km/s, so κ ≈ 13 newtons per metre and f_top ≈ 2.0 THz. The highest shakes measured in the two crystals are near 40 THz and near 2.2 THz. A ring is a rough picture of a crystal, and it gets the size right: light atoms on stiff springs shake nearly twenty times faster than heavy atoms on soft ones.

sky5.3A Wave Takes Energy in Steps

Each steady swing of the chain-wave technique is a spring of its own, of turn rate ω. 35·phys.5’s ladder technique gives its energies (n + ½)ħω, for n = 0, 1, 2, …. The wave can take energy only a whole step ħω at a time. Count the steps, and n is the number of the wave’s phonons, the accepted name for one step of a lattice wave.

Counted-step technique: at temperature T, a lattice wave of turn rate ω holds on average 1/(e^(ħω/kT) − 1) steps above its lowest rung, and so ħω/(e^(ħω/kT) − 1) of energy.

E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega,\ \ n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots @@ the ladder technique
\text{share of } n \propto e^{-n\hbar\omega/kT} @@ the energy-share technique (30·phys.1); the ½ is common to all and cancels
\bar n = \tfrac{\sum n x^n}{\sum x^n} = \tfrac{x}{1 - x},\ \ x = e^{-\hbar\omega/kT} @@ geometric sums (22e), and x times their slope
\htmlClass{new}{\bar n = \tfrac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 17·phys’s lumpy average, divided by ħω

It is 38·phys.4’s share for particles of the sign +1, with μ = 0: steps are made and lost freely, so nothing keeps their number. At 293 K, kT/h = 6.1 THz. Diamond’s top wave at 37 THz has ħω/kT = 6.1 and holds 0.0023 steps: asleep. Lead’s top wave at 2.0 THz has ħω/kT = 0.32 and holds 2.6, with energy close to kT. One crystal is awake at room temperature and the other is mostly not.

sky5.4Slow Waves Never Freeze

30·phys.5 froze each atom’s springs alone, all at one turn rate. Fit that rate to copper’s heat near room temperature, about 240 K in units of k, and at 10 K it gives 3R x²e^(−x) with x = 24: 5 × 10⁻⁷ joules per mole per kelvin. Copper measures 0.055, a hundred thousand times more. A wave of one rate freezes completely. A crystal has waves of every rate down to 0, and the slow ones never freeze.

Awake-share technique: in a crystal of N atoms in which every wave runs at one speed v, the waves of turn rate below ω are a share (ω/ω_D)³ of all 3N, where ω_D = v k_D and k_D³ = 6π²n for n atoms per unit volume.

k = \tfrac{2\pi}{L}(m_1, m_2, m_3) @@ a cube of side L wrapped round, as 42·phys.4
\text{three waves per } k \text{: one along, two across} @@ each atom moves in three directions
\#\{\lvert k\rvert < K\} = 3 \cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi K^3 \big/ (2\pi/L)^3 @@ one k per box; a ball, as the ball-of-states technique
\text{cut at } K = k_D \text{ with total } 3N:\ \ k_D^3 = 6\pi^2 n @@ the shortest-wave technique: no more waves than moves
\htmlClass{new}{\#\{\omega' < \omega\} = 3N(\omega/\omega_D)^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ ω = v|k|, so the count grows as the cube

Write θ = ħω_D/k. The waves with ħω below kT are a share (T/θ)³, each holds about kT by the half-kT technique, and the rest are nearly asleep. So the energy above the coldest grows about as 3NkT(T/θ)³, as T⁴, and the heat as T³: halve the temperature and the heat falls by eight. The count is rough. A wave at 2kT is not asleep; the next footing counts every wave with its true share.

sky5.5The Cube Law, Exactly

Give each wave its true share by the counted-step technique, and add. By the awake-share technique there are 9Nω²/ω_D³ waves per unit of turn rate. Put x = ħω/kT.

Cube-law technique: a crystal of N atoms whose waves all run at one speed has energy 9NkT (T/θ)³ ∫₀^(θ/T) x³/(eˣ − 1) dx above its coldest. Far below θ its heat is (12π⁴/5) Nk (T/θ)³; far above θ it is 3Nk.

U = \int_0^{\omega_D} \tfrac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3}\,\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}\,d\omega = 9NkT\,(T/\theta)^3\!\int_0^{\theta/T}\!\tfrac{x^3\,dx}{e^x - 1} @@ the awake-share and counted-step techniques; ω = kTx/ħ
T \ll \theta:\ \ \int_0^\infty \tfrac{x^3}{e^x - 1}dx = \textstyle\sum_{n \ge 1}\int_0^\infty x^3e^{-nx}dx = \sum 6/n^4 @@ 17·phys’s I; 1/(eˣ − 1) as a geometric sum, and parts three times
\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^4dx = \tfrac{\pi^4}{9} + \textstyle\sum_{n \ne 0}\tfrac{4}{n^4} \Rightarrow \sum_{n \ge 1}\tfrac{1}{n^4} = \tfrac{\pi^4}{90} @@ the energy-kept technique for x², whose coefficients are π²/3 and 2(−1)ⁿ/n²
U = \tfrac{3\pi^4}{5}NkT^4/\theta^3,\ \ C = \tfrac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk\,(T/\theta)^3 @@ I = π⁴/15 ≈ 6.494; the slope along T
T \gg \theta:\ \ \tfrac{x^3}{e^x - 1} \approx x^2,\ \ \int_0^{\theta/T}x^2dx = \tfrac13(\theta/T)^3 \Rightarrow U = 3NkT @@ eˣ − 1 ≈ x for small x
\htmlClass{new}{C \approx 1944\,(T/\theta)^3 \text{ J/(mol K) cold};\ \ C \to 3R = 24.9 \text{ warm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ Nk = R for a mole

17·phys squeezed I to 6.494 and left its exact value for later; it is π⁴/15. The cold law is the rough count of sky5.4 with a factor near 20 more: waves a few times kT hold a good share. For copper, θ = 343 K, and at 10 K the law gives 0.048 joules per mole per kelvin. The electrons of 42·phys.9 add about 0.007 more, and the two make the 0.055 that is measured. The law of the formula itself holds within 1% up to about θ/12.

sky5.6Diamond Against Lead

θ comes from the speed of sound and the count of atoms, both measured with no heat at all. A crystal carries one wave along and two across, at different speeds v_L and v_T; the count of the awake-share technique then uses the v̄ with 3/v̄³ = 1/v_L³ + 2/v_T³.

Sound-to-heat technique: θ = (ħv̄/k)(6π²n)^(1/3), where 3/v̄³ = 1/v_L³ + 2/v_T³ and n is the number of atoms per unit volume. It is the awake-share technique with each of the three waves counted at its own speed.

\text{diamond: } n = \tfrac{3515}{0.012011}\times 6.022\times 10^{23} = 1.76\times 10^{29}\ \text{m}^{-3} @@ density over the mass of a mole
v_L \approx 18.0,\ v_T \approx 12.3\ \text{km/s} \Rightarrow \bar v = 13.4\ \text{km/s},\ \ \theta \approx 2240\ \text{K} @@ the sound-to-heat technique; measured from the cold heat: 2230 K
\text{lead: } n = 3.30\times 10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3},\ \ v_L \approx 2.16,\ v_T \approx 0.70\ \text{km/s} \Rightarrow \theta \approx 76\ \text{K} @@ the slow waves across dominate v̄
\htmlClass{new}{T = 293\text{ K}:\ \ T/\theta = 0.13 \text{ for diamond},\ \ \approx 3 \text{ for lead}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one far below its θ, the other far above

Lead at room temperature is far above its θ, about 105 K from its cold heat, and the cube-law technique gives 24.8, near 3R. Lead measures 26.4: its springs are not quite springs, and a solid warmed at fixed pressure also pays for swelling. Diamond is far below its θ, and the technique gives 3.9 against the 6.1 measured. Asked to fit 6.1, it wants θ = 1830 K, not 2230. The flaw is the straight line ω = v|k|. The chain-wave technique bends below it: with the same speed of sound, the chain tops out at 2v/a and the straight line at πv/a, so the real crystal has more slow waves than the count allows, and more heat. θ read at one temperature is not θ read at another. In lead, the speed across changes with direction and with cooling, and the gap between 76 K and 105 K is that rough.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞5.oq.1Why does glass hold too much heat when cold? Below about 1 K a glass takes heat in proportion to T, not T³, and near 10 K it holds more than the cube-law count of its sound speeds allows. In 1972 it was proposed that some atoms or groups of atoms in a glass can sit in either of two places and tunnel between them, a two-state body of 30·phys.2 with every possible gap. The picture fits many measurements. What tunnels, in silica or in a plastic, is not known, and why the size of the effect is so nearly the same in glasses of every kind is still argued.
∞5.oq.2How well can a crystal carry heat, at best? An electrical insulator carries heat by its waves alone. Diamond leads at room temperature, about 2000 watts per metre per kelvin, and more when made of one isotope only. In 2013 a calculation predicted that boron arsenide would come close, and in 2018 three groups measured between about 1000 and 1300. Can any crystal beat diamond at room temperature, and is there a bound that says how far? The waves knock each other because the springs are not exact springs, and how strongly they knock is computed from the atoms, at great cost, and argued wherever the computation and the measurement part.
∞5.oq.3Is there a floor to how badly a crystal can carry heat? It was argued in 1992 that a solid cannot carry heat worse than if every wave were knocked within one wavelength. In 2007 thin films of stacked sheets, each in order and each turned at random against the next, came in several times below that floor. Whether the floor is wrong, or those films are not the kind of solid it speaks of, is argued, and the question matters for turning waste heat into current.
∞5.oq.4When does heat in a crystal move as a wave? Usually it creeps, as the heat in the ground of 33·then does, because the steps knock each other at random. If most knocks keep the total momentum of the steps, warmth can travel as a wave of its own. That was seen in a few very pure crystals near a few kelvin from the 1960s, and in graphite near 100 K in 2019 and near 200 K in 2022. Can it happen in some solid at room temperature, and would it be of any use?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

1819 is where the story starts. Two chemists in Paris measured the heat of thirteen solid elements and found that the heat of a mole was nearly the same for all of them. The rule was used to fix the weights of atoms, which were then in dispute, and for a while it was trusted more than the weights. The exceptions were known early: carbon, boron and silicon took far less heat than the rule. In 1875 a measurement in Germany found that diamond’s heat rose steeply with temperature, more than tenfold between −50 °C and 1000 °C, so the exception was not a fixed number but a temperature effect. Kinetic theory gave the 3R in the 1870s and had nothing to offer for the fall.

In 1907 the fall was read as a sign of quanta: a solid of atoms on springs, each with energy in steps, all of one frequency. It was the first time the steps of light from 1900 were used for matter, and many physicists took it as a curiosity. It fitted diamond fairly well. Then the cold measurements of 1910 to 1912 in Berlin, made possible by liquid hydrogen, showed the heat falling more slowly than one frequency allowed. The Berlin group, which championed the quanta, at first patched the fit with two frequencies.

In 1912 two answers came in the same year. One, from Zurich, treated the crystal as a block of jelly carrying sound, cut off at the right number of waves: the count of this page, with the T³ law. The other, from Göttingen, solved the lattice of atoms itself, as the ring of this page does, and was more exact but much harder to use. The jelly won the textbooks for a generation because it gave one number per crystal and a law that could be checked. The Göttingen side argued that the one number was a fiction, and in the 1930s careful cold measurements and lattice sums proved them partly right: θ read from the heat changes with temperature by a tenth or more, as diamond’s 2230 K against 1830 K shows here.

Were the steps real, or only bookkeeping? The name for a step, made by analogy with the steps of light, came into use in the early 1930s, and for some years it was a manner of speaking. In the 1950s neutrons from research reactors, among them the reactor at Chalk River in Ontario, were bounced off crystals, and each neutron was seen to give or take one step of a definite k and ω. The bent curve of the chain-wave technique was measured point by point. That work was recognised with a Nobel prize in 1994, almost forty years after it was done.

What is argued now is less the heat a crystal holds than how it carries heat: in glasses, where the waves of this page are not the whole story, in films a few atoms thick, and in materials meant to turn waste heat into current, where carrying heat badly is the goal. Since the 1990s the waves of real crystals have been computed from the atoms with no fitted numbers, and they mostly agree with the neutrons. Where they do not, it is not always clear which side is wrong.

The accepted names

The 3R rule is the law of Dulong and Petit. The chain-wave technique gives the dispersion relation of a monatomic chain, and the hours of k from −π/a to π/a fill the first Brillouin zone. The step of a lattice wave is a phonon, and the counted-step technique gives the Bose–Einstein distribution with zero chemical potential, or the Planck distribution. The frozen single rate of 30·phys.5 is the Einstein model of a solid, and the count of this page is the Debye model: θ is the Debye temperature, ω_D the Debye frequency, k_D the Debye wave number, and the cold law C ∝ T³ is the Debye T³ law. The waves along and across are longitudinal and transverse acoustic phonons, and v̄ is the Debye average sound velocity. The exact value I = π⁴/15 is 6 ζ(4), from Euler’s value ζ(4) = π⁴/90, here by Parseval’s theorem. The heat in proportion to T of a glass, and the picture of atoms tunnelling between two places, are the two-level systems of a glass.

Who said it first

Pierre Louis Dulong and Alexis Thérèse Petit gave the rule in 1819. Heinrich Friedrich Weber measured diamond, boron and silicon in 1872 to 1875. Albert Einstein gave the quantum solid of one frequency in 1907. Walther Nernst and his group in Berlin measured heats down to liquid hydrogen temperatures from 1910, and Nernst and Frederick Lindemann proposed a fit with two frequencies in 1911. Peter Debye gave the count of sound waves in 1912, and Max Born and Theodore von Kármán the lattice itself the same year, with the wrap-round of 42·phys. Moses Blackman showed in the 1930s that θ changes with temperature. Igor Tamm treated the steps of sound as particles in 1930, and the word phonon is usually traced to Yakov Frenkel in 1932. Bertram Brockhouse measured the curves with neutrons at Chalk River from the 1950s, and shared the Nobel prize in 1994. The tunnelling picture of glass is from Philip Anderson, Bertrand Halperin and Chandra Varma, and separately William Phillips, both 1972, after measurements by Robert Zeller and Robert Pohl in 1971. The figures for sound speeds and θ here are handbook values, averaged over direction.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each atom has three springs, each spring holds kT, so a mole of any solid holds 3RT at every temperature.” Not allowed: the half-kT technique holds only where each spring can take any energy. The springs take energy in steps, and a wave whose step is far above kT holds almost nothing. The rule is right far above θ and wrong below it.
