17·crypt · cryptography

How long would it take to guess a password?

How long would it take to guess a password? A password is a short secret, and a machine that wants it can simply try one guess after another. So the question comes down to two counts. How many passwords are there of the kind you chose? And how many guesses a second can the machine make? The first count grows so fast that its numbers stop meaning anything when they are written out in full. Logs bring them back to a size a person can read and compare. Sites used to tell people to add capitals, digits and odd symbols, and people did, and their passwords fell anyway. This page counts the passwords, measures the count in bits, which are logs base two, and shows why adding letters beats adding odd symbols. Then it turns bits into years at real guessing speeds. And it finds where the count stops telling the truth: people do not choose at random.

A password of a given length from a given set of symbols is one of the set's size to the power of the length. Its bits are the log base two of that count, which is the length times the bits of one symbol. One more symbol adds a whole symbol's worth of bits. A bigger set of symbols adds only a little to each. On average a guesser finds a randomly chosen password after trying half of them, and every ten bits multiply the time by about a thousand. All of this measures the way a password was chosen. A password a person made up is guessed in the order people make them up, and its bits are far fewer than its length suggests.

Counting the passwords

From here on, on this page, an alphabet is the set of symbols a password may use, and A is the number of its members. A password of length L is a list of L symbols from it: a function from L to the alphabet, as 10·crypt's texts were. By counted-functions (4k) there are A^L of them.

The common alphabets: 26 lowercase letters; 52 with capitals; 62 with digits too; 95 for every symbol a keyboard types, space included. A word list for dice has 7776 = 6⁵ words, one for each throw of five dice, and a passphrase is a list of words from it.

26^8 \approx 2.09 \times 10^{11} @@ 8 lowercase letters; counted-functions, in scientific form (8·phys)
95^8 \approx 6.63 \times 10^{15} @@ 8 symbols from the whole keyboard
26^{12} \approx \htmlClass{new}{9.54 \times 10^{16}} @@ 12 lowercase letters: more than the 8 from the whole keyboard

Bits

From here on, on this page, the bits of a way of choosing among N passwords, each equally likely, are log₂ N. One bit is one fair coin toss: N = 2^b.

Bits technique: the bits of a password chosen at random are its length times the bits of one symbol. Precisely: log₂(A^L) = L log₂ A.

\log_2 (A^L) = \tfrac{\ln (A^L)}{\ln 2} @@ log-base-p (17g)
= \tfrac{L \ln A}{\ln 2} @@ log-of-a-power (17c), L whole
= \htmlClass{new}{L \log_2 A} \quad \blacksquare @@ log-base-p again
\log_2 26 = \tfrac{\ln 26}{\ln 2} \approx \tfrac{3.258}{0.693} \approx 4.70 @@ change-of-base (17h)
\log_2 95 \approx 6.57,\ \ \log_2 62 \approx 5.95,\ \ \log_2 7776 = 5 \log_2 6 \approx 12.92 @@ the same way
8 \cdot 6.57 \approx 52.6,\ \ 12 \cdot 4.70 \approx 56.4,\ \ 6 \cdot 12.92 \approx 77.5 @@ 8 from the keyboard, 12 lowercase, 6 dice words

Length-beats-symbols technique: one more symbol adds a whole symbol's bits, while a bigger alphabet adds only the log of the ratio to each. Precisely: going from length L to L + 1 adds log₂ A bits. Going from alphabet A to a bigger alphabet B adds L log₂(B/A) bits, by log-of-a-quotient (17c).

\text{from } 26 \text{ to } 95 \text{ at length } 8:\ \ 8 \log_2 \tfrac{95}{26} \approx 8 \cdot 1.87 \approx 15.0 \text{ bits} @@ log-of-a-quotient
\text{four more lowercase letters}:\ \ 4 \cdot 4.70 = 18.8 \text{ bits} @@ more than the whole keyboard gave
\text{to match at length } L:\ \ k \log_2 26 \ge L \log_2 \tfrac{95}{26} \iff k \ge \htmlClass{new}{0.40\,L} @@ about four letters for every ten in the password

And length is easy to remember. Twelve lowercase letters, or a few words, are easier to type than eight symbols from every row of the keyboard, and they have more bits.

Bits into years

A guesser tries the passwords one at a time, in some order, never twice. If the password was chosen at random, every place in the order is equally likely. The average place is (1 + 2 + ⋯ + N)/N = (N + 1)/2, by doubled-sum (4k). So on average a guesser needs about half the count.

How fast is a guesser? A site does not keep the passwords. It keeps a scrambled form of each that can be checked against a guess but not run backwards. When a site's stored file is stolen, the thief can test guesses on his own machines as fast as they run. In 2023 one gaming graphics card could test on the order of 10¹¹ guesses a second against a fast scrambled form, and on the order of 10⁵ against a slow form built to resist guessing.

Years technique: every ten more bits multiply the time to guess by about a thousand. Precisely: at g guesses a second, the average time for b bits is about 2^(b − 1)/g seconds, and 2^10 = 1024. A year is about 3.16 × 10⁷ s, about 2^24.9 s.

\tfrac{95^8}{2 \cdot 10^{11}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^4 \text{ s} \approx 9 \text{ hours} @@ 52.6 bits, against a fast scrambled form
\tfrac{95^8}{2 \cdot 10^{5}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{10} \text{ s} \approx 1050 \text{ years} @@ the same password against a slow form
\tfrac{26^{12}}{2 \cdot 10^{11}} \approx 4.8 \times 10^5 \text{ s} \approx 5.5 \text{ days} @@ 56.4 bits, fast form
\tfrac{7776^6}{2 \cdot 10^{11}} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12} \text{ s} \approx \htmlClass{new}{35\,000 \text{ years}} @@ 77.5 bits, six dice words, fast form

The slow form bought six powers of ten, about 20 bits, without the user doing anything. That is the site's share of the work. A site that locks an account after a few wrong tries makes a guesser on the site itself slower still, a few guesses a day.

What the count assumes

Everything above assumed that each password was equally likely. People do not choose that way. In 2009 a list of about 32 million passwords leaked from a games website, stored as plain text. The most common, 123456, was used by about 290 000 accounts. A guesser starts from such lists, then common words, then words with a capital at the front and a digit or a mark at the end.

So "Password1!" has 10 symbols from 95, which the count makes 65.7 bits. It sits near the top of every guessing list. Its bits are what was random in choosing it, and very little was.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

2b set two sizes of truth beside every and some, and 17·chem put them far apart: a half-life for the crowd of nuclei, and no date for any one. Bits live at the size of the crowd. They count every password a way of choosing could have made, each as likely as the next. The average of half the count is a truth about that crowd. One password from it may fall on the first guess; another on the last.

Bits measure the way a password was chosen, not the string itself.

The same string can carry many sizes of bits. Twenty-five lowercase letters thrown with dice are 117.5 bits. The same twenty-five letters, made of four words from a 7776-word list, are 51.7 bits. As four words from a famous joke about passwords, they are almost none.

Two people each type ten lowercase letters. One rolled dice for every letter. The other typed the first letters of the lines of a well-known song. Which password has 47 bits?

Who said it first

Shannon proposed measuring information by the logarithm of the number of possible messages, and wrote that with logarithms to the base 2 the units may be called binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey.

Claude Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948); paraphrase

The rules of 17·crypt.4.4 are Appendix A of the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology's Special Publication 800-63 (2003), written by Bill Burr. Its 2017 revision, 800-63B, dropped the rules for mixing kinds of symbol and for changing passwords on a schedule, and asked sites to check new passwords against lists of leaked ones. Burr told a newspaper in 2017 that he regretted much of what he had written.

The dice word list is Diceware, published by Arnold Reinhold in 1995; the Electronic Frontier Foundation published a long list of 7776 words for it in 2016. The leaked list of 2009 was RockYou's. The famous joke about passwords made of four common words is a comic strip of 2011 by Randall Munroe.

Accepted names: the bits of a way of choosing are its entropy, measured in bits; trying every password is a brute-force attack, and trying the likely ones first a dictionary attack; the scrambled form a site keeps is a password hash, and the slow forms are key-stretching hashes such as bcrypt (1999) and Argon2 (2015); locking out after wrong tries is rate limiting.
