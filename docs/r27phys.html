What keeps a spinning thing steady?

What keeps a spinning thing steady? A top stands up while it spins and falls over when it stops. A football thrown with a spiral flies true, and one thrown without wobbles end over end. Toss a hardcover book in the air spinning about its longest edge and it spins cleanly. Toss it spinning about the edge of middle length and it flips over every time, however carefully you throw it. A car wheel that is out of balance shakes the whole car at highway speed, though nothing is pushing it sideways. And the earth itself, a spinning ball, keeps its axis pointed at nearly the same star for thousands of years. What makes the question hard is that a spin has a direction as well as a size, and the direction of the spin is not always the direction of the turning. Rung twenty-three measured a turning push by a single number. This page makes the turning push an arrow, finds that spin is kept when nothing turns the body from outside, and finds that each body has three axes, the arrows of one grid that it only stretches, about which its spin and its turning agree. Two of those axes are steady and one is not. Then it turns to the spinning earth, which pushes every moving thing sideways, and to the sun, whose pull makes the planets sweep equal areas and ride on ellipses, as 18·phys found in the sky and could not explain.

A turning push is an arrow along the axis it turns about, as long as the push times the arm. A body at rest has its pushes and its turning pushes adding to nothing, about any point at all. A body pulled only toward one point keeps its spin, so it stays in one plane and sweeps equal areas in equal times. A solid turning about an axis has a rotational inertia, the sum of each piece’s mass times the square of its distance from the axis, and a hoop rolls more slowly than a ball. In general the spin is a grid times the turning, and the grid’s stretched arrows, three of them at right angles, are the axes about which the two agree. About the axes of most and least inertia a free body spins steadily; about the middle one it flips. Seen from the turning earth, everything that moves is pushed sideways. And a pull that falls off as the square of the distance keeps one more arrow fixed, which bends every path into an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola.

27phys1A turning push is an arrow

23·eng measured a turning push about a pivot by one number, τ = Fr sin φ. But a turning push also has an axis, and a way round that axis. 27i’s cross technique carries all three. For r the arrow from the pivot O to the place where a force F pushes, τ = r × F points along the axis, has size rF sin θ, and points the way your right thumb points when your fingers curl the way the push turns.

\tau_O = \textstyle\sum r_i \times F_i @@ forces Fᵢ pushing at places rᵢ, measured from O (the cross technique, 27i)
\tau_Q = \textstyle\sum (r_i - Q) \times F_i = \tau_O - Q \times \textstyle\sum F_i @@ the cross product spreads over sums, as its formula does (27i)
\textstyle\sum F_i = 0 \Rightarrow \tau_Q = \tau_O \text{ for every point } Q @@ the last term is gone
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at rest}:\ \textstyle\sum F_i = 0 \text{ and } \tau = 0 \text{ about one point, so about every point}} \quad \blacksquare @@ pick the point that makes the sums easiest

Any-pivot technique: if the forces on a body add to nothing, their turning push is the same about every point; so a body at rest has its forces and its turning pushes adding to nothing, about any point you choose.

\text{crank } 0.17 \text{ m level},\ \text{a push of } 400 \text{ N straight down}:\ \ |\tau| = 0.17 \cdot 400 = 68 \text{ N·m} @@ θ is a right angle: sin θ = 1
\text{front sprocket}: 68 = T\,r_1;\ \ \text{rear sprocket}: \tau_2 = T\,r_2 @@ the chain pulls with T on both; each sprocket is balanced about its own axle
\tau_2 = 68 \cdot \tfrac{16}{44} \approx 24.7 \text{ N·m} @@ 44 teeth in front, 16 behind: the radii are in that ratio
\htmlClass{new}{\text{push at the road} \approx \tfrac{24.7}{0.34} \approx 72.7 \text{ N}} @@ the wheel, radius 0.34 m, balanced about its axle

A steady turning push τ turning a body through θ radians does work τθ, as 23·eng found for a whole turn, 2πτ. Gears and chains trade turning push for turning speed and leave the work alone: the rear wheel turns 44/16 = 2.75 times for each turn of the crank.

27phys2Spin is kept

A body of mass m at r, moving at v, has a spin about O: L = r × mv. From here on, L is the angular momentum of the body about O.

L' = v \times mv + r \times mv' = r \times F @@ the moving-dot technique (27i); v × v = 0; mv′ = F (13·phys)
F \text{ along } r \Rightarrow L' = 0 @@ a pull toward O, as the sun’s on a planet
r \cdot L = 0 \text{ for every } t @@ the cross technique (27i): r × mv is at right angles to r, so the path stays in the plane square to the fixed L
\text{area swept in } \Delta t \approx \tfrac12|r \times v\,\Delta t| = \tfrac{|L|}{2m}\Delta t @@ half the parallelogram on r and vΔt: the cross technique (27i)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the swept area grows at } \tfrac{|L|}{2m},\ \text{a fixed rate}} \quad \blacksquare @@ equal areas in equal times

Kept-spin technique: a body pulled only toward a fixed point keeps its angular momentum, so it moves in one plane, and the line from the point to it sweeps equal areas in equal times. At its nearest and farthest places the motion is square to r, so |L| = mrv there and rv is the same at both ends.

\text{nearest}: 147.1 \text{ million km at } 30.29 \text{ km/s};\ \ \text{farthest}: 152.1 \text{ at } 29.29 @@ measured
147.1 \times 30.29 \approx 4455.7,\ \ 152.1 \times 29.29 \approx 4455.0 @@ rv at each end
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same to the rounding of the table}} @@ the kept-spin technique, seen in the sky

27phys3How hard a solid is to turn

A solid turning at ω radians a second about a fixed axis carries each piece round a circle. A piece at distance ρ from the axis moves at ρω (21j).

\tfrac12\textstyle\sum m_i(\rho_i\omega)^2 = \tfrac12I\omega^2,\ \ I = \textstyle\sum m_i\rho_i^2 @@ the energy of motion, piece by piece
L_{\text{axis}} = \textstyle\sum m_i\rho_i(\rho_i\omega) = I\omega @@ each piece’s spin about the axis has size ρᵢmᵢvᵢ
\text{rod about its middle}: \int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2}x^2\,\tfrac{M}{\ell}\,dx = \tfrac{M\ell^2}{12} @@ cut into thin pieces and add (15)
\text{disc}: \int_0^R\rho^2\,\tfrac{M}{\pi R^2}\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho = \tfrac{MR^2}{2};\ \ \text{hoop}: MR^2 @@ rings of radius ρ; a hoop has all its mass at R
\htmlClass{new}{\text{rolling}:\ v = R\omega,\ \ Mgh = \tfrac12Mv^2 + \tfrac12I\omega^2 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{\tfrac{2gh}{1 + I/(MR^2)}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the work-energy technique (16·phys): the drop pays for sliding and for turning

Spin-energy technique: a solid turning at ω about a fixed axis has energy ½Iω² and spin Iω about the axis, where I = Σmρ², the sum of each piece’s mass times the square of its distance from the axis. A ball, cut into discs, has I = (2/5)MR². So rolled from one height, a ball beats a disc, and a disc beats a hoop, whatever their masses and sizes: the hoop spends half its energy on turning, the ball only two sevenths.

With no turning push from outside, Iω is kept. A figure skater spinning with arms out who pulls them in, to a third of her rotational inertia, spins three times as fast.

27phys4Spin that is not along the turning

A body turning about an axis through O, at ω radians a second, is described by one arrow ω along the axis. A point of the body at r moves at ω × r: square to both, of size ω times its distance from the axis. So every arrow u fixed in the body has slope ω × u.

L = \textstyle\sum m_i\,r_i \times (\omega \times r_i) = \textstyle\sum m_i\big(|r_i|^2\omega - (r_i\cdot\omega)\,r_i\big) @@ r × (ω × r), checked entry by entry from the cross technique’s formula (27i)
L = \Theta\,\omega,\ \ \Theta = \textstyle\sum m_i\big(|r_i|^2I - r_ir_i^{\mathsf T}\big) @@ a straight map of ω: a 3 × 3 grid, with I the grid that does nothing
\Theta = \Theta^{\mathsf T}:\ \ (\Theta u)\cdot v = u\cdot(\Theta v) @@ r rᵀ turned is itself; the two-slot technique (27h)
\Theta u = \lambda u,\ \Theta v = \mu v \Rightarrow \lambda\,u\cdot v = \mu\,u\cdot v @@ move Θ across the dot product
\bar w^{\mathsf T}\Theta w \text{ equals its own conjugate} \Rightarrow \text{every stretch of } \Theta \text{ is real} @@ for Θw = λw, w complex: it is λ times |w|², which is real and not 0
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda \ne \mu \Rightarrow u \cdot v = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ (λ − μ) u·v = 0

Principal-axis technique: a body’s inertia grid Θ is equal to its own turn, so its stretches are real, and stretched arrows with different stretches are square to each other; spun about one of these axes the spin points along the turning, and about any other it does not. The three stretches I₁, I₂, I₃ are the body’s rotational inertias about its three axes.

\text{two } 1 \text{ kg lumps at } \pm(0.1, 0.1, 0) \text{ m, axle along } x:\ \ \Theta = \begin{pmatrix}0.02 & -0.02 & 0\\ -0.02 & 0.02 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0.04\end{pmatrix} @@ each lump: |r|² = 0.02
\omega = (10, 0, 0):\ \ L = \Theta\omega = (0.2, -0.2, 0) @@ not along the axle
L' = \omega \times L = (0, 0, -2) \text{ N·m} @@ L is fixed in the wheel, and turns with it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the axle must push with a turning push of } 2 \text{ N·m that goes round with the wheel}} @@ a shake, ten turns in 2π seconds

The stretched arrows of that Θ are (1, 1, 0) with stretch 0, the line of the lumps, and (1, −1, 0) and (0, 0, 1) with stretch 0.04. The axle is none of them. A balancing machine spins the wheel, reads the shake, and clips small weights to the rim until the axle is a principal axis.

27phys5The book that flips

Throw a body into the air. Gravity pulls on every piece in proportion to its mass, which turns it about its centre not at all, so its spin about the centre is kept. Write the turning along the body’s own principal axes, which turn with it: ω = ω₁e₁ + ω₂e₂ + ω₃e₃.

0 = L' = \textstyle\sum I_i\omega_i'e_i + \textstyle\sum I_i\omega_i\,(\omega \times e_i) = \textstyle\sum I_i\omega_i'e_i + \omega \times L @@ L = Σ Iᵢωᵢeᵢ; each eᵢ is fixed in the body (27·phys.4)
I_1\omega_1' = (I_2 - I_3)\,\omega_2\omega_3,\ \ I_2\omega_2' = (I_3 - I_1)\,\omega_3\omega_1,\ \ I_3\omega_3' = (I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_1\omega_2 @@ the parts of ω × L along e₁, e₂, e₃, by the cross technique’s formula (27i)
\omega_1 \approx \Omega,\ \ \omega_2' = \tfrac{(I_3 - I_1)\Omega}{I_2}\,\omega_3,\ \ \omega_3' = \tfrac{(I_1 - I_2)\Omega}{I_3}\,\omega_2 @@ spun about e₁, with a small wobble; ω₁ changes only by a product of two small numbers
T = 0,\ \ \Delta = -\tfrac{(I_3 - I_1)(I_1 - I_2)}{I_2I_3}\,\Omega^2 @@ the grid of the wobble (27·cb24.3)
\htmlClass{new}{I_1 \text{ between } I_2 \text{ and } I_3 \Rightarrow \Delta \lt 0:\ \text{a saddle; the wobble grows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ otherwise Δ > 0, a centre, and the kept energy and kept spin hold it there

Middle-axis technique: a free body spun about its axis of largest or of smallest rotational inertia keeps spinning about it; spun about the middle axis, a wobble grows like e^(st), with s = Ω√((I₃ − I₁)(I₁ − I₂)/(I₂I₃)), and the body flips. The book of 27·phys.4 has I = 0.00272, 0.00592 and 0.00832 kg·m². Spun about its middle axis at two turns a second, s ≈ 7.3 a second: a wobble of one degree grows a hundredfold in about 0.63 s, about one toss. A tennis racket flips the same way when spun about the axis that lies across its strings.

27phys6The sideways push of a turning earth

The earth turns once every 23 hours 56 minutes, at Ω = 7.292 × 10⁻⁵ radians a second, about the arrow Ω from the south pole to the north. Mass times acceleration holds for arrows measured against the stars. We measure against the ground, which turns.

u'_{\text{stars}} = u'_{\text{ground}} + \Omega \times u @@ the parts along axes fixed in the ground change, and those axes turn at Ω × each (27·phys.4)
v_{\text{stars}} = v + \Omega \times r @@ applied to the place r; v is the speed seen from the ground
a_{\text{stars}} = a + 2\,\Omega \times v + \Omega \times (\Omega \times r) @@ applied again; Ω is steady
\htmlClass{new}{m\,a = F - 2m\,\Omega \times v - m\,\Omega \times (\Omega \times r)} \quad \blacksquare @@ m a_stars = F (13·phys): seen from the ground, two more pushes appear

Sideways-push technique: seen from the turning earth, a body moving at v is pushed by −2mΩ × v, square to its motion; in the northern half of the earth it pushes level motion to the right, by 2Ωv sin(latitude) for each kilogram. It is square to v, so it does no work: it bends paths and never speeds them up. The last term, −mΩ × (Ω × r), pushes outward from the axis and only reshapes gravity a little.

\text{a wind of } 10 \text{ m/s at latitude } 47.6^\circ:\ \ 2\Omega v\sin 47.6^\circ \approx 0.0011 \text{ m/s}^2 @@ tiny, but it acts for hours
\text{over an hour}: 0.0011 \times 3600 \approx 3.9 \text{ m/s of sideways speed} @@ the wind is bent hard to the right
\text{the swing of a long pendulum turns at } 15.04^\circ\sin(\text{latitude}) \text{ an hour} @@ a measured rate, which this push explains; its proof is held in trust
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Griffith Observatory, } 34.1^\circ:\ \ 8.43^\circ \text{ an hour, a full turn in } 42.7 \text{ hours}} @@ the pendulum there knocks over pegs as its swing turns

Air flowing in toward a low is bent to its right, so it circles the low the other way: storms off the coast of British Columbia turn counter-clockwise, seen from above.

27phys7Why the paths are ellipses

18·phys found that the measured places of Mars lie on an ellipse with the sun at a focus, and held in trust why. A measured law gives the sun’s pull on a planet of mass m: F = −(k/|r|³) r, with k = GMm, falling off as the square of the distance. Write p = mv for the planet’s momentum.

p' \times L = -\tfrac{k}{|r|^3}\,r \times (r \times mv) = -\tfrac{km}{|r|^3}\big((r\cdot v)\,r - |r|^2v\big) @@ L is kept (27·phys.2); the double cross of 27·phys.4
\big(\tfrac{r}{|r|}\big)' = \tfrac{v}{|r|} - \tfrac{(r\cdot v)}{|r|^3}\,r @@ the quotient rule, with |r|′ = (r·v)/|r|
W = p \times L - km\,\tfrac{r}{|r|} \text{ has } W' = 0 @@ the two lines cancel term by term, since L′ = 0
r\cdot W = r\cdot(p \times L) - km|r| = |L|^2 - km|r| @@ (u × v)·w = det[u v w] (27i); moving the columns round once is two swaps, which keep the sign (27e)
|r|\,|W|\cos\theta = |L|^2 - km|r| \Rightarrow |r| = \tfrac{\ell}{1 + e\cos\theta},\ \ \ell = \tfrac{|L|^2}{km},\ \ e = \tfrac{|W|}{km} @@ θ the angle from the fixed arrow W to r
\htmlClass{new}{|r| = \ell - e\,x,\ \ x = |r|\cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out: 18·phys’s lift technique, so the path is an ellipse with the sun at a focus when e < 1

Fixed-arrow technique: under a pull toward a point that falls off as the square of the distance, the arrow p × L − km r/|r| is kept; so every path is |r| = ℓ/(1 + e cos θ), an ellipse with the pulling point at a focus when e < 1, a parabola when e = 1, and a hyperbola when e > 1. W points from the sun to the nearest place of the path. For the earth, e = (152.1 − 147.1)/(152.1 + 147.1) ≈ 0.0167. That pays what 18·phys held in trust.

The accepted names

The turning push τ = r × F is the torque, or moment, of F; the any-pivot technique is the balance of torques. L = r × mv is angular momentum, and equal areas in equal times is Kepler’s second law. I = Σmρ² is the moment of inertia. The inertia grid is the inertia tensor, its stretched arrows the principal axes, and its stretches the principal moments of inertia. The rules I₁ω₁′ = (I₂ − I₃)ω₂ω₃ are Euler’s equations for a free rigid body, and the middle-axis technique is the intermediate axis theorem, or tennis racket theorem. The sideways push is the Coriolis force, and −mΩ × (Ω × r) the centrifugal force; the pendulum is a Foucault pendulum. W is the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, and ℓ the semi-latus rectum.

Who said it first

Archimedes gave the balance of a lever about 250 BCE. Johannes Kepler found equal areas in 1609, and Isaac Newton derived them, and the ellipses, from a pull toward the sun in 1687. Leonhard Euler wrote the rules of a spinning solid and found its principal axes in 1765. Louis Poinsot pictured the free spin in 1834. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis gave the sideways push in 1835, and Léon Foucault hung his pendulum in the Panthéon in Paris in 1851. The fixed arrow W was found by Jakob Hermann in 1710 and again by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1799, and was given its later names by Carl Runge and Wilhelm Lenz in the 1900s. In 1985 the cosmonaut Vladimir Dzhanibekov saw a wing nut, spinning free in orbit, flip over and over. In 27·phys.9.5, William Rowan Hamilton’s quaternions (1843) were defended by Peter Guthrie Tait against Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside, who split the product into dot and cross; Hermann Grassmann (1844) had a product of two arrows that is neither.
