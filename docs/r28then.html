28·then · newsflash

How Good Can an Engine Ever Be?

How good can an engine ever be? Every power plant on the Columbia, every car on the Interstate and every refrigerator in a kitchen moves heat and work between a hot place and a cold one. Engineers spent the nineteenth century making engines better, and kept asking whether there is a best, a ceiling that no cleverness can pass. What makes the question hard is that the state of a gas has several knobs at once. Its pressure, its volume and its temperature move together, and heat put in along one road does not match heat put in along another. Rung nineteen’s engine page could only cap one kind of engine, the four strokes of a car. This page uses the slopes of rung twenty-eight, taken along one knob at a time, to find which quantities belong to the state of a gas and which only to the road it took. From there it finds the ceiling for every engine, the most work a chemical change can give, the cost of crowding a gas, and the slope of the line where water boils.

Heat taken in depends on the road, but heat divided by temperature does not: it is the change of the entropy that 22·comp counted. Because second slopes taken in either order agree, one quantity that cannot be measured, how entropy changes as a gas spreads out, equals one that can, how pressure rises with temperature. A real gas left to spread into empty space cools a little, and the pull between its molecules says by how much. No engine running between a hot room and a cold one can turn more than one minus the ratio of their temperatures of the heat it takes in into work, and an engine that runs slowly enough can come as close as it likes. At a fixed temperature and pressure the free energy caps the work a change can give, and crowding a gas costs a logarithm of the pressure. Where two phases meet, the slope of the boundary is the heat of the change over the temperature times the change in volume. Water boils cooler on a mountain, and ice melts only a little under a skate.

28then1Heat Follows the Road, Entropy Does Not

Take one mole of air. Its temperature T and volume V fix its state, and the gas law gives its pressure, P = RT/V. A measured law of 19·eng says that heat taken in at a fixed volume is C_V times the rise in temperature, with C_V ≈ 2.5R for air. Another measured law, the first law of heat, says that heat taken in pays for a rise in the gas’s energy and for the work it does pushing out. So a small slow step dT, dV takes in heat C_V dT + P dV.

A small step M dT + N dV is the step of some function of the state, a function whose partial slopes are M and N, only if M’s slope along V equals N’s slope along T. That is the swap-knobs technique of 28d, read backwards: the function’s two mixed second slopes would be those two numbers. 28n takes up the converse.

\delta Q = C_V\,dT + \tfrac{RT}{V}\,dV @@ the first law of heat, with P = RT/V; δ marks a small amount that need not be the step of anything
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial V}C_V = 0,\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial T}\tfrac{RT}{V} = \tfrac RV @@ the one-knob technique (28a); 0 ≠ R/V
\text{no function of } (T, V) \text{ has the heat as its step} @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d): its mixed slopes would have to agree
\tfrac{\delta Q}{T} = \tfrac{C_V}{T}\,dT + \tfrac RV\,dV,\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial V}\tfrac{C_V}{T} = 0 = \tfrac{\partial}{\partial T}\tfrac RV @@ divide by T: now the slopes agree
\htmlClass{new}{S = C_V\ln T + R\ln V + \text{a constant}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its partial slopes are C_V/T and R/V (17a), so its step is δQ/T

From here on, a quantity fixed by the state alone, whatever road led there, is a state function. Heat-over-T technique: the heat a gas takes in depends on the road, but the heat over the temperature is the step of a state function, its entropy; for a mole of ideal gas, S = C_V ln T + R ln V, up to a constant. Halve the volume at a fixed temperature, and S falls by R ln 2: per molecule that is k ln 2, the bit of 22·comp’s halved-room technique.

(300 \text{ K}, V_0) \to (600 \text{ K}, 2V_0),\ \ C_V = 2.5R,\ \ R = 8.314 \text{ J/K} @@ one mole of air
\text{road A: heat at fixed } V, \text{ then spread at } 600 \text{ K}:\ \ Q = 6236 + 3458 = 9693 \text{ J} @@ spreading at a fixed temperature takes in RT ln 2, 22·comp’s area
\text{road B: spread at } 300 \text{ K}, \text{ then heat}:\ \ Q = 1729 + 6236 = 7964 \text{ J} @@ a different heat
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta S = 2.5R\ln 2 + R\ln 2 \approx 20.17 \text{ J/K on both}} @@ the heat-over-T technique

28then2The Cross Relations

For slow steps, the heat-over-T technique says δQ = T dS. So the first law of heat reads dU = T dS − P dV for the energy U. Entropy is hard to measure: no meter reads it. Temperature, volume and pressure are easy. Build a state function whose knobs are the easy ones.

F = U - TS \Rightarrow dF = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT = -S\,dT - P\,dV @@ the free energy at a fixed temperature; the product rule along both knobs
\tfrac{\partial F}{\partial T} = -S,\ \ \tfrac{\partial F}{\partial V} = -P @@ read off the partial slopes
\tfrac{\partial^2 F}{\partial V\,\partial T} = \tfrac{\partial^2 F}{\partial T\,\partial V} @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d), when F’s second slopes are continuous
\htmlClass{new}{\big(\tfrac{\partial S}{\partial V}\big)_T = \big(\tfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\big)_V} \quad \blacksquare @@ the subscript names the knob held still

Cross-relation technique: how entropy grows as a gas spreads at a fixed temperature equals how its pressure rises as it warms at a fixed volume. The left side cannot be read off any meter. The right side is a pressure gauge and a thermometer. Three more cross relations come the same way from U, from U + PV and from U + PV − TS.

\big(\tfrac{\partial U}{\partial V}\big)_T = T\big(\tfrac{\partial S}{\partial V}\big)_T - P = T\big(\tfrac{\partial P}{\partial T}\big)_V - P @@ dU = T dS − P dV along V, then the cross relation
\text{ideal gas}: P = \tfrac{RT}{V} \Rightarrow T\cdot\tfrac RV - P = 0 @@ its energy does not care how spread out it is
\text{the corrected law}: P = \tfrac{RT}{V - b} - \tfrac{a}{V^2} \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{\big(\tfrac{\partial U}{\partial V}\big)_T = \tfrac{a}{V^2}} @@ 15·chem’s law; the b part cancels
\text{into empty space}: \Delta U = 0 \Rightarrow C_V\,\Delta T = -a\big(\tfrac{1}{V_1} - \tfrac{1}{V_2}\big) \quad \blacksquare @@ no heat, no work; the pull between molecules costs energy, paid out of the temperature

Expansion-energy technique: at a fixed temperature, a gas’s energy changes with its volume at the rate T(∂P/∂T) − P, which is 0 for an ideal gas and a/V² for the corrected law. One mole of carbon dioxide, a = 0.364 Pa·m⁶ per mole squared and C_V ≈ 28.5 J/K, let out of one litre into two through a valve into empty space, cools by about 6.4 K.

28then3The Best Engine

An engine takes heat Q_h from a hot room at T_h, gives heat Q_c to a cold room at T_c, and turns the difference into work W. After each turn it is back where it started, so every state function of the engine, its energy and its entropy among them, is back too. 22·comp’s second law of heat says that the total entropy of the engine and its rooms does not fall.

\Delta S_{\text{engine}} = 0,\ \ W = Q_h - Q_c @@ one full turn: the state returns, and energy is kept
\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0 - \tfrac{Q_h}{T_h} + \tfrac{Q_c}{T_c} \ge 0 @@ the second law; the hot room loses Q_h/T_h, the cold room gains Q_c/T_c
Q_c \ge Q_h\tfrac{T_c}{T_h} @@ multiply by T_c > 0
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{W}{Q_h} = 1 - \tfrac{Q_c}{Q_h} \le 1 - \tfrac{T_c}{T_h}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the share of the heat turned to work

Best-engine technique: no engine working between rooms at T_h and T_c turns more than 1 − T_c/T_h of the heat it takes in into work, whatever it is made of. One engine reaches the ceiling: spread a gas slowly at T_h, spread it fast until it cools to T_c, squeeze it slowly at T_c, then squeeze it fast back up to T_h. Its two slow legs make no entropy at all, so the ≥ is an =. The picture runs it.

The Columbia Generating Station near Richland makes steam at about 286 °C, 559 K, and gets rid of heat at about 27 °C, 300 K. Its ceiling is 1 − 300/559 ≈ 46%. It delivers about 34%. A car’s four strokes with r = 10 have 19·eng’s share 60%; between the air at 300 K and a flame near 2500 K the ceiling is 88%. Run backwards, the engine is a heat pump: a house in Seattle drawing heat from air at 5 °C into floors at 35 °C can move at most T_h/(T_h − T_c) = 308/30 ≈ 10 joules of heat for each joule of work. Real ones move about 3.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a law with an exception rate

The second law of heat is a measured law, and 22·comp counted where it comes from. A body that can sit in W ways has S = k ln W. A state with entropy ΔS lower has e^(−ΔS/k) times as many ways. So a drop in entropy is not forbidden. It is rare, and the counting says how rare.

State the second law with the size of the crowd: a drop ΔS in entropy has a chance of about e^(−ΔS/k).

For a litre of air, a drop of even a billionth of a joule per kelvin is e to the minus 10¹⁴: it has never happened anywhere. For ten molecules, all ten in the left half of their box is one chance in 1024, and a tiny engine could beat the ceiling now and then. Experiments with a single bead held in a beam of light have watched it happen, for a fraction of a second.

A motor protein in a muscle cell takes steps that each hand out about 20 kT of energy. Would you expect it ever to step backwards, and roughly how often?

28then4The Most Work at a Fixed Temperature

A battery, a muscle and a fuel cell all work at the temperature and pressure of the air around them. For them the right state function is G = U + PV − TS, whose easy knobs are T and P. The same steps as 28·then.2 give dG = −S dT + V dP, and the second law gives that a change at fixed T and P can hand out at most −ΔG as work, beyond pushing back the air. That is the ΔG of 25·cb7’s energy technique.

dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP \Rightarrow \big(\tfrac{\partial G}{\partial P}\big)_T = V @@ G = F + PV, and dF from 28·then.2
\big(\tfrac{\partial G}{\partial P}\big)_T = \tfrac{nRT}{P} @@ for an ideal gas
G(P) - G(P^\circ) = \int_{P^\circ}^{P}\tfrac{nRT}{p}\,dp = nRT\ln\tfrac{P}{P^\circ} @@ undo the slope along P, with T held still (16, 17a)
\htmlClass{new}{\mu = \tfrac Gn = \mu^\circ + RT\ln\tfrac{P}{P^\circ}} \quad \blacksquare @@ per mole

From here on, the free energy per mole of one substance in a mixture is its chemical potential, μ. Crowding-cost technique: at a fixed temperature, a mole of ideal gas at pressure P has μ = μ° + RT ln(P/P°), so crowding it from P₁ to P₂ costs at least RT ln(P₂/P₁). That is 19·bio’s ratio-coin technique, now found from the gas law instead of laid down. Add the μ of every substance in a change, each times its count, and RT ln Q appears: 25·cb7’s ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, and at rest 25·chem’s K.

A hydrogen car in California fills its tank to 700 bar. Squeezing a mole of hydrogen from 1 bar at 25 °C costs at least RT ln 700 ≈ 16.2 kJ, about 8 MJ for each kilogram. A kilogram of hydrogen gives about 120 MJ in a fuel cell, so the squeeze alone takes at least 7% of it. Real squeezes are fast and warm the gas, and take about twice that.

28then5Where Two Phases Meet

Water and steam sit together only along a line of (T, P): at 100 °C under one atmosphere, cooler under less. On that line a mole moved from liquid to gas changes nothing, so the two chemical potentials are equal there. That is one equation in two knobs, and 28f’s fixed-by-an-equation technique says it fixes P as a function of T near any point where the two slopes along P differ.

\mu_\ell(T, P(T)) = \mu_g(T, P(T)) @@ the boundary, as the function P(T) of 28f’s fixed-by-an-equation technique
\tfrac{\partial\mu}{\partial T} = -s,\ \ \tfrac{\partial\mu}{\partial P} = v @@ dG = −S dT + V dP, per mole: s and v are the entropy and volume of a mole
-s_\ell + v_\ell P' = -s_g + v_gP' @@ the roads-add technique (28c), along the boundary, on both sides
P' = \tfrac{s_g - s_\ell}{v_g - v_\ell} @@ solve for the slope
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{dP}{dT} = \tfrac{L}{T\,\Delta v}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the heat of the change is L = T(s_g − s_ℓ), taken in at a fixed temperature

Boundary-slope technique: the line where two phases meet climbs at the heat of the change over the temperature times the change in volume of a mole. For boiling, the steam’s volume RT/P swamps the water’s, so dP/dT ≈ LP/(RT²). That says ln P climbs at L/(RT²), and undoing the slope gives ln(P/P₀) = −(L/R)(1/T − 1/T₀).

L = 40.66 \text{ kJ per mole},\ \ T_0 = 373.15 \text{ K at } 1 \text{ atm} @@ measured, for water
\text{Mount Rainier, } 4392 \text{ m}: P \approx 0.58 \text{ atm} @@ a standard atmosphere
\tfrac 1T = \tfrac{1}{373.15} + \tfrac{8.314\ln(1/0.58)}{40\,660} \approx 0.002792 @@ the boundary-slope technique, undone
\htmlClass{new}{T \approx 358 \text{ K} \approx 85\,^\circ\text{C}} @@ water on the summit boils 15 degrees cooler
\text{ice}: \Delta v \approx -1.6 \text{ cm}^3/\text{mol},\ \ \tfrac{dP}{dT} \approx -13.5 \text{ MPa per K} @@ ice is bulkier than water, so its line leans the other way

So squeezing ice melts it, but barely. A skater of 70 kg standing on one square centimetre of blade presses with 7 MPa, which lowers the melting point by about half a degree. Rink ice is kept near −5 °C.

The accepted names

The first law of heat is the first law of thermodynamics; a small amount δQ that is not the step of anything is an inexact differential, and a step of a state function is an exact differential. F = U − TS is the Helmholtz free energy and G = U + PV − TS the Gibbs free energy. The cross relations are Maxwell’s relations, and the expansion-energy technique is the thermodynamic equation of state; spreading into empty space is a Joule expansion. The best engine is the Carnot engine, its ceiling the Carnot efficiency, and heat moved per joule of work in a heat pump its coefficient of performance. The boundary-slope technique is the Clapeyron equation, and its form for boiling the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Who said it first

Sadi Carnot found the ceiling in 1824, still thinking of heat as a fluid. Rudolf Clausius gave entropy its name in 1865, and William Thomson (Kelvin) fixed the absolute temperature from Carnot’s engine in 1848. James Clerk Maxwell wrote the cross relations in his Theory of Heat (1871), and Josiah Willard Gibbs built free energies and chemical potentials in 1875 to 1878. Émile Clapeyron gave the boundary slope in 1834, Clausius its boiling form in 1850. The bead in a beam of light was watched by Gavin Wang, Denis Evans and colleagues in 2002. In 28·then.7.5, James Thomson predicted pressure melting in 1849 and John Joly offered it for skating in 1886; Michael Faraday proposed the wet surface layer in 1850; Frank Bowden and T. P. Hughes argued for melting by rubbing in 1939. The question is still argued.
