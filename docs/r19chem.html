Which Moves Leave a Molecule Unchanged?

Which moves leave a molecule unchanged? A century and a half ago, chemists could weigh what went into a reaction and count the atoms in a formula, but no one could see a molecule. They could count something else: how many different compounds share one formula. Swap two of the six hydrogens of benzene, C₆H₆, for chlorine, and you get exactly three different liquids, with three different boiling points. Swap two of methane’s four hydrogens, and you get only one. What makes it hard is that a count of bottles says nothing about shape directly. This page shows, with 19’s moves, that a guessed shape predicts a count, and that the count can kill the guess.

A molecule’s moves are the turns in space that put every atom where an atom of the same kind was. Two ways of swapping in new atoms give one compound when a move carries one to the other. The ways sort into classes, and the size of a class times the moves that keep one way fixed is the number of moves.

19chem1A Molecule’s Moves

From here on, on this page, a move of a molecule is a turn of the whole molecule in space that puts every atom where an atom of the same kind was. Each move shuffles the atoms, and two moves count as one when they make the same shuffle.

A water molecule is bent, with the oxygen at the corner. Turn it half a turn about the line through the oxygen that splits the corner in two, and the two hydrogens swap places. So water has two moves: doing nothing, and that half turn. Benzene is a flat ring of six carbons, a regular hexagon, with a hydrogen standing out from each. Its moves are the hexagon’s, by 19f: six turns on the table and six turns over in space.

Molecule-moves technique: the moves of a molecule form a part-group of the shuffles of its atoms. The proof is 19f1’s. Doing nothing is a move. Two moves in a row are a move. The undo of a turn is a turn, and it sends atoms back to atoms of the same kind.

\text{water, } \mathrm{H_2O}:\ \ 2 \text{ moves} @@ do nothing; the half turn swaps the hydrogens
\text{benzene, } \mathrm{C_6H_6}:\ \ 6 \cdot 2 = 12 \text{ moves} @@ place-then-count (19f2): carbon 1 anywhere, its neighbour one of two
\htmlClass{new}{\text{methane, } \mathrm{CH_4},\ \text{as a square}:\ 8;\ \ \text{as a tetrahedron}:\ 12} @@ two guesses at one shape; 19f3, and 19f6

19chem2One Compound, Drawn Two Ways

Swap two of benzene’s hydrogens for chlorine. Number the carbons 1 to 6 round the ring. The chlorines can sit on any 2 of the 6 places, and there are 15 such pairs (4k). But a pair on places 1 and 2 and a pair on places 3 and 4 are the same molecule, turned. Only a move can tell you that two drawings are one compound.

From here on, on this page, two placements are carried one to the other when some move of the molecule sends the first to the second.

Carried-is-sorting technique: being carried by a move is an equivalence relation, and the three demands of a group are exactly what make it one. Each class is one compound.

e\,x = x @@ doing nothing: every placement is carried to itself
g\,x = y \Rightarrow g^{-1} y = x @@ one inverse (19b): the undo carries it back
g\,x = y,\ h\,y = z \Rightarrow (hg)\,x = z @@ two moves in a row are a move
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reflexive, symmetric, transitive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ an equivalence relation (2h); its classes are the compounds
  
\{1, 2\}, \{2, 3\}, \dots, \{6, 1\}:\ \ 6 @@ next to each other: one class, carried round by turns
\{1, 3\}, \{2, 4\}, \dots, \{6, 2\}:\ \ 6 @@ one place between: one class
\{1, 4\}, \{2, 5\}, \{3, 6\}:\ \ 3 @@ straight across: one class
\htmlClass{new}{6 + 6 + 3 = 15:\ \ 3 \text{ compounds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no move changes the distance round the ring, so no two classes merge

There are three bottles on the shelf, with three different boiling points. The count and the shelf agree.

19chem3Count by the Moves That Keep It

Class-times-keepers technique: the size of a class times the number of moves that keep one of its placements is the number of moves. It is 19f2’s place-then-count, worked for any placement instead of one corner.

K = \{k : k\,x = x\} @@ the keepers of x: a part-group (19d1)
g\,x = y \Rightarrow \{h : h\,x = y\} = gK @@ h x = y exactly when g⁻¹h keeps x: a shifted copy (19d2)
|gK| = |K|,\ \ \text{copies never overlap} @@ copies-same-size; copies-never-overlap (19d3)
\htmlClass{new}{|G| = (\text{size of the class of } x) \cdot |K|} \quad \blacksquare @@ every move sends x into its class, in exactly one copy
  
\{1, 2\}:\ \ 6 \cdot 2 = 12 @@ kept by doing nothing, and the turn over that swaps 1 and 2
\{1, 3\}:\ \ 6 \cdot 2 = 12 @@ kept by doing nothing, and the turn over that swaps 1 and 3
\{1, 4\}:\ \ 3 \cdot 4 = 12 @@ kept by doing nothing, the half turn, and two turns over

19chem4Square or Tetrahedron?

Now use the count to test a shape no one could see. Methane is a carbon with four hydrogens. Guess one: the hydrogens sit at the corners of a flat square round the carbon. Guess two: they sit at the corners of a tetrahedron, every two of them equally far apart. Swap two hydrogens for chlorine, and count the compounds each guess allows.

\text{square}:\ \ \{1, 2\}, \{2, 3\}, \{3, 4\}, \{4, 1\} \ \ \big|\ \ \{1, 3\}, \{2, 4\} @@ sides and diagonals: no move turns a side into a diagonal
\text{square}:\ \ 2 \text{ compounds} @@ check: 4 · 2 = 8 and 2 · 4 = 8
\text{tetrahedron}:\ \ \text{all } 6 \text{ pairs in one class} @@ every two corners are joined; a turn carries any edge to any other
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tetrahedron}:\ \ 1 \text{ compound}} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 6 · 2 = 12

Every attempt to make a second compound of this formula has given the first one again. So the square guess is dead. A pyramid with the carbon on top dies the same way: its four turns keep sides and diagonals apart too.

A guessed shape predicts a count; a count that disagrees with the shelf kills the guess, and one that agrees only lets it live. The tetrahedron has survived. That is not a proof that it is right, only that this test could not catch it.

The accepted names

A molecule’s moves, counted as turns and flips together, form its point group; water’s is C₂ᵥ, ammonia’s C₃ᵥ, methane’s T_d and benzene’s D₆ₕ. The turns alone form the rotation subgroup. Compounds with one formula and different structures are isomers. Benzene’s three with two chlorines are ortho-, meta- and para-dichlorobenzene. Class-times-keepers is the orbit-stabiliser theorem; a class is an orbit and the keepers a stabiliser. Two compounds that no turn carries to each other but a flip does, as in 19·chem.6.5, are enantiomers, and a molecule with such a twin is chiral.

Who said it first

August Kekulé proposed the six-carbon ring for benzene in 1865. Wilhelm Körner (1874) counted substitution products of benzene to fix which places were which, his “absolute method”. In the same year Jacobus Henricus van ’t Hoff and Joseph Achille Le Bel argued for the tetrahedron by exactly this page’s count: one dichloromethane, and two forms of a carbon with four different neighbours. Louis Pasteur had already sorted two forms of tartrate crystals, from the tartar left in wine barrels, by hand under a lens in 1848. Arthur Cayley counted isomers of whole families with trees in 1874; George Pólya’s counting theorem of 1937 counts classes for any group.
