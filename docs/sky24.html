What Is the Largest Sofa That Turns a Corner?

What is the largest sofa that turns a corner? A hallway one unit wide turns a right angle. A flat shape slides along it, turns, and slides on down the other arm, never leaving the hallway. How large can its area be? A square of side 1 passes, area 1. Half a disc of radius 1 passes, turning about the inner corner, area about 1.57. In 1968 a shape like an old telephone receiver was found, two quarter discs pushed apart with a half disc bitten out of the block between them, area about 2.2074, and in the same paper a short argument showed that nothing passes with area above about 2.83. This page proves both. In 1992 a shape built from eighteen curved pieces, every piece pressed against a wall at some moment of the turn, did slightly better, about 2.2195, and was guessed to be best. The ceiling came down by computer to 2.37 in 2018. Then in November 2024 a young mathematician posted a proof, 119 pages long and with no computer search, that nothing beats the 1992 shape. It has been read and admired; by early 2026 it was under review at a leading journal, and as far as this page could find it has not yet been accepted. So the answer is very probably known, and the field is waiting to be sure.

A sofa is a connected shape moved by slides and turns through the hallway L of width 1. The half disc passes; the receiver family, two quarter discs set 2r apart with a half disc of radius r removed, passes for every r in [0, 1] because the inner corner runs round the removed half disc, and its area π/2 + 2r − πr²/2 is largest at r = 2/π: π/2 + 2/π ≈ 2.2074. At the moment the sofa has turned by π/4 it lies in two strips that cross at 45°, so its area is at most 2√2 ≈ 2.83. A shape of 18 pieces gives 2.2195; since 2024, a proof that this is best awaits its referees.

sky24.1A Hallway and a Shape

Put the hallway’s inner corner at the origin. One arm runs off to the left, the strip 0 ≤ y ≤ 1; the other runs down, the strip 0 ≤ x ≤ 1; they share the square [0, 1] × [0, 1], whose far corner (1, 1) is the outer corner. Call the whole region L. A sofa is a closed connected shape that can be moved by 20c’s slides and turns, changing continuously, from far down the left arm to far down the other, staying inside L all the time. The question, posed in 1966: how large can a sofa’s area be?

Pivot-turn technique: the half disc of radius 1 is a sofa. Slide it, flat side down, along the left arm until its centre is at the inner corner; turn it about the corner by a quarter turn clockwise; slide it down.

\text{after a turn by } \theta \in [0, \pi/2]:\ \ \{|p| \le 1,\ \text{direction of } p \in [-\theta, \pi - \theta]\} @@ a half disc about the corner
[-\theta, \pi - \theta] \subseteq [-\pi/2, \pi] @@ never the open quarter x < 0, y < 0, which is wall
|p| \le 1 \text{ in the quarters } [0, \tfrac\pi2],\ [\tfrac\pi2, \pi],\ [-\tfrac\pi2, 0] \Rightarrow p \in L @@ there x ≤ 1 and y ≤ 1, and x ≥ 0 or y ≥ 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area } \pi/2 \approx 1.5708 \text{ passes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 21n3’s disc technique, halved

A square of side 1 slides round without turning, area 1. The half disc buys more by turning, and the best sofas turn while they slide.

sky24.2Two Quarter Discs and a Bite

Pull the half disc apart. In the sofa’s own frame, fix r in [0, 1] and take the left quarter disc of radius 1 centred at (−r, 0), the right quarter disc of radius 1 centred at (r, 0), both above the line y = 0, and between them the block [−r, r] × [0, 1] with the half disc of radius r about the origin removed. Call it S_r. At r = 0 it is the half disc.

Now move the hallway, not the sofa. At turn t in [0, π/2], put the inner corner at q = r(cos 2t, sin 2t), on the bitten half circle, and measure τ = (p + (r, 0)) · (−sin t, cos t) and σ = (p − (r, 0)) · (cos t, sin t). These are coordinates with q at (0, 0), turned by t, so the hallway at turn t is L_t = {σ ≤ 1, τ ≤ 1, not both σ < 0 and τ < 0}.

Semicircle-path technique: for every r in [0, 1] and every t in [0, π/2], S_r lies in L_t; so S_r is a sofa.

\text{the inner walls } \tau = 0,\ \sigma = 0 \text{ pass through } (-r, 0),\ (r, 0) \text{ and meet at } q @@ 18f’s angle in a semicircle: they meet at a right angle on the circle
\text{left piece: } \tau \le |p + (r, 0)| \le 1;\ \ \text{block and right piece: } x + r \ge 0,\ y \le 1 \Rightarrow \tau \le y\cos t \le 1 @@ the wall τ = 1
\text{right piece: } \sigma \le |p - (r, 0)| \le 1;\ \ \text{block and left piece: } x - r \le 0 \Rightarrow \sigma \le y \sin t \le 1 @@ the wall σ = 1
\{\sigma < 0,\ \tau < 0\} \cap \{y \ge 0\} = \text{inside of the triangle } (-r, 0),\ q,\ (r, 0) @@ the corner’s wedge, above the line
\text{that triangle lies in the bitten half disc} @@ its corners are on the half circle, and a disc holds its chords
\htmlClass{new}{S_r \subseteq L_t \text{ for every } t} \quad \blacksquare @@ at t = 0 and t = π/2 it sits at the mouth of each arm
  
\text{area}(S_r) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{4} + 2r \cdot 1 - \tfrac{\pi r^2}{2} @@ 15e: pieces add; 21n3 for each disc
\tfrac{d}{dr}:\ \ 2 - \pi r = 0 \Rightarrow r = 2/\pi \approx 0.6366 @@ the area falls on either side
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac\pi2 + \tfrac4\pi - \tfrac2\pi = \tfrac\pi2 + \tfrac2\pi \approx 2.2074} \quad \blacksquare @@ r = 0 gives 1.5708, r = ½ gives 2.1781, r = 1 gives 2.0000

sky24.3A Ceiling From One Moment

A ceiling needs an argument about every sofa at once. Fix the sofa’s frame at the start, where it lies in the left arm, a strip H of width 1. Watch the angle φ by which it has turned; it changes continuously from 0. Two strips of width 1 crossing at angle θ share a parallelogram of area 1/|sin θ|, by 18g4: base 1/|sin θ| and height 1.

Forty-five-degree technique: every sofa has area at most 2√2.

\text{if } \varphi \text{ reaches } \pm\pi/4:\ \ S \subseteq H \cap (\text{arm at } 45°) \cup H \cap (\text{arm at } 135°) @@ L is two half-strips, now both at 45° to H
\text{area} \le \tfrac{1}{\sin 45°} + \tfrac{1}{\sin 135°} = \sqrt2 + \sqrt2 @@ two parallelograms
\text{if not: } |\varphi| < \pi/4 \text{ throughout} @@ φ starts at 0 and changes continuously
\text{at the end } S \subseteq H \cap (\text{the down arm, at } 90° - \varphi) \Rightarrow \text{area} \le 1/\cos\varphi < \sqrt2 @@ one parallelogram
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} \le 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.8284} \quad \blacksquare @@ in every case

Any other angle gives a weaker ceiling: at turn π/6 the two parallelograms total 1/sin 30° + 1/cos 30° ≈ 3.155. The gap between 2.2074 and 2.8284 stood from 1968 to 2018, when the same idea, using several angles at once and a computer to bound the shapes that fit in all five, brought the ceiling to 2.37.

sky24.4Eighteen Pieces

The receiver wastes room. During its turn the corner runs round a half circle, but the shape is never pressed against both outer walls and the corner at once except at the middle. In 1992 a shape was built by asking for the opposite: at every moment, every part of the boundary that could be pushed out is touching a wall. That condition turns into equations for the path, and the shape that comes out has a boundary of eighteen pieces, some straight and the others curved, traced against the walls as they turn.

\text{half disc} = 1.5708,\ \ \text{receiver} = 2.2074 @@ sky24.1, sky24.2
\text{eighteen pieces} \approx 2.2195 @@ the area, as the 1992 paper computed it, held in trust
2.2195 - 2.2074 = 0.0121,\ \ \text{about } 0.55\% @@ the gain
\htmlClass{new}{\text{floor } 2.2195,\ \ \text{ceiling } 2.37 \text{ (2018)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the state of things before 2024

Its area is a total of pieces in 15e’s sense: each piece of boundary is a curve whose area under it is a total, and the pieces add. The curves are not circles, so the totals are not simple; in 2016 the whole shape was written exactly as the solution of 22 equations in 22 unknowns.

sky24.5A Proof Awaiting Its Referees

The 2024 proof does not search shapes. As it has been described (this page has not checked the 119 pages), it works over the space of all sofas that turn by a quarter turn, and defines for each a quantity Q that is never less than its area and equals the area for the eighteen-piece shape. Q is built to behave better than area: as the shape changes, Q bends like a parabola opening downwards, so finding its largest value is a question of 28g’s kind, the best point under constraints, where the best point can be pinned down by its slopes. Showing that Q is largest at the eighteen-piece shape then gives: no sofa has more area.

\text{area}(S) \le Q(S) \text{ for every sofa } S @@ Q never underestimates
Q(G) = \text{area}(G) \text{ for the eighteen-piece shape } G @@ Q is exact there
Q(S) \le Q(G) \text{ for every } S @@ the long part: 119 pages, by hand
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area}(S) \le Q(S) \le Q(G) = \text{area}(G) \approx 2.2195} \quad \blacksquare @@ if the 119 pages hold

The proof was posted on 29 November 2024. In early 2026 it was reported to be under review at a leading journal. Early reactions from specialists were favourable. As far as this page could find, in November 2026 no journal has yet accepted it, so the page holds the result in trust: very probably right, not yet refereed.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞24.oq.1Is the 2024 proof right? It runs to 119 pages and uses no computer search, which makes it checkable by people, but checking it is a long task, and in November 2026 it is still with its referees.
Why it is hard: the proof’s key quantity Q is new, and every claim about how Q bends over the whole space of sofas must hold with no exceptions; one gap in one case would reopen the question.
∞24.oq.2A hallway with a right-angled corner to the left and then one to the right asks for a sofa that turns both ways. A shape of area about 1.64495 was found in 2016 and published in 2018, built by the same touching-every-wall rule. Is it the largest?
Why it is hard: the 2024 method was built for one turn; whether its quantity Q has a counterpart for two turns is not known.
∞24.oq.3Can the proof be checked by a machine, as 38·now’s proofs were? Nobody has announced a complete formal check.
Why it is hard: the proof works over a space of shapes and uses estimates on curves of many kinds, and the libraries that checkers rely on hold far less of this geometry than of algebra.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question was printed in 1966 in a journal’s problem column, though people had moved furniture round corners and wondered about it before. Two years later a short paper gave both the receiver and the ceiling of 2√2, and for a while that was felt to be most of the story: the receiver looked natural, and few expected more than a small improvement.

The improvement came in 1992, from a mathematician who asked what a best sofa must satisfy and built the shape that satisfies it. The eighteen-piece shape was better by about half a percent. He conjectured it was best, and the conjecture was widely believed, with the caution that it had been derived from conditions a best shape must meet, not from a proof that a best shape exists with those features.

From 2016 a mathematician in California, later with a coauthor, studied the problem with new tools: exact equations for the eighteen-piece shape, a left-and-right version, and in 2018 a computer-assisted ceiling of 2.37. Their method could lower the ceiling further with more computer time, but each step was costly and the gap to 2.2195 looked unlikely to close that way. Some thought a full answer would need a computer proof of the kind of 42·now; others hoped for an idea.

In November 2024 a researcher in Seoul, who had worked on the problem through his doctorate, posted a 119-page proof with no computer search. Reaction was quick and warm: magazines covered it in early 2025. Experts were careful to add that the proof had not yet been refereed, and that 119 new pages take time to check.

In January 2026 newspapers reported the paper under review at a leading journal. This page has found no report of acceptance since. What is argued now is less whether the result is true, which most believe, than what counts as settled: some say a public proof that specialists have read and found sound is settled in practice; others say a result enters the record when the referees sign, and that this one, however promising, is held in trust until they do.

The accepted names

This is the moving sofa problem. The receiver is Hammersley’s sofa, and the 2√2 ceiling is Hammersley’s upper bound. The eighteen-piece shape is Gerver’s sofa, its area Gerver’s constant, about 2.21953. The 2.37 ceiling is the Kallus–Romik bound. The left-and-right question is the ambidextrous moving sofa problem, and its candidate is Romik’s sofa. The 2024 paper is “Optimality of Gerver’s sofa”, arXiv:2411.19826.

Who said it first

Leo Moser posed the problem in SIAM Review (1966). John Hammersley gave the sofa and the upper bound (1968). Joseph Gerver found his sofa (1992). Dan Romik wrote it as 22 equations and found the ambidextrous sofa (2016, published 2018); Yoav Kallus and Dan Romik proved the 2.37 ceiling (2018). Jineon Baek posted the proof that Gerver’s sofa is best on 29 November 2024, and in January 2026 it was reported under review at the Annals of Mathematics.

A proof that is not allowed

“The sofa lies in the horizontal strip at the start and in the vertical strip at the end, so in their intersection, a unit square: area at most 1.” Not allowed: the strips are in the hallway’s frame, and a sofa that turns lies at the end in a strip that is turned in its own frame. The receiver, of area 2.2074, is the proof that the step fails.
