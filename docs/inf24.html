What Space Does a Ring Live On?

What space does a ring live on? Take the prime parts of a ring, as rung 44 defined them, and call them points. A member of the ring is then a function on those points: its value at a prime is its remainder there. The number 5 is a function on the primes of ℤ that vanishes at one point, the prime 5, and 12 vanishes at 2 and at 3. The closed sets are the points where some members all vanish, and they make a space, though a strange one: one point lies close to every other. Rung 44’s failed factoring becomes a picture of two points lying over one. And a point of an equation with values in any ring, read by rung 47’s arrows, is a solution in that ring, so solving on a clock and solving over ℚ are finding points of one space. Since 2011 spaces of this kind have been built to pass between clocks of prime powers and number systems like the line.

Points of R: its prime ideals. The value of w at P is w + P in R/P. Closed sets: V(I) = {P : I ⊆ P}; V(I) ∪ V(J) = V(IJ). Points of ℤ: (0) and (p); n vanishes at (p) iff p ∣ n. Points of ℂ[x]: (0) and (x − a); of ℝ[x], also (x² + 1). In ℤ[ω], ω² = −5: P² = (2), QQ′ = (3), one point over 2 and two over 3. Points of ℤ[x, y]/(x² + y² − 1) with values in R are solutions (a, b) in R.

inf24.1The Points of ℤ

44b4 called an ideal P prime when a product lies in P only if a factor does. In ℤ the prime ideals are (0) and (p) for each prime p. Call them the points of ℤ. A whole number n has a value at each point: at (p), its remainder n mod p in the clock of p, 43a2; at (0), n itself. The values live in different number systems at different points.

Vanish technique: n vanishes at the point (p) exactly when p divides n, and only 0 vanishes at (0).

n \bmod p = 0 \iff p \mid n @@ the remainder is 0 exactly when p divides
5:\ \text{vanishes at } (5) \text{ only} @@ 5 has one prime factor
12 = 2^2 \cdot 3:\ \text{vanishes at } (2), (3) @@ its prime factors
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the zeros of } n \ne 0 \text{ are the primes that divide it}} \quad \blacksquare @@ at (0) the value is n itself

12 vanishes at (2) twice over, as a function vanishes doubly where its graph touches the axis: 12 is 0 mod 2, and also 0 mod 4. The prime factoring of a number is the list of its zeros, each with how often.

inf24.2Closed Sets Make a Space

Call a set of points closed when it is all the points at which some members all vanish: V(I) = {P : I ⊆ P}. 34b1 asks that open sets, the rests of closed ones, be closed under unions and finite meets; for closed sets that is finite unions and any meets.

Closed-union technique: V(I) ∪ V(J) = V(IJ), so a union of two closed sets is closed.

P \supseteq I \text{ or } P \supseteq J \Rightarrow P \supseteq IJ @@ IJ is made of products, each inside I and inside J
P \supseteq IJ,\ P \not\supseteq I:\ \text{pick } w \in I \setminus P @@ suppose P misses I
wb \in P \text{ for every } b \in J \Rightarrow b \in P @@ P is prime and w is not in P
\htmlClass{new}{P \supseteq J,\ \text{so } V(IJ) = V(I) \cup V(J)} \quad \blacksquare @@ both directions

In ℤ the closed sets are the finite sets of primes and the whole space. So the point (0) lies in every open set that is not empty: it is close to every point at once, and the space does not keep points apart. That point stands for “in general”, a number before any remainder is taken.

inf24.3Points Over the Line

Now the polynomials in x over a field. Over ℂ, every polynomial splits into factors x − a, so the prime ideals are (0) and (x − a) for each complex a: the points are the complex numbers, and the value of f at (x − a) is f(a). Over ℝ there are more kinds of point.

Paired-point technique: in the polynomials over ℝ, (x² + 1) is a prime ideal, a single point that stands for the pair i, −i.

x^2 + 1 \text{ has no real root, so no factor } x - a @@ a real root would make it 0
fg \in (x^2 + 1) \Rightarrow x^2 + 1 \mid f \text{ or } x^2 + 1 \mid g @@ it cannot split, so it is a prime among polynomials
\text{value of } f \text{ at } (x^2 + 1) = f \bmod x^2 + 1 = f(i) \text{ read as } a + bi @@ the remainder is a + bx, and x² is −1
\htmlClass{new}{(x^2 + 1) \text{ is one point for } \pm i} \quad \blacksquare @@ f(i) = 0 exactly when f(−i) = 0, for real f

inf24.4Two Points Over One

44e2 found that in ℤ[ω], with ω² = −5, 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + ω)(1 − ω), two factorings into pieces that cannot split. 44e3 found prime ideals P, Q, Q′ with P² = (2) and QQ′ = (3). The map from ℤ into ℤ[ω] turns round into a map of spaces: a prime of ℤ[ω] meets ℤ in a prime of ℤ.

Lying-over technique: over the point (3) of ℤ lie two points Q and Q′ of ℤ[ω], over (2) lies one point P, and the two factorings of 6 are one product P²QQ′ grouped two ways.

Q \cap \mathbb{Z} \ni 3,\ \ 1 \notin Q \Rightarrow Q \cap \mathbb{Z} = (3) @@ a prime of ℤ holding 3; the same for Q′
P \cap \mathbb{Z} = (2) @@ P² = (2) puts 2 in P
(6) = (2)(3) = P^2 \cdot QQ' @@ 44e3
\htmlClass{new}{(1 + \omega)(1 - \omega) = PQ \cdot PQ' = P^2 QQ'} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same four points, grouped two ways

So the failure to factor uniquely in ℤ[ω] is a fact about the picture: the point (3) of ℤ has split into two points upstairs, and each number factor picks up only some of them.

inf24.5Points With Values in Any Ring

Take the circle’s equation x² + y² = 1 and the ring A = ℤ[x, y]/(x² + y² − 1). A ring map out of A is fixed by where it sends x and y, and it must send x² + y² − 1 to 0. This is 47g1’s arrows-from-one technique for rings instead of groups.

Points-are-solutions technique: the ring maps from A to a ring R match the solutions (a, b) of a² + b² = 1 in R.

\varphi(x) = a,\ \varphi(y) = b,\ \ a^2 + b^2 = 1 @@ a map out of A is a pair that satisfies the equation
R = \mathbb{Q}:\ (\tfrac35, \tfrac45) @@ 9/25 + 16/25 = 1
R = \mathbb{Z}/5:\ (0, \pm 1),\ (\pm 1, 0) @@ the squares mod 5 are 0, 1, 4, and 2 is not one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four points with values in } \mathbb{Z}/5} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked by trying all 25 pairs

So solving an equation on a clock, as rung 43 did, and solving it over ℚ are finding points of one space with values in two rings. Spaces made this way glue along open pieces, as surfaces were glued from pages: that is how spaces of this kind are built in general.

∞24.oq.1Can the bad points of every space over a finite field be smoothed out? For spaces given by equations over fields like the line’s numbers, this was done in 1964. For spaces over finite fields it is done in dimensions up to 3, the last case finished in 2008–9, and open from dimension 4 on.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1964 a mathematician showed that every space given by equations over a field like the line’s numbers can be changed, without touching its good points, into one with no bad points at all.

In 2011 a mathematician posted a theory of spaces that pass between the clocks of prime powers taken endlessly far and number systems of the line’s kind, invented to compare questions on the two sides.

The accepted names

The space of prime ideals is the spectrum Spec R with the Zariski topology; V(I) is the vanishing set, and (0) in Spec ℤ is the generic point. The value of w at P lies in the residue field. A point over another is a point in the fibre of Spec ℤ[√−5] → Spec ℤ; (2) ramifies and (3) splits. Points with values in R are R-points, and reading a space by its maps in is the functor of points. Spaces glued from spectra are schemes. Smoothing out bad points is resolution of singularities; the 2011 spaces are perfectoid spaces.

Who said it first

Alexander Grothendieck, schemes (date not yet revisited). Heisuke Hironaka, resolution of singularities in characteristic 0 (1964). Vincent Cossart and Olivier Piltant, dimension 3 in every characteristic (2008, 2009). Peter Scholze, “Perfectoid spaces” (arXiv 2011; published 2012).
