34f · proof

Surfaces classified

Rung 29 cut a closed surface into triangles, counted corners, edges and faces, and found that corners minus edges plus faces is fixed by the total bend of the surface: two for a ball, zero for a doughnut, two less for each hole. That proof needed a ruler. This step gets the same count with no ruler at all, from drawings on the sphere and from cutting a face or an edge in two. Then it builds surfaces the way a tailor builds a jacket: a flat polygon with its edges sewn together in pairs. A doughnut is a square sewn left to right and top to bottom. Sew one pair the wrong way round and the surface has only one side. With a few cutting and sewing moves, every closed surface comes down to one of a short list: a sphere, a sphere with handles, or a sphere with twisted caps. The count, together with whether the surface has one side or two, tells the list apart. The history of the count is a history of monsters, and the illegal moves are full of them.

A drawing in one piece on the sphere has corners minus edges plus faces equal to two, by taking edges away one at a time. Cutting an edge or a face in two never changes the count, so a surface has one count, its characteristic, whatever the cutting. A polygon with its edges sewn in pairs makes a closed surface whose characteristic is its corners left after sewing, minus its pairs, plus one. A pair sewn the same way round makes a twisted band, and the surface has one side. Every closed surface is a sphere, a sphere with handles, or a sphere with twisted caps, and the characteristic with the count of sides tells which.

34f1Drawings on the sphere

A drawing on the sphere is finitely many points, its corners, and finitely many paths between corners, its edges, which meet only at their ends. What is left of the sphere falls into pieces, its faces. V, E and F count them.

29f found V − E + F = 2 − 2g for every cutting of a closed surface into triangles, with total bend as the go-between. Here is a proof for the sphere that uses no ruler and no triangles. It holds one fact in trust: a loop drawn on the sphere from finitely many edges cuts it into two pieces, so an edge on a loop has a different face on each side. That fact is proved much higher up the ladder.

Sphere-drawing technique: a drawing on the sphere that is in one piece, with at least one corner, has V − E + F = 2.

E = 0:\ \ V = 1,\ F = 1,\ \ V - E + F = 2 @@ in one piece with no edges: one corner, and the rest of the sphere is one face
\text{some edge lies on a loop of edges} @@ the case with a loop
\text{take it away}:\ \ E \mapsto E - 1,\ \ F \mapsto F - 1 @@ held in trust: its two sides were different faces, and now merge; still in one piece
\text{no edge lies on a loop: some corner meets exactly one edge} @@ walk along edges without turning back; with no loop, the walk must stop
\text{take it and its edge away}:\ \ V \mapsto V - 1,\ \ E \mapsto E - 1 @@ the faces do not change; still in one piece
\htmlClass{new}{V - E + F = 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ each case keeps V − E + F; induction on E (4b)

A box from the hardware store in Bellingham, with its 8 corners, 12 edges and 6 faces, is a drawing on a sphere once its surface is pushed out round: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. So is a soccer ball, 60 − 90 + 32, and a map of the states and provinces of the coast pulled over a globe.

34f2The count does not see the cutting

A cutting of a closed surface is a drawing on it whose faces are each the same shape as an open disc. 29f used cuttings into triangles. Faces with more sides are allowed here.

Finer-cut technique: cutting an edge in two, cutting a face in two along a new edge, or adding a corner inside a face with one edge to its rim, keeps V − E + F.

\text{a new corner on an edge}:\ \ V + 1,\ E + 1 @@ one edge becomes two
\text{a new edge across a face}:\ \ E + 1,\ F + 1 @@ the face becomes two discs
\text{a new corner in a face, joined to its rim}:\ \ V + 1,\ E + 1 @@ the face stays one disc
\htmlClass{new}{V - E + F \text{ unchanged}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each move adds as much to one side as to the other

Two cuttings of one surface, with edges that cross each other only finitely often, have a common finer cutting: lay one over the other and add a corner at each crossing. Then the count is the same for both. This step holds in trust that any two cuttings can be put in that position, which is proved much higher up the ladder. 29f3 got the same fact from the total bend, for surfaces with a ruler.

So a closed surface S has one number, χ(S) = V − E + F for every cutting, its characteristic. A same-shape map carries a cutting to a cutting, so χ is a shape-property: the sphere has 2, and 29f’s surfaces with g holes have 2 − 2g.

34f3Sewing a polygon

Take a polygon with 2n edges, and pair the edges. Put an arrow on each edge, and a letter on each pair. Sew each pair so the arrows match: a glued space of 34b5. Read the letters round the rim, writing x⁻¹ where an edge runs against the reading. The word names the surface.

x x⁻¹ is the sphere: a two-sided bag zipped shut. x y x⁻¹ y⁻¹ is the torus of 34k: a square sewn left to right and top to bottom. x x is the cross-cap: a disc whose rim is sewn to itself with each point joined to the point straight across. x y x y⁻¹ is the one-sided bottle.

Word-count technique: a surface sewn from one polygon with 2n edges in n pairs, whose corners fall into V classes after sewing, has characteristic V − n + 1.

\text{one face: the inside of the polygon} @@ a disc, so the sewn polygon is a cutting
\text{n edges: one for each pair} @@ the two edges of a pair become one
\text{V corners: one for each class} @@ sewing joins corners into classes
\htmlClass{new}{\chi = V - n + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the characteristic of this cutting
  
x\,x^{-1}:\ \ V = 2,\ n = 1,\ \chi = 2 @@ the two corners stay apart: the sphere
x\,y\,x^{-1}\,y^{-1}:\ \ V = 1,\ n = 2,\ \chi = 0 @@ all four corners of the square sew to one: 29f’s doughnut, g = 1
x\,x:\ \ V = 1,\ n = 1,\ \chi = 1 @@ each edge’s start is sewn to the other’s start
\htmlClass{new}{x\,y\,x\,y^{-1}:\ \ V = 1,\ n = 2,\ \chi = 0} @@ the bottle has the torus’s count

A pair sewn the same way round, x … x, carries a strip across the polygon whose two ends are sewn with a half twist: a twisted band. Walk a small turning arrow along it and it comes home turning the other way, which is 20g’s flip. A surface holding a twisted band has one side. A surface whose every pair appears as x … x⁻¹ has two sides. Same-shape maps carry twisted bands to twisted bands, so the number of sides is a shape-property too. The bottle and the torus have the same count, and different numbers of sides.

34f4Every closed surface

Two surfaces are summed by cutting a small open disc out of each and sewing the two rims together. Sum-count technique: χ(S summed with T) = χ(S) + χ(T) − 2. Take a cutting of each with a triangle as one face, and take those two faces away: F drops by 2. Sew the rims: 3 corners and 3 edges of one are matched with 3 of the other, and V and E each drop by 3. So the count drops by 2.

Surface-list technique: every closed surface in one piece that can be cut into triangles is the same shape as exactly one of these: the sphere; g tori summed, for some g ≥ 1; k cross-caps summed, for some k ≥ 1. The first two kinds have two sides and χ = 2 − 2g; the third has one side and χ = 2 − k.

S \text{ cut into triangles} @@ held in trust: every closed surface can be, proved much higher up the ladder
\text{sew them into one polygon, its rim edges in pairs} @@ S is in one piece: each next triangle shares an edge with one already sewn
\ldots x\,x^{-1} \ldots \to \ldots @@ fold a neighbouring pair shut, unless the word is x x⁻¹: the sphere
\text{all corners in one class} @@ cut off a corner of a rarer class along a diagonal and sew it on along its pair; repeat
\ldots x \ldots x \ldots \to \ldots z\,z \ldots @@ cut along a diagonal between the two x’s and sew along x: a cross-cap side by side
\ldots x \ldots y \ldots x^{-1} \ldots y^{-1} \ldots \to \ldots u\,v\,u^{-1}\,v^{-1} \ldots @@ two cuts of the same kind; with one corner class, every x … x⁻¹ is crossed by such a y
u\,v\,u^{-1}\,v^{-1}\ \text{beside}\ z\,z \to \text{three same-way pairs} @@ a torus beside a cross-cap is three cross-caps: a cut-and-sew move carried out on 34k
\text{the word is } x_1y_1x_1^{-1}y_1^{-1}\cdots x_gy_gx_g^{-1}y_g^{-1} \text{ or } x_1x_1\cdots x_kx_k @@ each move cuts along a path and sews along an edge: the shape is kept
\htmlClass{new}{\chi = 2 - 2g \text{ or } 2 - k;\ \text{two sides or one}} \quad \blacksquare @@ word-count, one corner class; χ and the sides are shape-properties, so exactly one

So the sphere, the doughnut and every pretzel are the two-sided surfaces, sorted by their count alone. The one-sided ones are sorted by their count too: the cross-cap has 1, the bottle 0, and so on down. None of the one-sided closed surfaces can be built in space without passing through itself. That is proved much higher up the ladder. Every surface you can hold in your hand in Seattle has two sides.

The accepted names

The characteristic is the Euler characteristic, χ, and the sphere-drawing technique is Euler’s formula for polyhedra. A cutting is a cell decomposition; a cutting into triangles is a triangulation. A word is a polygon presentation of a surface. The sum is the connected sum, #. The cross-cap is the real projective plane, ℝP², and the one-sided bottle is the Klein bottle. The twisted band is the Möbius band; a surface with one side is non-orientable. The surface-list technique is the classification of closed surfaces, and the triangle fact held in trust is Radó’s theorem; the loop fact held in trust on 34f1 is the Jordan curve theorem.

Who said it first

René Descartes noted the count around 1630, and Leonhard Euler stated V − E + F = 2 in 1750–52. Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave a proof in 1813, and Simon Lhuilier answered in 1813 with solids that break it, the picture frame among them. August Möbius and Johann Listing found the one-sided band in 1858. Bernhard Riemann, Möbius and Camille Jordan sorted the two-sided surfaces in the 1860s; Walther von Dyck added the one-sided ones in 1888; Max Dehn and Poul Heegaard gave the first proof by cutting and sewing in 1907. Tibor Radó proved that every surface can be cut into triangles in 1925. Imre Lakatos told the whole story as a classroom dialogue in Proofs and Refutations (1963–64), with monster-barring, monster-adjustment and lemma-incorporation.

A proof that is not allowed

“Take any solid with flat faces. Remove one face and stretch the rest flat on a table. Remove edges one at a time; each removal takes away an edge and a face. So V − E + F = 2.” Not allowed: Cauchy’s proof, with Lhuilier’s answer. A picture frame with one face removed does not stretch flat on a table, and a face with a hole is not taken away by removing one edge. The proof used, without saying so, that the surface is a sphere.
