The rules of plus and times stand on their own

Every rung so far has added and multiplied: whole numbers, fractions, the line, polynomials, grids, the hours of a clock, true and false. This rung takes the rules of plus and times on their own, with nothing said about what the members are, and asks what follows from the rules alone. A collection with a plus and a times that keep those rules is a ring; one in which every member but 0 can be divided by is a field. Inside a ring, the multiples of a member, and more generally its ideals, let us make new rings as quotients, the way 41 made the clock from the whole numbers. Division with remainder, where a ring has it, gives unique factoring; in some rings factoring is not unique, and ideals repair it. A field can be stretched by adding a root, and the size of the stretch is a dimension, so the degrees multiply up a tower. The finite fields come one for each prime power. The line itself is the one regular number system with no holes. And the structure technique for modules sorts every finite group whose combining turns about, and, in the call back, every grid. The last steps work by hand: division and the count of where curves meet, stretched fields, and the algebra of true and false.

A ring is a collection with plus and times keeping the familiar rules, and a field one where every member but 0 can be divided by. Quotients by ideals make new rings, and a quotient is a field exactly when its ideal is largest. Division with remainder makes every ideal the multiples of one member, and then factoring is unique; where it is not, ideals factor uniquely instead. Fields stretch by roots, with degrees that multiply. There is one finite field of each prime-power size, its members other than 0 are the powers of one, and there is exactly one regular number system with no holes. Modules over a ring with one maker for every ideal split into clocks, and so do finite groups whose combining turns about.

What it means to say it

To say that the rules of plus and times stand on their own is to say that the familiar rules, that plus and times regroup, that plus turns about and can be undone, that 0 and 1 do nothing, and that times spreads over plus, are enough by themselves to carry the arguments. A proof that uses only these rules holds in every collection that keeps them: in the whole numbers, in polynomials, in grids, on clocks, and in collections no earlier rung has met.

Taken alone, the rules of plus and times define rings and fields, and factoring, quotients and extensions are moves inside them: a quotient by an ideal is a ring, and a field exactly when the ideal is largest; a ring in which every ideal has one maker factors uniquely; a field stretched by a root of an unsplittable polynomial of degree d has degree d, and degrees multiply; there is one finite field of each prime-power size; there is exactly one complete ordered field; and every finitely made module over a principal ring is a list of clocks and copies of the ring. The rung proves it, and some steps of the proof are held in trust where it says so.

Where it comes from

The rules of plus and times were first written down for their own sake in the 1800s, as algebra moved from solving equations to asking what equations are about. In 1847 a proof that no two pth powers add to a pth power failed because factoring in a ring of turnings is not unique, and the repair, ideal numbers, was given at the same time; ideals as sets of members came in 1871. Finite fields were described in 1830 by a young man killed in a duel two years later. The structure of finite groups that turn about was found in 1870 and 1878. The word for a ring is from 1897, the rules were set out as axioms in 1914 and 1921, and modules over principal rings became the standard road in the 1920s and 1930s. The names are in the basements of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things.

First, a ring is a collection with plus and times keeping a short list of rules, and a field is a ring that turns about in which every member but 0 has a reciprocal. A finite cancelling ring is a field.

Second, an ideal is a part of a ring closed under plus and under times by anything in the ring. Its classes form a ring, R/I, and what a ring map loses is an ideal whose quotient matches what it reaches. R/I has no zero products exactly when I is prime, and is a field exactly when I is largest, so ℤ/p is a field.

Third, polynomials over a field can be divided with remainder, so every ideal of F[x] is the multiples of one polynomial. In any ring where every ideal has one maker, unsplittable members are prime, and every member factors into unsplittables in one way, up to order and undoable factors.

Fourth, in ℤ[√−5], 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5), two factorings that cannot be paired off. Its ideals still factor in one way into prime ideals.

Fifth, a field holding a smaller field is a space over it, and adding a root of an unsplittable polynomial of degree d gives a field of degree d. Degrees multiply up a tower.

Sixth, every finite field has a prime-power size; there is one of every prime-power size; and its members other than 0 are the powers of one of them. There is also exactly one regular number system with no holes, the line, and it has no moves but doing nothing.

Seventh, every grid over ℤ or F[x] becomes diagonal by undoable moves, so every finitely made module over such a ring is a list of clocks and copies of the ring, and every finite group that turns about is a list of clocks d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₖ, in exactly one way.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 43j divides polynomials, finds their greatest common divisor by a remainder chain, tests by a single number whether two share a factor, and counts where two curves meet. 43k works in ℚ(√2) and ℚ(∛2): reciprocals by flipping signs and by grids, and the least polynomial of a mix. 43l finds the laws of true and false, their mirror, their normal forms and the ring where x² = x. The call back to rung 27 finds that every grid is nearly diagonal. The annexes read damaged messages, share a secret on a curve, find where a molecule’s electrons sit, find the few directions of most variation in genes, follow two cubes that never add to a cube, and ask whether today’s codes survive quantum computers.

What you can do because you know it

You can check whether a collection is a ring or a field, build new rings as quotients, and say when a quotient is a field. You can divide polynomials, factor them, and test whether one can be split. You can build the fields ℚ(√2), ℚ(∛2) and the finite fields of 4, 8 and 9 members, and compute in them. You can say why the line is the only one of its kind, and sort any finite group that turns about into clocks.

And you can say exactly where a technique stops: where factoring is not unique, which rules a proof spent, and which collections it therefore covers.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 4’s divisibility and primes, rung 8’s line, rung 25’s complex numbers, rung 27’s spaces, dimensions and grids, and rung 42’s clocks. Five things are held in trust: that ideals of ℤ[√−5] factor uniquely, on 43e; that there is only one field of each prime-power size, until rung 44; that a module’s list of clocks is its only one, on 43i, left as a standing question there; the exact count of where two curves meet, on 43j; and, on the call back, that the list of a grid’s nearly diagonal blocks comes from bringing xI − A to a diagonal. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say the rules of plus and times stand on their own, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
