Which Guess Makes the Data Most Likely?

Which guess makes the data most likely? A coin lands heads seven times in ten. A baseball team wins eighty-eight games of a hundred and sixty-two. A gardener counts round peas and wrinkled ones. Each time there is a chance nobody can see, and a record everybody can. People want the chance. The question is hard because every guess at the chance could have made the record: a fair coin can land heads seven times in ten, and so can a coin bent toward heads. No record proves the chance. But some guesses make the record seen far more likely than others do. This page writes the chance of the record as a function of the guess, and takes the guess where that function is greatest. Rung 14's slopes find it, and it is the share seen. Then the page asks how far the other guesses fall behind, and what happens when the best guess sits at an end, where the slope is not zero.

Fix the record and let the guess move: the chance of exactly what was seen, read as a function of the guess, is the likelihood. Its slope has the sign of the heads seen less the guess times the tosses. So it climbs up to the share seen, and falls after it. The best guess is the share seen. Other guesses make the record less likely, and how much less is part of the answer. When none of the tosses land heads, or the guess is held to a range, the best guess sits at an end, and there the slope is not zero.

14stat1The Chance of a Record

4·stat and 5·stat built chance by counting equally likely cases. A coin bent so that heads has chance 3/10 is like a spinner with ten equal slots, three of them marked heads. Toss it n times, each toss a fresh spin. Then every list of n slots is equally likely, and the lists can be counted.

Chance-of-a-record technique. Let each toss be one of b equally likely slots, a of them heads, so that p = h/b. Let every list of n slots be equally likely. Then a given order of k heads and n − k tails has chance pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, and k heads in any order have chance (n choose k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ.

\text{lists of } n \text{ slots: } b^n @@ counted-functions (4k): functions from n to b slots
\text{lists with heads in the given } k \text{ places: } h^k(b - h)^{n - k} @@ stages-multiply (4k), place by place
\tfrac{h^k(b - h)^{n-k}}{b^n} = \big(\tfrac ab\big)^k\big(1 - \tfrac ab\big)^{n-k} = p^k(1 - p)^{n-k} @@ chance as a fraction (5·stat); 5c's rules
\htmlClass{new}{\tbinom nk\, p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}} \text{ for } k \text{ heads in any order} \quad \blacksquare @@ the orders are separate piles, one for each k-member part (count-of-successes, 4·stat)

A real coin is no spinner, and its chance need not be a fraction. The ladder cannot yet say what a chance is when it is not a share of equally likely cases. So take the formula as the model for every p from 0 to 1: a guess that agrees with every fraction, and has no jumps between them. Its accepted name is the binomial model.

14stat2The Likelihood

From here on, on this page, the record is k heads in n tosses, and it is fixed. The guess p moves over [0, 1]. The likelihood is L(p) = (n choose k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ: the chance of the record seen, if the coin's chance were p.

The likelihood is the chance of what was seen, read as a function of the guess. It is not the chance that the guess is right. For 7 heads in 10, L(0.5) ≈ 0.117 and L(0.7) ≈ 0.267. A coin with chance 0.7 makes the record more than twice as likely as a fair one does. That says nothing yet about which coin it was.

The best guess is a p in [0, 1] where L is greatest. One exists: L is a polynomial, so continuous on the closed interval [0, 1], and highest-and-lowest (12c) says it reaches a greatest value. 14a's candidates say where to look: at the two ends, and at inputs inside where the slope is 0.

14stat3The Slope of the Likelihood

\big(p^k\big)' = k\,p^{k-1} @@ power for slopes (13d)
\big((1 - p)^{n-k}\big)' = (n - k)(1 - p)^{n-k-1} \cdot (-1) @@ the chain rule (13j): the inside 1 − p has slope −1
L'(p) = \tbinom nk\big(k\,p^{k-1}(1 - p)^{n-k} - (n - k)\,p^k(1 - p)^{n-k-1}\big) @@ product for slopes (13c); constant-factor
= \tbinom nk\, p^{k-1}(1 - p)^{n-k-1}\big(k(1 - p) - (n - k)p\big) @@ take out the common factor
\htmlClass{new}{L'(p) = \tbinom nk\, p^{k-1}(1 - p)^{n-k-1}(k - np)} @@ the bracket: k − kp − np + kp

Inside (0, 1), every factor before the bracket is positive. So the slope of L, 13i2’s three factors at work, has the sign of k − np. It is positive while p < k/n, 0 at p = k/n, and negative after.

Seen-share technique. Let 1 ≤ k ≤ n − 1. The guess that makes k heads in n tosses most likely is the share seen, k/n. The slope is 0 inside (0, 1) only at k/n, positive before it and negative after, so first-test (14f) makes k/n a top. One-critical-point (14f) makes it the greatest value on (0, 1). The ends give L(0) = L(1) = 0, less than L(k/n). ∎

\text{7 heads in 10}:\ L'(p) = 120\,p^6(1 - p)^2(7 - 10p) @@ zero inside only at 0.7
L(0.7) = 120 \cdot 0.7^7 \cdot 0.3^3 \approx 0.267 @@ the best; L(0.5) ≈ 0.117
\text{Seattle Mariners, 2023: 88 wins in 162} @@ best guess at the chance of a win: 88/162 ≈ 0.543
\text{a coin tossed 10\,000 times by hand: 5067 heads} @@ best guess 0.5067
\text{peas, 1866: 5474 round of 7324} @@ best guess ≈ 0.747, close to the 3/4 the gardener expected

14stat4How Far Behind the Other Guesses Fall

The best guess is one number. The record allows others. A plain way to measure how far behind a guess falls is the ratio L(p)/L(k/n), at most 1. For 7 heads in 10 the fair coin's ratio is about 0.44: it makes the record less than half as likely, but far from impossible. Halving (12e) on L(p) − L(0.7)/10 finds where the ratio falls to a tenth: at about 0.37 and about 0.93. Every guess between makes the record at least a tenth as likely as the best guess does.

More tosses narrow the stretch. For the 10 000 tosses with 5067 heads, the fair coin's ratio is about 0.41: a fair coin is no worse a fit for 10 000 tosses than for 10. But a coin with chance 0.53 has ratio below a hundredth. For the pea counts, the ratio at 3/4 is about 0.88.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting

4k set beside counting a principle of the second kind of care: random things in a crowd make smooth, steady patterns. One toss can land either way. Ten thousand tosses pile up near one share, and the likelihood gathers into a narrow peak around it. Ten tosses leave a wide hump.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it.

“The best guess is 0.7” says where the hump peaks. The stretch from 0.37 to 0.93 says how wide it is. A report of the peak alone treats ten tosses like ten thousand.

A coin tossed 10 000 times landed heads 5067 times. The best guess is 0.5067, not 0.5. Is the coin fair?

14stat5When the Best Guess Sits at an End

The seen-share technique left out k = 0 and k = n. Take k = 0: no heads at all. Then L(p) = (1 − p)ⁿ, and its slope is −n(1 − p)ⁿ⁻¹, negative on [0, 1). By slope-sign-order (14d), L falls all the way. The best guess is p = 0, at an end, and the slope there is −n, not 0. Flat-top (14a) promised a slope of 0 only at a top inside the interval.

A real case. In the 44 years from 1981 to 2024, Mount St. Helens had no eruption as large as that of 18 May 1980. Count each year as a toss. The best guess at the chance of such an eruption in a year is 0. A best guess at an end can say “never” after a record that only says “not yet”. The spread tells the rest: every chance up to about 0.05 a year makes the record at least a tenth as likely as 0 does.

A guess can also be held to a range on purpose. Suppose a coin is known to be bent toward heads: p ≥ ½. It lands heads 4 times in 10. On [½, 1], k − np = 4 − 10p is negative, so L falls. End-guess technique: when the share seen lies outside the range the guess is held to, the best guess is the end of the range nearest the share. Here it is ½, where the slope of L is about −0.82.

14stat6Two Records of One Coin

Toss a coin 10 times and see 7 heads; toss it 30 more and see 9. Each record has its likelihood. Fresh spins keep every list equally likely, so the chance of both records is the product of their chances. The product is a constant times p¹⁶(1 − p)²⁴, and its best guess is 16/40 = 0.4. Two records of one coin are one record: the best guess is the share seen in both together. It is not the average of the two shares, 0.7 and 0.3, which is 0.5.

14stat8Running It Backwards

Handed a best guess and the number of tosses, the heads are their product: 0.35 from 20 tosses means 7 heads. Handed the slope of a likelihood written as a constant times p to a power, times (1 − p) to a power, times a bracket, read the powers back: they are k − 1 and n − k − 1. Handed a best guess at an end, read the record: a best guess of 0 on [0, 1] means no heads at all.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli asked in 1778 for the guess that makes a set of observations most probable. Carl Friedrich Gauss used the same idea in 1809 to justify 13·stat’s least squares. Ronald Fisher made it a method in 1912 and named the likelihood in 1921 and 1922. The 10 000 tosses are John Kerrich’s, made by hand while he was held prisoner in Denmark during the Second World War, and published in 1946. The pea counts are Gregor Mendel’s, from his papers of 1866 (5·bio).

Fisher insisted that likelihood is not a probability. It does not add up to one over the guesses, and it obeys none of the laws of chance; it only ranks the guesses by how well each accounts for what was seen.

after R. A. Fisher, “On the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics” (1922); paraphrase

In 14·stat.9.4, the claim that two people with likelihoods that differ by a constant factor must reach the same verdict has the accepted name the likelihood principle; Allan Birnbaum argued for it in 1962. The 9 heads and 3 tails are the example of Dennis Lindley and Lawrence Phillips (1976): a test that counts tosses fixed in advance and a test that counts until the third tail give different verdicts on a fair coin. The way of stopping is the stopping rule.

For a record with no heads, Pierre-Simon Laplace proposed in 1814 to guess (k + 1)/(n + 2) instead of k/n, and asked with it the chance that the sun rises tomorrow; its accepted name is the rule of succession.

Accepted names

The likelihood function; the best guess is the maximum likelihood estimate, written p̂, and the method is maximum likelihood. The model of k heads in n fresh tosses is the binomial distribution; the model of the crabber’s pots, failures until the first success, is the geometric distribution. The guesses within a set ratio of the best form a likelihood interval.
