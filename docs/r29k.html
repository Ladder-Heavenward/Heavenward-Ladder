29k · technique

Curvature by hand

Rung twenty nine measured the bend of a curve when it is traced at a steady speed of one, and the bend of a surface through two main bends and their product. Real curves rarely come at speed one. A spring, a spiral staircase and the double strand of a living cell are traced by turning and climbing at whatever pace the formula gives. This step finds the bend of a curve traced at any speed, from two slopes and a cross. Then it finds the surface bend of any hillside written as height over a map, at every point and not only at the top. Last it takes surfaces made by spinning a curve, like a doughnut or a tyre, and finds both of their main bends at every point, so that the outside and the inside of a ring can be told apart by hand.

A curve traced at any speed bends by the size of the cross of its first two slopes, over the cube of its speed. A hillside written as height over a map has a surface bend at every point, from its second slopes and its tilt. A spun surface bends along its curve and around its axis: a bowl outside a ring, and a saddle inside.

29k1The bend of a curve at any speed

Let γ(t) be a curve in space whose slope γ′(t) is never 0. Its speed is v = |γ′|, and its direction T = γ′/v has length 1. The distance travelled s grows at ds/dt = v (18i). 29e1 measured the bend as κ = |dT/ds|, the turning per unit of distance.

Any-speed-bend technique: a curve γ(t) with γ′ never 0 has bend κ = |γ′ × γ″| / |γ′|³. In the plane this is |x′y″ − y′x″| / (x′² + y′²)^(3/2), and for a graph y = f(x) it is |f″| / (1 + f′²)^(3/2).

\gamma^{\prime} = v\,T,\ \ \gamma^{\prime\prime} = v^{\prime}\,T + v\,\tfrac{dT}{dt} @@ slope of a number times an arrow, place by place (13)
\gamma^{\prime}\times\gamma^{\prime\prime} = v\,v^{\prime}\,(T\times T) + v^2\,\big(T\times\tfrac{dT}{dt}\big) = v^2\,\big(T\times\tfrac{dT}{dt}\big) @@ the cross is straight in each slot, and T × T = 0 (27i3)
\big|T\times\tfrac{dT}{dt}\big| = \big|\tfrac{dT}{dt}\big| @@ an arrow of length 1 moves at right angles to itself (27i4, 29e1); the cross of arrows at right angles has the product of their sizes (27i3)
\tfrac{dT}{dt} = \tfrac{dT}{ds}\,v @@ the chain technique (13), with ds/dt = v
|\gamma^{\prime}\times\gamma^{\prime\prime}| = v^3\,\kappa @@ the lines above
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa = \frac{|\gamma^{\prime}\times\gamma^{\prime\prime}|}{|\gamma^{\prime}|^3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by v³; in the plane the cross is (0, 0, x′y″ − y′x″), and a graph is x = t, y = f(t)
  
\gamma = (a\cos t,\ a\sin t,\ bt) @@ a helix of radius a, climbing 2πb each turn
\gamma^{\prime} = (-a\sin t,\ a\cos t,\ b),\ \ \gamma^{\prime\prime} = (-a\cos t,\ -a\sin t,\ 0) @@ slopes of cos and sin (21g)
\gamma^{\prime}\times\gamma^{\prime\prime} = (ab\sin t,\ -ab\cos t,\ a^2),\ \ |\cdot| = a\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} @@ the cross technique, and sin² + cos² = 1 (21c)
\kappa = \frac{a\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}{(a^2 + b^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} @@ the any-speed-bend technique, the same at every t
\text{DNA: } a \approx 1.0\ \text{nm},\ \ 2\pi b \approx 3.4\ \text{nm},\ \ b \approx 0.541\ \text{nm} @@ the double helix: radius about a nanometre, about 3.4 nm per turn
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa \approx 0.774\ \text{per nm},\ \ \text{hugging circle radius} \approx 1.29\ \text{nm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the climb straightens it: the hugging circle is wider than the helix

The circle that hugs a curve best at a point has radius 1/κ. For the graph y = x²/(2R) at 0, f′ = 0 and f″ = 1/R, so κ = 1/R: near its bottom, the parabola is hugged by the circle of radius R. That is how a road engineer turns a design curve into a radius, and why a ramp drawn as a parabola can be signed with a single safe speed near its tightest point.

29k2The bend of a hillside at every point

29e3 found the surface bend of z = f(x, y) at a point where the surface is level, as fxxfyy − fxy², the determinant of 28l’s grid of second slopes. On a slope, the ruler and the outward arrow both tilt, and the formula changes.

Graph-bend technique: the surface z = f(x, y) has surface bend K = (fxxfyy − fxy²) / (1 + fx² + fy²)² at every point.

r = (x,\ y,\ f),\ \ r_x = (1,\ 0,\ f_x),\ \ r_y = (0,\ 1,\ f_y) @@ the graph as a patch, with u = x and v = y (29d1)
E = 1 + f_x^2,\ \ F = f_x f_y,\ \ G = 1 + f_y^2,\ \ EG - F^2 = 1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2 = W^2 @@ the dots; multiply out, and f_x²f_y² cancels
r_x\times r_y = (-f_x,\ -f_y,\ 1),\ \ n = \tfrac{1}{W}(-f_x,\ -f_y,\ 1) @@ the cross technique (27i3); its length is W
r_{xx} = (0, 0, f_{xx}),\ \ r_{xy} = (0, 0, f_{xy}),\ \ r_{yy} = (0, 0, f_{yy}) @@ mixed slopes agree (28d)
L = \tfrac{f_{xx}}{W},\ \ M = \tfrac{f_{xy}}{W},\ \ N = \tfrac{f_{yy}}{W} @@ the bending grid (29e2): only the last place of n counts
\htmlClass{new}{K = \frac{LN - M^2}{EG - F^2} = \frac{f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2}{(1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the surface-bend technique (29e3)
  
f = xy:\ \ f_x = y,\ f_y = x,\ f_{xx} = f_{yy} = 0,\ f_{xy} = 1 @@ the saddle of 28l
K = \frac{-1}{(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2} @@ the graph-bend technique
K(0, 0) = -1,\ \ K(1, 1) = -\tfrac19,\ \ K(10, 0) = -\tfrac{1}{10\,201} @@ sharpest at the centre, nearly flat far out
f = 919 - \tfrac{x^2}{2000} + \tfrac{y^2}{1000}\ \ (\text{metres}) @@ a smooth model of Snoqualmie Pass, the road running along x and the ridge along y
K(0, 0) = \big(-\tfrac{1}{1000}\big)\cdot\tfrac{1}{500} = -2\times10^{-6}\ \text{per m}^2 @@ level at the pass, so the bottom is 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{main bends } -\tfrac{1}{1000} \text{ and } \tfrac{1}{500}:\ \text{circles of } 1000\ \text{m and } 500\ \text{m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the road falls away both ways from the pass; the ridge rises both ways from it

The surface bend of the saddle is −1 at its centre, like the disc plane, but only there. Far out it flattens. 29k.4.1 asks for a surface that bends by −1 at every point.

29k3The two bends of a ring

29f2 spun a curve, traced at speed 1 as (ρ(u), z(u)), about the upright axis, and found from the ruler alone that K = −ρ″/ρ. From outside, the surface bends in two main ways, and the product hides which is which. On a doughnut the bends tell the outside from the inside.

Spun-bends technique: on a spun surface, the main bends at each point are ρ′z″ − z′ρ″ along the spun curve and z′/ρ around the axis, and their product is −ρ″/ρ.

r_u = (\rho^{\prime}\cos v,\ \rho^{\prime}\sin v,\ z^{\prime}),\ \ r_v = (-\rho\sin v,\ \rho\cos v,\ 0) @@ the patch of 29f2: E = 1, F = 0, G = ρ²
r_u\times r_v = \rho\,n,\ \ n = (-z^{\prime}\cos v,\ -z^{\prime}\sin v,\ \rho^{\prime}) @@ the cross technique (27i3); n has length 1, since ρ′² + z′² = 1
L = r_{uu}\cdot n = \rho^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime} - z^{\prime}\rho^{\prime\prime},\ \ M = r_{uv}\cdot n = 0,\ \ N = r_{vv}\cdot n = \rho\,z^{\prime} @@ r_uu = (ρ″ cos v, ρ″ sin v, z″), r_uv = ρ′(−sin v, cos v, 0), r_vv = −ρ(cos v, sin v, 0)
b(\theta) = L\cos^2\theta + \tfrac{N}{\rho^2}\sin^2\theta @@ the normal-bend technique (29e2) along cos θ r_u + sin θ r_v/ρ, of length 1
\text{main bends } L \text{ and } \tfrac{N}{\rho^2} = \tfrac{z^{\prime}}{\rho} @@ b(θ) is a share of one plus the rest of the other, so it lies between them
L\,\tfrac{z^{\prime}}{\rho} = \tfrac{\rho^{\prime}z^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime} - z^{\prime 2}\rho^{\prime\prime}}{\rho} = \tfrac{-\rho^{\prime 2}\rho^{\prime\prime} - z^{\prime 2}\rho^{\prime\prime}}{\rho} @@ ρ′ρ″ + z′z″ = 0, the slope of ρ′² + z′² = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa_1\kappa_2 = -\rho^{\prime\prime}/\rho} \quad \blacksquare @@ ρ′² + z′² = 1 again; it agrees with 29f2, as the remarkable technique (29e4) says it must
  
\rho = 4 + 2\cos\tfrac{u}{2},\ \ z = 2\sin\tfrac{u}{2}\ \ (\text{cm}) @@ a circle of radius 2 about a centre 4 from the axis, at speed 1; put t = u/2
\rho^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime} - z^{\prime}\rho^{\prime\prime} = \tfrac12(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t) = \tfrac12 @@ the bend around the tube is 1/2 everywhere: the circle of radius 2
\tfrac{z^{\prime}}{\rho} = \tfrac{\cos t}{4 + 2\cos t} @@ the bend around the axis
\text{outside } (t = 0):\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac16,\ \ K = \tfrac{1}{12};\ \ \text{inside } (t = \pi):\ \tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ \ K = -\tfrac14 @@ the spun-bends technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{top and bottom } (t = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}):\ \tfrac12,\ 0,\ \ K = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the outside is a bowl, the inside a saddle, and the two circles between are flat in one direction

Around the axis, a point of the bagel travels on a circle of radius ρ, which bends by 1/ρ. The surface bends by only z′/ρ that way, because the circle leans out of the surface: at the top of the bagel, the circle lies flat on the surface and the surface does not bend along it at all.

The accepted names

The any-speed-bend technique is the curvature formula κ = |r′ × r″| / |r′|³; in the plane, κ = |x′y″ − y′x″| / (x′² + y′²)^(3/2). The hugging circle is the osculating circle, its radius 1/κ the radius of curvature and its centre the centre of curvature. A curve’s twisting out of its plane, which a helix also has, is its torsion. The graph-bend technique is the Gaussian curvature of a Monge patch. A spun surface is a surface of revolution; its curve is the profile, or meridian, and its main bends are along the meridians and the parallels, its lines of curvature. The surface of the build challenge is the pseudosphere, spun from a tractrix. A surface with K = 0 everywhere is developable: it can be unrolled flat.

Who said it first

Christiaan Huygens studied the circle that hugs a curve in 1673, for his pendulum clocks. Isaac Newton gave the radius of curvature of a curve in his work on fluxions around 1671, and Gottfried Leibniz named the hugging circle the osculating (kissing) circle in 1686. Leonhard Euler studied the bends of surfaces along every direction in 1760, and found the two main ones at right angles. Jean Baptiste Meusnier related the bend of a slanted curve on a surface to the normal bend in 1776. Eugenio Beltrami used the pseudosphere in 1868 to show that a piece of the geometry of the disc plane sits in ordinary space. Rosalind Franklin’s X-ray photographs and the model of James Watson and Francis Crick fixed the shape of the double helix in 1953.
