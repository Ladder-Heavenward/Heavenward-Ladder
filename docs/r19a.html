19a · proof

Groups Defined

Rung 9 composed functions, and ran them backwards when it could. This step asks what a family of moves needs so that any two of them can be done one after the other, any one of them can be undone, and doing nothing is one of them. Those three demands, and one about brackets, are all there is. What comes out is the first object on the ladder made of nothing but what its members do to one another.

A group is a statement that names a set with one way of combining two members into a member. Combining three gives the same however they are grouped. One member does nothing when combined with any other. And each member has an undoer, which combines with it to give the one that does nothing.

19a1Moves That Combine

Swap two cups on a table. Turn a wheel. Shuffle three cards. Each is a move: it takes things where they are and puts them somewhere. Do one move and then another, and the two together are again a move. That is the first demand.

From here on, a shuffle of a finite set A is a function from A to A that is one-to-one and onto (9c). For A = {1, 2, 3}, the list [2 3 1] names the shuffle that sends 1 to 2, 2 to 3 and 3 to 1. The swap sᵢⱼ trades i and j and leaves every other member where it is. Its accepted name is a permutation.

From here on, an operation on a set G is a function that takes two members a, b of G, in order, to one member of G, written ab. For shuffles, ab is the composite a ∘ b of 9b: do b, then a.

Shuffles-compose technique: a shuffle done after a shuffle is a shuffle.

19a2Brackets Do Not Matter

The second demand is about three moves. Do c, then b, then a. You can fuse b and c into one move bc and then do a; or do c and then the fused move ab. The demand is that both ways give the same move.

From here on, an operation regroups when (ab)c = a(bc) for all a, b, c in G, as + and × did on rung 4. Its accepted name is the associative law.

Composing moves always regroups, so every family of moves meets this demand for free. That is 9b’s regroup technique for composing: both sides send x to a(b(c(x))).

Numbers can fail it. Subtraction on ℤ is an operation, and (5 − 3) − 1 = 1 while 5 − (3 − 1) = 3.

19a3Doing Nothing, and Undoing

The last two demands. One member does nothing: combined with any member, on either side, it changes nothing. And every member can be undone: for each a there is a member that, combined with a on either side, gives the one that does nothing.

A group is a statement that names a set of moves that combine, can be undone, and include doing nothing.

From here on, a group is a statement that names a set G with an operation such that: (ab)c = a(bc) for all a, b, c in G; there is a member e with ea = ae = a for every a; and each a has a member a⁻¹ with aa⁻¹ = a⁻¹a = e. e is the identity of G (not 17e’s number e), and a⁻¹ is the inverse of a. Its accepted name is a group.

Shuffles-form-a-group technique: the shuffles of any set, composed, form a group. The group of the shuffles of {1, …, n} is written Sₙ. By 4k it has n! members.

19a4Which Families Pass

Turns of a wheel are moves too. Turn by a fraction of a whole turn, then by another fraction, and the wheel has turned by the sum. A whole turn puts every spoke back where it was, so it does nothing. The turns by whole numbers of twelfths make a group of 12 members. The quarter turns, written e, q, q², q³, make a group of 4.

\mathbb{Z},\ + @@ a group: 0 does nothing, −a undoes a, + regroups
\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\},\ \times @@ a group: 1 does nothing, 1/a undoes a
\{e, q, q^2, q^3\} @@ a group: q⁴ is a whole turn, which does nothing, so q³ undoes q
\mathbb{N},\ + @@ not a group: nothing in ℕ undoes 1, since 1 + n ≥ 1
\mathbb{Z},\ \times @@ not a group: nothing in ℤ undoes 2, since 2n = 1 has no answer in ℤ
\mathbb{R},\ \times @@ not a group: 0 · x = 0 ≠ 1, so nothing undoes 0

Each failure is one demand missing, and each is fixed by what an earlier rung added. Rung 5 put the negatives into ℤ so that every adding could be undone, and the fractions into ℚ so that every multiplying by a non-zero could be.

19a5When Order Matters

In ℤ, a + b = b + a: rung 4’s turn-about technique. In S₃ it fails: s₁₂s₂₃ = [2 3 1] and s₂₃s₁₂ = [3 1 2]. In a group, ab and ba can be different moves. A sock and then a shoe is not a shoe and then a sock.

From here on, a group is turn-about when ab = ba for all a and b in it. ℤ under +, the non-zero reals under ×, and the turns of a wheel are turn-about. S₃ is not.

Every group of numbers the ladder had built so far was turn-about. S₃ is the smallest group that is not, and a standing question on 19d asks why.

The accepted names

A turn-about group is called commutative, or abelian, after Niels Henrik Abel. The table of a small group, every product written out, is its Cayley table, after Arthur Cayley, who gave the first definition of a group made only of how its members combine (1854). Sₙ is the symmetric group. A shuffle written as a loop, (1 2 3) for 1 to 2 to 3 to 1, is in cycle notation; the ladder keeps the list.

Who said it first

In 19a.4.5, the teacher of algebra is George Peacock, whose principle of the permanence of equivalent forms (1830) asked new algebras to keep the old rules. The mathematician of 1843 is William Rowan Hamilton, whose quaternions carved i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1 into a bridge in Dublin.
