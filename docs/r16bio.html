16·bio · biology

How much of a dose is still in the blood?

How much of a dose is still in the blood? A pill is swallowed, and over the next hour or so the drug soaks from the gut into the blood. All the while the liver and the kidneys are taking it out again. Nobody can see inside the body's books. A nurse can draw blood and measure how much drug is in each litre, but only now and then, and only as a level, never as an amount. What a doctor wants is how much of the dose the body took up at all, how much is still in it now, and how long the level stays where it helps without harming. The hard part is that the drug is coming in and going out at the same time, at rates nobody measures. This page finds the amount taken up as the area under the curve of blood level against time, by running a rate backwards. From a table of samples, thin strips estimate that area, and the samples leave a tail behind them. And two pills with the same area turn out not to do the same thing.

The body clears the drug at a rate in proportion to its level in the blood. So the amount cleared up to any hour is that fixed proportion times the area under the level so far. Run to the end, everything taken up is cleared, so the amount taken up is the proportion times the whole area. Samples give the area by thin strips, and the time after the last sample adds a tail. Stretch a level curve so that it lasts longer and rises less high, and its area stays the same. So the area tells how much was taken up, but not how high the level went or for how long.

The body's books

From here on, on this page, C(t) is the level of the drug in the blood, in milligrams a litre, t hours after the pill. Q(t) is the amount of drug in the body, in milligrams, and a(t) the rate at which it comes in from the gut, in milligrams an hour.

The law, measured, with a range: the body clears the drug at a rate in proportion to its level, Cl · C(t) milligrams an hour. The fixed number Cl, in litres an hour, is the clearance. The law holds for most drugs at ordinary doses. It fails where the liver works flat out: alcohol, past a drink or so, is cleared at about a fixed amount an hour, whatever its level.

Dose-area technique: the amount the body took up is the clearance times the whole area under the blood level. Precisely: let Q(0) = 0, let a and C be continuous, and let Q′ = a − Cl · C. Let the amount taken up head to F · D as t grows, where D is the dose and F the share that reaches the blood, and let Q head to 0. Then F · D = Cl · ∫₀^∞ C(t) dt.

Q(T) = Q(0) + \textstyle\int_0^T \big(a(t) - Cl \cdot C(t)\big)\,dt @@ net-change (16·then), with Q′ = a − Cl·C
\textstyle\int_0^T a = Q(T) + Cl \int_0^T C @@ Q(0) = 0; areas-add, areas-scale (15e): what came in is what is still there plus what went out
T \to \infty:\ \ \textstyle\int_0^T a \to F \cdot D,\ \ Q(T) \to 0 @@ the hypotheses: the dose is taken up and cleared in the end
\htmlClass{new}{F \cdot D = Cl \cdot \textstyle\int_0^\infty C(t)\,dt} \quad \blacksquare @@ so the areas up to T head to F·D/Cl: the improper integral (16i)

Its accepted name is the area under the curve, AUC. The units check: milligram hours a litre, times litres an hour, is milligrams. F is the bioavailability. Morphine swallowed as a pill reaches the blood at roughly a third of the dose, since the liver breaks much of it down on the way in.

A pill, worked

A model of one pill: C(t) = 8t/(1 + t)³ mg/L, with clearance Cl = 5 L/h. The level starts at 0, rises, and falls away.

\textstyle\int_0^\infty \frac{8t}{(1 + t)^3}\,dt = 8 \int_1^\infty \frac{u - 1}{u^3}\,du @@ substitution (16d) with u = 1 + t, so du = dt; the ends 0 and ∞ go to 1 and ∞
= 8\Big(\textstyle\int_1^\infty u^{-2}\,du - \int_1^\infty u^{-3}\,du\Big) @@ areas-add (15e)
= 8\big(1 - \tfrac12\big) = \htmlClass{new}{4} \text{ mg·h/L} @@ out-to-forever (16i): 1/(p − 1) at p = 2 and at p = 3
F \cdot D = 5 \cdot 4 = \htmlClass{new}{20} \text{ mg} @@ dose-area; if the pill held 25 mg, F = 0.8

How much is still in? The antiderivative of (u − 1)/u³ is −1/u + 1/(2u²), so the area up to T is 4(1 − 1/(1 + T))². At T = 3 that is 4 · (3/4)² = 2.25 mg·h/L. The body has cleared 5 · 2.25 = 11.25 mg of the 20 it will take up. The other 8.75 mg is still in the body, or still in the gut on its way in. The books cannot tell those two apart without a(t).

C'(t) = \tfrac{8(1 + t)^3 - 8t \cdot 3(1 + t)^2}{(1 + t)^6} = \tfrac{8(1 - 2t)}{(1 + t)^4} @@ the quotient rule (13i); cancel (1 + t)²
C' \gt 0 \text{ on } [0, \tfrac12),\ \ C' \lt 0 \text{ after} @@ the sign of 1 − 2t
C(\tfrac12) = \tfrac{4}{27/8} = \tfrac{32}{27} \approx \htmlClass{new}{1.19} \text{ mg/L} @@ crest (14f): the highest level, half an hour in

From a table of samples

A lab cannot draw a curve. It draws blood at set times. Here are samples of the model above, to three figures, as a lab would take them. Each strip takes the average of the levels at its two ends, the averaged strip sum: the left and right sums of 15h, halved and added.

C:\ 0,\ 1.185,\ 1,\ 0.768,\ 0.593,\ 0.375,\ 0.256,\ 0.140,\ 0.088,\ 0.044 @@ mg/L
\textstyle\sum \Delta t_i\, \tfrac{C_{i-1} + C_i}{2} \approx \htmlClass{new}{3.31} \text{ mg·h/L} @@ the averaged strip sum to 12 h; the true area to 12 h is about 3.41
\text{tail: } C \approx \tfrac{k}{t^2},\ k = 0.044 \cdot 144 \approx 6.34;\ \ \textstyle\int_{12}^\infty \tfrac{k}{t^2}\,dt = \tfrac{k}{12} \approx 0.53 @@ a guess at the shape after the last sample; out-to-forever (16i)
\text{total} \approx 3.31 + 0.53 = 3.84 \text{ mg·h/L} @@ against 4: the strips cut the peak short, and the tail is a guess

The last sample is not the end. After 12 hours the level is small, but it goes on for a long time, and here the time after the last sample holds about a seventh of the whole area. Every lab report of an area says how it filled in that tail.

Same area, different pill

Stretched-curve technique: stretch a level curve to last k times longer and stand k times lower, and its area stays the same. Precisely: for k > 0 let C_k(t) = (1/k) C(t/k). Then ∫₀^∞ C_k = ∫₀^∞ C, and C_k reaches its highest level k times later and k times lower.

\textstyle\int_0^T \tfrac1k C\big(\tfrac tk\big)\,dt = \int_0^{T/k} C(s)\,ds @@ straight-inside (16d), s = t/k, so dt = k ds
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^\infty C_k = \int_0^\infty C} \quad \blacksquare @@ as T heads to ∞, so does T/k (16i)
C_k(k t_0) = \tfrac1k C(t_0) @@ the crest moves from t₀ to kt₀, and stands k times lower

A slow-release pill of the same drug is made to dissolve slowly. Model it as k = 2: C₂(t) = 2t/(1 + t/2)³. Same area, 4 mg·h/L, so the same 20 mg taken up. Its crest is 32/54 ≈ 0.59 mg/L, an hour in. Now let the drug help above 0.5 mg/L and harm above 1 mg/L.

C(t) = 1 \iff 8t = (1 + t)^3 \iff (t - 1)(t^2 + 4t - 1) = 0 @@ the quick pill at the harmful level
t = \sqrt5 - 2 \approx 0.236 \text{ and } t = 1 @@ the quick pill is above 1 mg/L for about 46 minutes
C(t) = 0.5 \text{ at } t \approx 0.08 \text{ and } 2.35 @@ bisection (12e) on 16t − (1 + t)³: above 0.5 mg/L for about 2.27 h
C_2(t) = 0.5 \iff C(t/2) = 1 \iff t = 2(\sqrt5 - 2) \text{ or } t = 2 @@ the slow pill is above 0.5 mg/L from 0.47 h to 2 h, and never above 1

The quick pill helps for longer, and harms for three quarters of an hour. The slow pill never harms, and helps for about an hour and a half. The area cannot tell them apart. It says how much was taken up, not how high or when.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, closed at the end

1a's ledger, again. 16·then kept a lake's books by the second. A body keeps a drug's books the same way, but nobody can read its columns. The blood level is the one window. The dose-area technique closes the books at the end, when everything taken up has gone out through the clearance, and only then.

Every milligram taken up is still in the body, or has left it.

Before the end, the books have a column nobody measures: what is still in the gut. After the end, the area alone says what came in. A drug that also leaves by the breath, or hides in fat for weeks, has another column, and a clearance read from the blood alone will miss it.

A dose goes straight into a vein, so all of it enters at once. Twelve hours later, the clearance times the area so far comes to 70% of the dose. Where is the other 30%?

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

Dost stated that the area under the blood-level curve is the same whatever way a dose enters the blood, so long as all of it enters and the body clears it at a rate in proportion to its level; so the areas of two ways of giving a drug compare the amounts that reached the blood.

Friedrich Hartmut Dost, Der Blutspiegel (1953); paraphrase

Torsten Teorell, in two papers of 1937, first described a drug's passage through the body as amounts flowing between compartments at rates in proportion to their levels. Dost's book of 1953 named the field pharmacokinetics and set out the law of corresponding areas, now often called Dost's law.

Accepted names: the area under the curve (AUC); clearance (CL); bioavailability (F); the peak level and its time (Cmax and tmax); the range between the helpful and harmful levels, the therapeutic window. A drug cleared at a fixed amount an hour, as alcohol is past small amounts, is said to follow zero-order elimination; one cleared in proportion to its level, first-order. Much of a swallowed drug lost in the liver before it reaches the rest of the body is the first-pass effect.

In 16·bio.4.4, the rule is bioequivalence: the 90% confidence range of the ratio of the copy's AUC and Cmax to the original's must fall within 80% to 125%. Health Canada narrows the AUC range to 90% to 112% for a short list of critical dose drugs, among them warfarin and cyclosporine.
