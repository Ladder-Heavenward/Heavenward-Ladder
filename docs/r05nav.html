Why does a calendar need leap years?

Why does a calendar need leap years? A clock and a calendar are ways of writing a count. The count is of seconds, or of days. The writing uses places, like 4i's tens numerals, but each place has a base of its own: 60 seconds make a minute, 60 minutes an hour, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Turning a count into a time is 4j's division with remainder, done once for each place. Then the sky has its say. The year is not a whole number of days, so a calendar has to guess, and its leap years are a run of better and better guesses. This page measures how far each guess drifts. It ends with the day by the stars, and with how a clock and the sun's noon once told a ship where it was.

Divide by the last place's base and keep the remainder. Divide the quotient by the next base, and go on to the top. To find when two cycles come back in step, find the least common multiple of their lengths. To judge a calendar, take its average year less the measured year, and multiply by the number of years.

A count written in places

5j set the units of time by line: 1 min = 60 s, 1 h = 60 min, 1 day = 24 h. One more joins them.

From here on, 1 week = 7 days.

In a tens numeral every place has the same base, ten. A count of seconds can be written in places too, but each place has its own base. The seconds place holds 0 to 59, and so does the minutes place. The hours place holds 0 to 23, and the days place 0 to 6. Read 100 000 seconds that way, one place at a time, by division with remainder.

100\,000 = 60 \cdot 1666 + \htmlClass{new}{40} @@ divide by 60: 40 seconds are left, and 1666 whole minutes go up a place
1666 = 60 \cdot 27 + \htmlClass{new}{46} @@ divide by 60 again: 46 minutes are left, and 27 whole hours go up
27 = 24 \cdot 1 + \htmlClass{new}{3} @@ divide by 24: 3 hours are left, and 1 whole day goes up
100\,000\ \text{s} = 1\ \text{day}\ 3\ \text{h}\ 46\ \text{min}\ 40\ \text{s} @@ read the remainders from the top place down

Going the other way is multiplying up and adding. 1 day is 24 · 60 · 60 = 86 400 s, and 1 h is 3600 s. So 1 day 3 h 46 min 40 s is 86 400 + 3 · 3600 + 46 · 60 + 40 = 100 000 s.

Is that the only way to read 100 000 s? It is, and the reason is 4i's one-split. Clock-reading technique: a count of seconds can be read as days, hours, minutes and seconds in exactly one way. Let t be a count of seconds. A reading of t is a list d, h, m, s of numbers. They must have t = 86 400d + 3600h + 60m + s. And they must have h < 24, m < 60 and s < 60.

\htmlClass{new}{h' = h,\ \ d' = d} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-split, with base 24

It is 4i's rolling over, with wheels of different sizes. Add one second to 23 h 59 min 59 s. The seconds place reaches 60 and rolls over to 0, carrying one minute up. The minutes roll over the same way, then the hours, and the count reads 1 day 0 h 0 min 0 s.

From here on, a time of day is written as the hours and minutes since midnight, with a colon between: 21:00 is 21 h after midnight, and 07:05 is 7 h 5 min after it. Seconds, when they are needed, follow a second colon, as in 12:07:44. The colon here only separates places. 5j's colon of a ratio and 2d's colon “such that” keep their jobs.

Places that come round out of step

Each place comes round again. The seconds run from 0 to 59 and start over; the hours run from 0 to 23. The days of the week run from Monday to Sunday. Two hours past 22:00 is 00:00, and that is 5·cb2's clock: [22] + [2] = [24] = [0] in ℕ/∼₂₄.

The places above the day do not fit so neatly. A year of 365 days is 52 weeks and 1 day, since 365 = 7 · 52 + 1. So each year starts one weekday later than the last. After a year of 366 days it starts two later. The months fit nothing, not even each other: they have 28, 29, 30 or 31 days.

So a calendar is a set of places with a base of its own for each, whose cycles do not come round together. This page's name for it is modulated bases with asynchronous repetition. Modulated, because each base is set to suit its place: 60, 60, 24, 7. Asynchronous is from the Greek a- (not), syn (together) and chronos (time): the cycles do not keep time together. Each place has a base of its own, and the places come round out of step.

Which day of the week? Remainders do it

The days of the week are the classes of ℕ/∼₇, given names. Thursday 29 October 2026 is the start. 29 October 2027 is 365 days later. 365 ∼₇ 1, so it falls one weekday on, a Friday. The next year's run crosses 29 February 2028, so it has 366 days. 366 ∼₇ 2, so 29 October 2028 is two weekdays on from Friday: a Sunday.

5·cb2's remainders-add says the sum of the remainders does the work of the sum of the days. So the big count never has to be written out.

\text{Thursday} + 5 = \text{Tuesday} @@ Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday

When do two cycles come back in step? Say they start together, and one has length p and the other length q. After t steps the first is back at its start exactly when p ∣ t. The second is back exactly when q ∣ t. Both are back exactly when t is a common multiple of p and q. Two cycles first come back in step at the least common multiple of their lengths. That is 5h's lcm.

With a leap year every 4th year, four years hold 4 · 365 + 1 = 1461 days. The prime list of 1461 is 3 · 487, and 7 is not in it. So lcm(1461, 7) = 3 · 7 · 487 = 10 227 days, which is 7 runs of four years: 28 years. Only after 28 years do dates and weekdays come back in step. The rule in use today does better, as the next part shows. Its 400 years hold 146 097 days, and 146 097 = 7 · 20 871 exactly. So the calendar of 2026, weekdays and all, is the calendar of 2426.

The year is not a whole number of days

The year a calendar must follow is the sun's year. It runs from the spring day, in March, when day and night are about equally long, to the next spring day. It is measured, not proved. It is about 365.2422 days: by 5h's line that is 365 + 2422/10 000, and it is not a whole number. A calendar must give each year a whole number of days. So it must guess, and it must say how wrong the guess is.

The first guess is 365 days every year. It is short by 0.2422 days a year, which comes to 24.22 days a century. In a century the spring day would wander most of a month through the calendar.

The second guess makes every 4th year a leap year of 366 days (the Julian calendar). It ruled Europe from 45 BC.

The third guess skips the leap day in the years 100 divides. The fourth puts it back in the years 400 divides (the Gregorian rule). That is the rule in use today. A year is a leap year when 4 divides it, except when 100 divides it, unless 400 divides it. So 2028 is a leap year, 2100 is not, and 2000 was.

365 + \tfrac14 = 365.25 @@ second guess: one leap day in every 4 years
365.25 - 365.2422 = 0.0078 @@ too long by this much each year: 0.78 days a century
365 + \tfrac14 - \tfrac1{100} = 365.24 @@ third guess: skip the century years
365.24 - 365.2422 = -0.0022 @@ too short: 0.22 days a century, 2.2 days a thousand years
365 + \tfrac14 - \tfrac1{100} + \tfrac1{400} = 365 + \tfrac{97}{400} @@ fourth guess: keep every 400th. 100 − 4 + 1 = 97 leap years in 400
\tfrac{97}{400} = \tfrac{2425}{10\,000} = 0.2425,\ \ \text{so}\ \htmlClass{new}{365.2425} @@ by 5h: scale the bottom to a power of ten
365.2425 - 365.2422 = 0.0003 @@ too long: 0.03 days a century, 0.3 days a thousand years

A drift is a decimal times a count of years, so it grows without stopping. A guess's drift is its average year less the measured year, times the number of years. When the drift is positive, the calendar's years are too long. Then the sun's spring day comes earlier and earlier in the calendar. When it is negative, the spring day comes later.

The second guess was undone by its own drift. In 325 the spring day fell about the 21st of March. By 1582, 1257 years later, the calendar's years had run long by about 1257 · 0.0078 ≈ 9.8 days. The spring day was coming near the 11th of March. So ten days were cut. In Rome, Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October 1582. The dates jumped ten, and the weekdays did not jump at all: the two places are out of step even here. Britain and its colonies waited until 1752, when the drift was 11 days. There, Wednesday 2 September was followed by Thursday 14 September.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The year's length is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity, with its range and its exceptions. It is about 365.2422 days, good to four decimal places over the few thousand years of records. Past that edge it fails, and in a known direction. The tides drag on the turning Earth, so the day grows longer, by about 2 thousandths of a second a century. A longer day means fewer days in a year. The fourth guess is off by 0.0003 days a year. Over a few thousand years, the year's length in days changes by about that much.

So a better leap rule than the fourth guess buys little. Its error is already smaller than the doubt in the year it was built to follow.

The day grows longer by about 2 thousandths of a second each century. Could any fixed leap rule keep the calendar with the sun for a million years?

The day by the sun, and the day by the stars

The sun day runs from one noon to the next, where noon is the moment the sun stands highest. Its length wanders a little through the year, and averaged over the year it is 24 h. 5j's day is this average.

The star day runs from a star crossing due south to the same star crossing due south again. It is about 23 h 56 min 4 s. So each night a star crosses about 3 min 56 s earlier by the clock than the night before.

Why are they different? The Earth turns, and in a year it also goes once round the sun, in the same sense as it turns. Count the turns over one year. Going once round the sun undoes one turn, as seen from the sun. So against the sun the Earth turns one time fewer than against the stars. In a year the stars come round one more time than the sun does. A year of 365.2422 sun days holds 366.2422 star days.

366.2422\ \text{star days} = 365.2422\ \text{sun days} @@ one year, counted both ways
1\ \text{star day} = 86\,400\ \text{s} \cdot \tfrac{365.2422}{366.2422} @@ a sun day is 86 400 s; scale both sides by 1/366.2422
= 86\,400\ \text{s} - \tfrac{86\,400}{366.2422}\ \text{s} @@ 365.2422/366.2422 = 1 − 1/366.2422
\approx \htmlClass{new}{86\,164.09\ \text{s}} @@ rounded as in 5h
86\,164 = 3600 \cdot 23 + 3364 @@ divide by 3600: 23 h
3364 = 60 \cdot 56 + 4 @@ divide by 60: 56 min 4 s, and the 0.09 s left over

Over a month the few minutes add up. 30 · (3 min 56 s) = 118 min, which is 1 h 58 min. A star that crosses due south at 21:00 tonight crosses near 19:02 thirty days from now. By the end of the year it has gained a whole day, which is the extra star day.

Noon tells you where you are

The Earth turns once against the sun in 24 h. So noon sweeps westward round the Earth, one twenty-fourth of the way round each hour. Greenwich, by London, is where the count starts. If noon comes to a place h hours after it comes to Greenwich, the place is h/24 of the way round to the west.

The Point Pinos light, at Pacific Grove on the Monterey peninsula, has its average noon at about 20:08 by a Greenwich clock. That is 8 h 8 min after Greenwich's noon, and 8 h 8 min is 488 min. A whole day is 1440 min, so Point Pinos is 488/1440 = 61/180 of the way round, west of Greenwich. Pacific Standard Time is Greenwich time less 8 h, so the clocks in Monterey read about 12:08 at that average noon. Salinas lies inland, to the east, and its noon comes about a minute sooner.

The sun keeps the average only on average. At Point Pinos it stands highest at about 11:51 Pacific Standard Time on 3 November, and near 12:22 on 11 February. The clock keeps the average day and lets the sun run up to about a quarter of an hour either side of it.

A ship at sea can use this. It carries a clock set to Greenwich time. It watches for noon, reads the clock, and the difference says how far round it has come. Everything rides on the clock. A clock that gains 2 s a day is 2 min fast after 60 days, and 2 min is 2/1440 = 1/720 of the way round. At the equator the way round is about 40 075 km, so that is an error of about 56 km.

Who said it first

Counting by sixties goes back to the scribes of Babylon, whose numerals had sixty in each place; the hour's sixty minutes and the minute's sixty seconds come from them through the Greek astronomers. Hipparchus, in the second century BC, measured the sun's year as a little less than 365¼ days. The every-4th-year calendar was set up by Julius Caesar in 45 BC, on the advice of the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria. The council of 325 is the Council of Nicaea. The fourth guess was worked out by Aloysius Lilius and Christopher Clavius and made law by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582. Britain changed by an Act of Parliament of 1750, taking effect in 1752. Omar Khayyam's calendar of 1079 used 8 leap years in every 33. The sea clock that first kept time well enough to find a ship's place was John Harrison's, tried on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 and 1762. Greenwich was agreed as the place where the count starts at a conference in Washington in 1884. The second was tied to the turning Earth until 1967, and since then to caesium (5j). Leap seconds began in 1972; in 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures resolved to stop adding them by 2035.

Accepted names

A writing in places whose bases differ is a mixed radix numeral. The sun day averaged over the year is the mean solar day, and the star day is the sidereal day. The sun's year from spring day to spring day is the tropical year, and the spring day is the March equinox. The every-4th-year calendar is the Julian calendar, and the rule in use today is the Gregorian calendar. How far round from Greenwich a place lies is its longitude. The wandering of the sun's noon about the clock's noon is the equation of time. A minute given a 61st second has a leap second.
