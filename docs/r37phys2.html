Why Do Ice Ages Come and Go?

Why do ice ages come and go? For the last million years, ice sheets have spread over the northern lands and melted back again and again, roughly every hundred thousand years. Nothing on earth keeps such time. The beat comes from the sky: the earth’s orbit and tilt change slowly, shifting how much summer sun the far north gets. This page sets out three slow cycles of the orbit, writes summer sunlight as a sum of turnings (32), finds that close cycles beat as 37i’s rhythms do, reads the cycles out of an ice core with 37f’s spectral view, and meets the puzzle that the strongest beat has the weakest push.

Summer sun in the far north rises and falls with the tilt of the axis, about every 41,000 years, and with where on its slightly oval orbit the earth is in northern summer, about every 23,000 and 19,000 years. As a sum of turnings, these two nearby periods beat, giving swells about every 100,000 years. The ice keeps time with them, but most strongly at 100,000 years, where the direct push on sunlight is weakest.

37phys23Close Cycles Beat

Two turnings at nearby periods drift in and out of step, as two nearby notes do (37i). Their sum swells and fades at the difference of their frequencies.

\cos a + \cos b = 2\cos\dfrac{a + b}{2}\cos\dfrac{a - b}{2} @@ the sum formulas (21d)
\text{beat frequency} = \dfrac{1}{19} - \dfrac{1}{23} \approx 0.0092 \text{ per thousand years} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{beat period} \approx 110\,000 \text{ years}} @@ the swells of the wobble’s swing

Beat-reading technique: when a record holds two nearby periods, it swells and fades at the difference of their frequencies; read the beat from the spacing of the swells, and the two periods from the swings inside them. The orbit’s roundness itself changes at about this beat, so the wobble’s swings swell when the orbit is most oval.

37phys24Reading the Cycles Out of an Ice Core

Ice cores from deep in the Antarctic ice and mud cores from the sea floor hold a record of past temperature and ice volume, layer by layer, going back hundreds of thousands of years. Broken into frequencies (37f), the record shows its strongest periods.

peak in the record’s spectrummatches
about 100,000 yearsthe beat, and the orbit’s roundness
about 41,000 yearsthe tilt
about 23,000 and 19,000 yearsthe wobble

The ice’s spectrum shows peaks at the orbit’s own periods, so the ice ages keep the orbit’s time. Researchers who first broke a sea-floor record into frequencies found the three peaks sitting where the orbit predicts, to within the uncertainty of the record’s dating.

37phys25The Hundred-Thousand-Year Puzzle: the Strongest Beat Has the Weakest Push

The roundness of the orbit changes the year’s total sunlight by only about a fifth of a percent: by far the weakest direct push of the three. Yet for the last 800,000 years the 100,000-year cycle has been the strongest in the ice. Before about a million years ago, the ice kept 41,000-year time instead.

\text{direct push at } 100\,000 \text{ years: tiny} @@ 
\text{answer of the ice at } 100\,000 \text{ years: largest} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the ice must amplify its own slow swings: big sheets grow slowly and collapse fast}} @@ not a plain sum

The orbit sets the ice ages’ timing, but the size of the 100,000-year cycle comes from the ice and the climate themselves, through slow growth, fast collapse, and the air’s carbon dioxide rising and falling with them. Which mix of these does it is still argued, and is a test every climate model must pass.

Accepted names

The three changes are the Milankovitch cycles: obliquity (tilt), axial precession (wobble) and eccentricity (roundness). The puzzle is the 100,000-year problem, and the switch a million years ago the Mid-Pleistocene Transition.

Who said it first

Milutin Milanković worked out the summer sunlight curves between the 1920s and 1941; James Hays, John Imbrie and Nicholas Shackleton found the orbital peaks in a sea-floor record in 1976 (not yet revisited for the dates).
