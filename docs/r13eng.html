How Much Power Does a Car Need to Climb?

How much power does a car need to climb? Drive north out of San Luis Obispo on US 101 and the road climbs the Cuesta Grade. Cars keep their speed up it. Loaded trucks crawl in the slow lane. Every engine is sold with a number in horsepower, and every car on the grade spends part of it lifting itself, part pushing air aside and part on its tyres. The question is old: the unit was set by a maker of engines selling them against horses. It is hard because the power needed changes at every instant, with the speed and with the road. A total over a trip says little. This page builds power at an instant as a slope, the rate at which work is done. Then it counts what a climb at a steady speed takes, and finds by 13m's tangent steps how fast a truck can go.

Power is the slope of the work done. A steady force on a thing moving at some speed delivers the force times the speed. Raising a mass takes its weight times how fast it rises. On a road that climbs, at a steady speed, with rolling and the air pulling back, the power needed is the speed times the sum of the lifting, the rolling and the air’s drag, which grows with the square of the speed. Its slope says what one more metre per second costs.

13eng1Power at an Instant

7·phys built power as energy moved per second, in watts: 1 W = 1 J/s. A kettle's 1500 W is steady. A climbing car's is not. It changes as the road steepens and the driver presses down. So power needs the move rung 13 made for speed: from an average over a stretch of time to a value at an instant.

From here on, on this page, W(t) is the work done, in joules, from the start up to the instant t. The average power from t to t + h is the chord slope (W(t + h) − W(t))/h. The power at the instant t is its limit as h → 0, the slope P(t) = W′(t), written dW/dt. Its unit is joules per second: watts.

Page letters, said out loud. Slanted W(t) is work done (not 12·phys's weight, nor 7·phys's upright W for the watt). P is power (7·phys's letter). m a mass, g ≈ 9.8 m/s² and F a force keep 12·phys's meanings. x is position along the road and v = x′ the velocity, as on 13·phys. y is height. r is a road's rise per metre of road (not 13e's inside r). c and k are measured constants of a car, given below (not a constant function, nor 13d's k).

Power is how fast work is being done: the slope of the work. A person of 70 kg who runs up 3 m of stairs in 4 s does 70 · 9.8 · 3 = 2058 J of lifting. That is an average of 514.5 W over the 4 s. At the steepest moment of the run it is more.

13eng2Force Times Speed

Force-times-speed technique: a steady force pushing a thing along its motion delivers power equal to the force times the speed. Precisely: let a force F that does not change act along the motion of a thing at position x(t). Then P(t) = F · v(t).

W(t) = F\,\big(x(t) - x(0)\big) @@ 12·phys's work: force times the distance moved along it
W(t) = F\,x(t) - F\,x(0) @@ spread (5i); F x(0) does not depend on t
W'(t) = F\,x'(t) - 0 @@ slopes-add; constant-factor for slopes; a constant has slope 0 (13d)
\htmlClass{new}{P(t) = F\,v(t)} \quad \blacksquare @@ v = x′ (13·phys)

When the force changes as the thing moves, work itself is a sum of many small pieces, and rung 16 builds it. Whether the same line, P = Fv at each instant, is true there too is rung 16's question. On this page every force is steady, or kept steady at a steady speed.

13eng3The Price of Climbing

Lifting a mass m by a height h takes m · g · h joules (7·phys's line, used again on 12·phys). A car going uphill is lifting itself, a little at each instant.

Climbing-power technique: raising a mass m takes power mg times the rate at which its height grows. Precisely: if a thing of mass m is at height y(t), the power spent lifting it is m · g · y′(t). On a road that rises r metres for each metre of road, at speed v, that is mgrv.

W(t) = mg\,\big(y(t) - y(0)\big) @@ the work of lifting from y(0) to y(t)
W'(t) = mg\,y'(t) @@ constant-factor for slopes, slopes-add, as before
y(t) = y(0) + r\,\big(x(t) - x(0)\big) @@ the road rises r metres per metre of road
y'(t) = r\,v(t) \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{P_{\text{climb}} = mg\,r\,v} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same moves on y

A road's rise per metre is its grade, often written as a percent. Road signs measure the metre level, not along the road. For grades like these the two figures differ by less than a quarter of a percent of the figure. That is held in trust here, and rung 18 shows why. The steep part of the Cuesta Grade rises about 7 m for every 100 m: r ≈ 0.07.

m = 1500 \text{ kg},\ \ r = 0.07,\ \ v = 29 \text{ m/s} @@ 29 m/s is about 104 km/h, or 65 miles an hour
P_{\text{climb}} = 1500 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 0.07 \cdot 29 \approx \htmlClass{new}{29\,800} \text{ W} @@ climbing-power: about 30 kW, just to rise

13eng4Two Laws That Pull Back

A car on a level road at a steady speed still needs power. Two regularities, measured, take it. Each is a law: it works over a range, and fails outside it in a known way.

The rolling law: a tyre rolling on a hard road pulls back with a force of about c · mg, a fixed share of the weight. For car tyres on good pavement c is about 0.010 to 0.015; for a loaded truck's tyres, about 0.006; for a racing bicycle's, about 0.005. It works well at road speeds, and grows on soft ground and with soft tyres.

The air law: the air pulls back on a moving car with a force of about k · v², growing with the square of the speed. For a family car k is about 0.3 to 0.4 kg/m; for a big truck, about 3 to 4 kg/m. It works well above walking pace, and fails for things so slow or small that the air flows round them smoothly. Both constants are measured by letting the car coast on a level road and watching it slow.

At a steady speed the velocity's slope is 0, so the force on the car adds to 0 (13·phys: force sets the second slope). The wheels push forward exactly as hard as everything pulls back. Each force is steady while the speed is, so force-times-speed prices each one.

Steady-climb technique: at a steady speed v on a grade r, a car needs power P(v) = (mg(r + c) + kv²)v at its wheels. Its slope is P′(v) = mg(r + c) + 3kv².

P(v) = mg\,r\,v + c\,mg\,v + kv^2 \cdot v @@ climbing-power; force-times-speed for the rolling law and the air law
= \htmlClass{new}{\big(mg(r + c) + kv^2\big)\,v} @@ collect (5i)
P'(v) = \htmlClass{new}{mg(r + c) + 3kv^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ polynomial-slope (13d), in the speed v
  
c = 0.012,\ \ k = 0.36 \text{ kg/m} @@ a family car, within the laws' ranges
\text{climb } 29\,841 + \text{rolling } 5116 + \text{air } 8780 \approx \htmlClass{new}{43\,700} \text{ W} @@ steady-climb at 29 m/s
\tfrac{43\,700}{745.7} \approx 59 \text{ hp} @@ one horsepower is 745.7 W: see below
\text{level road: } 5116 + 8780 \approx 13\,900 \text{ W} @@ r = 0: a third as much
P'(29) = 1500 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 0.082 + 3 \cdot 0.36 \cdot 29^2 \approx 2114 \text{ W per m/s} @@ one more m/s up the grade costs about 2.1 kW (13k's nearby line)

On the grade, two thirds of the power goes into height. On the level, most of it goes into the air, and more so the faster the car goes: the air's share grows as v³. The engine must also make up what is lost between it and the wheels, roughly a tenth.

13eng5How Fast Can a Truck Climb?

A loaded truck may weigh about 36 000 kg, close to the United States' usual limit of 80 000 pounds on its highways. A big truck engine gives about 330 kW. On the same grade, with c = 0.006 and k = 3.6 kg/m, at what steady speed does the power needed equal what it has? That is P(v) = 330 000, a cubic in v. It has exactly one answer above 0, since P rises there (its slope is positive), starts at 0 and passes every bound. 12a's in-between-value says one exists. 13m's tangent step finds it.

f(v) = 36\,000 \cdot 9.8 \cdot 0.076\,v + 3.6v^3 - 330\,000 @@ the power needed less the power it has
f'(v) = 26\,812.8 + 10.8v^2 @@ polynomial-slope
v_0 = \tfrac{330\,000}{26\,812.8} \approx 12.31 @@ a first guess: set the air aside
v_1 = v_0 - \tfrac{f(v_0)}{f'(v_0)} \approx 12.0716 @@ f(v₀) ≈ 6711: the air takes some; step back
v_2 \approx \htmlClass{new}{12.0714} \text{ m/s} @@ f(v₁) ≈ 7.4: about 43 km/h

So the truck crawls up at about 43 km/h while cars keep 104. The climb is priced by the kilogram, and a truck has far fewer watts per kilogram than a car. The car has 40 W per kg of mass; the truck about 9. That is why steep grades have a lane for slow trucks.

13eng6The Horse That Set the Unit

In the 1780s a maker of steam engines sold them to brewers and mine owners, who drove their mills and pumps with horses. To say what an engine could do, he counted it in horses. He reckoned that a mill horse could keep up 33 000 foot-pounds a minute: enough to raise 33 000 pounds by one foot each minute. One foot is 0.3048 m, and a pound of force, the weight of a pound, is about 4.448 N.

33\,000 \cdot 0.3048 \cdot 4.448 \text{ J per } 60 \text{ s} @@ the foot-pounds, turned into newton-metres, which are joules (12·phys)
\approx \tfrac{44\,742}{60} \approx \htmlClass{new}{745.7} \text{ W} @@ one horsepower

A century later the unit of power was named after him: the watt. A strong person can give about a horsepower for a few seconds on a stair. A trained cyclist keeps up about 250 W, a third of one, for an hour. A horse can give far more than one for a few seconds, and about one over a working day.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

A car keeping a steady 29 m/s up the grade looks like nothing is happening to it. Its speed does not change. But it is rising at about 2 m every second, and fuel is burning to pay for it. The steady speed is not a lack of change. It is two changes in balance: the wheels push forward exactly as hard as height, tyres and air pull back.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

Over a whole trip, the before and after can hide everything. A car that climbs the grade and comes back down ends where it began, at the same height and speed. The rates along the way say where the fuel went.

A car climbs the grade at a steady speed with its engine giving 44 kW. The same car rolls back down the other side at the same speed with its engine idle. At each instant, where is the power going?

13eng8Running It Backwards

Handed a power and a speed, find the force: F = P/v. Handed an engine's power and a grade, find the speed it can keep: solve P(v) = P_max, by tangent steps or by halving. Handed a speed and a power on a known grade, find the grade: r = P/(mgv) − c − kv²/(mg). Handed foot-pounds, minutes or horsepower, turn them into joules, seconds and watts first.

Who said it first

The maker of engines is James Watt, who settled on 33 000 foot-pounds a minute in the early 1780s. The unit named after him was proposed by William Siemens in 1882 and adopted by an international congress of electricians in 1889. The scientists of 13·eng.9.4 are R. D. Stevenson and R. J. Wassersug, “Horsepower from a horse”, Nature (1993), who used figures from a horse-pulling contest at a state fair.

Stevenson and Wassersug report that a horse can reach a peak of nearly 15 horsepower over a few seconds, and that a person can briefly exceed one horsepower, while Watt's figure suits a horse working steadily for a long time.

R. D. Stevenson and R. J. Wassersug, “Horsepower from a horse”, Nature 364 (1993); paraphrase

Accepted names

The rolling law's c is the rolling resistance coefficient. The air law's k is half the density of air times a measured drag figure of the car: its drag coefficient times its size seen from the front. The test that measures both by coasting is the coast-down test. The rise per metre of road is the grade; the power needed at the wheels, as a function of the speed, is the road load.
