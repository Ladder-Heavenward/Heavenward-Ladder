Can a Model Fit Too Well?

Can a model fit too well? A county health office in Oregon wants to know which things predict the weight of a newborn: the mother’s age, her height, whether she smokes, how many weeks the pregnancy ran. Each thing measured is a column, and the best fit is a shadow on the flat piece the columns build. Add a column and the shadow can only come closer, so the fit always looks better. Add enough columns, even columns of pure noise, and the fit becomes perfect, passing through every point. That perfect fit is worthless for the next baby. What makes the question hard is that the fit on the data in hand is the only thing anyone can see, and it always flatters. This page counts exactly how much it flatters, shows how to get an honest measure from data held back, and carries the shadow to yes or no answers, where the thing predicted is a chance.

With more columns the shadow on the data can only come closer, so the fit on the data in hand always flatters. With p columns and noise of spread σ², the misses on the data average (n − p)σ² in total, and the misses on fresh data (n + p)σ². Data held back measure the honest miss. For yes or no answers the log of the odds is fitted, and the best fit again has its misses at right angles to every column.

31stat1Many Columns, One Shadow

Put the n babies’ weights in a list y, and each thing measured in a column of X, with a column of 1s first: p columns in all. 31b3 found the best c as the shadow ŷ = Xc of y on the flat piece the columns build. The share of the spread taken, R² = |ŷ − ȳ1|²/|y − ȳ1|², is 13·stat’s r² for one column, now for many.

Never-worse technique: adding a column never makes the total squared miss larger, so it never lowers R². The new flat piece holds the old one, and a closest point in a bigger piece is at least as close (31b2).

\text{piece } W \subseteq W' \Rightarrow \min_{W'}|y - w| \le \min_W|y - w| @@ every point of W is in W′
p = n \text{ free columns} \Rightarrow W' = \text{every list of } n \text{ numbers} @@ n free arrows build the whole space (27b3)
\text{the miss is } 0,\ \ R^2 = 1 @@ y lies in the piece
\htmlClass{new}{1, x, \ldots, x^{n-1}\ \text{fit exactly}} \quad \blacksquare @@ columns of powers: the curve through every point, by the through-the-points technique (31m1)

So R² near 1 is no proof that a model is good. A model with as many columns as babies fits every weight exactly, including the noise in each one.

31stat2How Much the Fit Flatters

Suppose the truth is y = Xβ + ε, where the noise ε has independent places, each with average 0 and spread σ². Let P be the shadow map onto the piece, so ŷ = Py, and the miss is e = (I − P)y = (I − P)ε, since (I − P)Xβ = 0. This e is not 17e's number e.

Lost-directions technique: the total squared miss on the data in hand averages (n − p)σ²: each column spends one direction of the noise.

P = QQ^{\mathsf T},\ \ Q \text{ with } p \text{ right-angled columns} @@ the column-straightening technique (31c3); shadow-sum (31b2)
|e|^2 = |\varepsilon|^2 - |Q^{\mathsf T}\varepsilon|^2 @@ the square-sum technique: ε = Pε + e
\operatorname{E}|Q^{\mathsf T}\varepsilon|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{k \le p}\operatorname{E}(q_k\cdot\varepsilon)^2 = \sum_k \sigma^2|q_k|^2 = p\sigma^2 @@ the co-spreads of ε are σ² down the middle, 0 elsewhere (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}|e|^2 = n\sigma^2 - p\sigma^2 = (n - p)\sigma^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-adds technique

Fresh-data technique: new readings y′ at the same inputs, with fresh noise, miss the old fit by a total that averages (n + p)σ². y′ − ŷ = ε′ − Pε, with ε′ and ε independent, so its squared size averages nσ² + pσ². The gap between the two is 2pσ²: each column flatters the fit by 2σ².

n = 20,\ \ p = 5,\ \ \sigma = 0.4 \text{ kg} @@ a model of the county’s data
\text{in hand: } \tfrac{(20 - 5)(0.16)}{20} = 0.12 \text{ kg}^2 \text{ per baby} @@ the lost-directions technique
\text{fresh: } \tfrac{(20 + 5)(0.16)}{20} = 0.20 \text{ kg}^2 \text{ per baby} @@ the fresh-data technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the fit looks } \tfrac{0.20}{0.12} \approx 1.7 \text{ times better than it is}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and 15 more noise columns would make it look perfect

31stat3Holding Data Back

The fresh-data technique needs σ², and the truth to be one of the shadows. Neither is known in practice. The plain cure is to keep some data out of sight. Fit on one part, the training part, and measure the miss on the rest, the held-back part. The held-back readings are fresh for that fit.

Held-out technique: if the held-back readings are independent of the training readings and of the choice of model, their average squared miss is an honest estimate of the miss on fresh data, with a width that shrinks like one over the root of their number. Each held-back squared miss is an independent try at the same average, so the sample-average technique of 30q gives the estimate and its width.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

A held-back miss is an average of tries, and it wobbles like any average. With 50 held-back babies and squared misses of spread width 0.3 kg², the honest miss is reported give or take 0.3/√50 ≈ 0.04 kg².

A model’s miss on held-back data is the middle of a cloud and is reported with its width; once used to choose, it flatters.

Choosing among many models by their held-back misses uses the held-back data a little, and the winner’s miss flatters a little, as the winner of many tries always does. A third part, never looked at until the end, keeps the last report honest.

Ten models are tried on 40 held-back readings, and the best scores 0.18 against 0.20 for the next. Is that a real difference? What would you need before reporting the winner’s miss?

31stat4Yes or No Answers

Some answers are yes or no: was the baby born early? A shadow can give numbers below 0 or above 1, which no chance can be. The cure is to fit a straight model not to the chance p but to its log odds, ln(p/(1 − p)), which runs over every number. From here on, the logistic model says ln(pᵢ/(1 − pᵢ)) = (Xc)ᵢ, so pᵢ = 1/(1 + e^(−(Xc)ᵢ)), the logistic curve of 17·bio.

Log-odds technique: in the logistic model, one more unit of a column’s input multiplies the odds of yes by e to the power of that column’s number. The log odds rise by cⱼ, and odds are e to the log odds (30l3).

Right-angled-score technique: the most likely c for yes-or-no answers yᵢ = 1 or 0 makes the misses y − p at right angles to every column: Xᵀ(y − p) = 0.

\ell(c) = \textstyle\sum_i y_i\ln p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - p_i) @@ the log-likelihood (30r1)
\tfrac{dp_i}{dc_j} = p_i(1 - p_i)X_{ij} @@ the slope of the logistic curve (17·bio), by the chain rule (28c)
\tfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial c_j} = \textstyle\sum_i\big(\tfrac{y_i}{p_i} - \tfrac{1 - y_i}{1 - p_i}\big)p_i(1 - p_i)X_{ij} = \sum_i (y_i - p_i)X_{ij} @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{X^{\mathsf T}(y - p) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ at the most likely c every partial slope is 0 (28)

So least squares and the logistic fit are kin. Both leave the misses at right angles to the columns. In least squares the samenesses are straight in c and solved at once; here p depends on c through the curve, and a machine walks to the answer step by step. Models with a straight part inside a curve, fitted this way, are a family, and the shadow is the member with no curve at all.

31·stat.6.7seam5·stat.6.5: does evidence raise a chance?5·stat asked whether the turned-chance technique answers the claim that no number of sunrises proves the next one. The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Fitting y by the shadow on many columns is multiple linear regression, and R² is the coefficient of determination. The lost-directions technique says the residual sum of squares has expectation (n − p)σ², so s² = RSS/(n − p) is unbiased; n − p is the residual degrees of freedom. The gap 2pσ² is the optimism of the training error, and it underlies Mallows’s C_p and the Akaike information criterion. Holding data back is validation, with training, validation and test sets; doing it in turn over every part is cross-validation. Fitting the noise is overfitting. The logistic model is logistic regression, the log odds the logit, and models with a straight part inside a curve are generalised linear models; Xᵀ(y − p) = 0 are the score equations, solved by iteratively reweighted least squares.

Who said it first

In 8·stat.6.4, Roger Boscovich fitted by least total size in 1757, Adrien-Marie Legendre published least squares in 1805, and Carl Friedrich Gauss tied it to the bell in 1809. Francis Galton’s regression of 1886 grew into multiple regression with Karl Pearson and George Udny Yule by 1897. Joseph Berkson named the logit in 1944, and John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn set out generalised linear models in 1972. Colin Mallows (1973) and Hirotugu Akaike (1974) counted the optimism; Mervyn Stone (1974) and Seymour Geisser (1975) set out cross-validation.
