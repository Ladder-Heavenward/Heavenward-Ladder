15h · technique

Sums of thin strips by hand

A lower sum needs the least value of the function on every piece, and finding it can take a step of 14a's work. A sum that uses any value from each piece needs no such work. It still lands between the lower and upper sums, because every value on a piece lies between the least and the greatest. Such sums are how integrals are estimated in practice, from a formula or from measurements taken every hour. This step computes them by hand, says how far off they can be, and uses 15g’s formulas to get the areas under the square and the cube on any interval exactly.

Pick a point in each piece, take the function’s value there as the box’s height, and add up: a strip sum. It lies between the lower and upper sums, so it is within their gap of the integral; for a continuous function and narrow enough pieces, as close as you like.

15h1Any height from each piece

Stands on 15c (lower and upper sums, integrable) and 15f (every-continuous-is-integrable).

From here on, a tagged partition of [a, b] is a partition with one input cᵢ chosen in each piece [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ]; the cᵢ are its tags. The strip sum of f for it is S = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ f(cᵢ) Δxᵢ, and S is a name, for one number. With every cᵢ at the left end it is the left sum, at the right end the right sum, at the middle the midpoint sum. The largest width maxᵢ Δxᵢ is the mesh of the partition. Accepted names: tags, or sample points; left, right and midpoint sums; the mesh, or norm, written ‖P‖.

Strip-sum-between technique: for f bounded on [u, v] and any tagged partition, L(f, P) ≤ S ≤ U(f, P). So if f is integrable, |S − ∫ᵤᵛ f| ≤ U(f, P) − L(f, P).

m_i \le f(c_i) \le M_i @@ cᵢ is on the i-th piece; inf and sup are bounds there
m_i \Delta x_i \le f(c_i)\Delta x_i \le M_i \Delta x_i @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree
L(f, P) \le S \le U(f, P) @@ add over i (same-direction-add, induction with sum-one-more)
L(f, P) \le \textstyle\int_a^b f \le U(f, P) @@ the integral is the sup of the lower sums and the inf of the upper
\htmlClass{new}{|S - \textstyle\int_a^b f| \le U(f, P) - L(f, P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ two numbers in one window are no further apart than its width (unpack-the-bars)

Narrow-strips technique: if f is continuous on [u, v], then for every ε > 0 there is δ > 0 such that every tagged partition with mesh less than δ has |S − ∫ᵤᵛ f| < ε. Continuous-is-integrable's proof did it. Evenly-continuous gives δ with |f(s) − f(t)| < ε/(v − u) whenever |s − t| < δ. So on pieces narrower than δ every Mᵢ − mᵢ < ε/(v − u), and U − L < ε. Strip-sum-between finishes.

For an increasing f and n equal pieces, U − L = (f(v) − f(u))(v − u)/n (15a.4.4: 15a.2.1c's collapse, run on any increasing f). So every strip sum is within that of the integral, whatever the tags. That is a guarantee. The midpoint sum usually does far better.

15h2Three sums for one area

Stands on 15h1 and 15g (sum-of-squares).

\text{left: } \tfrac12(0 + \tfrac14 + 1 + \tfrac94) = 1.75 @@ tags 0, ½, 1, 3/2
\text{right: } \tfrac12(\tfrac14 + 1 + \tfrac94 + 4) = 3.75 @@ tags ½, 1, 3/2, 2
\text{midpoint: } \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1 + 9 + 25 + 49}{16} = 2.625 @@ tags ¼, ¾, 5/4, 7/4
\text{exact: } \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.667 @@ from the squeeze below
\text{errors: } -0.92,\ +1.08,\ \htmlClass{new}{-0.042} @@ the midpoint sum, twenty times closer
  
M_n = \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(\tfrac{i - 1/2}{n}\big)^2 = \tfrac{1}{n^3}\sum (i^2 - i + \tfrac14) @@ square-of-a-sum (5h)
= \tfrac{1}{n^3}\Big(\tfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} - \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} + \tfrac{n}{4}\Big) @@ sums-are-linear; sum-of-squares; doubled-sum
= \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}} @@ expand and collect
\text{left and right: } \tfrac13 \mp \tfrac{1}{2n} + \tfrac{1}{6n^2} @@ 15g's Lₙ and Uₙ

With ten pieces the left sum is off by about 0.045, the midpoint sum by less than 0.001. Doubling the pieces halves the left sum's error and quarters the midpoint sum's.

15h3Exact areas under x² and x³

Stands on 15h2 and 15g (two areas paid).

\textstyle\int_0^b x^2\,dx = \tfrac{b^3}{3},\ \ \int_0^b x^3\,dx = \tfrac{b^4}{4} @@ 15g.4.5: 15d's squeeze with sum-of-squares and h = b/n; the same with sum-of-cubes
\textstyle\int_a^b x^2\,dx = \int_0^b - \int_0^a = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{b^3 - a^3}{3}} @@ interval-cut-in-two (15e), for 0 ≤ a < b
\textstyle\int_0^2 (x^3 - 3x + 3)\,dx = \tfrac{16}{4} - 3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 2 = 4 @@ areas-add, areas-scale; 15d; 15c
\textstyle\int_1^3 (6x^2 - 4x)\,dx = 2(27 - 1) - 2(9 - 1) = 36 @@ areas-add, areas-scale; 15d's (b² − a²)/2

So far: ∫₀ᵇ 1 = b, ∫₀ᵇ x = b²/2, ∫₀ᵇ x² = b³/3, ∫₀ᵇ x³ = b⁴/4. Whether the pattern goes on, and why, is 15i's to find.

15h4An integral from measurements

Stands on 15h1 to 15h3.

A physical premise, carried from physics: the energy a solar array delivers over a stretch of time is the integral of its power over that time. A rooftop array in Davis is read at the middle of each two-hour stretch from 6:00 to 18:00, on a clear June day:

stretch6–88–1010–1212–1414–1616–18
power at its middle (kW)0.82.94.34.53.21.1
S = 2(0.8 + 2.9 + 4.3 + 4.5 + 3.2 + 1.1) @@ widths 2 h; tags at the middles
= 2 \cdot 16.8 = \htmlClass{new}{33.6 \text{ kWh}} @@ kW times hours: kilowatt-hours

No formula for the power exists here, and no lower or upper sum can be found, since the least and greatest power in each stretch were never measured. The sum is an estimate whose error the readings alone cannot bound. What they do give is a number, and for a power that rises and falls smoothly, the midpoint sum is usually close.

The definition began with a question: what should one understand by the integral of a function between two bounds? Divide the interval into pieces, and take in each piece any value of the function. Then ask when the sum of those values times the widths approaches one fixed number, however the pieces shrink.

Who said it first

Riemann asked what one should understand by the integral of a function between two bounds, divided the interval into pieces, took in each piece any value of the function, and asked when the sum of those values times the widths approaches one fixed number however the pieces shrink.

Riemann, “Über die Darstellbarkeit einer Function durch eine trigonometrische Reihe” (1854, printed 1868), section 4; paraphrase

Accepted names: a strip sum is a Riemann sum, after Bernhard Riemann. In the closer, the earlier definition is Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s (1823), and the definition of 1854 is Riemann’s.
