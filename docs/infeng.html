What Can Be Known Before It Is Built?

What can be known before it is built? Five engineering annexes, from rung 5 to rung 45, each settled something about a machine from its drawing: which teeth meet, whether a frame holds, where a heated room rests, whether a compiler can be trusted, where a switch sends a message. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. The pairs of teeth are things and one tooth passing is an arrow, so the pairs fall into loops. A frame is one straight arrow from the pulls in its bars to the forces at its joints. A heater with no memory is an arrow from the gap alone, and it cannot rest at the set point. A compiler is right when two paths agree, and a row of right passes is right. A switch’s table is an arrow only on a tree.

Pairs of teeth under “one tooth passes”: loops of lcm(g, h), and gh/lcm(g, h) of them. A : ℝᵇ → ℝ²ʲ, pulls to joint forces: s − m = b − (2j − 3). Push = u(e), u(0) = 0, colder outside: no rest at e = 0. m₁ ∘ c₁ = m₀ and m₂ ∘ c₂ = m₁ give m₂ ∘ (c₂ ∘ c₁) = m₀. On a tree, each address lies beyond exactly one port.

infeng.1The Teeth Meet in Loops

5·eng4 proved that when two tooth counts share no factor but 1, each tooth of one gear meets every tooth of the other. Draw the pairs as things: (i, j) is tooth i of G against tooth j of H, gh pairs in all. One tooth passing is an arrow from (i, j) to (i + 1, j + 1), with i counted round g and j round h.

Tooth-loop technique: the pairs fall into loops of lcm(g, h) pairs each, and there are gh/lcm(g, h) loops. A wear mark stays in its loop.

(i, j) \xrightarrow{\ t \text{ teeth}\ } (i + t,\ j + t) @@ t arrows in a row
(i + t,\ j + t) = (i, j) \iff g \mid t \text{ and } h \mid t \iff \mathrm{lcm}(g, h) \mid t @@ 5h’s lcm: the least number both divide, and it divides every other
\text{each loop holds } \mathrm{lcm}(g, h) \text{ pairs, whatever pair it starts from} @@ the first return is at t = lcm(g, h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{loops} = \tfrac{gh}{\mathrm{lcm}(g, h)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ gh pairs, shared out equally
  
g = 12,\ h = 36:\quad \mathrm{lcm} = 36,\ \ \tfrac{432}{36} = 12 \text{ loops} @@ a tooth of H meets one tooth of G for ever (5·eng4)
g = 13,\ h = 36:\quad \mathrm{lcm} = 468,\ \ \tfrac{468}{468} = 1 \text{ loop} @@ the every-tooth technique (5·eng4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one loop} \iff \mathrm{lcm}(g, h) = gh} @@ every pair is reached from every pair

What hides is the arrangement: which tooth is which. The loops are read from the two counts, before a tooth is cut.

infeng.2A Frame Is One Straight Arrow

18·eng3 wrote the force of a bar on its end as a scaled copy of the bar, and 18·eng4 solved a roof frame joint by joint. Put the pulls of all b bars in one list and the forces they put on all j joints in another. The bar-force technique makes the second a straight map A of the first, from ℝᵇ to ℝ²ʲ. A load the frame carries is a list that A reaches.

A bar pulls its two ends equally and oppositely along itself. So every list A reaches is in balance as a whole: no total force across, none up, no total turning. The lists in balance form a space of dimension 2j − 3.

Frame-count technique: let s be the dimension of the pulls that load no joint, and m the dimension of the balanced loads the frame cannot carry. Then s − m = b − (2j − 3).

\dim(\text{reached}) = b - s @@ the lost-plus-reached technique (27c): A loses the pulls that load no joint
\text{reached} \subseteq \text{balanced},\qquad \dim(\text{balanced}) = 2j - 3 @@ three demands on 2j numbers
m = (2j - 3) - (b - s) @@ what is balanced and not reached
\htmlClass{new}{s - m = b - (2j - 3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ rearranged
  
\text{the roof frame}:\quad b = 3,\ j = 3,\ \ s - m = 0;\quad s = 0,\ m = 0 @@ solved joint by joint, one answer (18·eng4)
\text{a square of four bars}:\quad b = 4,\ j = 4,\ \ s - m = -1;\quad s = 0,\ m = 1 @@ one balanced load pushes it over
\text{the square with both diagonals}:\quad b = 6,\ \ s - m = 1;\quad s = 1,\ m = 0 @@ diagonals pulling, sides pushing, and no load
\htmlClass{new}{\text{three joints in a line, three bars}:\quad b = 3 = 2j - 3;\quad s = 1,\ m = 1} @@ the count is met and the frame is badly placed (18·eng2)

What hides is the arrangement, as far as the count goes: s − m is read from b and j alone. Where the joints sit decides s and m themselves. So b = 2j − 3 says s = m, and the add-a-joint technique (18·eng2) is one way to make both 0.

infeng.3A Heater With No Memory Rests Short

24·eng3 found that a heater pushing in proportion to the gap settles short of the set point. 24·eng4 let the heater add up the gap, and the room settled on it. The first technique does not need the proportion. It needs only that the push is an arrow from the gap: P = u(e), with e = T_s − T and no push at no gap.

No-memory technique: if P = u(e) with u(0) = 0, and it is colder outside than the set point, then no state left alone has e = 0.

\text{left alone}:\quad u(e) = \lambda\,(T - T_o) @@ T′ = 0 in the leak statement (24·eng1)
e = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ u(e) = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ T = T_o @@ λ ≠ 0
e = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ T = T_s \gt T_o @@ colder outside
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a collision: no rest at } e = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ whatever u is
  
u(e) = Ge:\quad e^* = \tfrac{\lambda\,(T_s - T_o)}{\lambda + G} = \tfrac{250 \cdot 26}{2250} \approx 2.9 @@ the droop technique (24·eng3): short for every G
P = Ge + I,\ \ I' = \kappa e:\quad \text{the state is } (T, I) @@ the push does not pass through e alone
\htmlClass{new}{I' = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ e = 0,\qquad I^* = \lambda\,(T_s - T_o)} @@ the summing technique (24·eng4): the memory holds what the gap cannot

What hides is the arrangement: the form of u. A switch, a proportion, a curve are all ruled out at once, and the cure is read off: a larger thing for the arrow to act on.

infeng.4A Row of Right Passes Is Right

m_1(c(p)) \ne m_1(c'(p)) \ \Rightarrow\ \text{not both equal } m_0(p) @@ the second-opinion technique (40·eng1): one of c, c′ fails at p
m_1(c(p)) = m_1(c'(p)) @@ m₀(p) is not in the line: neither is shown right
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a test}:\ \text{one } p;\qquad \text{a proof}:\ m_1 \circ c = m_0} @@ tests count rows, proofs do not (40·eng3)

Pass-row technique: if m₁ ∘ c₁ = m₀ and m₂ ∘ c₂ = m₁, then m₂ ∘ (c₂ ∘ c₁) = m₀. So a row of passes, each keeping the runs, keeps the runs.

m_2 \circ (c_2 \circ c_1) = (m_2 \circ c_2) \circ c_1 @@ brackets move (47a)
= m_1 \circ c_1 = m_0 @@ the two demands, one after the other
\htmlClass{new}{m_k \circ (c_k \circ \cdots \circ c_1) = m_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on the number of passes (4c)

What hides is the arrangement: the languages in the middle. A proof for the whole compiler is cut into one proof for each pass, each short enough for a machine to check line by line. The demand itself, which two paths must agree, is not the machine’s to supply.

infeng.5A Switch’s Table Is an Arrow Only on a Tree

45·eng2’s switch writes down the port on which each sender’s address arrived, and sends later messages for that address out of that port. For the table to be an arrow from addresses to ports, each address must have one port. 45·eng2 said that switches in a loop agree on a tree. The tree is what makes the arrow.

One-port technique: when the links in use form a tree, each address lies beyond exactly one port of each switch. So the table a switch learns is an arrow, and it is the same whatever the order of the messages.

\text{from machine } a \text{ to switch } S:\ \text{exactly one path} @@ a tree (42b)
\text{the path ends on one link at } S:\ \text{the port } \pi_S(a) @@ fixed by the tree alone
\text{every message from } a \text{ reaches } S \text{ on } \pi_S(a) @@ it travels the links in use, and there is no other path
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the table learned} = \pi_S,\ \text{on the addresses heard so far}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the order of hearing changes when an entry is written, never what it is
  
a \text{ on } S_1;\quad \text{a message from } a \text{ reaches } S_2 \text{ on port } 1 \text{ and on port } 2 @@ two paths
\text{the table at } S_2:\ a \mapsto 1,\ \text{then } a \mapsto 2,\ \text{then } a \mapsto 1 @@ no arrow: one address, two ports
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a message for an address not yet heard is copied round the loop for ever}} @@ the hub’s technique (45·eng1), on a loop

What hides is the sequence: who spoke first. The tree is the plan, and the table is read from it.

The accepted names

A tooth count chosen to share no factor with its partner gives a hunting tooth. The loops of the pairs are the orbits of a cyclic group, their number gh/lcm(g, h) is gcd(g, h), and that one loop holds every pair when the counts share no factor is the Chinese remainder theorem. The map A is the equilibrium matrix; pulls that load no joint are states of self-stress, and a frame with s > 0 is statically indeterminate. A balanced load that cannot be carried goes with a way for the frame to move, a mechanism, by the map that A becomes when rows and columns change places, the compatibility matrix; that is held in trust here. The count is Maxwell’s rule in Calladine’s form. The gap at rest is the steady-state error, or droop; pushing by the gap is proportional control, adding it up is integral control, and a push worked out from the weather is feedforward. The demand m₁ ∘ c = m₀ is semantic preservation, drawn as a commuting diagram; running two compilers against each other is differential testing; a compiler proved pass by pass is a verified compiler, and its languages in the middle are intermediate representations. A switch’s table is its forwarding table, learned by MAC learning; the agreed tree is a spanning tree, and copies running round a loop are a broadcast storm.

What the arrows let hide

In the first three footings the arrows hide arrangement: which tooth, where the joints sit, the form of the heater’s statement. In the fourth they hide the languages in the middle, and in the fifth they hide sequence: the order in which the machines spoke. In each, the plan is read from the drawing, and it says what to ask before anything is built: do the counts share a factor, is s − m what it should be, does the push pass through the gap alone, which two paths must agree, is it a tree. No dated claim is made on this page.
