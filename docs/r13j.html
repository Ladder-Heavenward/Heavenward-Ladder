13j · technique

The Chain Rule by Hand

Most functions are built in layers: something is done to the input, and then something else is done to the result. 13e proved that rates through a chain multiply, the outside's taken where the inside stands. This step makes the moves automatic. Find the last thing done, and give a name to what it was done to. Take the outside’s slope as if that name were the input. Multiply by the inside's slope. Substitute for the expression. Then come a displacement and a multiple inside, chains of three links, and chains inside products. Last come the wrong moves everyone makes, by name, and the rule run backwards.

Find the last thing done, and give a name to what it was done to. Take the outside’s slope there, and multiply by the inside’s slope. Substitute back, so the answer speaks only of the input.

13j1The Dodge, and Your Letter

One popular calculus book calls it a dodge. When an expression is too tangled to take directly, write a single letter for the tangled inside. Then the outside becomes something you can manage at once. Its example is the power three halves of a sum, and its letter is u.

That book writes u; this ladder writes r, as 9f and 11c did and 13e proved with. The gesture is what matters. Once the inside has a one-letter name, the outside is a plain power, root or reciprocal of that letter, which 13h already handles. Two slopes, one product, and the letter goes back to being what it stood for.

13j2Where to Cut: The Last Thing Done

9f's test never fails. Imagine putting a number in for x and working out the value by hand, in 4i's order of operations. The last thing you do is the outside; whatever it was done to is the inside, r.

  • (x² + 1)³: square x, add 1, then cube. Outside r³, inside r = x² + 1.
  • √(x³ + 8): cube, add 8, then root. Outside √r = r^(1/2), inside r = x³ + 8.
  • 1/(3x + 2)²: the last two steps, square and then one over, are together the power −2. Outside r⁻², inside r = 3x + 2.
  • x²(x + 1)³: the last thing done is multiply. The outside is a product, so product for slopes comes first, and a chain turns up only inside one factor.
  • x³ + 1: the last thing done is add. No chain at all: 13h.

The tempting illegal move is cutting in the wrong place. In x²(x + 1)³, calling x²(x + 1) the inside and cubing it describes another function. The question is never “what is in a bracket?” but “what was done last?”

13j4Where Each Rate Is Taken

A train of gears is the picture to keep. Two meshed gears turn at a fixed ratio. In a train each gear drives the next, so the ratios multiply: 2 turns for 1, then 3 for 1, is 6 for 1. Say the inside r turns 2 for each 1 of x, and the outside y turns 12 for each 1 of r near where r stands. Then y turns 24 for each 1 of x. Rates through a chain multiply; they do not add, because the second gear is driven by the first. Unlike gears, a chain's rates change from place to place, and that is the one thing to watch. The outside's rate is taken where the inside has got to, at r₀, not at x. For (x² + 1)³ at x = 1, the inside stands at r₀ = 2. So the outside's rate is 3 · 2² = 12, not 3 · 1². Times the inside's rate 2, the slope is 24.

In d-marks, 13e's line is dy/dx = dy/dr · dr/dx, with dy/dr at r₀ and dr/dx at x₀. It looks like cancelling dr, and that is a fine memory aid. It is not the proof: chain for slopes's proof never divided by Δr.

13j5A Displacement and a Multiple Inside

The plainest insides are x − c, for a fixed real c, and kx. 13d's displaced-slopes was the first: x − c has slope 1, so f(x − c) has slope f′(x − c) · 1, and the displacement pattern rides through untouched. The second is new.

Multiple-inside technique: for a real k ≠ 0 and g(x) = f(kx), if f has a slope at kw then g has one at w, and g′(w) = k · f′(kw). It is chain for slopes with the inside kx, whose slope is k by slope-of-a-line. Read in pairs (9e): when f's slope function has the pair (b, m), g's has the pair (b/k, km).

\big((2x)^3\big)' = 3(2x)^2 \cdot \htmlClass{new}{2} = 24x^2 @@ multiple-inside with k = 2. Check: (2x)³ = 8x³ (power-of-a-product), whose slope is 24x²
\big((2x - 3)^2\big)' = 2(2x - 3)\cdot\htmlClass{new}{2} = 4(2x - 3) @@ inside 2x − 3, slope 2: multiple-inside and displaced-slopes together. 13h's (2x − 3)² answered
\big((1 - x)^3\big)' = 3(1 - x)^2 \cdot (\htmlClass{new}{-1}) = -3(1 - x)^2 @@ k = −1: the minus sign is the inside's slope

So a displacement inside changes where the slope is read, and a multiple inside multiplies it. Neither is a picture of moving anything; both are chain for slopes with a linear function inside.

13j6A Root and a Reciprocal

y = \sqrt{2x + 1},\ \ r = 2x + 1,\ \ y = r^{1/2} @@ last thing done: the root
y' = \tfrac12 r^{-1/2} \cdot 2 = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2x + 1}}} @@ slope-of-√ (or fraction-power for slopes) at r, times slope-of-a-line; substitute for the expression
y'(4) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt9} = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac13} @@ at x = 4 the inside stands at 9
y = \dfrac{1}{(3x + 2)^2} = (3x + 2)^{-2} @@ rewrite into a power first (13h)
y' = -2r^{-3}\cdot 3 = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{-6}{(3x + 2)^3}} @@ negative-powers at r, times 3; substitute for the expression

A check with chords: √(2x + 1) has the whole-number values 1, 3 and 5 at 0, 4 and 12. The chord from 0 to 4 has slope (3 − 1)/4 = ½. The chord from 4 to 12 has slope (5 − 3)/8 = ¼. The slope at 4, ⅓, lies between them. That it must, for a function like this one, is rung 14's question; here it is a check. And the reciprocal by quotient for slopes the long way: (0 − 1 · 2(3x + 2) · 3)/(3x + 2)⁴, then cancel one 3x + 2. Same answer, longer road.

13j7Chains of Three

A chain can have more links. Peel from the outside in. The last thing done gives the outside and its inside r. Then ask what was done last inside r, and name that one's inside s, as 9f did. Every link contributes a rate, each taken where its own input stands, and they all multiply.

Chains-of-three technique: three rates through a chain of three multiply. Precisely: let k have a slope at w, g have one at s₀ = k(w), and f have one at r₀ = g(s₀). Then f ∘ g ∘ k has a slope at w, and it is f′(r₀) · g′(s₀) · k′(w). By 9b's regroup for composing, f ∘ g ∘ k = f ∘ (g ∘ k). Use chain for slopes on g ∘ k, then chain for slopes again with g ∘ k as the inside. In d-marks, dy/dx = dy/dr · dr/ds · ds/dx.

y = \big((2x + 1)^2 + 1\big)^3 @@ last: cube. Inside that: add 1 to a square. Inside that: 2x + 1
s = 2x + 1,\ \ r = s^2 + 1,\ \ y = r^3 @@ name the links from the inside out
\text{at } x = 0:\ \ s_0 = 1,\ \ r_0 = 2 @@ where each input stands
\dfrac{dy}{dr} = 3r_0^2 = 12,\ \ \dfrac{dr}{ds} = 2s_0 = 2,\ \ \dfrac{ds}{dx} = 2 @@ one rate per link, each at its own place
y'(0) = 12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = \htmlClass{new}{48} @@ chains-of-three

The illegal move here is skipping a link: multiplying the outside's rate by the innermost rate and forgetting the middle one, 12 · 2 = 24 instead of 48. Count the layers before you start; you need one rate per layer.

13j10Running It Backwards

A chain-rule answer is an outside's rate, written in terms of the inside, times the inside's rate. So read 5(3x − 1)⁴ · 3: the 3 at the end is the slope of 3x − 1, and 5r⁴ is the slope of r⁵. It came from (3x − 1)⁵, or that plus any constant.

Harder: given a slope, build a function. Take x(x² + 1)². Guess the inside from the bracket, r = x² + 1, whose slope is 2x. Is 2x standing beside the bracket? Almost: x is, half of it. A constant factor can be supplied, by constant-factor for slopes. The slope of (x² + 1)³ is 6x(x² + 1)², so the slope of ⅙(x² + 1)³ is x(x² + 1)². But suppose the inside's slope is missing a changing factor, as in (x² + 1)² with no x beside it. Then it cannot be conjured the way ⅙ was: 1/x is not a constant and will not ride. There, expand and go term by term instead: x⁴ + 2x² + 1 comes from x⁵/5 + 2x³/3 + x.

One more thing running backwards shows. 4x(x² − 3) is the slope of (x² − 3)², by the chain. Expand the slope instead, 4x³ − 12x, and undo it term by term: x⁴ − 6x². Two answers? Expand the first: x⁴ − 6x² + 9. They differ by 9, a lift, which no slope can see (lifting). Rung 16 builds this backward reading into a method.

Who said it first

The dodge is Silvanus P. Thompson’s, in Calculus Made Easy. In question 13j.4.4 absolute time is Isaac Newton’s; time as the order of events is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s, in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke.

Thompson calls it a dodge: when an expression is too tangled to take directly, write a single letter for the tangled inside, and the outside becomes something you can manage at once. His example is the power three halves of a sum, and his letter is u.

Thompson, Calculus Made Easy, 2nd ed. (1914), ch. IX

Thompson ends the chapter by noting that the same process extends to three or more links, one rate for each.
