How Does Light Stay Inside a Glass Fibre?

How does light stay inside a glass fibre? A strand of glass thinner than a hair carries the internet under the oceans, with light bouncing along inside it for kilometres and almost none leaking out. Light bends where it changes speed, and past a certain angle it cannot leave at all. This page finds that angle from 20·phys’s bending rule and 21’s sines, finds the cone of light a fibre will accept, finds how far a pulse spreads because steep and shallow paths take different times, using 23’s matching polynomials, and counts how many pulses a second the fibre can carry.

Light crossing into slower glass bends so that sin θ divided by the speed is the same on both sides. Going the other way, from slow glass to faster glass, the angle grows; past the angle where it would reach 90°, nothing gets out, and every ray is turned back. Rays at different slants travel different lengths, so a short flash arrives smeared out.

23phys21Light Bending at a Surface

Claire tests fibre for a telephone company. Her fibre has a core of glass, and around it a cladding of slightly different glass. Light is slower in glass than in empty space; the index of a glass, n, is how many times slower.

From here on, on this page, n₁ is the index of the core and n₂ of the cladding, with n₁ = 1.48 and n₂ = 1.46, and every angle of a ray is measured from the line square to the surface it meets.

\dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \dfrac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2} @@ the quickest path bends this way (20·phys)
v = \dfrac{c}{n}:\ \ n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2 @@ c the speed of light in empty space
\htmlClass{new}{n_1 > n_2:\ \theta_2 > \theta_1} @@ into faster glass, the ray swings away from the square line

23phys22Past a Critical Angle, None Escapes

As the angle in the core grows, the angle in the cladding grows faster, and reaches 90° first. Beyond that, the bending rule asks for a sine above 1, which no angle has (21b). The light cannot leave, and all of it is turned back into the core.

n_1 \sin\theta_c = n_2 \sin 90° = n_2 @@ the ray just skims the surface
\sin\theta_c = \dfrac{1.46}{1.48} \approx 0.9865 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\theta_c \approx 80.6°} @@ any ray slanted more than this off square stays in

Critical-angle technique: light going from glass of index n₁ into glass of lower index n₂ is wholly turned back when its angle from the square line passes θ_c, where sin θ_c = n₂/n₁. A ray running nearly along the fibre meets the wall at a grazing angle, far past 80.6°, and so bounces down the whole length.

23phys23The Acceptance Angle at the Fibre’s End

Light enters the flat end from air, where n = 1. A ray tilted α from the fibre’s axis bends to a smaller tilt φ inside. It stays in only if it meets the wall past θ_c, that is, if φ is at most 90° − θ_c, about 9.4°.

\sin\alpha = n_1 \sin\varphi @@ bending at the end
\sin\varphi_{\max} = \cos\theta_c = \sqrt{1 - (n_2/n_1)^2} @@ the complement (21e), and sin² + cos² = 1 (21c)
\htmlClass{new}{\sin\alpha_{\max} = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2} = \sqrt{2.1904 - 2.1316} \approx 0.242} @@ α at most about 14°

A light source must aim into a cone about 14° each side of the axis. The number √(n₁² − n₂²) is printed on every fibre’s data sheet, because it says how hard the fibre is to feed.

23phys24Steep and Shallow Paths Take Different Times

A ray straight down the axis travels the fibre’s length L. The steepest ray that stays in travels slanted at φ_max, a longer zigzag of length L / cos φ_max. Both go at the core’s speed c/n₁.

\Delta T = \dfrac{n_1 L}{c}\left(\dfrac{1}{\cos\varphi_{\max}} - 1\right) = \dfrac{n_1 L}{c}\left(\dfrac{n_1}{n_2} - 1\right) @@ cos φ_max = sin θ_c = n₂/n₁
\dfrac{1}{\cos\varphi} \approx 1 + \dfrac{\varphi^2}{2} @@ the matching polynomial near 0 (23a)
\varphi_{\max} \approx 0.165:\ \ \tfrac12 \varphi^2 \approx 0.0136,\ \ \tfrac{n_1}{n_2} - 1 \approx 0.0137 @@ the match is close for small slants (23b)
\htmlClass{new}{L = 1 \text{ km}:\ \Delta T \approx \dfrac{1.48 \times 1000}{3 \times 10^8} \times 0.0137 \approx 68 \text{ ns}} @@ a flash spreads 68 billionths of a second per kilometre

Claire sends a flash a billionth of a second long into a kilometre of this fibre and watches the far end on a fast detector: it arrives about 68 billionths of a second wide, as the count says.

23phys25How Many Pulses a Second Fit

A message is a stream of flashes and gaps. Two flashes closer together than the spread run into each other, and the receiver cannot tell them apart.

lengthspreadpulses a second, at most
1 km68 nsabout 15 million
10 km680 nsabout 1.5 million
100 km6.8 μsabout 150 thousand

The spread grows in step with the length, so length times pulse rate is fixed for a fibre: about 15 million pulses a second over one kilometre. That is why long links use fibre with a core so thin that only the straight path survives; with one path, there is no spread between paths at all. A network engineer choosing cable for a building or for an ocean reads this product off the data sheet.

Accepted names

Being wholly turned back is total internal reflection; the bending rule is Snell’s law; √(n₁² − n₂²) is the numerical aperture. The spread between paths is modal dispersion, the fibre here is step-index multimode fibre, and the thin-cored kind is single-mode fibre.

Who said it first

Charles Kao and George Hockham proposed in 1966 that glass fibre, made pure enough, could carry telephone calls; Kao shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.
