31n · technique

Eigenvalues by Machine

Rung twenty seven found stretches as the roots of a polynomial. For a grid of three rows that is work; for a grid of a thousand rows, the polynomial is hopeless, since its numbers are huge and a tiny error in them moves the roots far. Machines do not use it. They use two ideas this rung has already proved. The first is to apply the grid again and again to one arrow: the largest stretch takes over, and the arrow swings onto its axis. Reading the arrow with the grid on both sides then gives the stretch, with an error that is the square of the arrow’s error. Undoing the grid after a shift aims the same trick at any stretch you like. The second idea straightens the columns, multiplies the pieces back in the other order, and does it again. It is the first idea run on every column at once, it never changes the stretches, and a symmetric grid slides towards its own axes form.

Applying a grid again and again swings an arrow onto the axis of the largest stretch, and the arrow read with the grid on both sides gives the stretch with a squared error. A shift and an undoing aim it at any stretch. Straightening, then multiplying back the other way, keeps the stretches and runs the same idea on every column at once.

31n1Again and Again

27l2 proved that Aᵏx/λ₁ᵏ heads to c₁v₁ when |λ₁| is the largest stretch. A machine cannot divide by λ₁ before it knows it, so it cuts to size 1 at each step: xₖ₊₁ = Axₖ/|Axₖ|. The arrows swing onto the axis of λ₁, and the error shrinks by |λ₂/λ₁| each step. For a symmetric A, read the stretch off with R(x) = xᵀAx/xᵀx.

Two-sided-reading technique: for symmetric A, if x makes an angle ε with an axis e of stretch λ, then |R(x) − λ| ≤ (λmax − λmin) sin²ε: the reading’s error is the square of the arrow’s.

x = \cos\varepsilon\,e + \sin\varepsilon\,w,\ \ |e| = |w| = 1,\ w \perp e @@ split x along e and at right angles; R does not change when x is stretched
x^{\mathsf T}Ax = \lambda\cos^2\varepsilon + (w^{\mathsf T}Aw)\sin^2\varepsilon @@ Ae = λe, and e ⊥ w, so the cross terms are 0
R(x) - \lambda = (w^{\mathsf T}Aw - \lambda)\sin^2\varepsilon @@ xᵀx = 1; cos² = 1 − sin²
\htmlClass{new}{|R(x) - \lambda| \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\sin^2\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ wᵀAw lies between λmin and λmax (31e1)
  
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix},\ \ x_0 = (1, 0) @@ stretches 3 and 1 (31d3)
Ax_0 = (2, 1),\ \ A(2, 1) = (5, 4),\ \ A(5, 4) = (14, 13) @@ cutting to size can wait: only directions matter
R(2, 1) = \tfrac{14}{5} = 2.8,\ \ R(5, 4) = \tfrac{122}{41} \approx 2.9756,\ \ R(14, 13) \approx 2.9973 @@ the two-sided reading
\htmlClass{new}{\text{errors } 0.2,\ 0.024,\ 0.0027} \quad \blacksquare @@ each about 1/9 of the last: the angle shrinks by λ₂/λ₁ = 1/3, the reading by its square

31n2Aiming at Any Stretch

Again-and-again finds only the largest stretch. To find the one nearest a guess s, change the grid. A − sI stretches the same axes by λ − s, and its undoing stretches them by 1/(λ − s), which is largest for the λ nearest s.

Near-shift technique: if s is nearer to the stretch λ than to any other, then applying (A − sI)⁻¹ again and again swings an arrow onto λ’s axis, its error shrinking each step by |λ − s| over the next nearest |λ′ − s|. It is 27l2 run on (A − sI)⁻¹, whose stretches are the 1/(λ − s) of A’s axes.

A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\ \ s = 3.1 @@ a guess near the middle stretch
x_0 = (1, 1, 1);\ \ \text{solve } (A - 3.1I)x_{k+1} = x_k,\ \text{cut to size} @@ solving by elimination (27j), not undoing
R(x_1) \approx 3.229,\ \ R(x_2) \approx 2.791,\ \ R(x_3) \approx 2.762 @@ a machine’s arithmetic
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda \approx 2.761} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other stretches are about 0.539 and 4.700

Solving with A − sI when s is near a stretch looks dangerous. Its stretch sizes have a huge ratio σ₁/σₙ (31f3), and a machine loses about that many powers of ten in digits. Here the lost digits all point along the wanted axis, which is the one direction the answer needs. The danger and the purpose are the same direction.

31n3Straighten, Then Multiply Back

Start with A₀ = A. At each step straighten the columns, Aₖ = QₖRₖ (31c3), and multiply back in the other order: Aₖ₊₁ = RₖQₖ.

Same-stretches technique: every Aₖ has the same stretches as A, and if A is symmetric so is every Aₖ. Rₖ = QₖᵀAₖ, so Aₖ₊₁ = QₖᵀAₖQₖ: a turn of the measuring directions, which keeps the stretch-test polynomial, since det(QᵀAQ − λI) = det(Qᵀ(A − λI)Q) = det(A − λI), and keeps symmetry, since (QᵀAQ)ᵀ = QᵀAᵀQ.

Powers-in-QR technique: (Q₀Q₁⋯Qₖ₋₁)(Rₖ₋₁⋯R₁R₀) = Aᵏ: the straightened columns of Aᵏ are Q₀⋯Qₖ₋₁, so the method applies A again and again to every column at once, keeping them at right angles.

k = 1:\ \ Q_0R_0 = A_0 = A @@ the first straightening
\text{suppose } (Q_0\cdots Q_{k-1})(R_{k-1}\cdots R_0) = A^k @@ induction (4b)
Q_0\cdots Q_{k-1}A_k = AQ_0\cdots Q_{k-1} @@ Aₖ = (Q₀⋯Qₖ₋₁)ᵀ A (Q₀⋯Qₖ₋₁), unwinding the same-stretches technique
A^{k+1} = A\,(Q_0\cdots Q_{k-1})(R_{k-1}\cdots R_0) = Q_0\cdots Q_{k-1}\,Q_kR_k\,(R_{k-1}\cdots R_0) @@ the line above, then Aₖ = QₖRₖ
\htmlClass{new}{A^{k+1} = (Q_0\cdots Q_k)(R_k\cdots R_0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a product of staircases is a staircase, its middle numbers above 0
  
A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1.2\end{pmatrix} @@ one step; the corner 2.8 is 31n1’s first reading
A_2 \approx \begin{pmatrix} 2.9756 & 0.2195 \\ 0.2195 & 1.0244\end{pmatrix} @@ the corner is the second reading
\htmlClass{new}{A_4 \approx \begin{pmatrix} 2.9997 & 0.0247 \\ 0.0247 & 1.0003\end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the off-corner number shrinks by 1/3 a step: the grid slides to its axes form

The accepted names

Again and again is the power method (or power iteration), and R(x) the Rayleigh quotient. The near-shift technique is inverse iteration with a shift. Straighten-then-multiply-back is the QR algorithm; machines add shifts and first squeeze the grid into a near-staircase (Hessenberg form). The same-stretches technique says the Aₖ are similar, by orthogonal similarity. The ratio σ₁/σₙ is the condition number.

Who said it first

Richard von Mises and Hilda Pollaczek-Geiringer described the power method in 1929. John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh, used the quotient for the tones of vibrating bodies in the 1870s. John Francis in England and Vera Kublanovskaya in the Soviet Union found the QR algorithm independently in 1959 to 1961, building on Heinz Rutishauser’s earlier method of 1958 with a different splitting. It is counted among the most important computations of the 20th century.
