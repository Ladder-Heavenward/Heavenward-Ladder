4e · technique

Proof shapes

An argument is a run of short claims, and every claim carries its reason. This step is about the shape of an argument. Before you say anything, the statement you are arguing for tells you how the argument must begin and how it must end. An if starts by supposing. An every starts by letting a name stand for anything at all. A not starts by supposing the thing after it and hunting for something totally false. Between the opening and the close you choose a route: straight through, through the turned-about if, or by splitting into cases. And when the statement is not so, a counterexample finishes it. Each shape is licensed by a tech of rung 1 or 2, and each is a different way of reading the same goal. 4a built the proof itself, so this step can call these shapes proof shapes.

Read the goal's outermost mark; it hands you the opening move and the closing move. Fill the gap by the route that flows: straight, turned about, or split in pieces. To kill an every, show an example that fails.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 4a (the proof, built) and on rungs 1 and 2: the techs of 1b to 2b, the rewrites turn-and-deny and cases of 1f, and the counterexample of 2b and 2i. Every rung from here on is written in these shapes. 4c's induction is a shape of its own, and rung 7 adds the shape withheld until then, proof by contradiction proper.

4e1An argument is a run of claims

Stands on 1a (an argument, a reason for each statement) and the techs of 1b to 2b.

Each change to a line names what allows it: given, a word's meaning unpacked, or a tech cited by name. A column of lines is a proof as 4a built it: one line to a row, the boxes as indents. Say each claim in a short line, say why it is true, and stop when the goal is reached. The oldest accepted word for such an argument is demonstration: a chain of reasoning that yields knowledge.

4e2Words do most of the work

Stands on 4e1 (an argument is a run of claims) and 1a (unpack and pack).

The arguments in this step are about everyday things, because everyday things carry the shapes most plainly. The shapes are what matter, and they will carry unchanged onto every later rung. A word's meaning does most of the work. For example, “grandparent” unpacks into a some (someone with a child who is a parent), “parent” into an every around an if, and “puppy” into an and (young, and a dog). An argument opens by unpacking the words into statements with marks. It works with the techs, and packs the words back at the end.

4e4Shapes nest, and what you have has a shape

Stands on 4e3 (the skeleton from the outermost mark).

Shapes nest. Example: “every grandparent is a parent” is an every around an if. So the argument opens “let n be anything”, then “suppose n is a grandparent”, and the argument closes in the reverse order, as 2b9 showed.

What you already have has a shape too, and rung 1 gave each shape a tech for using it. An and lets you keep either part. An or you have invites you to reason by cases. An if can be carried through. A some lets you name an example freshly. An every lets you apply it to whatever you have. A not clashes with the thing it denies. Example: if you have “every cat purrs” and a cat called Tibbles, the every is applied to Tibbles. Read the goal for the skeleton; read what you have for the techs that fill it.

4e5Straight through: direct proof

Stands on 4e4 (nested shapes) and 2b (speak of everything, show an example).

The plainest route: suppose the hypothesis, unpack the words, work with the techs, repack. Here is a nested one. If n is a parent of b, and b is a parent of c, then n is a grandparent of c. What “grandparent of c” means: someone with a child who is a parent of c.

Let P(x, y) say x is a parent of y, and G(x, y) that x is a grandparent of y. The claim, in marks:

Read the skeleton off the marks. Three everys open with “let n, b and c be anything”. The if opens with “suppose n is a parent of b, and b is a parent of c”. The argument closes in the reverse order.

Here is the argument, said forward. Let n, b and c be anything, and suppose n is a parent of b and b is a parent of c. Then b is a child of n, and b is a parent of c: keep each part, and join them. So n has a child who is a parent of c, with b as the example: show an example. That is what “grandparent of c” means, so n is a grandparent of c: pack the word. Drop the supposition, which proves the if; and n, b and c were anything, so speak of everything.

Written as a column, one line to a row with a reason beside each, it is a proof in the sense of 4a.

4e6Turned about: proof by contrapositive

Stands on 4e5 (direct proof), 1f9 (the contrapositive) and 1d (turn-and-deny).

Now try this straight through: Rex never barks at a cat; so if Rex barks at a thing, that thing is not a cat. Let n be anything and suppose Rex barks at n. And then? You have an every about cats, and n is not known to be a cat. The hypothesis in hand and the promise you have do not meet. The direct route is stuck at the opening.

1f's turn-and-deny rewrite swaps P ⇒ Q for ¬Q ⇒ ¬P, two statements that say the same thing, and 1d proved the forward direction by techs. So read the goal for n, “Rex barks at n ⇒ n is not a cat”, turned about: “n is a cat ⇒ Rex does not bark at n”. That turned-about if is exactly what the every you have says of n.

Let C(x) say x is a cat, and B(x) that Rex barks at x. What you have, and what you want:

Inside what we want, turn the if about: C(x) ⇒ ¬B(x). That is exactly the every we have, word for word.

Here is the argument, said forward, with its two suppositions. Let n be anything, and suppose Rex barks at n. To show n is not a cat, suppose n is a cat. Rex never barks at a cat, so Rex does not bark at n: use the every on n, and carry the if through. That clashes with “Rex barks at n”, so n is not a cat: refute. Drop the first supposition, which proves the if; and n was anything, so speak of everything.

4e7What licenses the turn-about

Stands on 4e6 (the contrapositive argued), 1f4 (the last column) and 1b (refute).

Look at what licenses the turn-about. The goal's conclusion was itself a denial, “n is not a cat”. For such a goal the turn-about is nothing but 1d's turn-and-deny technique for If, run under a supposition of the hypothesis, with refute closing it: techs alone, good for anything. When the conclusion is not a denial, the turn-about needs turn-and-deny's backward direction, which 1f marked as waiting for rung 7, or as fine for settled statements. In life the statements are settled (you can look at the cat), so the shape is yours. On the endless ranges of later rungs you will have to ask.

Notice too what this shape is not. It does not suppose the conclusion is not so and hunt for something totally false in general. It proves a different, equivalent if, directly. That keeps the contrapositive clear of proof by contradiction proper, which lives on rung 7.

4e8Split in pieces: proof by cases

Stands on 1c (reason by cases) and 1f13 (the cases rewrite).

If you have P ∨ Q, and you can reach R from P and also reach R from Q, then you have R. That is 1c8's tech, and 1f packs that tech into the cases rewrite, good both ways for any statement. What you must check is that the cases cover everything: the or has to be something you have, not something you wish. Here is the shape on Ann's whereabouts.

Given: Ann is at the bakery or at the library. The bakery is on Main Street, and so is the library.

Let B be “Ann is at the bakery”, L “Ann is at the library”, and H “Ann is on Main Street”:

Cases are not examples. Checking some of the people who might be on Main Street proves nothing about Ann. Each case above covers every way its option could come about, because the case supposes only that option. And the or was given, not invented.

The split everyone reaches for, “either it is so or it is not”, is 1f's either-way principle. This rung grants the either-way split only for settled statements. For anything else, find an or you actually have. The slides do a split inside a split. The picture lets you try every route on a goal, and see which flows and where each sticks.

4e9Show an example that fails: the counterexample

Stands on 2b (the counterexample technique) and 2i8 (pushing a not through an every).

To prove an every, you need an argument. To refute an every, you need an instance. 2b's counterexample technique is good for any statement: hand over a thing where P fails, and the every is dead. History is generous with examples. For a long time Europeans used “every swan is white” as the model of a safe generalisation. Then sailors on the far side of the world saw black swans on a river, and the line was dead. The rung has its own black swans. “Every pet is furry” sounds like the kind of thing that is so, and a goldfish kills it. The moral is lopsided and permanent: no pile of examples proves an every, and a counterexample, alone, refutes it. The hunter in the picture finds such creatures for you.

Nemo is a goldfish: a pet, and not furry. So some pet is not furry: Nemo is the example. And some pet is not furry, so not every pet is furry. That last step is the counterexample technique (2b), with negated-if's licensed direction (1f) inside.

4e10Refutation

Stands on 1b (refute) and 2b (use a some with a fresh name).

1b5's refute proves a not: suppose P, reach ⊥, conclude ¬P. Here it is on a claim with a some inside, which is the usual case: nothing is both a cat and a dog, given that no cat is a dog.

The want starts with a not, so the skeleton is refute: suppose something is both, use the some with a fresh name, and reach ⊥.

Here is the argument, said forward. Suppose something is both a cat and a dog, and call it b. No cat is a dog, so if b is a cat, b is not a dog: use the every on b. b is a cat, by keeping the left part, so b is not a dog: carry the if through. But b is a dog, by keeping the right part, and that clashes. The clash reached ⊥ without mentioning b, so ⊥ stands after the call, and the supposition is refuted: nothing is both a cat and a dog.

4e11Where refutation stops

Stands on 4e10 (refutation) and 1b (not-not in, and the gap at ¬¬P).

Here is the boundary, said once more before rung 7. Refutation proves statements that start with not. The famous tech “suppose not-P, reach ⊥, therefore P” is different. Run on ¬P, refutation gives only ¬¬P, and getting from there to P is the step withheld until rung 7. The step from ¬¬P to P is proof by contradiction proper, and it belongs to rung 7. You have seen how much can be done without it: every argument of rungs 1 to 4 goes through with the shapes above.

A mathematician once called this way of arguing one of the finest weapons a mathematician has, finer than any sacrifice in chess. A chess player may give up a pawn, or even a bigger piece, to win. A mathematician puts the whole claim at stake: suppose it, and if something totally false follows, the claim is gone. The mathematician meant the full weapon, suppose not-P and reach P. This rung hands you the half that proves a not.

4e12Find it backwards, write it forwards

Stands on 4e5 (the grandparent argument) and 1d (affirming the consequent).

Discovery and presentation run in opposite directions. To find an argument you usually start from the goal and ask what would give it. Scratch work is allowed to wander: “I want a grandparent; that needs a child who is a parent; b is a child of n; is b a parent of c? yes, given.” The write-up then runs forward from what is given, every claim following from claims already made.

Here is that scratch work written forward. b is a child of n: given. b is a parent of c: given. So n has a child who is a parent of c: join, then show an example, b. So n is a grandparent of c: pack the word.

The illegal move hides in the gap between finding and writing up. Starting from the thing you want to prove and arriving at something so proves nothing. Example: “suppose Rex is a cat; then Rex is a cat or a dog; that is so; so Rex is a cat.” Each step in that chain runs the wrong way. The if affirmed is “if Rex is a cat, then Rex is a cat or a dog”, and the chain is 1d's affirming the consequent in an argument's clothes. Scratch work may run backwards only if, when you write it up, every step can be run forwards.

4e13Illegal moves

Stands on 4e5 to 4e12 (the shapes).

Five moves borrow the shape of a method in this step and leave out the part that makes it convince. One proves an every from examples. One proves an if by proving its converse. One splits into cases that do not cover every way things can be. One takes a failed argument for P as a proof that P is not so. And one refutes a some with one counterexample.

\begin{aligned}&\text{every pet here is}\\ &\quad\text{furry: Rex is, and Tib is}\,?\end{aligned} @@ examples prove a “some”, never an “every”: the goldfish is the counterexample
\begin{aligned}&\text{to prove “if } P \text{ then } Q\text{”,}\\ &\quad\text{prove “if } Q \text{ then } P\text{”}\,?\end{aligned} @@ that is another statement. Turned about is “if not Q then not P”
\begin{aligned}&\text{case Rex is a dog;}\\ &\quad\text{case Rex is a cat; so done}\,?\end{aligned} @@ the cases must cover every way things can be; split on P or not P, and Rex may be a fish
\text{my proof of } P \text{ failed, so } P \text{ is not so}\,? @@ a failed argument refutes nothing; refute needs the totally false reached from P
\begin{aligned}&\text{a pet that is not furry}\\ &\quad\text{refutes “some pet is furry”}\,?\end{aligned} @@ one counterexample refutes an every; to refute a some, show that every one fails

The first two illegal moves are the commonest in the wild. Examples feel like evidence, and they are, for a some. The arrow turned without its denials sounds like the arrow turned with them; only the one with the denials says the same thing.

4e14Where you stand

Stands on 4e1 to 4e13 (the shapes, and the moves that only look like them).

You can take a statement apart into its marks and read its skeleton off its outermost mark. You can prove an if by supposing, an every by speaking of anything, a some by showing, and a not by refuting. You can turn an if about, split on an or you have, and kill an every with an example. You know the rulebook for rephrasing and which of its directions are yours. You know the moves that look like techs and are not, by name. That is the ordinary toolkit of reasoning, the one every mathematics book assumes without saying so. It is built here from the ground floor with nothing assumed. The gap at ¬¬P is still open, and you know exactly where it is.

Now you're a person who can use some logic.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • ∴ is read “therefore” and ∵ “because”; written proofs often put them at the start of a line in place of the reason.
  • The end mark is also written □, or Q.E.D., short for the Latin quod erat demonstrandum, “which was to be shown”.

Who said it first

“Demonstration” is Aristotle’s word; the chess line is G. H. Hardy’s, about the method the books call reductio ad absurdum, which Euclid loved. The black swans were seen by Willem de Vlamingh’s crew on the Swan River, in 1697.

“By demonstration I mean a syllogism productive of scientific knowledge.”

Aristotle, Posterior Analytics I.2 (71b), trans. G. R. G. Mure, in The Works of Aristotle, vol. I (Oxford, 1928)

Hardy called reductio ad absurdum, which Euclid loved, one of a mathematician’s finest weapons, finer than any gambit in chess. A chess player may offer a pawn or even a piece, he wrote; a mathematician offers the game.

Hardy, A Mathematician’s Apology (1940), §12; paraphrase

4e · Straight through: direct proof

This column proves directly that a parent of a parent is a grandparent.

Take n, b and c, anything at all. Suppose n is a parent of b, and b is a parent of c.

Then b is a child of n: that is what “parent of” means, turned about. So b is a child of n, and b is a parent of c. So someone is a child of n and a parent of c: b is the example. That is what “grandparent of c” means. So n is a grandparent of c.

Drop the supposition: we have proved an if. If n is a parent of b and b is a parent of c, then n is a grandparent of c. And n, b and c were anything, so it is true for every n, b and c. The closing moves match the opening ones, in reverse order.

One step took thought: joining “b is a child of n” to “b is a parent of c”. You know the shape you need at the end: a some about n's children. The only way to make a some is to show an example, and b is the only candidate. So you steer toward a claim about b that has the shape the some wants, and join supplies it. That is manipulating, not processing.

\text{let } n, b, c \text{ be anything} @@ the everys give the opening line
\qquad \text{assume } n \text{ is a parent of } b \land b \text{ is a parent of } c @@ the if gives the next: its hypothesis assumed
\qquad b \text{ is a child of } n @@ keep left, and what “parent of” means, turned about: b is a's child
\qquad \htmlClass{new}{b \text{ is a child of } n \land b \text{ is a parent of } c} @@ join, with keep right
\qquad \exists y\,(y \text{ is a child of } n \land y \text{ is a parent of } c) @@ show an example: b
\qquad n \text{ is a grandparent of } c @@ ︿ “grandparent of”: that is what the word means
(n \text{ is a parent of } b \land b \text{ is a parent of } c) \Rightarrow n \text{ is a grandparent of } c @@ prove an if
\htmlClass{new}{\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,(\dots)} \quad \blacksquare @@ speak of everything, for each of n, b, c: the closing lines match the opening ones, in reverse order

4e · Turned about: proof by contrapositive

This column proves, from “Rex never barks at a cat”, that whatever Rex barks at is not a cat, by the turned-about if.

Given: whatever is a cat, Rex does not bark at it. That is what “never barks at a cat” means.

Take n, anything at all. Suppose Rex barks at n. The conclusion we want, “n is not a cat”, is a denial. So suppose the thing after the not: suppose n is a cat.

Rex never barks at a cat, so Rex does not bark at n. That is the turned-about goal, and the every we have hands it over. It clashes with our first supposition: ⊥. So “n is a cat” is refuted: n is not a cat.

Drop the first supposition: if Rex barks at n, n is not a cat. And n was anything. So whatever Rex barks at is not a cat.

\forall x\,(x \text{ is a cat} \Rightarrow \neg(\text{Rex barks at } x)) @@ given: what “never barks at a cat” means
\text{let } n \text{ be anything} @@ the goal is an every
\qquad \text{assume Rex barks at } n @@ the goal inside is an if: assume its hypothesis
\qquad\quad \text{assume } n \text{ is a cat} @@ the conclusion wanted is a denial: suppose the thing after the ¬
\qquad\quad \htmlClass{new}{\neg(\text{Rex barks at } n)} @@ use an every, carry through: the turned-about goal is the line you have
\qquad\quad \bot @@ clash
\qquad \neg(n \text{ is a cat}) @@ refute
\text{Rex barks at } n \Rightarrow \neg(n \text{ is a cat}) @@ prove an if

4e · Split in pieces: proof by cases

This column proves by cases, from “Ann is at the bakery or the library”, that Ann is on the High Street.

Take the cases. Suppose Ann is at the bakery. Then she is on the High Street. Suppose instead that she is at the library. Then, again, she is on the High Street.

Both cases end at the same claim. So, by cases, Ann is on the High Street.

\text{Ann is at the bakery} \lor \text{Ann is at the library} @@ given: the or-line that supplies the cases
\text{Ann is at the bakery} \Rightarrow \text{Ann is on the High Street} @@ given: the bakery is on the High Street
\text{Ann is at the library} \Rightarrow \text{Ann is on the High Street} @@ given: so is the library

\qquad \htmlClass{new}{\text{Ann is on the High Street}} @@ carry an if through

4e · Refutation, and where it stops

This column refutes “something is both a cat and a dog”, given that no cat is a dog.

Given: no cat is a dog. Suppose, until we drop it, that something is both a cat and a dog. Use the some: give it a fresh name, b. So b is a cat and b is a dog.

b is a cat, and no cat is a dog, so b is not a dog. But b is a dog. That clashes: ⊥. The ⊥ does not mention b, so it stands outside the some.

Drop the supposition: it is refuted. Nothing is both a cat and a dog.

\forall x\,(x \text{ is a cat} \Rightarrow \neg(x \text{ is a dog})) @@ given: no cat is a dog
\qquad \text{assume } \exists x\,(x \text{ is a cat} \land x \text{ is a dog}) @@ suppose the thing to be refuted
\qquad\quad \text{call it } b\text{: assume } b \text{ is a cat} \land b \text{ is a dog} @@ use a some: a fresh name
\qquad\quad b \text{ is a cat} @@ keep left
\qquad\quad \htmlClass{new}{\neg(b \text{ is a dog})} @@ use an every, carry through
\qquad\quad b \text{ is a dog} @@ keep right
\qquad\quad \bot @@ clash
\qquad \bot @@ the some closes: ⊥ does not mention b
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\,\exists x\,(x \text{ is a cat} \land x \text{ is a dog})} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute
