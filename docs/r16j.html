16j · technique

Volumes by cross sections

Rung 15 laid down three axioms for area and squeezed every other area out of boxes. Volume is built the same way, with solid boxes in place of flat ones. Three axioms for volume, and one squeeze between stacks of thin slabs, prove that a solid's volume is the integral of the areas of its cross sections. From that come the prism’s volume, base times height, and the pyramid’s, a third of that, from the area under the square. Then comes the technique that solids with matching slices have the same volume. Then the volumes of solids built on a flat region with square or rectangular slices. And last, the mass of a solid whose density changes from slice to slice.

Slice the solid by parallel planes. The volume is the integral of the slices’ areas along the line that crosses them. A prism: base times height. A pyramid: a third of base times height. Matching slices, matching volumes. Mass is the integral of each slice’s area times its heaviness for its size.

16j1Three rules for volume

From here on, a point of space is a triple (x, y, z) of reals, as a point of the plane is a pair (9e). For a set S of points of space and a real k, the slice of S at x = k is the set of pairs (y, z) with (k, y, z) in S.

From here on, for p < q, c < d and e < f, the box [p, q] × [c, d] × [e, f] is the set of points (x, y, z) with p ≤ x ≤ q, c ≤ y ≤ d and e ≤ z ≤ f. Its accepted name is a rectangular box, or cuboid.

Box-volume axiom: the box [p, q] × [c, d] × [e, f] has volume (q − p)(d − c)(f − e). Inside-no-more axiom for volume: if one solid lies inside another, and both have volumes, the first volume is no more than the second. Side-by-side axiom for volume: if two solids with volumes share no points except some on a single flat wall x = k, y = k or z = k, then the two together have the sum of the two volumes. Each is earned as the area axioms were: without one of them, a box has no size, a part could outweigh the whole, or two halves need not make it.

These are rung 15's three rules, one dimension up. Nothing else is assumed. In particular, nothing yet says that a ball has a volume at all. The next footing squeezes one out.

In this step the names prism and pyramid are given by slices alone. A prism's slices all have one area. A pyramid's slices shrink evenly, in every direction, toward a point. Nothing more about them is used here than their slices.

16j2Cross sections squeeze the volume

Slab technique: if a flat region R has area A, and the solid [p, q] × R has a volume obeying the three volume axioms, that volume is A(q − p). Here R lies in the (y, z) plane, like a slice.

\text{boxes inside } R \text{ total } L,\ \ \text{boxes over } R \text{ total } U @@ R’s area is squeezed by boxes (15c, 16g): L ≤ A ≤ U, and U − L as small as you like
[p, q] \times \text{each box} \text{ is a solid box} @@ the box-volume axiom: its volume is (q − p) times the box’s area
(q - p)L \le V \le (q - p)U @@ side by side, the walls y = k between them; inside-no-more for volume
\htmlClass{new}{V = A(q - p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ squeezed between two totals that close on A(q − p)

From here on, the slices of a solid S between the planes x = u and x = v are tight when, for partitions of [u, v] as fine as you like, each piece has two flat regions with areas: an inner one, inside every slice over the piece, and an outer one, holding every slice over the piece. And the total, over the pieces, of (outer area − inner area) times the width can be made as small as you like.

Cross-sections technique: a solid’s volume is the integral of the areas of its cross sections. Let S lie between the planes x = u and x = v. Let its slices be tight. Let the slice at x have area A(x), with A integrable on [u, v]. If S has a volume obeying the three volume axioms, it is ∫ᵤᵛ A(x) dx.

[x_{k-1}, x_k] \times I_k \subseteq S \cap \text{slab}_k \subseteq [x_{k-1}, x_k] \times O_k @@ the slices are tight: I_k, of area a_k, inside every slice over the piece; O_k, of area b_k, holding them all
\textstyle\sum m_k \Delta x_k \le V \le \sum M_k \Delta x_k @@ the slab technique for each; side by side, the walls x = x_k between them; inside-no-more for volume
\textstyle\sum m_k \Delta x_k \le \int_u^v A \le \sum M_k \Delta x_k @@ m_k ≤ A(x) ≤ M_k on the piece (inside-no-more, 15a), so the sums sit outside L(A, P) and U(A, P)
\textstyle\sum (M_k - m_k)\Delta x_k \text{ as small as you like} @@ tight
\htmlClass{new}{V = \textstyle\int_u^v A(x)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ two numbers between the same two totals, which close together, are equal

From here on, a solid whose slices are tight, with integrable areas A(x), has volume ∫ᵤᵛ A(x) dx.

Nesting slices are tight. If on each piece of a partition the slices grow, each inside the next, or shrink, each inside the one before, take the smallest slice for I and the largest for O. Then the total of (Mₖ − mₖ)Δxₖ is U(A, P) − L(A, P), which the test for integrable (15c) makes as small as you like. Every solid in this step has nesting slices.

16j3Prisms and pyramids

Prism technique: if every slice of S for 0 ≤ x ≤ h has the same area B, then S has volume Bh. A box p × q × r has volume pqr. ∫₀ʰ B dx = Bh; for the box, B = qr (box-area).

Pyramid technique: if the slice at x, for 0 ≤ x ≤ h, is the base slice shrunk by the factor x/h in each direction, so that A(x) = B(x/h)², then the volume is Bh/3.

A(x) = B\,\tfrac{x^2}{h^2} @@ a set shrunk by a factor c each way has c² times the area (boxes first, then squeezed: 15a, 15c)
V = \textstyle\int_0^h \tfrac{B}{h^2}x^2\,dx = \tfrac{B}{h^2}\cdot\tfrac{h^3}{3} @@ cross-sections; the table (16f)
\htmlClass{new}{V = \tfrac13 Bh} \quad \blacksquare @@ a third of the prism on the same base

Matching-slices technique: if two solids lie between x = u and x = v and their slices at each x have the same area, they have the same volume. When both have tight slices, both are ∫ᵤᵛ A by the cross-sections technique. A pile of coins pushed into a slant has the same volume as the straight pile.

One of the first to slice solids this way compared a flat figure to a cloth woven of parallel threads, and a solid to a book made of parallel pages. He argued that two solids are to each other as the totals of their pages, compared page by page.

16j5Density

A solid's density is its mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V when it is the same throughout; its accepted name is density, in kg/m³.

16j6Mass by slices

Density technique: if the density depends only on x, as ρ(x), and the slice at x has area A(x), the mass is ∫ᵤᵛ ρ(x)A(x) dx. On a thin slab from x to x + Δx the mass lies between the least and greatest of ρA times Δx; the sums squeeze to the integral (15h).

A = 2 \text{ m}^2,\ \rho(x) = 1200 + 300x \text{ kg/m}^3,\ 0 \le x \le 3 \text{ m} @@ a core from a field near Manteca; x is depth
m = \textstyle\int_0^3 2(1200 + 300x)\,dx = 2(3600 + 1350) = 9900 \text{ kg} @@ density; area-is-a-change

Solid shapes

Prisms and pyramids as shapes in their own right, with faces and edges, belong to the geometry of space, which the ladder builds after the plane. This step uses only their slices.

Who said it first

Cavalieri compared a flat figure to a cloth woven of parallel threads and a solid to a book made of parallel pages, and argued that two solids are to each other as the totals of their pages, compared page by page.

Bonaventura Cavalieri, Geometria indivisibilibus (1635); paraphrase

In 16j.4.4, the one who summed the pages is Bonaventura Cavalieri, and the Jesuit who objected is Paul Guldin.

Accepted names

The cross-sections technique is Cavalieri’s principle, in its integral form. The three volume axioms are the start of Jordan content, in three dimensions.
