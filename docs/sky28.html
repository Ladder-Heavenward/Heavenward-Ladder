Does a Random Path Remember the Grid It Walked On?

Does a random path remember the grid it walked on? Toss a coin and step left or right, as on rung 30. Now take the steps shorter and faster together, in just the right proportion, and the zigzag settles into one random curve. It does not matter whether the steps were coin tosses, or dice, or a walker on a square grid who sometimes stands still: the limit is the same path, and its law at each time is the spreading bell of the heat rule. The path is continuous, and it has a slope at no time anyone can name in advance. In the plane something more happens. The chance that the walker leaves a region through one stretch of its edge is a still state of heat, and still states of heat are carried from one region to another by the maps with a complex slope, which keep angles. So the limit path forgets not only the grid but the shape of the region, up to such a map. That is why some of its curves are known exactly. The outer edge of a path in the plane has dimension four thirds, guessed in the early nineteen eighties and proved in two thousand and one, with a family of random curves found in two thousand. Colour every cell of a honeycomb by a coin, and ask whether one colour joins two sides of a region: the chance has a limit, given by a physicist’s formula of 1992, proved for the honeycomb in two thousand and one, and kept by every angle-keeping map. On a square grid the same is believed and not proved, and in three dimensions the numbers that describe these paths are not even known.

A walk Sₙ of steps with average 0 and spread 1, read as W(t) = S_{nt}/√n, settles to one path law whatever the steps: W(t) is a bell of spread t, its pieces are independent, and its density solves u_t = ½u_xx. The chance h of leaving a plane region through part of its edge averages its four neighbours, so in the limit Δh = 0; and Δ(h ∘ f) = |f′|² (Δh) ∘ f for f with a complex slope. So exit chances, and with them the path, are carried by angle-keeping maps; a honeycomb of fair coins gives a crossing of a rhombus with chance exactly ½, and in a triangle the limit chance is a share of a side.

sky28.1Shrink and Speed Up

30n2’s walker steps a length h once every τ seconds. Shrink both and look at time t: there are t/τ steps, and by 30n1’s root-n technique the spread at time t is (t/τ)h². Only one proportion keeps the picture alive as both shrink.

Shrink-and-speed technique: a walk of steps with average 0 and spread width h, one every τ seconds, has spread t h²/τ at time t; it stays finite and above 0 as τ shrinks exactly when h² is a fixed multiple of τ. With h = √τ the spread at time t is t.

\text{Var}\,S_{t/\tau} = \tfrac{t}{\tau}\,h^2 @@ the root-n technique (30n1), t/τ steps
h = \tau:\ \ \text{Var} = t\tau \to 0 @@ τ = 0.01 gives 0.01 t: the walk freezes
h = \tau^{1/3}:\ \ \text{Var} = t\tau^{-1/3} \to \infty @@ τ = 0.01 gives 4.64 t, τ = 0.0001 gives 21.5 t: it flies apart
\htmlClass{new}{h = \sqrt\tau:\ \ \text{Var} = t \text{ for every } \tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one proportion that keeps a picture

So write W⁽ⁿ⁾(t) = S_{nt}/√n, with τ = 1/n and h = 1/√n, reading S between whole steps along the straight piece. With 100 steps to a second, W⁽¹⁰⁰⁾(0.37) = S₃₇/10 has spread 37/100. Its pieces over times that do not overlap use different steps, so they are independent, and each has spread equal to the length of its time.

\text{coin: steps } \pm1,\ \ P(S_{100}/10 \le 1) = 0.864 @@ exact count, 2¹⁰⁰ ways
\text{lazy grid: steps } -1, 0, +1 \text{ with chance } \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14,\ \text{spread } \tfrac12 @@ the left-right part of a walker on a square grid
P(S_{200}/10 \le 1) = 0.853 @@ 200 steps of spread ½ make spread 100
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both head to } P(Z \le 1) = 0.841} \quad \blacksquare @@ the central limit technique (30j3)

By 30j3’s central limit technique, at each time the scaled walk lands like the bell of spread t, whatever the steps, so long as they average 0 and have spread 1 once scaled. That is the first sense in which the path forgets its grid. The 1951 result, stated: the whole path W⁽ⁿ⁾, not only its value at each time, settles in law to one random continuous path. Held in trust: its proof finds, among the laws of the scaled walks, a settling thread in the manner of 22b2, and shows that every thread settles to the same law.

sky28.2A Path Built From Midpoints

The limit path can be built directly, without walks, one midpoint at a time. Write W for it. W(0) = 0, W(1) is a bell of spread 1, and its pieces over separate times are independent bells, each of spread its length. Given the path at two times, what is it halfway?

Bridge-midpoint technique: if W(s) = x and W(t) = y, then W at (s + t)/2 is (x + y)/2 plus a bell of spread (t − s)/4, independent of x and y.

A = W(m) - W(s),\ B = W(t) - W(m),\ m = \tfrac{s+t}{2} @@ independent bells, each of spread v = (t − s)/2
\text{the pair } (A, B) \text{ has density } \propto e^{-(a^2 + b^2)/(2v)} @@ independent: the densities multiply (30m)
\text{it depends on } a^2 + b^2 \text{ only, so } \big(\tfrac{A+B}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{A-B}{\sqrt2}\big) \text{ has the same density} @@ a turn of the plane keeps a² + b² and area (30j)
A + B = y - x \text{ is independent of } A - B @@ the turned pair is again two independent bells
\htmlClass{new}{W(m) = x + A = \tfrac{x+y}{2} + \tfrac{A - B}{2},\ \ \text{spread } \tfrac{2v}{4} = \tfrac{t-s}{4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ simulated: 0.2504 for v = ½, want 0.25

Fill in midpoints level by level: at level k there are 2^(k−1) new times, each a correction to the straight line between its neighbours, a bell of spread 2^(−(k+1)). Join the known points by straight pieces; level k moves that broken line by at most its largest correction. The broken lines settle everywhere at once if the corrections shrink fast enough, and they do.

Fine-midpoints technique: with chance 1, from some level on, every correction at level k is at most 2^(−k/4); so the broken lines settle everywhere at once, and their limit W is continuous.

P(|Z| > x) \le \tfrac{2}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2} \le e^{-x^2/2} \text{ for } x \ge 1 @@ ∫ above x of e^(−u²/2) is at most ∫ (u/x)e^(−u²/2); checked at x = 1, 2, 3
\text{correction} > 2^{-k/4} \iff |Z| > 2^{-k/4}/2^{-(k+1)/2} = 2^{(k+2)/4} @@ Z the correction over its spread width
P(\text{level } k \text{ bad}) \le 2^{k-1}e^{-2^{k/2}},\ \ \textstyle\sum_k \le 0.97 @@ one bound per new time; level 10 is below 10⁻¹¹
\text{only finitely many levels are bad} @@ the finitely-often technique (30h)
\textstyle\sum_k 2^{-k/4} = \tfrac{1}{2^{1/4} - 1} = 5.29 @@ the rest are fenced by a geometric sum
\htmlClass{new}{\text{settles everywhere at once; } W \text{ continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the at-once comparison and kept-continuity techniques (23d)

That is the path of 1923, built in another way. By 30h’s finitely-often technique and 23d’s kept-continuity technique, it is a continuous curve with chance 1, and its values at the halving times have exactly the law asked for. Values at other times follow by continuity, held in trust.

sky28.3Continuous, a Slope Nowhere

13f5 met a function continuous everywhere with a slope nowhere, and called it strange. The limit path is such a function with chance 1. The chord from time t over a run h is (W(t + h) − W(t))/h, a bell of spread h divided by h: a bell of spread 1/h. As the run shrinks, the chord spreads out instead of settling.

Fixed-time technique: for each time t named in advance, the chance that W has a slope at t is 0.

\tfrac{W(t+h) - W(t)}{h} = Z/\sqrt h,\ \ Z \text{ a standard bell} @@ the piece has spread h (sky28.1)
P\big(|Z/\sqrt h| \le M\big) = P(|Z| \le M\sqrt h) \le M\sqrt h @@ the bell’s density is at most 1/√(2π) < ½
\text{slope at } t \text{ of size} < M \Rightarrow \text{for some } \delta,\ |\text{chord}| \le M + 1 \text{ for all } h < \delta @@ chords settle at the slope
P(\text{that, for one } \delta) \le (M + 1)\sqrt h \text{ for every } h < \delta,\ \text{so } 0 @@ a fixed chance below every positive number
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{slope at } t) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ countably many δ = 1/j and M, each chance 0 (30b)
  
M = 10,\ h = 0.01:\ \ P(|\text{chord}| \le 10) = 0.683 @@ P(|Z| ≤ 1)
h = 0.0001:\ \ P = 0.080 @@ P(|Z| ≤ 0.1)
\htmlClass{new}{h = 10^{-6}:\ \ P = 0.008} @@ the chord leaves every fence

The technique speaks of one time, or countably many, named in advance; it does not reach every time at once, because there are more times than any list holds (35b). That every time at once lacks a slope, with chance 1, is true and held in trust; its proof bounds three chords at a time over a fine grid of times. The path also copies itself: W(ct)/√c is again the same path, for every c > 0, since its pieces are bells of spread equal to their length. Zoom in on it and it looks the same; a curve with a slope flattens to a line as you zoom.

sky28.4The Spreading Bell, Step by Step

33b3 said heat is a crowd of walkers and left it as a picture. The coin walk makes it exact before any limit. Write pₙ(k) for the chance that the walk is at k after n steps.

Average-of-two technique: pₙ₊₁(k) = ½pₙ(k − 1) + ½pₙ(k + 1); so pₙ₊₁(k) − pₙ(k) = ½(pₙ(k + 1) − 2pₙ(k) + pₙ(k − 1)), the heat rule on a grid.

\text{at } k \text{ after } n + 1 \iff \text{at } k \mp 1 \text{ after } n,\ \text{then a step } \pm1 @@ the last step, chance ½ each, independent of the past
p_{n+1}(k) = \tfrac12 p_n(k-1) + \tfrac12 p_n(k+1) @@ two cases that cannot both happen (30f)
\htmlClass{new}{p_{n+1}(k) - p_n(k) = \tfrac12\big(p_n(k+1) - 2p_n(k) + p_n(k-1)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract pₙ(k) from both sides
  
p_4 = \tfrac1{16}, \tfrac4{16}, \tfrac6{16}, \tfrac4{16}, \tfrac1{16} \text{ at } -4, -2, 0, 2, 4 @@ the counts of 2⁴ ways
p_5(1) = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{6}{16} + \tfrac{4}{16}\big) = \tfrac{5}{16} = \tfrac{10}{32} @@ the count of 2⁵ ways agrees
p_{100}(0) = 0.07959 @@ exact
\htmlClass{new}{2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 100}} = 0.07979} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell, times the grid’s spacing 2

Now let the steps be h every τ, and divide by τ. The left side heads to a slope in time and the right to h²/(2τ) times a second slope in space: u_t = D u_xx with D = h²/(2τ), 30n2’s D. With h = √τ, D = ½, and 33b3’s spreading-bell technique gives the law of W(t): the bell e^(−x²/(2t))/√(2πt), of spread t, as sky28.1 found by counting. Two roads, one bell: the walk’s count and the heat rule’s smoothing are the same fact seen at two scales.

sky28.5Exit Chances Average Their Neighbours

Put the walker on a square grid in the plane, stepping east, west, north or south with chance ¼ each, inside a region whose edge is a ring of grid points. Colour part of the edge. Write h(x) for the chance that the walker started at x first meets the edge at a coloured point.

Neighbour-average technique: h is 1 on the coloured edge, 0 on the rest of the edge, and at every point inside, h is the average of its four neighbours. There is only one such function.

h(x) = \textstyle\sum_{\text{4 steps}} \tfrac14\,P(\text{coloured exit} \mid \text{first step to } y) @@ split by the first step (30f)
P(\cdot \mid \text{first step to } y) = h(y) @@ the walk after one step is the walk from y
h(x) = \tfrac14\big(h(x_E) + h(x_W) + h(x_N) + h(x_S)\big) @@ the grid’s balance
\text{two such } h, h' \text{: the largest of } h - h' \text{ inside equals all its neighbours} @@ an average equals its largest only if all equal it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so it spreads to the edge, where } h - h' = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines for the least: one function
  
h(\text{centre}) = \tfrac14 @@ four sides alike
h(\text{top middle}) = \tfrac{59}{112},\ \ h(\text{top corner}) = \tfrac37 @@ nine linear equations, solved exactly
\tfrac14\big(\tfrac37 + \tfrac37 + 1 + \tfrac14\big) = \tfrac{59}{112} @@ the top middle averages its neighbours
\htmlClass{new}{h(\text{bottom corner}) = \tfrac1{14},\ \ h(\text{bottom middle}) = \tfrac{11}{112}} @@ far from the top, little chance

That is 33d2’s circle-average property on a grid, and its uniqueness is 33d3’s one-still-state technique in miniature. Shrink the grid: the exit chances head to the one balanced function of 33d1 on the region that is 1 on the coloured edge and 0 on the rest (held in trust; the limit link between walks and balanced functions dates from the 1940s). Exit chances of the limit path are still states of heat.

sky28.6Angle-Keeping Maps Carry the Walk

A function f with a complex slope (26d) acts near z₀ like multiplication by f′(z₀). By 26a, f′(z₀) = r e^(iφ), so it turns every direction by φ and stretches every length by r.

Angle-kept technique: where f′(z₀) ≠ 0, f keeps the angle between any two directions at z₀. Two small runs h and k from z₀ go to about f′(z₀)h and f′(z₀)k; both are turned by φ, so the angle between them is unchanged. For f(z) = z² at 1 + i, f′ = 2 + 2i: the directions 1 and i go to 2 + 2i and −2 + 2i, still at a right angle, each stretched by 2√2.

Carried-balance technique: if u is balanced and f = p + iq has a complex slope, then Δ(u ∘ f) = |f′|² (Δu) ∘ f; so u ∘ f is balanced.

w = u(p, q):\ \ w_{xx} = u_{pp}p_x^2 + 2u_{pq}p_xq_x + u_{qq}q_x^2 + u_p p_{xx} + u_q q_{xx} @@ the chain rule twice (28)
\Delta w = u_{pp}(p_x^2 + p_y^2) + 2u_{pq}(p_xq_x + p_yq_y) + u_{qq}(q_x^2 + q_y^2) + u_p\Delta p + u_q\Delta q @@ the same for y, added
p_x = q_y,\ p_y = -q_x:\ \ p_xq_x + p_yq_y = 0,\ \ q_x^2 + q_y^2 = p_x^2 + p_y^2 = |f^\prime|^2 @@ the real-part technique (33d1)
\Delta p = \Delta q = 0 @@ the same technique
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta w = |f^\prime|^2 (u_{pp} + u_{qq})} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked by machine for z³ and e^z with three choices of u

So an exit chance in one region, carried by an angle-keeping map that sends it onto another, is the exit chance in the other: both are balanced, both are 1 on the matching coloured edge and 0 elsewhere, and there is only one such function. From the centre of the unit disc the chance of leaving through an arc is its share of the circle, by turning. From p = ½ use 29c’s disc move f_p(z) = (z − p)/(1 − p̄z), which sends p to 0 and keeps angles.

f_{1/2}(\pm i) = -0.8 \pm 0.6i @@ (i − ½)/(1 − i/2)
\text{the right half goes to the arc through 1 between angles } \pm 2.498 @@ π − 0.6435
\text{share} = \tfrac{2 \cdot 2.498}{2\pi} = 0.795 @@ the same by integrating the disc’s rim formula
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a grid walk, 40 steps to the radius: } 0.786 \text{ in } 20\,000 \text{ runs}} @@ the grid is still visible at this size

More is true and held in trust: the image under an angle-keeping map of the limit path itself is again the limit path, run at a changed speed. That is the sense in which, in the plane, the path forgets not only its grid but its region, up to such a map. Every curve the path draws whose law is fixed by angle-keeping and by forgetting its past belongs to one family, found in 2000, with one member for each positive number κ (stated). With it, the outer edge of the path up to time 1, the boundary of what it fences off, was shown in 2001 to have 35h’s dimension 4/3: covering it with boxes of side 1/1000 takes about 1000^(4/3) = 10 000 of them, against 1000 for a smooth curve and about a million for the whole path, whose dimension is 2.

sky28.7Crossings on a Board of Coins

Tile a rhombus with n by n hexagonal cells, like a board for the game of Hex, and colour each cell red or blue by a fair coin. A red crossing is a chain of red cells, each touching the next, from the left side to the right side; a blue crossing joins the top to the bottom. Each is a path in the sense of 34c3 through cells of one colour.

No-draw technique: every colouring of the board has a red crossing or a blue crossing.

\text{border: a red row on left and right, a blue row on top and bottom} @@ four borders; they meet at the corners
\text{walk the cell edges from a corner, red on the left, blue on the right} @@ start where a red border meets a blue one
\text{at each corner of three cells, the third cell turns the walk} @@ red: right; blue: left; red stays left, blue stays right
\text{no edge is walked twice} @@ each edge has one way forward and one way back, so a first repeat would be the start, on the border
\text{the board is finite: the walk ends at another red–blue corner} @@ it cannot stop inside
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the red cells on its left join left to right, or the blue on its right join top to bottom}} \quad \blacksquare @@ cells along the walk touch in turn

Half-chance technique: with fair coins on the rhombus, the chance of a red crossing is exactly ½. Not both can happen: a red chain from left to right and a blue chain from top to bottom would have to meet in a cell (held in trust; it is a fact about curves in the plane). Flip the board across its short diagonal, which swaps left and right sides with top and bottom, and swap the colours: red crossings become blue crossings, and every colouring is equally likely. So the two chances are equal and add to 1.

n = 2:\ \ 8 \text{ of } 16 \text{ colourings have a red crossing} @@ none has both, none has neither
n = 3:\ \ 256 \text{ of } 512 @@ the same, by a machine count
\htmlClass{new}{\text{exactly } \tfrac12 \text{ for every } n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the half-chance technique

Other shapes are not symmetric, and their crossing chances are not ½; but as the cells shrink they head to limits that depend only on the shape, and angle-keeping maps keep them. A physicist gave the formula in 1992; in 2001 it was proved for the honeycomb. In an equilateral triangle ABC it is simplest: the chance of a crossing from side AC to a stretch BX of side BC heads to the share BX/BC (stated). A machine run with 60 cells to a side gave 0.27, 0.50 and 0.74 for the shares ¼, ½ and ¾. The outlines of the coloured clusters head to the member of sky28.6’s family with κ = 6 (stated).

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞28.oq.1Do crossings on the square grid forget the grid? Colour the squares of a fine grid by fair coins, joining squares that share a side, and the chance of a crossing of a rectangle is believed to head to the same limit as on the honeycomb, kept by every angle-keeping map. No proof is known: the 2001 argument uses a symmetry the square grid lacks. Since 2020 it is known that several square-grid models forget the grid’s direction, at least for turning, in the limit; turning is one angle-keeping map among endlessly many.
∞28.oq.2What are the numbers in three dimensions? In the plane the exponents that say how fast crossing chances shrink are known exactly, and the outer edge of a path has dimension 4/3. In space nothing like sky28.6 is available: maps that keep angles in three dimensions are few, and the exponents are not known. Even whether a cube of fair coins at the critical chance can have an endless cluster is open in three dimensions.
∞28.oq.3Why does the plane forget so much? Walks, crossings and several other models settle to curves that angle-keeping maps preserve, and the 2000 family describes them. Whether there is one reason behind all of them, or a separate proof for each model, is asked; the proofs so far are separate, and each needs a quantity on the grid found by hand that already almost keeps angles.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The path came before the mathematics. Pollen grains in water jitter under a microscope, and in 1905 a physicist explained the jitter as the kicks of molecules, with a spread that grows in proportion to time: sky28.1’s proportion. Experiments in Paris in the years after measured it, and settled for most physicists a long dispute over whether atoms are real.

In 1923 the path was built as a mathematical object, a law of chance on continuous curves; in 1951 it was shown that every walk with steps of average 0 and finite spread settles to it, which is why it appears wherever many small independent nudges add up. Its strangeness was a cost and a lesson: a model of motion with no speed at any time is either wrong about small scales or right about something the old calculus could not see. Both readings are argued, and both are used.

For decades the plane case was a store of guesses. Physicists, reasoning with symmetry, gave in 1992 an exact formula for crossing chances, without a proof a mathematician would accept. A mathematician who drew pictures of paths guessed in the early 1980s that the outer edge of a planar path has dimension 4/3. In 1980 it had been proved that the critical chance for one square-grid model is exactly one half, but the finer predictions stayed out of reach.

The turn came at the end of the century. In 2000 a family of random curves, one for each number κ, was found that captured exactly the curves an angle-keeping, past-forgetting limit could draw. Within a year the 4/3 guess was proved with it, and crossings on the honeycomb were shown to keep angles, confirming the 1992 formula there. The best-known medal in mathematics went to work in this area in 2006 and in 2010, and in 2022 to work on phase changes in three and four dimensions.

What is argued now is reach. The proofs depend on the honeycomb or on special grids, and the square grid, where most experiments are run, is still not covered for crossings. Some see a single principle waiting; others expect the proofs to stay model by model. In three dimensions even the basic exponents are open, and the pictures that make the plane tractable have no counterpart.

The accepted names

The limit path is Brownian motion, also the Wiener process; the 1951 result is Donsker’s invariance principle, and the midpoint construction is Lévy’s construction. The fixed-time technique is a weak form of the Paley–Wiener–Zygmund theorem that Brownian paths are nowhere differentiable; self-copying is Brownian scaling. The average-of-two technique is the discrete heat equation, and the neighbour-average technique says exit probabilities are discrete harmonic functions; their limit is harmonic measure, and the link between Brownian motion and the Dirichlet problem is usually credited to Kakutani (1944). The turn-and-stretch technique says holomorphic maps are conformal; the carried-balance technique is the conformal invariance of the Laplacian, and the statement about paths is the conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion. The 2000 family is the Schramm–Loewner evolution SLE(κ); the outer edge is the Brownian frontier. The board is the Hex board, and the no-draw technique is the Hex theorem; the coin colouring is critical site percolation on the triangular lattice, the 1992 formula is Cardy’s formula, and its triangle form is Carleson’s.

Who said it first

Albert Einstein explained Brownian motion in 1905, and Jean Perrin’s experiments confirmed it. Norbert Wiener built the process in 1923 (“Differential space”); Monroe Donsker proved the invariance principle in 1951. Benoît Mandelbrot conjectured the dimension 4/3 of the frontier. Harry Kesten proved in 1980 that the critical probability of bond percolation on the square lattice is 1/2. John Cardy published his formula in 1992 (J. Phys. A 25). Oded Schramm introduced SLE in 2000 (“Scaling limits of loop-erased random walks and uniform spanning trees”, Israel J. Math.); he died in a hiking accident in September 2008. Gregory Lawler, Oded Schramm and Wendelin Werner proved the 4/3 conjecture (“The dimension of the planar Brownian frontier is 4/3”, arXiv 2000, Math. Res. Lett. 2001). Stanislav Smirnov proved Cardy’s formula on the triangular lattice in 2001 (C. R. Acad. Sci. Paris 333). Werner received the Fields Medal in 2006, Smirnov in 2010, and Hugo Duminil-Copin in 2022 “especially in dimensions three and four”. Duminil-Copin, Kozlowski, Krachun, Manolescu and Oulamara proved rotational invariance of several critical planar lattice models (arXiv 2020). The Hex theorem’s link to fixed points is David Gale’s (1979).

A proof that is not allowed

“The path is a limit of zigzags, each with a slope at all but finitely many times, so the path has a slope at most times.” Not allowed: slopes do not pass to limits, even when the curves settle everywhere at once. 13f5 already had a limit of curves with corners that has a slope nowhere; the fixed-time technique shows the limit path is of that kind at every time named in advance.
