19·crypt · cryptography

Can a letter ever stand for itself?

Can a letter ever stand for itself? A century ago, armies sent orders by radio, so anyone could listen. Machines with turning wheels scrambled each letter before it was sent, and the scramble changed after every letter. Each wheel was wired so that a letter going in came out as another. The signal went through the wheels, bounced off a last wheel, and came back through them. What makes it hard is that the scramble is a long string of moves, different for every letter, and checking each one by hand is hopeless. This page shows, with 19’s moves and socks and shoes, that such a machine can never send a letter to itself, and that one setting both locks and unlocks a message.

A wheel is a shuffle of the alphabet, and turning it one step gives a turned shuffle. The signal passes through the scrambling wheels, then a wheel that pairs the letters off, then the scrambling wheels undone. So the machine undoes itself, and no letter is ever sent to itself.

19crypt1A wheel is a shuffle

A wheel has 26 contacts on each face, wired across in some jumbled way. A letter in on one face comes out as one letter on the other, and different letters come out different. A wheel is a shuffle of the alphabet. Call it σ.

After each letter the wheel turns one step. Write ρ for the shuffle that moves every letter one along: A to B, …, Z to A. A letter entering the turned wheel meets the contact that used to sit one place back. So the turned wheel undoes the step, does σ, then steps again.

\text{turned once}:\ \ \rho\,\sigma\,\rho^{-1} @@ back one, through the wiring, forward one
\text{turned } k \text{ times}:\ \ \rho^{k}\,\sigma\,\rho^{-k} @@ by induction on k (4f); ρ⁻ᵏ undoes ρᵏ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every turned wheel is a shuffle}} \quad \blacksquare @@ shuffles compose to shuffles (19a)

19crypt2Through, and back

Three wheels in a row make one shuffle, S = σ₃σ₂σ₁ at the present turns. At the end sits a wheel that never turns. Its wires join the letters in 13 pairs, so it sends each letter to its partner and the partner back. Call it R. Then the signal comes back through the three wheels the other way.

Through-and-back technique: the machine’s shuffle is E = S⁻¹ R S. Going back through the wheels runs each one backwards, and the last wheel passed on the way in is the first on the way out: socks and shoes.

\text{in}:\ \ \sigma_3 \sigma_2 \sigma_1 = S @@ three wheels, first σ₁
\text{back}:\ \ \sigma_1^{-1} \sigma_2^{-1} \sigma_3^{-1} = (\sigma_3 \sigma_2 \sigma_1)^{-1} @@ each wheel backwards, σ₃ first: socks and shoes (19c)
\htmlClass{new}{E = S^{-1} R\, S} \quad \blacksquare @@ in, bounce, back

19crypt3Never itself, and its own undo

No-self technique: the machine never sends a letter to itself. Self-undo technique: the machine undoes itself, so one setting both locks and unlocks.

\text{assume } E\,x = x @@ aiming to refute
R\,(S x) = S x @@ apply S to both sides of S⁻¹RSx = x
R \text{ sends no letter to itself} @@ R pairs every letter with another
\htmlClass{new}{\bot,\ \text{so } E\,x \ne x \text{ for every } x} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute
  
E\,E = S^{-1} R\, S\, S^{-1} R\, S @@ two passes at one setting
= S^{-1} R\, R\, S = S^{-1} S @@ S S⁻¹ = e; R R = e, since R swaps pairs
\htmlClass{new}{E\,E = e} \quad \blacksquare @@ type the secret text in, and the message comes out

Add a board that swaps some letters in pairs before and after the wheels, a shuffle P. The machine becomes P⁻¹S⁻¹RSP, the same shape with SP for S. Both techniques still hold.

19crypt4Counting the settings

The keeper of the secret chose three wheels from a box of five, in order, then the starting turn of each, then ten pairs of letters on the board. Count by stages (4k).

5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 60 @@ three wheels, in order
26^3 = 17\,576 @@ the starting turns
\frac{26!}{6!\;10!\;2^{10}} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{14} @@ ten pairs: choose 20 letters, pair them, forget the order of pairs and within each
\htmlClass{new}{60 \cdot 17\,576 \cdot 1.5 \times 10^{14} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{20}} @@ stages multiply

Try a million settings a second, and the count takes about five million years. A count that large hides the key from anyone who only tries settings. It does not hide it from someone who can rule out settings without trying them.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The wheels are rotors, the bounce wheel is the reflector, and the board is the plugboard. ρσρ⁻¹ is σ conjugated by ρ, and S⁻¹RS a conjugate of R. A shuffle that undoes itself is an involution; one that sends no letter to itself is a derangement. The machine itself was the Enigma.

Who said it first

Arthur Scherbius patented the rotor machine in 1918; the reflector came in 1926. Marian Rejewski (1932) broke it with exactly this page’s algebra: he wrote the daily keys as conjugates and used the fact that conjugate shuffles have the same cycle shapes. Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman and others at Bletchley Park built on that work. In 19·crypt.6.5, the British codebreakers used the no-self rule to slide a guessed word along a message and rule out every place where a letter matched itself.
