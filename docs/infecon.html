What Stays Put in a Market?

What stays put in a market? Four economics annexes, from rung 7 to rung 42, each looked for a state that holds: a pair of choices neither prisoner will leave, a chain of exchange rates that comes home, a price where buyers and sellers match, a pairing that no two would break. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. In each, the state that holds is a thing some arrow sends to itself, or a loop that composes to do-nothing. Arrows also keep two questions apart that the annexes had to keep apart in words: whether such a thing exists, and whether a row of arrows ever arrives at it.

(a, b) is stuck exactly when a = B(C(a)) and b = C(a), for the reply arrows B and C. Every loop of rates composes to 1 exactly when r(X → Y) = v(X)/v(Y) for some values v. T(p) = p − λE(p) keeps exactly the matching prices, for every λ ≠ 0; the row p, T(p), T(T(p)), … closes in on one or does not. Among pairings that hold, the doctors’ order is the hospitals’ order turned round.

infecon.1A Stuck Pair Is Kept by a Loop

7·econ3 called a pair of choices stuck when neither player gains by changing alone, and found the one stuck pair of the prisoners’ table by checking all four. ∞16 built games from pieces by their best replies. Suppose each player has exactly one best reply to each choice of the other. Write B(b) for your best reply to her choice b, and C(a) for hers to your choice a.

Reply-loop technique: (a, b) is stuck exactly when a = B(b) and b = C(a). So the stuck pairs are the pairs (a, C(a)) with a kept by the loop B ∘ C. If B gives one choice whatever she does, there is exactly one stuck pair.

(a, b) \text{ stuck} \iff a = B(b) \text{ and } b = C(a) @@ neither gains by changing alone: each choice is the best reply to the other
a = B(b),\ b = C(a) \Rightarrow a = B(C(a)) @@ put the second into the first
a = B(C(a)) \Rightarrow (a, C(a)) \text{ is stuck} @@ with b = C(a): then a = B(b)
B(b) = a_0 \text{ for every } b \Rightarrow B(C(a)) = a_0:\ \text{the loop keeps } a_0 \text{ only} @@ the better-either-way technique (7·econ2) says B is of this kind
\htmlClass{new}{\text{exactly one stuck pair},\ (a_0, C(a_0))} \quad \blacksquare @@ for the prisoners: (tell, tell), as the stuck-pair technique (7·econ3) found
  
B(b) = b,\ C(a) = a:\quad B \circ C = \text{do-nothing} @@ each wants to match the other: every a is kept, and every (a, a) is stuck
B(b) = b,\ C(a) = \text{the other choice}:\quad B(C(a)) \ne a @@ one wants to match, one to differ: nothing is kept
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no stuck pair}} @@ the no-rest technique (∞16): the replies chase each other round

What hides is the arrangement: the years in the table. Only the two reply arrows are used. The trap of 7·econ3 is then one line: the loop keeps a pair, and nothing in a loop asks whether the pair is good for anyone.

infecon.2A Chain That Comes Home

8·econ2 carried money through a chain of exchange rates, and then met a booth whose round trip did not come home. Take the moneys as things and a rate as an arrow: r(X → Y) is how much of Y one unit of X buys. A chain is a composite, and its rate is the product.

Loop-free technique: every loop of rates composes to 1 exactly when there are values v with r(X → Y) = v(X)/v(Y) for every two moneys. Then every two chains from X to Y give the same amount.

r(X \to Y) = \tfrac{v(X)}{v(Y)}:\quad \tfrac{v(X_0)}{v(X_1)} \cdot \tfrac{v(X_1)}{v(X_2)} \cdots \tfrac{v(X_{k-1})}{v(X_0)} = 1 @@ leftward: the middle values cancel
\text{every loop is } 1:\ \text{pick one money } O,\ \text{put } v(X) = r(X \to O) @@ rightward: the worth of one X, in O
r(X \to Y) \cdot r(Y \to O) \cdot r(O \to X) = 1,\qquad r(O \to X) \cdot r(X \to O) = 1 @@ two loops
\htmlClass{new}{r(X \to Y) = \tfrac{r(X \to O)}{r(Y \to O)} = \tfrac{v(X)}{v(Y)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide the first loop by the second
\text{two chains } X \to Y:\ \text{the first, then the second run backwards, is a loop} @@ so their rates are equal
  
r(\text{USD} \to \text{CAD}) = 1.39,\quad r(\text{CAD} \to \text{MXN}) = 13.2:\quad v = (18.348,\ 13.2,\ 1) \text{ in pesos} @@ the rates of 8·econ2
r(\text{USD} \to \text{MXN}) = \tfrac{18.348}{1} = 18.348 @@ any other rate would open a loop that is not 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the booth}:\ 1.36 \cdot \tfrac{1}{1.42} \approx 0.958} @@ a loop that is not 1: no values v fit its two rates, and the gap is its fee

What hides is the sequence: the route. With loops at 1, the amount at the end depends on the two ends alone, and each money has one worth. A loop above 1 would be free money, and traders close such loops by running round them.

infecon.3A Price That Is Kept, and a Row That May Not Arrive

12·econ2 proved that a matching price exists, where the surplus E = S − D is 0. 12·econ5 then warned that the proof says nothing about whether a market gets there. Arrows hold the two apart. Let a market move its price against the surplus: T(p) = p − λE(p), with λ > 0 for how hard it moves.

Kept-price technique: T keeps p exactly when E(p) = 0, whatever λ ≠ 0 is. For a straight surplus E(p) = s(p − p*) with s > 0, the row p, T(p), T(T(p)), … closes in on p* exactly when 0 < λs < 2.

T(p) = p \iff \lambda E(p) = 0 \iff E(p) = 0 @@ λ is not 0: the kept prices are the matching prices
T(p) - p^* = (p - p^*) - \lambda s\,(p - p^*) = (1 - \lambda s)(p - p^*) @@ the straight surplus
T^k(p) - p^* = (1 - \lambda s)^k (p - p^*) @@ induction on k (4c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the gap shrinks to } 0 \iff |1 - \lambda s| \lt 1 \iff 0 \lt \lambda s \lt 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ a power of a number shrinks exactly when its size is below 1
  
s = 2,\ p^* = 4.5,\ p = 4:\qquad \lambda = 0.4:\ \text{gaps } -0.5,\ -0.1,\ -0.02,\ \dots @@ 1 − λs = 0.2: it closes in
\lambda = 1.2:\ \text{gaps } -0.5,\ 0.7,\ -0.98,\ 1.372,\ \dots @@ 1 − λs = −1.4: it overshoots, further each time
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both markets keep the same price},\ 4.5} @@ the kept thing is there either way

What hides in the first line is the sequence: the search. That a kept price exists is a fact about one arrow. Whether the row arrives is a fact about the arrow run again and again, and it can fail while the kept price sits in plain view.

infecon.4One Order, Read From Both Sides

42·econ.4 proved that when the doctors propose, each doctor ends with the best hospital possible for them, and each hospital with the worst doctor possible for it. That is two results. They are one, about the pairings that hold as an order. Write M ≥ M′ for the doctors when every doctor likes the partner in M at least as well as the partner in M′, and the same for the hospitals. An order has at most one arrow between two things, so this is a picture of arrows.

Turned-order technique: for pairings M and M′ that hold, M ≥ M′ for the doctors exactly when M′ ≥ M for the hospitals. The hospitals’ order is the doctors’ order with every arrow turned round.

\text{suppose } M \ge M' \text{ for the doctors, and a hospital } h \text{ prefers its doctor } d \text{ in } M \text{ to its doctor in } M' @@ aiming to refute (7f)
d \text{ is paired with } h \text{ in } M \text{ and with another hospital } h' \text{ in } M' @@ h has a different doctor in M′
d \text{ prefers } h \text{ to } h' @@ d likes M at least as well, and h is not h′
d \text{ and } h \text{ block } M' @@ each prefers the other to the partner in M′; but M′ holds
\htmlClass{new}{M \ge M' \text{ for the doctors} \Rightarrow M' \ge M \text{ for the hospitals}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and the other way by the same lines, with the sides swapped
  
M = u\text{–}J,\ v\text{–}K,\ w\text{–}H:\quad \text{doctors’ choices } 1, 2, 2;\quad \text{hospitals’ } 2, 2, 1 @@ the doctors propose
M' = u\text{–}K,\ v\text{–}J,\ w\text{–}H:\quad \text{doctors’ choices } 3, 3, 2;\quad \text{hospitals’ } 1, 1, 1 @@ the hospitals propose
\htmlClass{new}{M \ge M' \text{ for the doctors},\quad M' \ge M \text{ for the hospitals}} @@ the top of one order is the bottom of the other

What hides is the sequence: who proposed on which day. The run of proposals finds the top of the doctors’ order whatever order the doctors go in, and the top of one side’s order is the bottom of the other’s. Choosing who proposes is choosing which end of one order to stand at.

The accepted names

A stuck pair is a Nash equilibrium in pure strategies, B and C are best-response functions, a thing an arrow sends to itself is a fixed point, and a choice that is better either way is a dominant strategy. A loop of rates above 1 is an arbitrage, loops at 1 are no arbitrage, the rate through a third money is a cross rate, the booth’s gap is the bid-ask spread, and rates of the form v(X)/v(Y) are a coboundary: the loop-free technique says a cocycle on a connected picture with every loop trivial is one. A matching price is a market-clearing, or equilibrium, price; E is the excess supply; moving the price against it is tâtonnement; a kept price the row closes in on is stable, and the overshooting row is a cobweb. A pairing that holds is a stable matching; the run of proposals is deferred acceptance; the pairings that hold form a lattice, with the doctor-optimal matching at one end and the hospital-optimal at the other, and an order with its arrows turned round is the opposite order.

What the arrows let hide

In the first footing the arrows hide arrangement: the numbers in the table. In the second and fourth they hide sequence: the route of a chain, and the days of the proposals. In the third the sequence is hidden by the first line and brought back by the second, and that is the footing’s point: a kept thing and a row that arrives at it are two facts. In each footing the state that holds is said with one arrow: a thing a loop keeps, a loop that is do-nothing, a thing an arrow keeps, an end of an order. No dated claim is made on this page.
