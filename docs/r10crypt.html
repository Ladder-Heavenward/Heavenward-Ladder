10·crypt · cryptography

Is a huge number of keys enough?

Is a huge number of keys enough? 4·crypt's shift had 25 keys worth trying, and an attacker tried them all in a few minutes. The obvious repair is more keys. Let the key send each letter anywhere at all, so long as no two letters land on the same one. Then the keys number about four hundred million million million million, more than a machine trying a billion a second could finish in the age of the universe. For centuries, people who used such ciphers trusted them with their lives. It is hard to see why they were not, because the count is real. This page builds the cipher as a function and counts its keys. Then it proves that the key moves every letter's tally without changing it, and breaks the cipher from the tallies, without trying a single key.

A key is a matching of the alphabet with itself; it encrypts letter by letter, and its inverse decrypts. There are twenty-six factorial keys. But each cipher letter’s tally is the tally of the plain letter it stands for, so the tallies, ranked against English, read the key off a letter at a time.

The class · the cipher, its keys, the tallies, the attack

A key is a bijection

From here on, on this page, 𝒜 names the set of the 26 letters, and a text is a list of letters, a function from n to 𝒜 (9·cb4's lists). m names a plain text and c a cipher text, as on 9·crypt; m_i is the letter at place i.

A substitution cipher takes as its key a bijection g : 𝒜 → 𝒜 and encrypts letter by letter: c_i = g(m_i) at every place i. Decrypting is m_i = g⁻¹(c_i), by 9d's there-and-back. So the key that decrypts is the way back, g⁻¹, and it is again a bijection of 𝒜. The key must be a bijection. If it were not one-to-one, two letters would land on one, and the reader could not tell which was meant. The worked key below is the keyboard's top row, then the middle row, then the bottom row: A ↦ Q, B ↦ W, C ↦ E, and so on to Z ↦ M.

\begin{aligned}&g:\ \text{ABCDEFGHIJKLM} \\ &\quad \mapsto \text{QWERTYUIOPASD},\\ &\quad \ \ \text{NOPQRSTUVWXYZ} \\ &\quad \mapsto \text{FGHJKLZXCVBNM}\end{aligned} @@ a bijection: each letter used once on the right
g(\text{THE}) = \htmlClass{new}{\text{ZIT}} @@ letter by letter
g^{-1}(\text{ZIT}) = \htmlClass{keep}{\text{THE}} @@ there-and-back: read the table from right to left (9d's swapped pairs)

Counting the keys

Keys technique: the substitution ciphers on 26 letters have exactly 26! keys. A bijection g : 𝒜 → 𝒜 is fixed by its list g(A), g(B), …, g(Z), which uses each letter once. That list is an arrangement of 𝒜 (4k), and each arrangement is the list of exactly one bijection. By 4k's arrangements technique there are 26! of them. ∎

26! = 403\,291\,461\,126\,605\,635\,584\,000\,000 @@ 26 · 25 · ⋯ · 1
\approx \htmlClass{new}{4.03 \times 10^{26}} @@ scientific form, 3 figures
\tfrac{4.03 \times 10^{26}}{10^{9} \text{ per s}} \approx 4.03 \times 10^{17} \text{ s} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{10} \text{ years} @@ a year is about 3.16 × 10⁷ s; the universe is about 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years old

The shifts of 4·crypt are among these keys. With letters as 0 to 25, the shift by k sends x to the remainder of x + k on division by 26. There are 26 shifts, counting the shift by 0, which hides nothing. A shift by j after a shift by k is the shift by the remainder of j + k (4·crypt's shifts-add), and the shift by 26 − k undoes the shift by k (its key-back). So 26 of the 26! keys are shifts, and a shift is fixed by where it sends one letter. A general key is fixed only by where it sends 25 letters; the 26th has one place left.

The tallies travel with the letters

9·stat's frequency function gives each letter its tally in a text. Write f_m for the plain text's and f_c for the cipher text's, both as functions 𝒜 → ℕ, with tally 0 for a letter that does not appear.

Tallies-travel technique: for every letter x, the cipher text holds g(x) exactly as often as the plain text holds x: f_c(g(x)) = f_m(x). So f_c = f_m ∘ g⁻¹.

c_i = g(x) \iff g(m_i) = g(x) @@ the cipher: c_i = g(m_i)
\iff m_i = x @@ g is one-to-one (leftward: substitute for the expression)
\{i : c_i = g(x)\} = \{i : m_i = x\} @@ Ext: the same places
f_c(g(x)) = \htmlClass{keep}{f_m(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ one set of places, one number of members (4k)

The key moves every tally to a new letter. It cannot change the tallies themselves: the cipher text's list of tallies, put in order, is the plain text's. A bar chart of the cipher text is the plain text's bar chart with its labels shuffled. Whoever knows the plain text's chart can put the labels back.

English has a chart

Count the letters of a long stretch of English prose and share each tally by the total. The shares come out nearly the same from book to book: E about 12.7%, T 9.1%, A 8.2%, O 7.5%, I 7.0%, N 6.7%, down to Q and Z, each under 0.1%. That is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity, with a range and with exceptions. Its range is long English prose. Short texts wander from it, lists and names wander further, and a novel of 1939 runs to some 50 000 words without a single E.

Rung 10 gives the law its shape. Take one long text, and let u_n be the share of E among its first n letters: a sequence. The law guesses that such sequences head to about 12.7%, in 10f's sense. That is a guess about every n, and no finite text can prove it. What a text can show is how near it comes. In this page's passage about the Salinas River (402 letters), u_n is 10% at n = 50, 12% at 100, 13% at 200 and 13.9% at 402. Against a challenger's ε of 3 percentage points, it stays within ε of 12.7% from n = 54 on. Against an ε of 2, only from n = 341 on. Against an ε of 1, it never settles inside this passage. The attacker does not need the limit. She needs the top few letters in nearly the right order, and a few hundred letters give her that.

Breaking it without a key

The attack runs the tallies-travel technique backwards. Tally the cipher text. Its most frequent letter is probably g(E), the next g(T) or g(A), and so on down. Guess g⁻¹ from the ranks, then mend the guess with words: THE is the commonest word, and a guess that turns ZIT into TNE wants N and H swapped.

f_c:\ \ \text{T } 56,\ \text{Z } 35,\ \text{Q } 33,\ \text{K } 31,\ \text{F } 30,\ \text{I } 26,\ \ldots @@ the cipher text's top tallies
\text{guess by rank:}\ \text{T} \to \text{E},\ \text{Z} \to \text{T},\ \text{Q} \to \text{A},\ \text{K} \to \text{O},\ \text{F} \to \text{I},\ \text{I} \to \text{N} @@ English's order E, T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R, …
\text{ZITLQSOFQL} \mapsto \text{TNEHADRIAH} @@ E, T and A come out right; TNE looks like THE
\text{I} \to \text{H},\ \text{O} \to \text{I},\ \ldots \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{\text{THESALINAS}} @@ mended by words, a letter at a time

Each mended letter makes the next one easier, and the work never touches the 26! keys. It grows with the length of the text, not with the number of keys. A huge number of keys guards against one attack only: trying them all. A cipher is as strong as the cheapest attack on it, and here the cheapest attack uses the one thing the key cannot change.

A shift falls faster still. One tally is enough: if the top letter of the cipher text is X, then E went to X, and the shift is the remainder of 23 − 4 = 19. A general key needs a tally for each letter, and the rare letters, Q, Z, J, X, are settled last, by words.

Running it backwards

Handed g, decrypt with g⁻¹: read the key's table from right to left. Handed a cipher text and no key, tally it, rank the tallies against English, and mend the guess with common words. Handed two texts and asked whether one is a substitution of the other, compare the tallies first: if their ordered lists differ, no key joins them (tallies-travel, turned about). Handed a wish, a cipher whose tallies say nothing, change the cipher, not the count of keys: the tallies must stop travelling with the letters.

Who said it first

The tally attack was first written down by the Arab philosopher al-Kindi, in Baghdad in the ninth century, in a treatise on deciphering secret messages. In 1586 the letters of Mary, Queen of Scots, written in a substitution cipher with extra symbols for common words, were broken by Thomas Phelippes; they helped send her to her death in 1587. Edgar Allan Poe's story “The Gold-Bug” (1843) walks a reader through the attack. The novel without an E is Ernest Vincent Wright's Gadsby (1939). The demand that a cipher stay safe when everything but the key is known was set out by Auguste Kerckhoffs in “La cryptographie militaire” (1883).

Al-Kindi advised the reader to find a long plain text in the same language, count each letter in it, and then match the most frequent letter of the cipher text to the most frequent letter of the plain text, and so on down.

al-Kindi, Risāla fī istikhrāj al-muʿammā (ninth century); paraphrase

Accepted names: a substitution cipher on one alphabet is a simple substitution or monoalphabetic cipher; a shift is a Caesar cipher; the set of keys is the key space; trying every key is a brute-force attack; the tally attack is frequency analysis. A cipher that gives a letter several symbols is a homophonic cipher. The demand in 10·crypt.4.4 is Kerckhoffs's principle.
