Where a Curve Crosses Itself

The curve y² = x²(x + 1) crosses itself at the origin; y² = x³ comes to a point there. At such places the curve has no single tangent line. They are found by algebra: the equation and both its slopes vanish. A cubic that crosses itself can be traced by fractions of one letter, by lines through the crossing; a cubic with no crossing, like y² = x³ − 2 of 43l, cannot. Smooth curves of degree d have a number that says how far they are from a line, and for cubics it is 1. This step finds crossings, traces a crossing cubic, and counts how far a smooth curve is from a line.

A point of f = 0 is singular when f_x = f_y = 0 there too. y² = x²(x + 1): at (0, 0) the lowest terms y² − x² give two tangents y = ±x. Lines y = tx meet it once more, at x = t² − 1, y = t(t² − 1), so it is traced by t. A smooth curve of degree d has (d − 1)(d − 2)/2 holes: 0 for lines and conics, 1 for cubics.

45k1Finding the Crossings

f_x = -3x^2 - 2x,\ \ f_y = 2y @@ the slopes (28a)
f = f_x = f_y = 0 \Rightarrow y = 0,\ x = 0 @@ x = −2/3 is not on the curve
\text{lowest terms at } (0, 0):\ y^2 - x^2 = (y - x)(y + x) @@ two tangent lines
\htmlClass{new}{y^2 = x^3:\ \text{lowest terms } y^2,\ \text{one tangent counted twice: a sharp point}} @@ a crossing and a cusp

Crossing technique: a point of f = 0 where f_x and f_y also vanish has no single tangent; the lowest terms of f there factor into its tangent lines. Elsewhere the curve is smooth, with one tangent (28f).

45k2Tracing a Crossing Cubic

t^2x^2 = x^2(x + 1) @@ meet the cubic
x^2 \text{ twice at the crossing, so } x = t^2 - 1 @@ by the count of 45j, one more point
(x, y) = (t^2 - 1,\ t(t^2 - 1)) @@ every point but the crossing, once
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the crossing cubic is traced by fractions of one letter}} @@ as the circle was (43k1)

Line-tracing technique: a cubic with a crossing is traced by the lines through the crossing, so its points are fractions of one letter; the same lines through a fraction point traced the circle.

45k3How Far From a Line

\text{as a real surface: a sphere with } g \text{ handles} @@ the classification (34f)
g = \tfrac{(d - 1)(d - 2)}{2} @@ held in trust
d = 1, 2:\ g = 0;\ \ d = 3:\ g = 1,\ \text{a ring surface} @@ lines and conics are spheres
\htmlClass{new}{g \ge 1 \Rightarrow \text{not traced by fractions of one letter}} @@ y² = x³ − 2 is not, though its points form a group (43l)

A smooth curve of degree d over the turning numbers is a surface with (d − 1)(d − 2)/2 handles; curves with no handle are traced by one letter, and the smooth cubic, with one, is not. Each crossing lowers the count by one, which is why the crossing cubic can be traced.

Accepted names

A crossing or sharp point is a singular point, a crossing a node, a sharp point a cusp; traced by one letter is rational; the number of handles is the genus, and (d − 1)(d − 2)/2 is the degree-genus formula. A smooth cubic is an elliptic curve.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton classified the cubics in 1704; Bernhard Riemann counted handles in 1857; Alfred Clebsch and Max Noether gave the degree formula in the 1860s and 1870s.
