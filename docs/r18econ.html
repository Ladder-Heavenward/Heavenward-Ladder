18·econ · economics

How much do buyers flinch?

How much do buyers flinch? Every seller who raises a price asks it, and so does every government that taxes a good. If buyers barely flinch, a higher price brings in more money. If they flinch hard, it brings in less. What makes the question hard is that the slope of a demand curve does not answer it. The slope changes when the price is counted in cents instead of dollars, or the goods in dozens instead of ones, and the buyers have not changed at all. What does not change is the ratio of the two percent changes. This page shows that this ratio is the slope of demand on 17·then's log–log plot. Then it finds which price rises raise revenue, reads the ratio straight off a drawn demand line, and finds the one shape of demand that keeps revenue fixed whatever the price: a hyperbola.

Stretch is the percent change in the amount bought for each percent change in price, the slope of the logarithm of one against the logarithm of the other. A price rise brings in more money where buyers stretch less than one for one, and less where they stretch more. On a straight demand line, the stretch at a point is the piece of the line below it over the piece above. Money taken in is the same at every price exactly when the demand curve is a hyperbola.

A flinch with no units

Let Q(P) be how many of a good buyers take in a week at price P. A rise from P to P + ΔP is a rise of ΔP/P, as a fraction of the price. The amount bought changes by ΔQ/Q, as a fraction of the amount. The ratio of the two fractions has no units: change dollars to cents, or ones to dozens, and both fractions stay the same. Let ΔP shrink to 0.

From here on, for a demand Q with a slope and Q(P) > 0, the elasticity at P is ε(P) = P Q′(P)/Q(P), the limit of (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔP/P). Its accepted name is the price elasticity of demand. It is negative, since demand falls as the price rises: ε = −2 means that a 1% rise in price loses about 2% of sales.

Elasticity-is-a-slope technique: elasticity is the slope of demand drawn on a log–log plot. Let Q be positive with a slope on an interval of positive prices. Put u = ln P and w = ln Q(e^u). Then dw/du = ε(P).

w(u) = \ln Q(e^u) @@ the demand curve, in log–log coordinates; P = e^u
\tfrac{dw}{du} = \tfrac{1}{Q(e^u)} \cdot Q'(e^u) \cdot e^u @@ chain for slopes (13e) twice: slope-of-log (17b), slope-of-exp (17f)
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac{P\,Q'(P)}{Q(P)} = \varepsilon(P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^u = P

So a demand curve whose elasticity is the same at every price is a straight line on the log–log plot. By the log-log technique (17·then), it is Q = A P^ε. Its accepted name is constant-elasticity demand.

Artichokes at Castroville

Castroville, in Monterey County, calls itself the artichoke centre of the world. Take a farm stand there that sells 600 artichokes a week at $2 each, and tries $3 for a few weeks and sells 330. A model, with round numbers. Fit a constant-elasticity demand through the two measurements, as 17h fitted an exponential.

\varepsilon = \tfrac{\ln 330 - \ln 600}{\ln 3 - \ln 2} = \tfrac{\ln 0.55}{\ln 1.5} \approx -1.47 @@ the slope of the line through two points on the log–log plot (log-log, 17·then)
Q(P) = 600\,\big(\tfrac{P}{2}\big)^{-1.47} @@ through (2, 600) with that slope
\text{revenue}: 2 \cdot 600 = 1200 \text{ against } 3 \cdot 330 = 990 @@ the rise lost money

When a rise brings in more

Revenue-sign technique: a price rise brings in more money exactly where buyers flinch less than one for one. Let Q be positive with a slope, and R(P) = P Q(P). Then R′(P) = Q(P)(1 + ε(P)). So R rises with P where ε > −1, falls where ε < −1, and has slope 0 where ε = −1.

R'(P) = Q(P) + P\,Q'(P) @@ product for slopes (13c)
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{= Q(P)\Big(1 + \tfrac{P\,Q'(P)}{Q(P)}\Big) = Q(P)\,(1 + \varepsilon(P))}\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ Q(P) > 0; the sign of R′ is the sign of 1 + ε

Accepted names: demand is inelastic where |ε| < 1, elastic where |ε| > 1, and of unit elasticity where ε = −1. At Castroville, ε ≈ −1.47, so the rise to $3 was bound to lose money.

Measured elasticities follow a rough regularity. Goods that people can neither do without nor easily swap flinch little. Studies of cigarettes in many countries put ε near −0.4. Short-run studies of gasoline in the United States, from 2001 to 2006, put it between about −0.03 and −0.08. Goods that can be put off or swapped flinch more: estimates for holiday air travel often run past −1. In April 2017 California raised its tax on cigarettes by $2 a pack, to $2.87. At ε near −0.4, revenue-sign says the state would take in more, even as smokers bought less.

Reading elasticity off a drawn line

Economists draw demand with Q across and P up. Let a straight demand line meet the P axis at A = (0, h) and the Q axis at B = (c, 0). The price at A is so high that nobody buys; the amount at B is what is taken free.

Pieces-of-the-line technique: on a straight demand line, the elasticity at a point is the lower piece of the line over the upper piece. For X strictly between A and B, |ε| = d(X, B)/d(X, A).

X = A + t(B - A) = (tc,\ (1 - t)h),\ \ 0 \lt t \lt 1 @@ part-of-the-way (18a)
Q(P) = c\big(1 - \tfrac{P}{h}\big),\ \ Q'(P) = -\tfrac{c}{h} @@ the line through A and B, with Q written as a function of P
\varepsilon = \tfrac{(1 - t)h \cdot (-c/h)}{tc} = -\tfrac{1 - t}{t} @@ the meaning of ε, at P = (1 − t)a and Q = tc
d(X, A) = t \cdot d(A, B),\ \ d(X, B) = (1 - t) \cdot d(A, B) @@ part-of-the-way
\htmlClass{new}{|\varepsilon| = \tfrac{d(X, B)}{d(X, A)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ratio of the two pieces

So on one straight line every elasticity appears. Near B buyers barely flinch; near A they flinch without bound. At the midpoint |ε| = 1, and by revenue-sign the revenue PQ, the area of the rectangle under X, is largest there. The slope of the line is the same everywhere; the flinch is not.

The demand that keeps revenue fixed

Constant-revenue technique: revenue is the same at every price exactly when the flinch is one for one at every price. For Q positive with a slope on an interval of positive prices, PQ(P) = k for every P there exactly when ε(P) = −1 for every P there. The curve PQ = k is part of a hyperbola.

R'(P) = Q(P)(1 + \varepsilon(P)) = 0 \text{ everywhere} \iff \varepsilon = -1 \text{ everywhere} @@ revenue-sign; Q > 0
R \text{ constant} \iff R' = 0 \text{ everywhere} @@ zero-slope-is-constant (14d), and a constant has slope 0
\text{let } c = \sqrt{2k},\ F_1 = (c, c),\ F_2 = (-c, -c),\ X = (x, \tfrac kx),\ x \gt 0 @@ a point of PQ = k, with Q across and P up
d(X, F_1)^2 = x^2 + \tfrac{k^2}{x^2} - 2c\big(x + \tfrac kx\big) + 2c^2 = \big(x + \tfrac kx - c\big)^2 @@ square-of-a-difference (5i); x² + k²/x² = (x + k/x)² − 2k, and c² = 2k
d(X, F_1) = x + \tfrac kx - c,\ \ d(X, F_2) = x + \tfrac kx + c @@ x + k/x ≥ 2√k > c, since (√x − √(k/x))² ≥ 0; the same steps with −c
\htmlClass{new}{d(X, F_2) - d(X, F_1) = 2c = 2\sqrt{2k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hyperbola technique's condition (18e), with 2a = 2√(2k)

Its foci sit on the line P = Q, and the two axes are its asymptotes. Every rectangle under the curve, from the origin to a point of it, has the same area k. That is the picture of revenue that a price cannot move.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

A constant elasticity is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity with a range. The Castroville fit came from two prices, $2 and $3. Between them, and a little beyond, it may serve. Far outside, it fails, and in known directions. As the price falls toward 0, A P^ε with ε < 0 grows without bound, and no town eats endless artichokes. As the price climbs, real demand reaches 0 at some price, while A P^ε never does.

State the rule, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

So an elasticity is quoted with the prices it was measured at, and the time it had to act. Over a year, buyers find ways to flinch that they lacked over a week.

Q = 600(P/2)^(−1.47) artichokes a week. How many does it predict at 1 cent each? At $100? Would you change the law, or its range?

Who said it first

Marshall wrote that the elasticity of demand in a market is great or small as the amount demanded rises much or little for a given fall in price, and showed how to read it off a drawn demand curve by the two pieces into which a point divides the line that touches it.

Alfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890), book III, chapter IV; paraphrase

In 18·econ.4.5, the economist of 1895 is Marshall, in the third edition of his Principles, crediting Robert Giffen; a good bought more as its price rises is a Giffen good. The bread was later told as the potatoes of the Irish famine, where historians found little to support it. The study of 2008 is Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller's, of rice in Hunan.

The cigarette figure is the summary of the International Agency for Research on Cancer's handbook on tobacco taxes (2011); the gasoline figures are Jonathan Hughes, Christopher Knittel and Daniel Sperling's (2008). The 2017 tax was Proposition 56.

Accepted names: price elasticity of demand; elastic, inelastic, unit elastic; constant-elasticity (or isoelastic) demand; the rectangular hyperbola PQ = k; the arc elasticity, or midpoint formula, for two measured points.
