46f · proof

A₅ Is Simple

The even shuffles of five things make a group of sixty. This step proves that it cannot be cut: its only normal part-groups are the one that does nothing and the whole. The proof is a count. A normal part-group is made of whole families of shuffles that are the same up to relabelling, so it is a sum of family sizes, one of them the family of the identity. The families of the even shuffles of five have sizes one, fifteen, twenty, twelve and twelve, and no sum of them that includes the one divides sixty except one and sixty. So the group is simple, and it is not turn-about. That is the first piece on the ladder that is not a clock, and it sits inside the group of every equation of degree five whose group is all the shuffles.

Conjugating a shuffle relabels its loops, so conjugates have the same loop shape. A normal part-group is a union of conjugate families. In the even shuffles of five the families have sizes one, fifteen, twenty, twelve and twelve, and the only sums with the one that divide sixty are one and sixty. So that group is simple and not turn-about, and the shuffles of five or more things are not solvable.

46f1Conjugating Relabels

Two members x and y of G are conjugate in G when y = gxg⁻¹ for some g in G. Being conjugate is an equivalence relation (5·cb2’s kind), so G is cut into conjugate families. A normal part-group holds, with each member, its whole family.

Relabel technique: in Sₙ, g (a₁ a₂ ⋯ aₖ) g⁻¹ = (g(a₁) g(a₂) ⋯ g(aₖ)); so conjugate shuffles have the same loop shape, and any two of the same shape are conjugate in Sₙ.

g(a_1) \xrightarrow{g^{-1}} a_1 \xrightarrow{(a_1 \cdots a_k)} a_2 \xrightarrow{g} g(a_2) @@ follow g(a₁) through g⁻¹, the loop, then g
b \text{ not any } g(a_i):\ \ g^{-1}(b) \text{ is not any } a_i, \text{ so } b \mapsto b @@ the loop leaves g⁻¹(b) alone
\text{a shuffle is a product of loops on separate members; conjugate each} @@ g x y g⁻¹ = (g x g⁻¹)(g y g⁻¹)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same shape} \Rightarrow \text{conjugate: send each } a_i \text{ to the matching } b_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ that g is a shuffle, and gxg⁻¹ = y by the first lines

46f2The Families of A₅

A₅ holds the even shuffles of five: e; the double swaps like (1 2)(3 4); the 3-loops; the 5-loops. Count them: 1, then 5 · 3 = 15 double swaps, 10 · 2 = 20 three-loops, 4! = 24 five-loops: 60 in all. By 42·cb19 the family of x in a group H has |H|/|C(x)| members, where C(x) is the part-group of members that commute with x.

Families-of-A₅ technique: the conjugate families of A₅ have sizes 1, 15, 20, 12 and 12.

x = (1\ 2\ 3):\ \ C_{S_5}(x) = \{e, x, x^2\} \times \{e, (4\ 5)\},\ \ 6 \text{ members} @@ family in S₅ of 20, so 120/20 = 6; these six commute with x
C_{A_5}(x) = \{e, x, x^2\},\ \ \text{family in } A_5:\ 60/3 = 20 @@ (4 5) and x(4 5) are odd; all 20 three-loops, one family
x = (1\ 2)(3\ 4):\ \ |C_{S_5}(x)| = 120/15 = 8,\ \ |C_{A_5}(x)| = 4,\ \ \text{family } 15 @@ the odd (1 2) commutes with x, so half of C is odd
x = (1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5):\ \ |C_{S_5}(x)| = 120/24 = 5 = |\{e, x, \ldots, x^4\}|,\ \ \text{all even} @@ no odd member commutes with x

46f4S₅ Does Not Come Apart

Not-solvable technique: A₅ is not solvable, and Sₙ is not solvable for any n ≥ 5.

\text{A}_5 \text{ has one chain of pieces: } \{e\} \lhd A_5 @@ simple-sixty: nothing between
\text{its piece } A_5 \text{ is not a clock of prime size} @@ it is not turn-about
A_5 \text{ is not solvable} @@ the pieces technique (46e3) with the same-pieces technique
S_n,\ n \ge 5,\ \text{holds the even shuffles of } 1, \ldots, 5 \text{ as a part-group matching } A_5 @@ shuffles that leave 6, …, n alone
\htmlClass{new}{S_n \text{ is not solvable for } n \ge 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ the solvable-parts technique (46e2): a part of a solvable group is solvable

So S₂, S₃, S₄ come apart into clocks of prime size, and S₅ does not. The line between four and five, which 46a met as the end of the formulas, has turned up in the groups.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Conjugate families are conjugacy classes; C(x) is the centralizer of x, and the count |H|/|C(x)| is the orbit–stabilizer theorem for the action by conjugation. A union of classes adding to the order of the group is the class equation. The simple-sixty technique is the simplicity of A₅, and its extension to every n ≥ 5 is the simplicity of the alternating groups.

Who said it first

Évariste Galois stated in 1832, in his last letter, that the smallest group that cannot be cut has 60 members. Camille Jordan, in his Traité des substitutions (1870), proved that Aₙ is simple for n ≥ 5. Paolo Ruffini (1799) and Niels Henrik Abel (1824) had already argued, without the word group, that shuffles of five letters do not come apart as those of four do.

A proof that is not allowed

“A normal part-group of A₅ has size dividing 60, so it has size 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30 or 60. A₅ has part-groups of size 12, the turns of a tetrahedron, so one of them is normal.” Not allowed: a part-group need not be normal. Normal means a union of whole families, and the count of the simple-sixty technique shows that no union of size 12 includes e.
