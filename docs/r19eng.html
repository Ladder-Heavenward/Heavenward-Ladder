What Limits an Engine’s Work per Turn?

What limits an engine’s work per turn? A car engine burns fuel in a cylinder, and the hot gas pushes a piston. The same piston must then push the spent gas out and squeeze fresh air in, and that costs work too. Early engine builders drew the pressure in the cylinder against its volume on a moving card, and found that the work per turn was the area of a loop. What makes it hard is that the gas heats as it is squeezed and cools as it expands, so its pressure follows no straight line. This page shows, with 15’s areas and 17g’s real powers, why the work per turn is the area of a loop, and why the share of the fuel’s heat that becomes work is capped by how hard the engine squeezes.

Work done by a gas is the area under its pressure against its volume. Squeezed too fast for heat to leave, air keeps its pressure times a fixed power of its volume the same. Over a full loop the work is the area enclosed, and for the four strokes of a car engine the share of heat turned to work grows with how many times the air is squeezed.

19eng1Work as an Area

A gas at pressure P pushes on a piston of area A with force P · A. Move the piston a short way dx, and the work is P · A · dx = P · dV, where dV is the change in volume. Add the short ways up, and the work is an area. The work a gas does is the area under its pressure, drawn against its volume.

P \text{ fixed}:\ \ W = P\,(V_2 - V_1) @@ the area of a rectangle
P = \tfrac{nRT}{V},\ T \text{ fixed}:\ \ W = nRT \ln \tfrac{V_2}{V_1} @@ the measured gas law; ln as an area (17a), as on 19·bio
\htmlClass{new}{W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV} \quad \blacksquare @@ positive when the gas expands; negative when it is squeezed

19eng2Squeezed Too Fast to Cool

Squeeze air fast, and its heat has no time to leave; it gets hot. A measured law says that then P · V^γ stays fixed, with γ ≈ 1.4 for air. Since PV = nRT, the temperature changes too. Write r = V₁/V₂ for how many times the air is squeezed. Its accepted name is the compression ratio. Here γ (little gamma) means a number fixed by the gas.

Fast-squeeze technique: air squeezed r times without losing heat ends r^0.4 times hotter, and r^1.4 times the pressure.

P_2 V_2^{\gamma} = P_1 V_1^{\gamma} \Rightarrow \tfrac{P_2}{P_1} = r^{\gamma} @@ the measured law; real powers (17g)
T = \tfrac{PV}{nR} \Rightarrow \tfrac{T_2}{T_1} = r^{\gamma} \cdot \tfrac{1}{r} = r^{\gamma - 1} @@ the gas law at both ends
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{T_2}{T_1} = r^{0.4},\ \ \tfrac{P_2}{P_1} = r^{1.4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with γ = 1.4
  
r = 18,\ \ T_1 = 300 \text{ K} @@ a diesel squeezes its air about 18 times
18^{0.4} = e^{0.4 \ln 18} \approx e^{1.156} \approx 3.18 @@ real powers (17g)
\htmlClass{new}{T_2 \approx 950 \text{ K}} @@ hot enough to light the fuel sprayed in, with no spark

19eng3A Loop’s Area Is the Work per Turn

An engine returns to where it started every turn, as a move that comes back (19b). Its gas goes out along a high curve, pushing hard, and comes back along a low one, pushed gently. Cycle-area technique: the work in one turn is the area inside the loop.

W_{\text{out}} = \int_{V_2}^{V_1} P_{\text{high}}\,dV,\ \ W_{\text{back}} = -\int_{V_2}^{V_1} P_{\text{low}}\,dV @@ work done by the gas, then on it
W = \int_{V_2}^{V_1} (P_{\text{high}} - P_{\text{low}})\,dV @@ the two areas, subtracted
\htmlClass{new}{W = \text{the area inside the loop}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the area between two curves (15)

A loop that goes out and comes back along the same curve has no inside, so it does no work. A loop at one fixed temperature does exactly that: out and back on the curve PV = nRT. So an engine can turn heat into work only by being hotter in some part of its turn than in another.

19eng4The Four Strokes, and Their Limit

A car engine’s loop has four legs. Squeeze the air fast, from V₁ to V₂ = V₁/r. Burn the fuel at the top, so the pressure jumps with the volume fixed. Let the hot gas expand fast, back to V₁. Let the spent heat out, at V₁. A measured law says that heat taken in or let out at a fixed volume is proportional to the change in temperature.

Squeeze-ratio technique: the share of the fuel’s heat that the four strokes turn into work is 1 − r^(−0.4), whatever the fuel.

T_2 = T_1\,r^{0.4},\ \ T_3 = T_4\,r^{0.4} @@ fast-squeeze, for the squeeze and for the expansion run backwards
T_3 - T_2 = r^{0.4}\,(T_4 - T_1) @@ subtract; share out (5i)
\eta = \frac{\text{heat in} - \text{heat out}}{\text{heat in}} = 1 - \frac{T_4 - T_1}{T_3 - T_2} @@ the work is what is not let out
\htmlClass{new}{\eta = 1 - r^{-0.4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the temperatures cancel

With r = 10, η = 1 − 10^(−0.4) ≈ 0.60. A real car does about half as well, since its gas leaks heat, and none of its legs is as clean as the page’s. The cap rises with r but never reaches 1. A petrol engine cannot squeeze much past r = 10 or 12, since the fuel would light before the spark. A diesel, which wants it to, runs near 18. Here η (little eta) means the share of the fuel's heat that the engine turns into work.

The accepted names

The fast squeeze is adiabatic, the fixed-temperature one isothermal; γ is the heat capacity ratio. The drawing of pressure against volume is a PV diagram, or an indicator diagram when drawn by the engine itself. The four strokes are the Otto cycle (intake and exhaust left out), and η is the thermal efficiency. The cap on any engine running between a hot and a cold temperature is the Carnot efficiency, 1 − T_cold/T_hot. A fuel that lights too early knocks.

Who said it first

James Watt and John Southern built the indicator (about 1796), a pencil moved by the cylinder’s pressure on a card moved by the piston, and kept it a trade secret. Sadi Carnot (1824) found the cap on engines while holding that heat is a fluid, caloric. Émile Clapeyron (1834) drew Carnot’s argument as a loop. In 19·eng.6.5, the rival view was James Joule’s (1845): heat and work trade at a fixed rate. Rudolf Clausius (1850) and William Thomson kept Carnot’s cap and dropped the fluid. Nikolaus Otto built the four-stroke engine in 1876; Rudolf Diesel’s engine of 1893 lit its fuel by squeezing alone.
