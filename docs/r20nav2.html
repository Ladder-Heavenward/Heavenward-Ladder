How Far Can a Plane Fly on a Tank?

How far can a plane fly on a tank? An airliner starts a long flight with a third of its weight in fuel. As the fuel burns the plane gets lighter, needs less lift, makes less drag, and burns fuel more slowly. So the last hour costs less fuel than the first. This page finds that each kilometre burns a fixed share of the weight, like 17·phys2’s rocket; finds the range as a log of the weight ratio with 17’s logs; finds the speed of least drag with 14h; and weighs fuel against passengers and cargo.

In level flight lift equals weight and thrust equals drag, so thrust is weight over the lift-to-drag ratio L/D. Fuel burns in proportion to thrust, so each kilometre burns the same share of the current weight. Adding up gives range = (V·(L/D)/(g·c))·ln(W₀/W₁): a log of start weight over end weight.

20nav21The Plane Gets Lighter as Fuel Burns

Terry flies a long-range jet. In steady level flight four forces balance: lift up equals weight down, and engine thrust forward equals drag back. A wing makes lift with some drag; the ratio L/D, lift over drag, is about 18 for a modern airliner.

From here on, on this page, W is the plane’s weight at any moment, V its speed, L/D its lift-to-drag ratio, and c the fuel its engines burn per second for each newton of thrust (its thirst).

\text{lift} = W,\ \ \text{thrust} = \text{drag} @@ steady level flight
\text{drag} = \dfrac{\text{lift}}{L/D} = \dfrac{W}{L/D} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{thrust} = \dfrac{W}{L/D}} @@ a lighter plane needs less thrust

20nav22Each Kilometre Burns a Fixed Share of the Weight

Fuel burned per second is c times thrust, as a mass; its weight is g times that. In one second the plane travels V metres. So over a short distance dx, the weight falls by:

dW = -\,g\,c \cdot \dfrac{W}{L/D} \cdot \dfrac{dx}{V} @@ fuel weight burned over dx
\dfrac{dW}{W} = -\dfrac{g\,c}{V\,(L/D)}\,dx @@ a fixed share of W per metre
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the share per kilometre is the same all flight}} @@ as for 17·phys2’s rocket, per unit of speed

Heavy at the start, the plane burns many kilograms a kilometre; light near the end, few. The share of the current weight is what stays fixed.

20nav23The Range: a Log of the Weight Ratio

Add up the shares from the start weight W₀ to the end weight W₁. The sum of dW/W is ln W₁ − ln W₀ (17b: the slope of ln is 1/x).

\int_{W_0}^{W_1} \dfrac{dW}{W} = -\dfrac{g\,c}{V\,(L/D)} \cdot R @@ over the whole range R
\htmlClass{new}{R = \dfrac{V\,(L/D)}{g\,c}\,\ln\dfrac{W_0}{W_1}} @@ the range-log technique
V = 250 \text{ m/s},\ L/D = 18,\ c = 1.6 \times 10^{-5} \text{ kg/(N·s)}:\ \dfrac{V(L/D)}{g c} \approx 28\,700 \text{ km} @@ a modern airliner, roughly
W_0 / W_1 = 1.4:\ R \approx 28\,700 \cdot \ln 1.4 \approx 9\,700 \text{ km} @@ about the length of a long-haul flight

Range-log technique: a plane’s range is its speed times its lift-to-drag ratio over g times its engines’ thirst, times the log of its start weight over its end weight. The three factors are three trades: aerodynamics (L/D), engines (c), and structure and fuel (the weight ratio). An airline planner sets each flight’s fuel by this count, with reserves added.

20nav24Best Glide at the Speed of Least Drag

L/D is not fixed: it depends on speed. Drag has two parts. Friction drag grows as the square of the speed. The drag of making lift falls as speed grows, because a fast wing needs less tilt for the same lift, about as 1/V².

D(V) = a\,V^2 + \dfrac{b}{V^2} @@ friction plus the cost of lift
D\prime(V) = 2aV - \dfrac{2b}{V^3} = 0 @@ 14h
V^4 = \dfrac{b}{a}:\ \ a V^2 = \dfrac{b}{V^2} @@ the two parts equal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{least drag, so best } L/D, \text{ where friction equals the cost of lift}} @@

A plane glides furthest at the speed where its friction drag equals the drag of making lift. A glider pilot whose engine is a thermal flies that speed to stretch a glide. A jet’s range has V in front as well, so its best cruise is somewhat faster; and as the plane lightens, the best speed falls, which is why long flights climb as they burn fuel, keeping the wing near its best L/D in thinner air.

20nav25The Trade Between Fuel and Payload

A plane has a heaviest weight it may take off at. Fuel and payload (passengers and cargo) share what the empty plane leaves. More fuel means less payload, and the log makes extra fuel buy less and less range.

start weightfuelend weightln(W₀/W₁)range (km)
250 t50 t200 t0.223about 6,400
250 t75 t175 t0.357about 10,200
250 t100 t150 t0.511about 14,700

Each extra 25 tonnes of fuel adds less range than the last, because it must also be carried. Each also takes 25 tonnes of payload. An airline planning a route reads this table to find how far it can fly a full plane, and how many seats it must leave empty to fly further. That is why the longest flights carry fewer passengers.

Accepted names

The range-log technique is the Breguet range equation; c is the thrust specific fuel consumption, TSFC, and V(L/D)/(gc) is the range factor. The heaviest take-off weight is the maximum takeoff weight, MTOW, and a chart of payload against range is a payload-range diagram.

Who said it first

The equation is named after Louis Charles Breguet, the French aircraft builder, who used it in the early twentieth century; it is taught in every first course on aircraft performance.
