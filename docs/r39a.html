39a · proof

True and False as 1 and 0

Rung one wrote the two ways a statement can come out as two marks, and built a table for each joining word: feed in the marks, read off the mark of the whole. Rung three then brought in two names, the first two counting numbers. This step lets the two meet. The mark for true is written as the number one and the mark for false as the number zero, and nothing else about the tables changes. What changes is what can be done with them. A table that was a list of rows becomes arithmetic. Both is a product. Either is the greater of the two. Not is what is left when the number is taken from one. Exactly one of the two is the remainder when their sum is shared out in twos. And the circle from the trigonometry rung reads the same two numbers as shares: a full share when two directions agree, no share at all when they stand at right angles, and the right angle had long since been given the same mark as the totally false. From here on a wire can carry a statement, because a wire can carry a number.

True is one and false is zero. Both is a product, either is the greater, not is one take away, and exactly one is the remainder of the sum by two. One is the full share of the circle at no turn, zero the share at a quarter turn.

39a11 for True, 0 for False

1e1 wrote T and F under a statement, and 1e2 made each joining word a machine on those marks. The marks were only marks. From here on, in a table, T is written 1 and F is written 0, and the 1 and 0 are the numbers of 4b. Everything 1e built stays true with the new spelling. The difference is that a column of 1s and 0s can now be added and multiplied, by 4g, and a joining word becomes a function from pairs of numbers in {0, 1} to {0, 1}.

And-as-product technique: for x and y in {0, 1}, the mark of “both” is the product xy.

x = 1,\ y = 1:\ \ xy = 1 @@ 1e2: both true, the and is true; one times one
x = 1,\ y = 0:\ \ xy = 0 @@ 1e2: the and is false; times zero (4g)
x = 0,\ y = 1:\ \ xy = 0 @@ the same, zero times
x = 0,\ y = 0:\ \ xy = 0 @@ the same
\htmlClass{new}{x \land y = xy \text{ on every row}} \quad \blacksquare @@ four rows, four agreements: same column, same statement (1e14)

A number times itself in {0, 1} is itself: 0 · 0 = 0 and 1 · 1 = 1. So x² = x, which is 1f’s “saying it again adds nothing”, P ∧ P = P, in arithmetic. Off {0, 1} it fails: 2 · 2 = 4. The arithmetic of this step lives on the two numbers only.

The circle gives the same two numbers a second reading. 21c1 found cos 0 = 1 and cos(π/2) = 0: the vector at no turn has the full share of itself, and the vector a quarter turn on has no share at all. 27i2 showed why the right angle and the totally false share the mark ⊥: the denial of a direction is the right angle. So read true as the full share, 1, and the totally false as no share, 0. The circle’s numbers in between are the shares a statement cannot have in rung 7’s world; 39b puts them back on a wire, where a voltage can stand anywhere between.

39a2Or as the Greater, Not as One Take Away

“At least one” is true when the larger of the two marks is 1. “Not” turns 1 into 0 and 0 into 1, which is what taking the mark from 1 does.

Or-as-greater technique: for x and y in {0, 1}, the mark of “at least one” is the greater of x and y, which is also x + y − xy; and the mark of “not x” is 1 − x.

\max(1, 1) = 1 = 1 + 1 - 1 @@ both true: the or is true, and the overlap xy is taken off once
\max(1, 0) = 1 = 1 + 0 - 0,\ \ \max(0, 1) = 1 = 0 + 1 - 0 @@ exactly one true
\max(0, 0) = 0 = 0 + 0 - 0 @@ neither: the or is false (1e2)
1 - 1 = 0,\ \ 1 - 0 = 1 @@ the not column of 1e2
\htmlClass{new}{x \lor y = \max(x, y) = x + y - xy,\ \ \neg x = 1 - x} \quad \blacksquare @@ every row agrees

The sum x + y counts how many are true. It falls short of a mark only on the row where both are, by the overlap xy. The swap-the-denial rewrites of 1f become a line of algebra: 1 − xy = (1 − x) + (1 − y) − (1 − x)(1 − y), since the right side expands to 1 − xy. And 1 − (1 − x) = x is the double turn of 1e’s settled table, with no licence spent: on {0, 1} every row is settled.

39a3Exactly One, as a Remainder

1c built ⊕, “exactly one of the two”. Its column is 0, 1, 1, 0. The sum x + y is 0, 1, 1, 2. Share the sum out in twos, by 4h, and keep what is left over: 0, 1, 1, 0.

Remainder-by-two technique: for x and y in {0, 1}, the mark of “exactly one” is the remainder of x + y on division by 2, which is also x + y − 2xy.

x + y = 2 \cdot (xy) + (x + y - 2xy) @@ the swap axiom, both sides x + y
x + y - 2xy \in \{0, 1\} @@ rows: 0, 1, 1, and 2 − 2 = 0
\text{the remainder of } x + y \text{ by } 2 \text{ is } x + y - 2xy @@ 4h: one split by a base, a whole number of twos and less than 2 left
x + y - 2xy = 0, 1, 1, 0 \text{ on the rows } 00, 01, 10, 11 @@ worked row by row
\htmlClass{new}{x \oplus y = x + y - 2xy = (x - y)^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1c’s column; (x − y)² = x² − 2xy + y² = x + y − 2xy by x² = x

The first line is an adder in disguise: the sum of two marks is twice their and, plus their exactly-one. The twos place is xy and the ones place is x ⊕ y. 39i builds that in wires. The last form says ⊕ is the square of the gap between the marks, 1 when they differ and 0 when they agree: exactly-when, turned about, as 1 − (x − y)².

The if of 1d is now 1 − x + xy: it is 0 only on the row x = 1, y = 0, the promise the result denies. Every joining word of rung 1 is a short polynomial in x and y.

The accepted names

Reading true as 1 and false as 0, with and as a product, is Boolean algebra over the two-element field or ring; x² = x is idempotence, and a ring in which every member meets it is a Boolean ring. x ⊕ y as the remainder of x + y by 2 is addition in the field with two members, and “exactly one” is exclusive or (XOR). The three readings of and on [0, 1] in 39a.5.2 are the product, minimum and Łukasiewicz t-norms of fuzzy logic.

Who said it first

George Boole wrote logic as algebra on 0 and 1 in The Mathematical Analysis of Logic (1847) and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854), and took x² = x as its fundamental law. Gottlob Frege (1884) and Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead (1910–13) ran the building the other way, arithmetic from logic. Marshall Stone (1936) showed every Boolean algebra is an algebra of sets. Jan Łukasiewicz built logics with more than two values from 1920.

A proof that is not allowed

“x ∨ y = x + y − xy. Put x = y = ½: the or is ¾, so ‘half true or half true’ is three-quarters true.” Not allowed: the technique was proved row by row on {0, 1}, and ½ is on no row. A logic with more values has to be laid down, with its own tables, before any line can be written in it.
