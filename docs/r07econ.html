7·econ · economics

Should I trust the other prisoner?

Should I trust the other prisoner? Two people are arrested for a crime they did together, and questioned in separate rooms. Each is offered the same deal: tell on the other and go free. If both keep quiet, the police can prove only a lesser charge. People have asked this question about price wars, arms races and treaties, because the same shape keeps turning up. What makes it hard is that you must choose without knowing what the other will do. You cannot see into the other room. Rung 7 says that does not matter: the other prisoner tells or does not, and you can reason in each case. This page runs that reasoning, finds a choice that is better whatever the other does, and then finds the trap in it: when both make the better choice, both end up worse off.

Write the outcomes in a table, one row for each of your choices and one column for each of the other's. Split on the other's choice: if one row is better in every column, it is better whatever happens. Then check the pair both players pick: if both would gain by switching together, but neither by switching alone, the pair is a trap, and only a promise with teeth gets them out.

The table

Call the two prisoners you and her. Each has two choices: stay silent, or tell. Here are the years in prison for each pair of choices. Fewer years is better.

she stays silentshe tells
you stay silentyou 1, her 1you 10, her 0
you tellyou 0, her 10you 5, her 5

From here on, on this page, a pair of choices is written (yours, hers), so (tell, silent) means you tell and she stays silent. Y(pair) is your years and H(pair) is hers.

The table is the whole game. Each of you knows it, and neither knows what the other will choose. The numbers are a story, but every dilemma of this kind has the same order among them: going free beats a short term, which beats a middle term, which beats a long one.

Better whatever she does

She tells or she does not. You do not know which, and nothing you can do will settle it. Before rung 7, a proof could split into cases only on a settled statement. With the either-way principle, it can split on any statement, by 7d's proof by cases. So split on “she tells”.

\text{she tells} \lor \lnot(\text{she tells}) @@ the either-way principle
\text{case she tells}:\ Y(\text{tell, tell}) = 5 \lt 10 = Y(\text{silent, tell}) @@ telling is better
\text{case she stays silent}:\ Y(\text{tell, silent}) = 0 \lt 1 = Y(\text{silent, silent}) @@ telling is better
\htmlClass{new}{\text{telling gives you fewer years}} @@ reasoning by cases: the same conclusion in both

Better-either-way technique: if one choice does better than another in every case of what the other player does, it does better, whatever she does. Let A and B be your choices. Suppose A beats B when she tells, and A beats B when she does not. Then A beats B, by either-way and reasoning by cases. No knowledge of her mind is used.

She has the same table, turned about. The same column shows her that telling is better whatever you do. So both of you tell, and both serve 5 years. Had both kept silent, both would serve 1.

The trap

Call a pair stuck when neither player can do better by changing their own choice alone. Stuck-pair technique: (tell, tell) is the only stuck pair, and both players do worse there than at (silent, silent). The first part checks each pair. The second is a comparison of numbers.

(\text{tell}, \text{tell}):\ 5 \lt 10 \text{ for you},\ 5 \lt 10 \text{ for her} @@ a switch alone takes either from 5 years to 10: stuck
(\text{silent}, \text{silent}) \text{ stuck}?\ \text{suppose so} @@ for contradiction (7e)
Y(\text{tell}, \text{silent}) = 0 \lt 1 = Y(\text{silent}, \text{silent}) @@ you would gain by telling: a collision. Not stuck
(\text{silent}, \text{tell}):\ \text{you go from } 10 \text{ to } 5 \text{ by telling} @@ not stuck; (tell, silent) the same, for her
\htmlClass{new}{Y = H = 5 \gt 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ at the stuck pair both serve more than at (silent, silent)

So the pair where reasoning lands is not the pair both would choose if they could choose together. Two players who each make the choice that is better whatever the other does can both end worse off. Nothing went wrong in the reasoning. The trap is in the table.

Price wars and arms races

Two gas stations face each other across a crossroads, and each sets its price every morning. As a model, say each makes $400 a day if both keep the price up. If one cuts and the other does not, the cutter takes the trade and makes $600, and the other $100. If both cut, each makes $200. Here more is better.

\text{case the other keeps the price}:\ 600 \gt 400 @@ cutting does better
\text{case the other cuts}:\ 200 \gt 100 @@ cutting does better
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{(cut, cut)}:\ 200 \text{ each, where } 400 \text{ each was }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{possible}}\end{aligned} @@ better-either-way, then stuck-pair

An arms race has the same table, with safety in place of money. Each side is safer with more weapons whatever the other does, and both end poorer and no safer. In 1986 the world held about 70 000 nuclear warheads, nearly all American or Soviet. Treaties with counting and inspection brought the number down to about 12 000 by 2025.

Why promises need enforcing

Could the two prisoners promise each other silence before they are caught? A promise alone changes nothing in the table. In the room, telling is still better whatever she does, so the promise is broken. The way out is to change the numbers. Say a gang punishes a teller with c more years' worth of harm. Then telling costs c more in every row where you tell.

\text{she stays silent}:\ 1 \lt 0 + c \iff c \gt 1 @@ silence beats telling
\text{she tells}:\ 10 \lt 5 + c \iff c \gt 5 @@ same change to both sides
\htmlClass{new}{c \gt 5}:\ \text{silence is better whatever she does} @@ both cases at once, so the larger bound

A promise is kept only when breaking it costs enough to change the table. Contracts, courts, fines and inspections are the costs. So is the future. If the same two players meet every day, a teller today can be punished tomorrow. In tournaments where computer programs played the game round after round, a simple rule did best: stay silent at first, then copy what the other did last time.

Illegal moves

\begin{aligned}&\text{I do not know her choice,}\\ &\quad\text{so I cannot split on it}\,?\end{aligned} @@ either-way: she tells or she does not; reason in each case
\begin{aligned}&\text{telling is better either}\\ &\quad\text{way, so (tell, tell) is best}\,?\end{aligned} @@ best for each alone; both do better at (silent, silent)
\begin{aligned}&\text{(silent, silent) is better}\\ &\quad\text{for both, so it is stuck}\,?\end{aligned} @@ each gains by switching alone: not stuck
\text{we promised, so silence is safe}\,? @@ a promise with no cost leaves the table as it was
\begin{aligned}&\text{fewer years in one case, so better either way}\,\\ &\quad ?\end{aligned} @@ it must be better in every case

Who said it first

Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher set the game up at the RAND Corporation in 1950 and had two colleagues play it a hundred times; they often kept quiet together. Albert Tucker told it as a story of two prisoners the same year, to an audience of psychologists at Stanford. John Nash's stuck pairs are from his thesis of 1950. Robert Axelrod ran the computer tournaments around 1980, and Anatol Rapoport's rule, copy the last move, won them. The twin of 7·econ.4.4 is Douglas Hofstadter's, from his columns of 1983.

Accepted names

The game is the prisoner's dilemma. A choice better whatever the other does is a dominant strategy. A stuck pair is a Nash equilibrium. A pair no other pair beats for both players is Pareto efficient; (tell, tell) is not. Staying silent is called cooperating and telling defecting. Copying the last move is tit for tat. The deer hunt of 7·econ.3.1 is the stag hunt, and the one-lane road is the game of chicken. Hofstadter's answer is called superrationality.

Wrong moves, worked

\text{if she stays silent, telling is better} @@ fine: 0 years against 1
\text{she will probably stay silent} @@ a guess, not a case
\text{so telling is better whatever she does} @@ not allowed: one case is not every case; the case she tells must be checked too
