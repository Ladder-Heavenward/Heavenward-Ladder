15b · proof

Every under-estimate is below every over-estimate

15a's lower-below-upper compared the lower and upper sums of one partition. Boxes-trap-the-area said that if the region has an area, it sits between them. But two people may cut the same interval differently, and nothing yet says that one person's under-estimate cannot be bigger than the other's over-estimate. If it could, the two estimates would contradict each other, and boxes-trap-the-area would prove that the region has no area at all. This step shows that it cannot happen. The proof never mentions area. It is a technique about sums, and it is built from one small observation: cutting a piece in two can only raise the least height and lower the greatest.

A smaller set has a greatest lower bound at least as large and a least upper bound at least as small. So adding a point raises the lower sum and lowers the upper. Put the points of two partitions together. The lower sum of one is below the lower sum of the pair. That is below its upper sum, which is below the upper sum of the other.

15b1A smaller set, a tighter bound

Smaller-set-tighter-bound technique: if S is a non-empty part of T, and T is bounded, then inf T ≤ inf S ≤ sup S ≤ sup T.

t \ge \inf T \text{ for every } t \in T @@ inf T is a lower bound of T (8c)
s \ge \inf T \text{ for every } s \in S @@ every member of S is a member of T
\inf T \le \inf S @@ inf S is the greatest lower bound of S, and inf T is one (greatest-lower-bound)
\inf S \le s_0 \le \sup S @@ S is not empty: take any s₀ in it
\htmlClass{new}{\sup S \le \sup T} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same three lines, turned about (no-holes)

15b2One more point

From here on, a partition Q of [u, v] is a refinement of a partition P of [u, v] when every point of P is a point of Q. The common refinement of P and Q is the partition whose points are those of P and those of Q together, listed in increasing order. Its accepted name is a refinement; the common refinement is also called the union of the two partitions.

One-more-point technique: if f is bounded on [u, v] and P* is P with one more point y, then L(f, P) ≤ L(f, P*) and U(f, P*) ≤ U(f, P).

x_{k-1} \lt y \lt x_k @@ y lies inside one piece of P, the k-th
c_1 = \inf f \text{ on } [x_{k-1}, y],\ \ c_2 = \inf f \text{ on } [y, x_k] @@ the two new pieces
m_k \le c_1,\ \ m_k \le c_2 @@ smaller-set-tighter-bound: each new piece is part of the old one
c_1(y - x_{k-1}) + c_2(x_k - y) \ge m_k(y - x_{k-1}) + m_k(x_k - y) @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: each width is positive
= m_k \Delta x_k @@ the two widths add to the old one (spread for times over plus)
\htmlClass{new}{L(f, P^*) \ge L(f, P)} @@ every other term of the sum is unchanged
U(f, P^*) \le U(f, P) \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines with sup, and smaller-set-tighter-bound's other half

Refining-tightens technique: if Q is a refinement of P, then L(f, P) ≤ L(f, Q) ≤ U(f, Q) ≤ U(f, P). Q has finitely many points that P lacks; add them one at a time, using one-more-point at each step, by induction on how many there are. The middle inequality is lower-below-upper.

15b3Any two partitions

Every-lower-below-every-upper technique: if f is bounded on [u, v], and P and Q are any two partitions of [u, v], then L(f, P) ≤ U(f, Q).

R = \text{the common refinement of } P \text{ and } Q @@ a refinement of P and a refinement of Q
L(f, P) \le L(f, R) @@ refining-tightens, R refining P
L(f, R) \le U(f, R) @@ lower-below-upper (15a)
U(f, R) \le U(f, Q) @@ refining-tightens, R refining Q
\htmlClass{new}{L(f, P) \le U(f, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three lines chained

Nothing in the proof used area, or f ≥ 0, or continuity. It may be used for every bounded function, including 10d's q, where every lower sum is 0 and every upper sum is 1.

An ancient mathematician wrote to a friend that some results first became clear to him by a mechanical method, weighing figures against each other. They then had to be proved by geometry, since finding a thing by that method is not proving it. But it is easier to prove a thing once you have some notion of it.

15b5More points are not always better

Refining-tightens is about refinements. Two partitions with more and fewer points, where neither refines the other, need not compare. Let f be 0 except near ⅓, where it rises to a narrow peak: f(x) = 1 − 20|x − ⅓| for |x − ⅓| ≤ 1/20, and 0 elsewhere. With two equal pieces, only the first piece holds the peak, so U = ½. With three equal pieces, ⅓ is a point, and both pieces beside it hold the peak, so U = ⅔. Three pieces gave a worse over-estimate than two. Thirds do not refine halves.

Who said it first

Archimedes wrote to Eratosthenes that some results first became clear to him by a mechanical method, weighing figures against each other, and that they then had to be proved by geometry, since finding a thing by that method is not proving it; but it is easier to prove a thing once you have some notion of it.

Archimedes, The Method, letter to Eratosthenes (3rd century BCE); paraphrase

The mathematician of the closer is Archimedes, and the friend is Eratosthenes; the letter is The Method, recovered in 1906.
