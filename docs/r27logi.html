When Is It Worth Fixing a Machine That Still Works?

When is it worth fixing a machine that still works? A worn press still earns something, and a repair costs a week’s work. Fixing now looks like a loss; running it into the ground loses more later. Each week’s choice changes what next week can bring, by chance. This page writes a rule for every week as a system of equations of rung 27 and solves it for what the rule is worth, improves the rule by looking one week ahead until nothing changes, and shows with rung 24’s shrinking maps that working back week by week always arrives at the best.

Name the states, the choices in each, the week’s earnings and the chances of where each choice leads. A rule picks one choice per state. Its worth V obeys V = r + γPV, a system of equations: solve it. Improve the rule by picking, in each state, the choice best for one week plus γ times the worth after; stop when nothing changes. Or repeat V ↦ max(r + γPV) from 0: the map shrinks distances by γ, so it settles at the best worth.

27logi1States, Choices and Chances

Jean runs a press. Each Monday it is good or worn. A good press earns 10 that week, in hundreds of dollars, and stays good next week with chance 0.8, else wears. A worn press can be run, earning 4 and staying worn, or repaired, costing 6 that week and good next week.

statechoiceearnsnext week
goodrun10good 0.8, worn 0.2
wornrun4worn
wornrepair−6good

From here on, on this page, a rule picks one choice for each state, and its worth V(state) is the sum of the earnings from that state on, each week counted γ = 0.9 times the week before, as in 23·econ, averaged over the chances.

27logi2A Rule’s Worth Is a System of Equations

This week’s earnings, plus γ times the worth of wherever the press is next week: that is the worth. Under the rule “never repair”:

V_W = 4 + 0.9\,V_W @@ worn stays worn
V_W = 4/0.1 = 40 @@ take 0.9V_W from both sides
V_G = 10 + 0.9\,(0.8\,V_G + 0.2\,V_W) @@ average over next week
0.28\,V_G = 10 + 7.2 @@ elimination (27l)
\htmlClass{new}{V_G \approx 61.4,\ V_W = 40} @@ the worth of never repairing

Worth technique: a rule’s worth solves V = r + γPV, where r lists the week’s earnings and P the chances, a system of equations with one unknown per state. With γ < 1 it has exactly one solution.

27logi3The Rule That Repairs

Now repair whenever the press is worn.

V_W = -6 + 0.9\,V_G @@ repair, good next week
V_G = 10 + 0.72\,V_G + 0.18\,V_W @@ as before
V_G = 10 + 0.72\,V_G + 0.18\,(-6 + 0.9\,V_G) @@ substitute (27l)
0.118\,V_G = 8.92 @@ gather V_G
\htmlClass{new}{V_G \approx 75.6,\ V_W \approx 62.0} @@ more in both states

The repair costs a week and pays for itself: a worn press is worth 62 under this rule against 40. The loss this week is outweighed by the good weeks it buys back.

27logi4Improve by Looking One Week Ahead

Is there a better rule still? In each state, compare every choice by one week’s earnings plus γ times the worth, under the current rule, of where it leads.

\text{worn, run: } 4 + 0.9 \cdot 62.0 \approx 59.8 @@ worth under the repair rule
\text{worn, repair: } -6 + 0.9 \cdot 75.6 \approx 62.0 @@ the rule’s own choice
\text{good: only one choice} @@ nothing to compare
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no state changes its choice: the rule is best}} @@ the improve check passes

Improve technique: switch each state to the choice best for one week plus γ times the current worth after; the new rule is worth at least as much everywhere, and when no state switches, no rule is worth more. Starting from “never repair”, the worn state switches at once: repair gives −6 + 0.9 · 61.4 ≈ 49.3, against 4 + 0.9 · 40 = 40 for running.

27logi5Working Back Always Arrives

Another way, with no rule to start: begin with V = 0, and each round set every state’s worth to its best choice’s one week plus γ times last round’s worth.

(V_G, V_W):\ (10, 4),\ (17.9, 7.6),\ (24.3, 10.8),\ (29.4, 15.8), \ldots @@ round 4: worn switches to repair
|TV - TU| \le 0.9\,|V - U| @@ a best choice of averages moves by at most γ times
\text{a shrinking map has one fixed point (24c)} @@ the shrinking-map technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the rounds settle at } (75.6,\ 62.0)} @@ the best worth, whatever the start

Rounds technique: repeating best-choice rounds is a map that shrinks distances by γ, so the rounds settle at the one best worth, and the choices that reach it make the best rule. Each round is one more week of looking ahead; after k rounds the error is at most γk times the first.

27logi7Uptime From Failures and Repairs

A machine runs, fails, is repaired, runs again: a cycle of two states (27logi1). In the long run the share of time it is up is the average run divided by the average whole cycle. A repair rule that cuts the repair time raises it, and so does one that lengthens the runs; this one number lets them be compared.

\text{uptime} = \tfrac{190}{190 + 10} = 95\% @@ 
\text{a fleet of 20: about 19 up at a time} @@ 
\text{repairs cut to 5 hours: } \tfrac{190}{195} \approx 97.4\% @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{or runs raised to 390: } \tfrac{390}{400} = 97.5\%} @@ two routes to the same uptime

Uptime technique: uptime = mean time between failures ÷ (mean time between failures + mean time to repair); compare repair rules by the uptime and cost each gives. Shown in a depot’s log: a fleet of 20 trucks, with runs averaging 190 hours and repairs 10, has on average 19 on the road over a year, the computed 95%.

Accepted names

States, choices, earnings and chances make a Markov decision process. A rule is a policy, its worth the value function, and V = r + γPV the Bellman equation; γ is the discount factor. Improving by looking one week ahead is policy iteration, and best-choice rounds are value iteration. The same ideas, with the chances learned from trying, are reinforcement learning.

Who said it first

Richard Bellman set out the equation and working back in Dynamic Programming, 1957. Ronald Howard gave policy iteration in Dynamic Programming and Markov Processes, 1960.
