42e · proof

A clock hand that reaches every hour

On the clock of seven, the powers of three reach every hour but twelve o’clock before they come back. The powers of two reach only three of them. On the clock of twelve no hour does it: every unit there, done twice, is one. So some clocks have a hand that reaches every unit, and some do not. This step proves that every prime clock has one. The proof does not find the hand. It counts. A unit that comes back after d steps is a root of a polynomial of degree d, and on a prime clock such a polynomial has at most d roots, as on the line. That caps how many units can come back after each number of steps. A second count, of fractions sorted by their lowest terms, shows that the caps are all reached, and the cap for the largest number of steps is not zero. Finding the hand is then a matter of testing a few candidates.

A unit of order m, raised to the power k, has order m over gcd(m, k). On a prime clock a polynomial of degree d has at most d roots. The counts of units of the clocks that divide n add to n. Together these force a unit of order p − 1 on every prime clock, and give the number of them.

42e1A hand that reaches every unit

A full hand of the clock of n is a unit whose powers are every unit, so a unit whose order is the count of units. [3] is one on the clock of 7, since its powers run 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, and [3] is one on the clock of 10 (42b3). [2] is not one on the clock of 7: 2, 4, 1. The clock of 12 has none, since every unit there squared is [1]. Its accepted name, used from here on, is a primitive root, from the Latin primitivus, first of its kind: every unit is a power of it.

Power-order technique: if [a] has order m, then [a]ᵏ has order m/gcd(m, k).

g = \gcd(m, k),\ \ m = gm',\ k = gk',\ \ \gcd(m', k') = 1 @@ 5h: dividing out the gcd leaves numbers sharing nothing
([a]^k)^t = [1] \iff m \mid kt @@ [a]ʲ = [1] exactly when m ∣ j (division with remainder: the remainder is below the order)
\iff m' \mid k't \iff m' \mid t @@ cancel g; factor-that-shares-nothing (5h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the order of } [a]^k \text{ is } m' = m/\gcd(m, k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the least such t is m′

So if [g] is a full hand of a clock with c units, [g]ᵏ is a full hand exactly when gcd(k, c) = 1. On the clock of 7, [3]ᵏ for k = 1 and 5 are full hands: [3] and [5]. That is two, the count of units of the clock of 6.

42e2Few roots on a prime clock

9g proved that a polynomial of degree n has at most n zeros. Its proof used two things only: if f(r) = 0 then f(x) = (x − r)q(x) with q of degree one less, by long division; and a product is 0 only when a factor is. Long division needs undoes for the leading coefficient. The second needs no two nonzero numbers to multiply to 0. A prime clock has both, since it is a field.

Few-roots technique: on a prime clock, a polynomial of degree d whose leading coefficient is not [0] has at most d roots. The lines are 9g’s, read on the clock of p. The prime-clock-field technique supplies each undo and the no-zero-product line.

x^2 - 1 \text{ on the clock of } 8:\ \ [1], [3], [5], [7] @@ 1, 9, 25, 49 are all 1 more than a multiple of 8
(x - 1)(x + 1) = [0] \text{ at } x = [3]:\ \ [2][4] = [8] = [0] @@ neither factor is [0]: 42a4’s failed cancelling
x^2 - 1 \text{ on the clock of } 7:\ \ [1], [6] \text{ only} @@ check 2² to 5²: 4, 2, 2, 4
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a degree-2 polynomial with 4 roots needs a clock that is no field}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the few-roots technique, contrapositive

On the clock of 8 every unit squared is [1], so no unit reaches order 4, the count of units: the clock of 8 has no full hand, and the four roots are why.

42e3The counts of units add up

Write c(d) for the count of units of the clock of d, so c(1) = 1, c(12) = 4. The letter c is a name for this footing and the next.

Divisor-sum technique: for every n ≥ 1, the counts of units c(d) of the clocks whose sizes d divide n add up to n.

\tfrac1n, \tfrac2n, \ldots, \tfrac nn: \ n \text{ fractions} @@ one for each k from 1 to n
\tfrac kn = \tfrac jd,\ \ \gcd(j, d) = 1,\ \ d \mid n @@ lowest terms, exactly one (5h’s one-lowest-terms technique)
\tfrac jd \text{ with } d \mid n,\ 1 \le j \le d,\ \gcd(j, d) = 1 \text{ is } \tfrac{jn/d}{n} @@ and each such j/d comes once
\text{the fractions with bottom } d \text{ number } c(d) @@ the units of the clock of d
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{d \mid n} c(d) = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the n fractions, sorted by their bottoms

For n = 12: c(1) + c(2) + c(3) + c(4) + c(6) + c(12) = 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 12. The fractions 1/12, 2/12, …, 12/12 in lowest terms have bottoms 12, 6, 4, 3, 12, 2, 12, 3, 4, 6, 12, 1.

42e4Every prime clock has a full hand

Full-hand technique: every prime clock has a full hand, and the clock of p has exactly c(p − 1) of them.

N(d) = \text{the number of units of order } d,\ \ d \mid p - 1 @@ every order divides p − 1 (42d1)
N(d) \ge 1:\ [a] \text{ of order } d \Rightarrow [a]^0, \ldots, [a]^{d-1} \text{ are } d \text{ roots of } x^d - 1 @@ different, since d is the least return
\text{they are all its roots, so every unit of order } d \text{ is some } [a]^k @@ the few-roots technique: at most d
\text{of those, order } d \text{ exactly when } \gcd(k, d) = 1:\ \ N(d) = c(d) @@ the power-order technique
\textstyle\sum_{d \mid p-1} N(d) = p - 1 = \sum_{d \mid p-1} c(d),\ \ N(d) \le c(d) @@ every unit has one order; the divisor-sum technique; N(d) is 0 or c(d)
\htmlClass{new}{N(p - 1) = c(p - 1) \ge 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ no N(d) can fall short, since none is above its c(d)

The proof shows a full hand exists and does not point at one. To find one, test candidates. [a] is a full hand of the clock of p exactly when [a]^((p − 1)/q) ≠ [1] for every prime q dividing p − 1, since an order below p − 1 divides some (p − 1)/q. On the clock of 13, 12 = 2² · 3: 2⁶ = 64 ≡ 12 and 2⁴ = 16 ≡ 3, so [2] is a full hand. The full hands are [2]ᵏ with gcd(k, 12) = 1: [2], [6], [11], [7]. That is c(12) = 4.

Which other clocks have full hands is stated, not proved, here: the clocks of 1, 2 and 4, of an odd prime power pᵏ, and of twice one, and no others. [2] is a full hand of the clock of 9. The clock of 8 has none (42e2).

The accepted names

A full hand is a primitive root modulo n, and the full-hand technique says the multiplicative group (ℤ/pℤ)ˣ is cyclic, with φ(p − 1) generators. The power-order technique is the order of a power, ord(aᵏ) = ord(a)/gcd(ord(a), k). The few-roots technique is Lagrange’s theorem on polynomial congruences. The divisor-sum technique is Gauss’s identity Σ_{d∣n} φ(d) = n. The list of moduli with primitive roots is Gauss’s. The share of 42e.6.4 is Artin’s conjecture on primitive roots; its guessed value, Artin’s constant, is about 0.3740.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler named primitive roots and guessed they exist for every prime in 1773; his proof had a gap. Adrien-Marie Legendre gave a proof in 1785, and Carl Friedrich Gauss gave two in the Disquisitiones (1801), with the divisor sum and the full list of moduli. Emil Artin made his guess in 1927. Christopher Hooley proved it in 1967 on the strength of the unproved hypothesis of 42·now2, and Roger Heath-Brown showed in 1986 that at most two primes fail to be primitive roots for endlessly many primes, without saying which.

A proof that is not allowed

“The clock of p has p − 1 units and the full-hand technique shows one of order p − 1. So the clock of n has a unit of order c(n) for every n, by the same proof.” Not allowed: the proof spends the few-roots technique, which needs a field. On the clock of 8 the polynomial x² − 1 has four roots, and the count N(2) is 3, above c(2) = 1.
