Every and Some by Hand

2b gave “every” and “some” their marks and their techs. This step handles them at speed: writing English into them and back, choosing the right joining word inside each, negating them by pushing a not through, and spotting the statement that looks right and says nothing. Alongside the marks runs the oldest piece of logic there is: four kinds of sentence about every and some, and a diagram of how they oppose one another. With them come the syllogisms, the old arguments built from those four kinds: chains of the techs you already own, with a famous form that the techs refuse.

Every goes with if; some goes with and. A not turns each into the other as it passes. When the subject is empty, every is cheap and some is impossible, and the old diagram cracks along exactly the links that assumed otherwise.

2j1Every and Some Over a List

When the things in question can be listed, an every is an and across the list and a some is an or across it. Example: the people in the room are Zoe, Randy and Jean, and P(x) is “x is asleep”. Then:

Reading an every as an and across a list is a way of seeing, not one of 1f’s techniques, and over a room it is foreclosed. The marks ∀ and ∃ on their own range over every thing whatever, not over a room. To swap the every for the and, you would need the room as a collection with exactly these members and nobody else. A collection is a set, and “exactly these and nobody else” is 2d's listing. So the swap holds only over a listed set, as 2d4 showed; over every thing whatever it never holds. Footing 2j2 writes the restriction for statements that range over every thing.

But the seeing explains everything that follows: whatever 1c and 1f did with ∧ and ∨, this step does with ∀ and ∃. Use an every is keep-a-part, applied to the part you want. Example: with Zoe, Randy and Jean, using the every to get “Zoe is asleep” is keep-a-part. Show an example is add-an-option: showing Zoe asleep gives “Zoe or Randy or Jean is asleep”. Use a some is reason by cases over the list. And pushing a not through is not-through.

2j3The Some-With-If Trap

∃x (A(x) ⇒ B(x)) is made true by anything at all that is not an A, as 1e’s if machine shows. Here is the some-with-⇒ spelling, read in a world where it ought to fail.

Take a world with three things: Serena the grey cat t, a shoe s and Finn the black dog r. Let D(x) say “x is a dog” and B(x) say “x is brown”. “Some dog is brown” is not so here: the only dog is black.

Now read it. The shoe is not a dog: that is given. So D(s) ⇒ B(s) has a false condition, and 1e’s if machine keeps an if whose condition fails: “if the shoe is a dog, the shoe is brown” conforms to true. Nothing has been learned about the shoe’s colour.

2j4An Every With Nothing to Check

The same cheapness of ⇒ makes “every living mammoth is purple” true. There is no living mammoth to check, so the if is never broken. The ladder calls such an every an empty truth (the accepted word is vacuously true). An empty truth is not a defect. It is what the if machine does with an empty hypothesis, and 1e showed why it must; 2b12 showed that the way down from every to some needs a member. It matters for the old sentences, as footing 2j7 shows.

A statement about everything on an empty list is true, and so is its denial. Take a set that names nothing, say “the chimeras.” Every chimera breathes fire; no chimera breathes fire; both are so, because there is no chimera on the list to fail either. That is an empty truth, and it comes from referencing the content of a list that has no members. When an every and its denial both come out true, you are reading an empty list. The chimeras have no members and one subset, “nothing else,” and that is all that can be said of them.

2j5The Four Old Sentences

The oldest logic that has come down to us sorts sentences of the form “S is P” into four kinds. Medieval teachers named the kinds with the vowels of the Latin AffIrmo (I affirm) and nEgO (I deny):

sentencein marks
Aevery S is P∀x (S ⇒ P)
Eno S is P∀x (S ⇒ ¬P)
Isome S is P∃x (S ∧ P)
Osome S is not P∃x (S ∧ ¬P)

Example, with S for cats and P for animals or for things asleep: A, every cat is an animal; E, no cat is a dog; I, some cat is asleep; O, some cat is not asleep. Here the letters S and P are names of kinds, as A(x) and B(x) were in footing 2j2, and not the statement letters of 1c.

2j6The Diagram of Opposition

Then the old logic asks how the four kinds oppose one another, and later teachers drew the answers as a diagram. A and E sit along the top, I and O along the foot, with links between them. (The picture draws the diagram.)

  • Contradictories (the crossing links, A with O and E with I): never both so and never both not so. Each is the other's denial.
  • Contraries (A and E): never both so, though both can be not so. Example: “every swan is white” and “no swan is white” are both not so, because some swans are white and some are not.
  • Subcontraries (I and O): never both not so.
  • Subalterns (A to I, E to O): if every S is P, then some S is P.

Tap a bin to put things in it or empty it: things that are S but not P, things that are both, things that are P but not S. (Try S as swans, P as white things.)
Letters: teal so, ember not so. A link is solid teal when its relation is true in this world, dashed ember when the world breaks it. Empty S entirely and watch which links survive.

2j7When the Subject Is Empty

Now the catch. Contraries, subcontraries and subalterns quietly assume that there are some S. Empty the subject (no living mammoths) and A, “every living mammoth is purple”, is an empty truth. So is E, “no living mammoth is purple”. So two contraries are both so. I and O each need a living mammoth to point at, so both are not so. So two subcontraries are both not so, and A no longer gives I.

Only the crossing links survive, because they are not a fact about mammoths at all. They are the negation techniques of 2b, which may be used whatever the subject. Modern logic keeps the crossing links for every subject. It keeps the other links only when “and there is an S” is added as a given. In the picture of footing 2j6, empty S and watch the diagram crack along exactly those links.

2j8Pushing a Not Through an Every

The denial of “every” is “some … not”, and the denial of “some” is “every … not”, which is “no”. 2b proved these as the techniques not-some and counterexample. 2b also marked the direction that waits: from “not every” to “some … not”.

Pushing a not through a run of marks takes a move for each mark. Each mark turns into its partner, and the ⇒ inside turns to ∧ by 1f's negated-if. Example first, in words: “not every cat is asleep” becomes “some cat is not asleep”. Here is the same push in marks, on the A line.

Start from : not every S is P. Push the not through the every. The every becomes some-not: . That direction waits for rung 7. Over a subject you can list, you look.

Then push the not through the if, by 1f's negated-if. The negation of an if is an and: . By itself, that is negated-if's rung-7 direction too. Inside a some the place is not turned about, so the step still waits for rung 7, as the list marks it.

The result is O, “some S is not P”. The diagram's crossing link is nothing but this push. Two marks change: the every becomes a some, and the ⇒ turns into ∧. Every goes with ⇒, some with ∧, as promised. And the ¬ ends up on the kind P.

The other way, from O to “not A”, is 2b's counterexample technique and negated-if's licensed direction: good for any subject at all. So the crossing link is true: by techs in the licensed direction, and by looking in the other. That is why the crossing link survives the empty subject: a counterexample kills an every whether or not anything else exists.

2j9A Not Through Several Marks

With more than one mark the method is the same: a change for each mark. Example: “everyone has a mother”, ∀x ∃y M(y, x). Denied, it becomes ∃x ¬∃y M(y, x), and then ∃x ∀y ¬M(y, x): someone has nobody for a mother. The order of the marks stays put; only the kinds of mark change.

2j10Books That Balance, and the Window

Every step so far worked on paper. This step puts a careful paper argument next to the world, to see what the paper can and cannot promise.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, out the window

1a6 kept books: every change paid for by its technique, and a description whose books don't balance is wrong. That technique ran one way. The other way is worth a look. A ledger is a list of claims, each with what pays for it. Here is a weather forecast kept as a ledger. Each line is a claim the forecast makes, or is paid for by techs you have.

Every line is paid for. Nowhere in town is it raining, and wherever it rains, the ground gets wet. The books balance.

Now put your head out the window. There are clouds, and the sun is out too. That denies line 1. A light rain is falling through the sunshine. That is rain and shine together, and it denies line 4. Under the porch roof, rain falls past a dry step. That is a counterexample to line 10, in 2b's sense.

One look, and three entries fail. No step failed. Each tech did what it promises: true in, true out. The trouble was in what went in. Books can balance on paper and still be false the moment you look out the window.

A real forecast keeps 1a's books for the air: water, heat, wind. The ledger is the size of the whole world. Weather anywhere becomes weather here within days, so no town's books balance alone. The books would have to list every parcel of air on Earth, and the sky is a crowd, 2b's other size of statement. No one can write down every parcel. So the ledger is kept in chunks, each many kilometres wide. Each entry is a little off, and the error grows each day the books run forward. That is why a forecast reaches a week or so ahead and stops.

A forecast reads: “tomorrow it rains somewhere in the valley, or it rains nowhere.” Can a window prove it false? If not, what has it told you?

2j11The Best Syllogism

A syllogism draws a conclusion from given lines, each an A, E, I or O sentence. The given lines of a syllogism are called its premises. The premises are linked by a middle term, a kind that appears in both premises and vanishes from the conclusion. Example: every mammal is an animal; every dog is a mammal; so every dog is an animal. “Mammal” is the middle term.

Medieval students memorised the valid forms by names whose vowels spell the sentence kinds. The best of them is Barbara, A, A, A: every M is P, every S is M, so every S is P. Each valid form is a brief argument by 2b's techs. Barbara's argument uses nothing but a fresh case, using an every, and carrying an if through, again and again.

Rows hold every M is P, and every S is M. Let n be any S: a fresh name, with “n is S” said of it.

Every S is M, so n is M: use the every on n, then carry the if through. The middle term has appeared.

Every M is P, so n is P: use that every on n, and carry it through. The middle term has done its work and vanishes.

The case used nothing about n but that n is S, so, back at the margin, every S is P: .

2j12Three More Valid Forms

Celarent is E, A, E: no M is P, every S is M, so no S is P. Example: no fish is a mammal, every shark is a fish, so no shark is a mammal.

Darii is A, I, I: every M is P, some S is M, so some S is P. Example: every dog is an animal, some pet is a dog, so some pet is an animal.

Ferio is E, I, O: no M is P, some S is M, so some S is not P. Example: no fish has fur, some pet is a fish, so some pet has no fur.

The three run the way Barbara runs. Where a premise says “no”, the argument reaches a clash. Where a premise or the conclusion says “some”, the argument calls the example and later shows it.

2j13Two Forms That Fail

The commonest invalid form is the undistributed middle: every cat is a mammal, every dog is a mammal, so every dog is a cat. Try to run Barbara's argument. You set in the case of any dog and reach mammal, and then no premise leaves mammal. Both kinds sit inside the middle, and the middle never links them. Gomez convicts the form: both premises are true, and he is a dog that is not a cat, a counterexample in 2b's sense. In 2b19’s words: a world of one dog and one cat, both mammals, makes both premises so and the conclusion not so, and one such world is all a form needs to fail.

And a form the old logic accepted fails the modern reading: Darapti, “every M is P, every M is S, so some S is P”. If there are no M, both premises are empty truths, and the conclusion, a some, has nothing to point at. Example: every unicorn is purple, every unicorn has a horn, so something with a horn is purple. Darapti is the diagram's empty-subject problem again, and it is mended the same way: add “there is an M” as a given. A world of a single horse does it: no unicorns, so both everys are empty truths, and nothing in it is purple.

2j14Finding the Missing Premise

Run a syllogism backwards. Given a conclusion and a premise, find the other: the missing premise must supply the link the middle term makes. Wanting “no S is P” from “every S is M”, you need the middle M kept apart from P: “no M is P”, and the form is Celarent. Wanting “some S is not P” from “no M is P”, you need some S to be M: Ferio. Wanting an every, the premises must both be everys (Barbara, Celarent); a some in the premises gives only a some in the conclusion. The syllogism bench in footing 2j13 lets you try forms against a small world and see which premise is doing the work.

A writer of puzzle books set chains of syllogisms to be untangled. One gives three lines: babies are illogical; nobody is despised who can manage a crocodile; illogical persons are despised. (“Illogical” means unable to reason; “despised” means looked down on.)

Who said it first

The four lines and the syllogisms are Aristotle’s; the diagram is the square of opposition, drawn by later commentators from his remarks. The puzzle writer is Lewis Carroll.

“The affirmation ‘every man is white’ is the contradictory of the denial ‘not every man is white’, or again, the proposition ‘no man is white’ is the contradictory of the proposition ‘some men are white’.”

Aristotle, On Interpretation 7 (17b), trans. E. M. Edghill (Oxford, 1928)

“If A is predicated of all B, and B of all C, A must be predicated of all C.”

Aristotle, Prior Analytics I.4 (25b), trans. A. J. Jenkinson (Oxford, 1928)

The ledger out the window: Edward Lorenz found in 1961 that his weather model, rerun from numbers rounded from six decimal places to three, drifted to entirely different weather within about two months of model time. A small error in the entries, run forward, swamps the books. It is why forecasts stop where they do.

Lewis Carroll wrote chains of syllogisms meant to be untangled. One gives three lines: babies are illogical; nobody is despised who can manage a crocodile; illogical persons are despised. Carroll asked what follows about babies and crocodiles.
