44b · proof

Symmetric in the Roots

The coefficients of a polynomial are made from its roots, and made in a way that does not care which root is called first. Shuffle the names of the roots and the coefficients stay put. This step proves the converse, which is much stronger: anything built from the roots by adding and multiplying, if it stays put under every shuffle of the roots, is already built from the coefficients. So a quantity like the sum of the squares of the roots can be found without finding a single root. The proof is a recipe that strips off the largest term again and again, and it stops for the reason the recipes of rung 4 stop. The step ends with the number that tells whether two roots meet, which turns out to be the number under the square root in the cubic formula.

The coefficients are the elementary symmetric sums of the roots, with signs. Every polynomial in the roots that every shuffle leaves alone is a polynomial in those sums. So a symmetric expression in the roots of a rational polynomial is rational, and the discriminant, the product of the squared differences, is read off the coefficients.

44b1Coefficients From Roots

By 25’s splitting, a polynomial with top coefficient 1 and degree n is (x − r₁)(x − r₂)⋯(x − rₙ) for roots r₁, …, rₙ in ℂ. Write e₁ = r₁ + ⋯ + rₙ for the sum of the roots, e₂ for the sum of the products of two different roots, and on to eₙ = r₁r₂⋯rₙ. These are the elementary symmetric sums.

Roots-to-coefficients technique: (x − r₁)⋯(x − rₙ) = xⁿ − e₁xⁿ⁻¹ + e₂xⁿ⁻² − ⋯ + (−1)ⁿeₙ.

n = 1:\ \ x - r_1 = x - e_1 @@ the start
(x - r_1)\cdots(x - r_n)(x - r_{n+1}) @@ suppose it for n roots, and multiply by one more
\text{the } x^{n+1-k} \text{ term: } (-1)^k\big(e_k + r_{n+1}e_{k-1}\big) @@ from x times the kth term, and −rₙ₊₁ times the (k − 1)th
e_k + r_{n+1}e_{k-1} = e_k \text{ of the } n + 1 \text{ roots} @@ the k-products without rₙ₊₁, then those with it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sign pattern holds for } n + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction (4c)

For the cubic y³ + py + q of 44a, e₁ = 0, e₂ = p and e₃ = −q. Each eₖ is unchanged by every shuffle of the roots: a shuffle of r₁, …, rₙ only reorders the terms of each sum.

44b2Every Symmetric Polynomial Is One in the Sums

A polynomial in letters x₁, …, xₙ is symmetric when every shuffle of the letters (19a) leaves it the same polynomial. Order its terms like a dictionary: x₁ᵃ¹⋯xₙᵃⁿ comes before x₁ᵇ¹⋯xₙᵇⁿ when, at the first place where the powers differ, a is the larger. The first term of a symmetric polynomial has a₁ ≥ a₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ aₙ, since otherwise the shuffle that swaps two letters would give a term earlier still.

Symmetric-is-coefficients technique: every symmetric polynomial is a polynomial in e₁, …, eₙ, and its coefficients stay among whole numbers when the symmetric polynomial’s do.

\text{first term } c\,x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n},\ \ a_1 \ge \cdots \ge a_n @@ the dictionary order, and symmetry
e_1^{a_1 - a_2} e_2^{a_2 - a_3} \cdots e_n^{a_n} \text{ has first term } x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_n^{a_n} @@ the first term of eₖ is x₁⋯xₖ, and first terms multiply
s' = s - c\,e_1^{a_1 - a_2} \cdots e_n^{a_n} \text{ is symmetric, with a later first term} @@ the first terms cancel; the eₖ are symmetric
\text{terms of the same total degree are finitely many} @@ each power is at most the degree
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the recipe stops, and } s \text{ is the sum of what it subtracted}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the falling-measure technique (4l): the first term moves later each time
  
s = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2:\ \ \text{first term } x_1^2,\ a = (2, 0, 0) @@ symmetric
s - e_1^2 = -2(x_1x_2 + x_1x_3 + x_2x_3) = -2e_2 @@ e₁² = s + 2e₂
\htmlClass{new}{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the next first term was x₁x₂, of shape (1, 1, 0)

44b3A Symmetric Quantity Is Rational

Symmetric-is-rational technique: if f has rational coefficients and top coefficient 1, every symmetric polynomial with rational coefficients, evaluated at the roots of f, is a rational number.

s(r_1, \ldots, r_n) = P(e_1, \ldots, e_n) @@ the symmetric-is-coefficients technique, P with rational coefficients
e_k = (-1)^k \cdot (\text{the coefficient of } x^{n-k}) @@ the roots-to-coefficients technique
\htmlClass{new}{s(r_1, \ldots, r_n) \in \mathbb Q} \quad \blacksquare @@ a polynomial with rational coefficients at rational numbers
  
x^3 - 2:\ \ e_1 = 0,\ e_2 = 0,\ e_3 = 2 @@ roots-to-coefficients
x_1^3 + x_2^3 + x_3^3 = e_1^3 - 3e_1e_2 + 3e_3 @@ the recipe: first term x₁³, then x₁²x₂, then x₁x₂x₃
\htmlClass{new}{(\sqrt[3]2)^3 + (\omega\sqrt[3]2)^3 + (\omega^2\sqrt[3]2)^3 = 6} \quad \blacksquare @@ each cube is 2

A quantity in the roots that is not symmetric need not be rational: ∛2 alone is not. The rest of the rung is built on that difference. A quantity unchanged by some shuffles and not by others lives in a field between ℚ and the roots.

44b4Whether Two Roots Meet

The product of the squared differences of the roots, Δ = ∏ (rᵢ − rⱼ)² over the pairs i < j, is symmetric: a shuffle reorders the pairs and changes only the signs of the differences, which the squares remove. It is 0 exactly when two roots meet. Its accepted name is the discriminant.

Cubic-discriminant technique: the roots of y³ + py + q have Δ = −4p³ − 27q², which is −108 times the D of 44a.

V = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ r_1 & r_2 & r_3 \\ r_1^2 & r_2^2 & r_3^2 \end{pmatrix},\ \ \det V = (r_2 - r_1)(r_3 - r_1)(r_3 - r_2) @@ the grid of powers; its determinant is 0 when two columns agree (27e), and has degree 3
\Delta = (\det V)^2 = \det(V V^{\mathsf T}) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & s_1 & s_2 \\ s_1 & s_2 & s_3 \\ s_2 & s_3 & s_4 \end{pmatrix} @@ det-product (27e); entry (i, j) of VVᵀ is the sum of the (i + j − 2)th powers, sₖ
s_1 = 0,\ s_2 = -2p,\ s_3 = -3q,\ s_4 = 2p^2 @@ symmetric-is-coefficients: e₁ = 0, e₂ = p, e₃ = −q; and rᵢ³ = −prᵢ − q
\Delta = 3(s_2s_4 - s_3^2) - s_2^3 = 3(-4p^3 - 9q^2) + 8p^3 @@ the cofactors along the first row, with s₁ = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2 = -108\,D} \quad \blacksquare @@ D = q²/4 + p³/27

So 44a’s three-real-roots technique reads: D < 0 exactly when Δ > 0. For y³ − 3y − 1, Δ = 108 − 27 = 81 = 9². That Δ is the square of a rational number will matter on 44k.

The accepted names

The roots-to-coefficients technique is Vieta’s formulas. The symmetric-is-coefficients technique is the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials; the dictionary order is the lexicographic order on monomials. The sums of powers sₖ are the power sums, and the identities that tie them to the eₖ are Newton’s identities. The grid V is the Vandermonde matrix and its determinant the Vandermonde determinant. Δ is the discriminant.

Who said it first

François Viète wrote the coefficients of low degree as sums of roots around 1591, and Albert Girard stated them for every degree in 1629. Isaac Newton gave the identities for the sums of powers in notes of the 1660s, printed in his Arithmetica Universalis (1707). Edward Waring (1770) and Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1770–71) used the symmetric-is-coefficients technique on equations, and Carl Friedrich Gauss gave a proof by the dictionary order in 1816. Alexandre-Théophile Vandermonde’s name is on the grid, from work of 1771.

A proof that is not allowed

“The sum of the cubes of the roots of x³ − 2 is (e₁)³ = 0, since cubing a sum cubes the parts.” Not allowed: (r₁ + r₂ + r₃)³ has cross terms. The recipe of the symmetric-is-coefficients technique gives e₁³ − 3e₁e₂ + 3e₃ = 6.
