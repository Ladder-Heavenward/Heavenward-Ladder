How Does a Robot Arm Know How to Reach?

How does a robot arm know how to reach? An arm on a factory floor is told where its hand must be, not how to bend. Its motors turn only its joints. Going from the joints to the hand is easy; going back from the hand to the joints is a puzzle with two answers or none. This page builds a two-joint arm with cos and sin, finds its joint angles for a target by the law of the triangle, uses rung 28’s best flat map to steer the hand in a straight line, finds the poses where the arm cannot move its hand some way at all, and turns a weight in the hand into the turning effort each motor must give.

Write the hand as a function of the joint angles with cos and sin. Go back with the triangle law: cos θ₂ = (x² + y² − a² − b²)/(2ab), with two elbows. For small moves, the slope grid J sends joint speeds to hand speeds; solve J θ′ = p′. det J = ab sin θ₂: where it is 0 the arm is stretched or folded and some hand moves are impossible. A force F at the hand needs turning efforts Jᵀ F at the joints.

28eng1From Joints to Hand

Sarah programs an arm with an upper arm of length a from the shoulder at the origin and a forearm of length b. The shoulder turns the upper arm to the angle θ₁; the elbow turns the forearm a further θ₂. Each piece is an arrow with cos and sin parts, as in 21·phys, and the arrows add.

\text{elbow} = (a\cos\theta_1,\ a\sin\theta_1) @@ the upper arm
x = a\cos\theta_1 + b\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) @@ the forearm turned by both angles
y = a\sin\theta_1 + b\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) @@ the same, with sin
\htmlClass{new}{a = b = 1,\ \theta_1 = 0,\ \theta_2 = 90°:\ (x, y) = (1, 1)} @@ elbow at (1, 0), forearm straight up

From here on, on this page, the pose is the pair of joint angles (θ₁, θ₂), and the hand is the point (x, y) the pose puts the hand at.

28eng2From Hand Back to Joints

Given a target (x, y), the shoulder, elbow and hand make a triangle with sides a, b and r = √(x² + y²). The triangle law (18f) fixes the angle at the elbow.

r^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos\theta_2 @@ the triangle law, θ₂ measured from straight
\cos\theta_2 = \dfrac{x^2 + y^2 - a^2 - b^2}{2ab} = \dfrac{2 - 2}{2} = 0 @@ solve for cos θ₂ (5g)
\theta_2 = 90° \text{ or } -90° @@ elbow one way or the other
\htmlClass{new}{(0°, 90°) \text{ or } (90°, -90°)} @@ two poses reach the same hand

Reach technique: a two-joint arm reaches a target at distance r from the shoulder in two poses when |a − b| < r < a + b, in one at either end, and in none outside. Its program must choose an elbow, and keep to it, or the arm flips through on the way.

28eng3Steering the Hand in a Straight Line

To slide the hand steadily to the right, the joints must turn at speeds that change as the pose changes. Near a pose, rung 28’s best flat map turns small joint changes into small hand changes.

J = \begin{pmatrix} -a\sin\theta_1 - b\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -b\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ a\cos\theta_1 + b\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & b\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{pmatrix} @@ the slope grid (28b)
= \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} @@ at θ₁ = 0, θ₂ = 90°
J\,\theta\prime = (0.1,\ 0) @@ the hand to the right at 0.1 a second
\htmlClass{new}{\theta_1\prime = 0,\ \theta_2\prime = -0.1} @@ only the elbow turns, clockwise

Steering technique: at each moment, solve J θ′ = p′ for the joint speeds that give the hand speed p′; as the pose changes, J changes, and the solve is done again. Arms do it hundreds of times a second.

28eng4Poses Where the Hand Is Stuck

The solve needs J undoable, which is det J ≠ 0 (27e). Expand the determinant.

\det J = (-a s_1 - b s_{12})\,b c_{12} + b s_{12}\,(a c_1 + b c_{12}) @@ s, c for sin, cos of the angles
= ab\,(s_{12} c_1 - s_1 c_{12}) @@ the b² terms cancel
= ab\,\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2 - \theta_1) @@ the sum formulas (21d)
\htmlClass{new}{\det J = ab\sin\theta_2} @@ zero when the arm is straight or folded

Stuck-pose technique: where det J = 0, the arm is stretched straight or folded back, and the hand cannot move along the arm at all, however the joints turn. Near such a pose, small hand moves ask for huge joint speeds, so arms are kept away from them.

28eng5A Weight in the Hand

The hand holds a weight that pulls with a force F. Each motor must give a turning effort τ₁, τ₂. If the joints turn by a small δθ, the hand moves by J δθ, and the work the motors put in must equal the work done against F.

\tau \cdot \delta\theta = F \cdot (J\,\delta\theta) @@ work in equals work out
= (J^{\mathsf T} F) \cdot \delta\theta @@ a dot product moves across a grid as its side-turn (27g)
\tau = J^{\mathsf T} F @@ for every small δθ
\htmlClass{new}{F = (0, -10):\ \tau = (-10,\ 0)} @@ the shoulder holds it all

Effort technique: the turning efforts that hold a force F at the hand are Jᵀ F. Here the forearm stands straight up, so the weight pulls straight through the elbow, and the elbow motor need give nothing.

Accepted names

From joints to hand is forward kinematics, and back is inverse kinematics. The pose is the configuration, and the two poses are elbow up and elbow down. The slope grid is the arm’s Jacobian, a stuck pose a singularity, and the reachable ring the workspace. τ = Jᵀ F is the principle of virtual work for the arm, and the turning efforts are torques.

Who said it first

Jacques Denavit and Richard Hartenberg gave a standard way to write any arm’s joints in 1955. Daniel Whitney steered a hand by solving with the Jacobian, resolved-motion rate control, in 1969.
