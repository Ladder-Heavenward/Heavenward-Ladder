The Age of the Calculus

The calculus is found twice, then put to work on everything from the planets to the chance of a fair game. On this page the rules multiply faster than the proofs, and the gap between them becomes the business of the next era.

26 names, in order of birth. Each line says what the person did that the ladder uses; the links under it go to the steps where the work stands, with the name kept to the cellar there.

1655–1705Jacob Bernoulli

In 1683 he asked what a sum lent at interest becomes if the interest is added at every moment, and met the number e between two and a half and three. He proved the first law of large numbers, published 1713, and challenged his brother with the hanging chain.

1667–1754Abraham de Moivre

A Huguenot in London. He found the formula for powers of a turn, that n! grows like √n (n/e)ⁿ, and in 1733 read a chance as an area under the bell curve for the counts of many coins.

The younger brother. His slope-ratio rule went to the Marquis de l'Hôpital in letters under a paid contract and was printed in 1696 under l'Hôpital's name; he taught Euler and set the brachistochrone.

In 1706 he blended two arctangents, of 1/5 and of 1/239, and reached a hundred places of π.

1682–1716Roger Cotes

Newton's editor. In 1714 he measured angles by the radius, found a form of the turning formula, and with Newton worked out the rules for sums over evenly spaced points.

1685–1731Brook Taylor

In 1715 he published the general recipe for the endless sum of a function from its slopes at one point, and in 1713 found a string's lowest tone from its tension.

1685–1753George Berkeley

Bishop of Cloyne. The Analyst of 1734 asked how a quantity could be used and then declared nothing, the ghosts of departed quantities, and the question stood until the limit answered it.

1690–1764Christian Goldbach

Euler's correspondent. His letter of 1742 made the even-sum guess that is still open, and his letters of 1729 drew from Euler the factorial between the whole numbers.

1698–1746Colin Maclaurin

His Treatise of Fluxions of 1742 answered Berkeley with proofs in the manner of Archimedes, laid sums beside areas, and made the series at zero famous.

1700–1782Daniel Bernoulli

Son of Johann. He put the vibrating string together from its tones in 1753, asked in 1778 for the guess that makes observations most probable, and gave the pressure law of flowing water.

1702–1761Thomas Bayes

A minister at Tunbridge Wells. His essay, published in 1763 after his death by Richard Price, turned a given chance around; he also noticed that Stirling's sum wanders.

1707–1783Leonhard Euler

The most prolific of them all, in Basel, Petersburg and Berlin. The Introductio of 1748 made functions the subject; e, i, π, Σ and f(x) are his marks; the bridges of Königsberg, the sum of the inverse squares, the product over primes, the polyhedron formula and the circles for classes are his. He is on more pages of this ladder than anyone.

1710–1761Thomas Simpson

Weaver, then teacher. He stated the root-finding rule in 1740, published the parabola rule for areas in 1743, and argued that astronomers should take the mean of many observations rather than trust one.

1713–1765Alexis Clairaut

Used partial derivatives in the 1740s, gave the condition for a total differential, wrote the tangent plane of a surface, and measured the shape of the earth.

1717–1783Jean d'Alembert

Of the Encyclopédie. He gave the wave equation of the vibrating string in 1747, the principle that turns motion into balance in 1743, and the ratio test, and wrote on chance with more doubt than most.

Showed π irrational in 1761 by an endless fraction for the tangent, gave the cosine law of light in Photometria, drew the equal-size map of 1772, and wrote on the fifth postulate.

The maker of engines, who settled on 33 000 foot-pounds a minute for his horse in the early 1780s and kept his indicator a trade secret.

1736–1813Joseph-Louis Lagrange

From Turin to Berlin to Paris. Four squares in 1770, the multiplier method in 1788, the mean value technique in 1797 in a book that tried to build the calculus without limits, the mechanics in one principle, and the count of cosets that bears his name.

1743–1794Antoine Lavoisier

Kept the weighing ledger. He found the calx heavier than the metal in the 1770s, named oxygen, laid down in 1789 that in every operation nothing is created, built the ice calorimeter with Laplace, and died on the guillotine.

Answered Galvani's animal electricity with his pile of metals, described in a letter of 1800 to Joseph Banks.

A surveyor in Denmark. In 1799 he drew the numbers of the plane as points and their products as turns, before Argand and Gauss.

1749–1827Pierre-Simon Laplace

The writer on chance. He stated the turned-chance rule in general in 1774, the rule of succession in 1814, and the great limit of the bell; his Mécanique céleste settled the stability of the heavens, and his intelligence that knows every position and speed is the ladder's standing question on determinism.
