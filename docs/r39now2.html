39·now2 · research front

Is checking easier than finding?

Is checking easier than finding? A sudoku takes minutes to solve and seconds to check. A proof takes years to find and a day to check. It feels obvious that finding is harder, and nobody can prove it. 39h put the question exactly: is every question whose yes answers can be checked fast also a question that can be answered fast? In 2000 it was named one of seven problems with a prize of a million dollars for a solution, and it is still open. This page says what it would take to answer, how slow the best methods for the hardest questions are now, with the numbers, what would break if checking and finding were the same, and why almost everyone who works on it bets they differ. It also says why the tools of this rung, the diagonals that settled stopping and more time, cannot settle it alone: they work the same for machines given any free question, and there are free questions that make the two classes equal and others that make them differ.

Checking equals finding exactly when making a statement true is fast. The best methods for clauses of three still take a fixed power above one to the number of letters. If finding were as fast as checking, short proofs could be found as fast as checked and every lock could be picked fast. The diagonal cannot settle it, since free questions can tilt it either way.

39now21The question, exactly

One-test technique: P equals NP exactly when the satisfiable statements are fast.

P = NP \Rightarrow \text{satisfiable }\allowbreak \text{is }\allowbreak \text{fast} @@ it is in NP, with a row as certificate (39h2)
\text{satisfiable }\allowbreak \text{fast},\ \allowbreak \ A \in NP @@ the other way
A \text{ fast-reduces }\allowbreak \text{to }\allowbreak \text{satisfiable} @@ the tableau technique (39h3)
\htmlClass{new}{A \text{ is fast}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power-stays technique (39h1): a fast reduction, then a fast decider

So the whole question is one question about one set, or about any of the thousands of NP-complete ones: three-colouring, by 39k, or the best route through a list of cities, or packing boxes. A proof that any one of them is fast, or that it is not, settles all of them. The prize is for either.

39now22How slow the best methods are

Trying every row takes 2ⁿ for n letters. For clauses of three, a better way is to branch on a clause, as 38m’s divide and conquer did, and count with a recurrence.

Clause-branch technique: a statement in clauses of three with n letters is decided in about 1.84ⁿ steps.

\text{pick }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{clause } a \lor b \lor c \text{ not }\allowbreak \text{yet }\allowbreak \text{true} @@ if none is left, the row so far works
\text{branch: } a \text{ true; }\allowbreak \text{or } a \text{ false }\allowbreak \text{and } b \text{ true; }\allowbreak \text{or } a, b \text{ false }\allowbreak \text{and } c \text{ true} @@ every true row is in exactly one branch
\text{the }\allowbreak \text{branches }\allowbreak \text{fix } 1, 2, 3 \text{ letters: } T(n) \le T(n - 1) + T(n - 2) + T(n - 3) @@ a recurrence, 38m
T(n) \le x^n \text{ when } x^3 = x^2 + x + 1 @@ induction: x^(n−1) + x^(n−2) + x^(n−3) = x^(n−3)(x² + x + 1) = xⁿ
\htmlClass{new}{x \approx 1.839} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one real root, by halving (12e)
  
2^{100} \approx 1.27 \times 10^{30}\text{: at } 10^{18} \text{ rows }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{second, }\allowbreak \text{about } 40\,000 \text{ years} @@ every row
1.84^{100} \approx 3.0 \times 10^{26} @@ branching on clauses
1.307^{100} \approx 4.2 \times 10^{11}\text{, but } 1.307^{1000} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{116} @@ the best known for clauses of three, from 2019
\htmlClass{new}{1000^3 = 10^9} \quad \blacksquare @@ a power of n, for comparison

Programs that solve satisfiability in practice handle statements with millions of letters from chip design and planning, because those statements have structure. The worst cases are another matter. Every known method takes cⁿ steps on some statements, for some c above 1. Whether c can be pushed down to 1, with a power of n in front, is the question.

39now23What would break

Suppose P equals NP, with a method that is fast in practice, not only in principle.

Prefix-undo technique: if P equals NP, then for every fast function f there is a fast machine that, given y = f(x) with x of known length, finds some x′ with f(x′) = y.

B = \{(y, p) : \text{some } x \text{ beginning }\allowbreak \text{with } p \text{ has } f(x) = y\} @@ the certificate is x; checking f(x) = y is fast
B \in NP\text{, so } B \text{ is fast} @@ P = NP
\text{ask } (y, 0)\text{: }\allowbreak \text{if }\allowbreak \text{yes, }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{first }\allowbreak \text{letter }\allowbreak \text{is } 0\text{, else } 1 @@ one letter found
\text{ask }\allowbreak \text{again }\allowbreak \text{with }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{longer }\allowbreak \text{prefix, }\allowbreak \text{letter }\allowbreak \text{by }\allowbreak \text{letter} @@ n questions in all
\htmlClass{new}{x\prime \text{ found }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{in } n \text{ fast }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{questions}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power-stays technique

So every locked box of 39l could be opened, and every secret shared over a wire, the subject of 41·comp, read. And proofs: “PA reaches φ by a proof of at most n marks” is in NP, with the proof as certificate, checked by 37b3’s recipe. The same letter-by-letter questions would find the proof. 37·now2’s search through proofs would become as fast, up to a power, as checking one, for every statement with a proof short enough to write. Mathematicians would still choose what to ask. They would not spend years finding what a machine checks in a day.

39now24Why the bet, and why it is hard

The bet that P differs from NP rests on evidence of the kind 30w weighs: thousands of NP-complete questions from every field, studied by people who wanted fast methods, and none found. In a poll of researchers in 2019, close to nine in ten said they believed P differs from NP. Evidence is not a proof, and some well-known researchers keep the other bet open.

The proof is hard for a reason that can itself be proved. 39d.5.3 gave machines a free question about a set A. Every argument of this rung that works by running machines and doing the opposite works just as well for machines with any free question: it never looks inside them. In 1975 it was shown that there is a set A for which P and NP with free questions about A are equal, and a set B for which they differ. So a proof that settles the question must fail for one of A or B, and the diagonal alone cannot do it.

  • In 1994 a second wall was found: a wide family of arguments that lower-bound circuits, if they worked here, would also break the locked boxes 39l relies on.
  • In 2008 a third: arguments that survive the first wall by using polynomials, as 39i2 and 39l4 did, meet a version of the same obstacle.
  • Since 2001 a programme has tried to separate the two classes with the symmetry of polynomials. It has found smaller results and no end yet.

Open: P against NP; whether NP equals the sets whose outsides are in NP; whether BPP equals P; and whether any NP-complete question needs more than a fixed power above one to the number of letters, the guess called the exponential time hypothesis. Each would settle part of the others.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The question is P versus NP, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems, announced in Paris in May 2000. The one-test technique is that SAT is NP-complete, read both ways. The clause-branch technique is the Monien–Speckenmeyer bound, and 1.307ⁿ is the bound of the PPSZ algorithm as sharpened by Hansen, Kaplan, Zamir and Zwick (2019). The prefix-undo technique says that P = NP implies no one-way functions exist. The free-question result is relativization, by Theodore Baker, John Gill and Robert Solovay (1975); the second wall is natural proofs, by Alexander Razborov and Steven Rudich (1994); the third is algebrization, by Scott Aaronson and Avi Wigderson (2008). The programme of 2001 is geometric complexity theory, by Ketan Mulmuley and Milind Sohoni. The exponential time hypothesis is from Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi (2001).

Who said it first

Kurt Gödel asked John von Neumann in a letter of 1956 whether proofs of length n could be found in about n or n² steps, which is the question of this page before it had a name. Stephen Cook (1971) and Leonid Levin (1973) gave it its form. William Gasarch’s polls of researchers in 2002, 2012 and 2019 found growing majorities for P ≠ NP, about 88% in the last.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every method for satisfiability must at some point consider every row, since a row not considered might be the only true one.” Not allowed: a method can rule out many rows at once, as the clause-branch technique rules out every row that leaves a chosen clause false. Whether rows can be ruled out fast enough is the question, and the line assumes the answer.
