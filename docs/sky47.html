∞47 · newsflash

Can a Vote Be Fair?

Can a vote be fair? Each voter ranks the candidates, and a rule turns the rankings into one ranking for everyone. With two candidates the majority is fair in every sense anyone has proposed. With three, the majority can go round in a circle: most voters prefer the first candidate to the second, most prefer the second to the third, and most prefer the third to the first. That was noticed in the 1780s. In 1951 the circle became a wall. Ask only three things of a rule: if every voter puts one candidate above another, so does the result; the result between two candidates depends only on how the voters rank those two; and no single voter decides everything. With three or more candidates, no rule does all three. In the 1970s a second wall followed: every rule that picks a winner from three or more, without a dictator, sometimes rewards a voter for lying. Then the question changed from whether a rule must fail to how often. Write each voter’s preference between two candidates as a plus or minus one, and a rule for a pair becomes a table on the corners of a cube; the turnings on the cube that settled a question about sensitive tables in 2019 also measure how often a rule goes round in a circle. In 2002 the chance of a circle was written as a sum over those turnings, and the wall of 1951 came out of it in three lines. In 2009 it was proved that a rule that stays a fixed distance from every dictator goes round in a circle with a chance that cannot shrink to nothing, however many voters there are. This page builds rankings and the circle, the three demands, the proof of 1951 by hand, the lying voter, the cube and its formula, the measured failure, and ends with what is still asked of the numbers.

Rankings are orders (2h). With three candidates, pair-by-pair majority can cycle: 12 of the 216 ways for three voters to rank. Unanimity, pair-alone and no dictator cannot all hold (1951): a decisive group for one pair is decisive for all, and a decisive group splits into a decisive part, down to one voter. For one table f used on each pair, P(no cycle) = ¾ − ¾ Σ_S f̂(S)² (−⅓)^|S|, so a rule with no cycle ever has all its weight on single voters and is a dictator; majority of three cycles with chance 1/18, of many about 8.8%.

sky47.1Rankings, and a Majority That Goes Round

A ranking of the candidates A, B, C is a relation in the sense of 2h, “is above”, that is transitive (2h12), never puts a candidate above itself, and for two different candidates puts one above the other. Each is a list from top to bottom, so three candidates have 3! = 6 rankings. A profile lists one ranking for each voter. The plainest rule decides each pair by majority.

Going-round technique: with three voters and three candidates, deciding each pair by majority can put A above B, B above C and C above A, which is no ranking.

\text{voter 1: } A > B > C,\quad \text{voter 2: } B > C > A,\quad \text{voter 3: } C > A > B @@ each candidate first once, second once, last once
A \text{ over } B:\ \text{voters 1, 3} @@ two of three
B \text{ over } C:\ \text{voters 1, 2} @@ two of three
C \text{ over } A:\ \text{voters 2, 3} @@ two of three
\htmlClass{new}{A > B > C > A:\ \text{not transitive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ transitivity would give A > A

How often? Three voters can rank in 6³ = 216 ways. Exactly 12 of them make the majority go round (checked by script): each of the two circles, A > B > C > A and A > C > B > A, arises when the three voters take the three rankings of that circle, in 3! = 6 orders. So with rankings drawn at random the chance is 12/216 = 1/18. With five voters it is 5/72, with seven 875/11 664, and it climbs, as sky47.5 will show, to about 8.8%.

sky47.2Three Demands

A rule takes every profile to one ranking for everyone. Three demands, each modest. Unanimity: if every voter puts x above y, so does the rule. Pair-alone: whether the rule puts x above y depends only on how each voter ranks x against y, not on where the voters put a third candidate. No dictator: there is no voter whose ranking the rule always copies.

The rules people use keep some and break others. The same nine voters: 4 rank A > B > C, 3 rank C > B > A, 2 rank B > C > A. Counting first places, A wins with 4 against C’s 3 and B’s 2. Dropping the last and counting again, B’s 2 voters go to C, and C wins 5 to 4. Giving 2 points for first and 1 for second, B wins with 11 against 8 and 8. And B beats each of the others head to head, 5 to 4 against A and 6 to 3 against C. Three rules, three winners.

Points-break technique: giving 2, 1, 0 points for first, second and third breaks the pair-alone demand.

3 \text{ voters } A > B > C,\ \ 2 \text{ voters } B > C > A @@ a profile of five
A: 3 \cdot 2 = 6,\ \ B: 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 7 @@ so B is above A
\text{the first three move } C \text{ up: } A > C > B @@ each still puts A above B; the other two unchanged
A: 6,\ \ B: 0 + 4 = 4 @@ now A is above B
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same ranks of } A \text{ against } B,\ \text{different result}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the third candidate moved the pair

A dictator rule, copy voter 1, keeps unanimity and pair-alone and has a dictator. Majority keeps unanimity, pair-alone and has no dictator, and is not a rule at all: sky47.1 found a profile it gives no ranking for. The three demands are each kept by some rule; the next footing shows no rule keeps all three.

sky47.3Decisive Groups

Fix a rule with at least three candidates that keeps unanimity and pair-alone. A group G of voters is decisive for x over y when every profile in which all of G put x above y has the rule put x above y, whatever the others do; G is decisive when it is decisive for every pair. By unanimity, the group of all voters is decisive. The proof shrinks it to one voter.

Spread-decisive technique: if G is decisive for some x over y, then G is decisive. Split-group technique: if G is decisive and is split into two groups G₁ and G₂, one of them is decisive.

G\!: x > y > z;\ \ \text{others}\!: y > z,\ x \text{ anywhere} @@ G decisive for x over y; z a third candidate
\text{rule: } x > y,\ \ y > z @@ G’s decisiveness; unanimity
\text{rule: } x > z @@ transitivity
\text{only } G \text{’s ranks of } x, z \text{ are fixed} @@ the others may put x above or below z
G \text{ decisive for } x \text{ over } z @@ pair-alone: the result for x, z is the same on every profile with these ranks of x, z
\text{the same with } G\!: z > x > y,\ \text{others } z > x \Rightarrow \text{decisive for } z \text{ over } y @@ unanimity gives z > x, then x > y
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y) \to (x, z),\ (z, y)\text{: every ordered pair is reached}} \quad \blacksquare @@ by script with three candidates: from (A, B) all six pairs
  
G_1\!: x > y > z,\ \ G_2\!: z > x > y,\ \ \text{others}\!: y > z > x @@ one profile
\text{rule: } x > y @@ all of G put x above y
\text{case } x > z:\ \text{only } G_1 \text{ put } x \text{ above } z @@ G₂ and the others put z above x
\quad G_1 \text{ decisive for } x \text{ over } z,\ \text{so decisive} @@ pair-alone, then spread-decisive
\text{case } z > x:\ \text{then } z > y;\ \text{only } G_2 \text{ put } z \text{ above } y @@ transitivity; G₁ and the others put y above z
\htmlClass{new}{G_2 \text{ decisive for } z \text{ over } y,\ \text{so decisive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rule gives a ranking, so one case holds

Lone-voter technique: with three or more candidates, every rule that keeps unanimity and pair-alone has a dictator. The group of all voters is decisive; split it in halves, keep the decisive half, and repeat. The groups shrink and stay decisive, so after finitely many splits one voter is decisive alone: whenever that voter puts x above y, so does the rule. With 8 voters, three splits.

sky47.4The Voter Who Lies

Now ask a rule only for a winner. A voter gains by lying at a profile when reporting some other ranking makes the winner one the voter truly ranks higher. On the nine voters of sky47.2, counting first places makes A win; the three voters C > B > A prefer B to A, and if they report B first, B wins with 5. Their true favourite cannot win, so they vote for the one who can. With two candidates no lie helps.

Two-candidates technique: with two candidates, under majority, no voter ever gains by lying.

\text{a voter prefers } x;\ \text{the winner is } y @@ the only case where a lie could help
\text{the voter voted } x @@ the truth
\text{a vote for } y \text{ only adds to } y @@ the only other report
\htmlClass{new}{y \text{ still wins}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a lie can only move votes away from x

With three or more candidates the wall returns, held in trust here (its proof runs like sky47.3’s): every rule that picks one winner, can make each of at least three candidates win, and has no dictator, gives some voter, at some profile, a gain by lying (1973, 1975). Lying is better whatever the others do in the sense of 7·econ only at some profiles; the question is how many.

The rules are in use. Australia has elected its lower house by ranking, dropping the last and counting again, since 1918, and Maine became the first state of the United States to use the same rule statewide, from 2018. Neither escapes the walls: each keeps unanimity, and in the nine voters above that rule picked C, though B beats both head to head.

sky47.5Votes on the Cube

Take n voters, rankings drawn independently and at random, each of the 6 equally likely. For voter i let xᵢ be 1 if A is above B and −1 if not; yᵢ the same for B above C, and zᵢ for C above A. Use one table f on the corners of the n-cube for every pair: A goes above B when f(x) = 1, B above C when f(y) = 1, C above A when f(z) = 1. The result goes round exactly when f(x) = f(y) = f(z). A single voter has xᵢ = yᵢ in 2 of the 6 rankings (A > B > C and C > B > A), so the average of xᵢyᵢ is (2 − 4)/6 = −⅓, and the same holds for y, z and for z, x.

Three-pairs technique: P(the result is a ranking) = ¾ − ¾ Σ_S f̂(S)² (−⅓)^|S|, the sum over all sets S of voters, with f̂(S) the coefficients of the cube-turnings technique.

\text{for } p, q, r = \pm 1:\ \ [\text{not all equal}] = \tfrac34 - \tfrac14(pq + qr + rp) @@ all equal: ¾ − ¾ = 0; otherwise one product is 1 and two are −1: ¾ + ¼ = 1
P(\text{ranking}) = \tfrac34 - \tfrac34\operatorname{E}[f(x) f(y)] @@ averages add (30g); the three pairs alike
f(x)f(y) = \textstyle\sum_{S, T} \hat f(S)\hat f(T)\,\chi_S(x)\chi_T(y) @@ the cube-turnings technique (∞19)
\operatorname{E}[\chi_S(x)\chi_T(y)] = \textstyle\prod_i \operatorname{E}[x_i^{[i \in S]}\, y_i^{[i \in T]}] @@ voters independent
= 0 \text{ unless } S = T,\ \ \text{and } (-\tfrac13)^{|S|} \text{ if } S = T @@ E xᵢ = E yᵢ = 0; E xᵢyᵢ = −⅓
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{ranking}) = \tfrac34 - \tfrac34\textstyle\sum_S \hat f(S)^2 (-\tfrac13)^{|S|}} \quad \blacksquare @@ collect

Majority of three, from ∞19: f̂ = ½ on each single voter and −½ on all three. The sum is 3 · ¼ · (−⅓) + ¼ · (−1/27) = −7/27, so P(ranking) = ¾ + ¾ · 7/27 = 17/18, the 1/18 of sky47.1 again. A dictator f = x₁ has the sum −⅓ and P = 1. For majority of many voters the chance of going round tends to ¼ − (3/2π) arcsin ⅓ ≈ 0.0877 (the limit held in trust; by script 0.0556, 0.0694, 0.0750, 0.0780 at 3, 5, 7, 9 voters).

Cube-dictator technique: if one table f, used on every pair, never goes round and keeps unanimity, then f is a dictator. Never going round gives Σ f̂(S)² (−⅓)^|S| = −⅓, while Σ f̂(S)² = 1 and each (−⅓)^|S| ≥ −⅓ with equality only at |S| = 1; so f = Σ cᵢxᵢ. Flipping xᵢ changes f by 2cᵢxᵢ, which must be 0 or ±2, so each cᵢ is 0 or ±1, and Σ cᵢ² = 1 leaves one. Unanimity makes it +1: f = xᵢ.

sky47.6How Often a Rule Must Fail

The 1951 wall says every rule without a dictator fails somewhere. sky47.5 says more for one table used on every pair: the chance of going round is ¼ + ¾ Σ f̂(S)² (−⅓)^|S|, and it is 0 only for a dictator. A table that is close to a dictator, say a dictator except at a few profiles, goes round rarely. So the right question is quantitative: how far from every dictator must a rule be to go round often?

Stated, not proved here (posted 2009, in print 2012): for every ε above 0 there is a δ above 0, the same for every number of voters, such that every rule on three candidates that keeps pair-alone, puts each candidate of each pair on top with chance at least ε, and differs from every dictator on a share of at least ε of the profiles, gives no ranking with chance at least δ when rankings are drawn at random. The proof starts from the formula of sky47.5, but the rules may use a different table on each pair, and it holds in trust results on how a table on the cube responds to noise in its inputs. A bound of the right shape followed in a paper titled a tight version, and the lying voter of sky47.4 got its own measured version: rules without a dictator can be lied to at a share of profiles that cannot be made tiny (2008 on).

The step from “some” to “often” goes back through ∞16. The some-outcome technique turns a positive chance into an outcome: if the chance of going round is at least δ above 0, some profile goes round, which is the 1951 wall again for rules far from dictators. The quantitative result says more: the profiles that go round are not rare, so a rule cannot be repaired by fixing a few of them.

f = x_1 \text{ except where } x_2 = \cdots = x_n = -x_1 \text{, where } f = -x_1 @@ voter 1 decides unless everyone else disagrees
n = 3:\ \ f = \text{majority} @@ the exceptions are where voter 1 is outvoted
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{no ranking}) = \tfrac1{18},\ \ \tfrac{25}{648},\ \ \tfrac{301}{23\,328} \text{ at } n = 3, 5, 7} @@ by sky47.5 and script: closer to a dictator, rarer circles

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞47.oq.1Which rule is best? Fix a measure of loss, for instance the chance that a rule fails to give a ranking, or the chance that a voter gains by lying, or how far the winner is from the one who beats every other head to head when there is one, and ask which rule, among those far from a dictator, loses least. For two candidates and one kind of noise the majority is known to be best (2005); for three or more, under most losses, the answer is not known.
Why it is hard: each loss favours different rules, and the rules people use (counting first places, dropping the last, giving points) are each worst at something.
∞47.oq.2What are the right constants in the 2009 result? For a rule at distance ε from every dictator, how small can the chance of going round be, as a function of ε, with the best constant? A tight shape is known; this page has not revisited the exact constants.
Why it is hard: the extreme rules are near dictators in subtle ways, and the formula of sky47.5 covers only one table used on every pair.
∞47.oq.3Does any of this describe real elections? The chances on this page draw rankings at random, every ranking equally likely, which real voters do not do. How often real electorates have a majority that goes round is argued from data, and the answers depend on how the data is read.
Why it is hard: a real election records only the winner or the first places, so a circle among the full rankings is rarely seen even when it is there.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The circle came first. In 1785 a French mathematician and reformer showed that pair-by-pair majorities can go round, and argued for the candidate who beats every other head to head when one exists. A rival proposal, made to the French Academy of Sciences in 1770, gave points by place. The two members of the Academy disagreed on which was right, and the disagreement is the one of sky47.2: the nine voters there pick B by points and by head-to-head, and A by first places.

In 1951 a young economist turned the argument into a wall, in a short book on social choice. The demands look too weak to fail, and the reading has been argued since: whether the wall indicts voting, or only rankings, which carry no strengths of preference, so that rules giving up pair-alone, like points by place, are the honest escape. The author shared the Nobel prize in economics in 1972, and the result became the founding statement of a field.

In 1973 and 1975 two papers, one in philosophy and one in economics, showed the lying voter: every rule that picks a winner from three or more and has no dictator can sometimes be gamed. Again the reading split. One side took it as a reason to study rules that are hard to game in practice, for instance because finding a useful lie is itself a hard computation, a line opened in computer science in 1989. The other side answered that hardness in the worst case says little about the typical election, and the measured results below were in part that answer.

The measured turn came from the analysis of tables on the cube. In 2002 the chance of a circle was written as the sum over turnings of sky47.5, giving the wall for one table in three lines, and asking what happens to a rule that is nearly fair. A 2008 paper showed that rules far from a dictator can be lied to often, not just sometimes, and the 2009 quantitative wall for circles followed, with a tight version soon after.

In practice the walls have not stopped reform; they have shaped its arguments. Australia has ranked candidates for its lower house since 1918, and Maine adopted ranked voting statewide from 2018. The case for it is that a vote for a hopeless favourite is not wasted; the case against is the nine voters of sky47.2, where dropping the last passes over a candidate who beats everyone head to head. Both sides cite the same mathematics, and what is argued now is which failure matters more, and how often it happens to real voters, not to random ones.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A rule from profiles to rankings is a social welfare function; one that picks a winner is a social choice function. The three demands are unanimity (the weak Pareto condition), independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) and non-dictatorship; the lone-voter technique is Arrow’s impossibility theorem, and the decisive-set proof here is the one of the field expansion and group contraction lemmas. The going-round technique is the Condorcet paradox; the candidate who beats every other head to head is the Condorcet winner. Counting first places is plurality; dropping the last and counting again is instant-runoff voting, also called ranked-choice or preferential voting; points by place is the Borda count. The voter who lies is strategic voting, and the wall of 1973 and 1975 is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. Random rankings, all equally likely, are the impartial culture. The three-pairs technique is Kalai’s formula, and the limit ¼ − (3/2π) arcsin ⅓ ≈ 8.77% is Guilbaud’s number. The 2009 result is Mossel’s quantitative Arrow theorem; the tight version is Keller’s; the measured lying voter is the quantitative Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. The best rule for two candidates under noise is Majority Is Stablest.

Who said it first

The Marquis de Condorcet published the paradox in 1785; Jean-Charles de Borda proposed his count to the Academy in 1770, printed in 1784. Kenneth Arrow proved the impossibility in his thesis, published as Social Choice and Individual Values (1951), and shared the 1972 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with John Hicks. Allan Gibbard (1973) and Mark Satterthwaite (1975) proved the manipulation theorem. Gil Kalai gave the Fourier view (Advances in Applied Mathematics, 2002). Ehud Friedgut, Gil Kalai and Noam Nisan gave the first quantitative Gibbard–Satterthwaite (2008), extended by Elchanan Mossel and Miklós Rácz. Elchanan Mossel posted the quantitative Arrow theorem in March 2009 (arXiv 0903.2574; Probability Theory and Related Fields, 2012); Nathan Keller gave the tight version (arXiv 1003.3956; Journal of the European Mathematical Society, 2012). Mossel, Ryan O’Donnell and Krzysztof Oleszkiewicz proved Majority Is Stablest (2005).

A proof that is not allowed

“Majority is fair between every two candidates, so it is fair between three: just put the candidates in the order the pairs give.” Not allowed: the pairs can give a circle, as sky47.1 shows, and a circle is no order. The wall is not about any one pair; it is about making the pairs agree.
