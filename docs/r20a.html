20a · proof

Isometries and Similarities

Rung 18 built the plane and rung 19 built moves. This step puts the moves on the plane. A move of the plane sends every point somewhere. The moves that matter to geometry are the ones that keep distances, and the ones that change every distance by the same factor. With them, “the same shape” and “the same shape and size” stop being words about looking and become claims a move can prove.

An isometry is a move of the plane that keeps the distance between every two points. A similarity multiplies every distance by the same positive factor. Both send segments onto segments, and any circle onto any other by a similarity.

20a1Moves That Keep Distances

From here on, a move of the plane is a shuffle of ℝ²: a function f from ℝ² to ℝ² that is one-to-one and onto (9c). A move f is an isometry when d(f(P), f(Q)) = d(P, Q) for all points P and Q. The word is Greek for “equal measure”.

An isometry moves a figure without changing any length in it. And because 18b made “straight” a fact about lengths, an isometry cannot bend a straight line.

Keeps-segments technique: an isometry sends every segment PR onto the segment from f(P) to f(R).

Q \in PR \iff d(P, Q) + d(Q, R) = d(P, R) @@ equality-on-the-segment (18b3)
\iff d(fP, fQ) + d(fQ, fR) = d(fP, fR) @@ f keeps each of the three distances
\iff fQ \in f(P)f(R) @@ equality-on-the-segment again
\htmlClass{new}{f(PR) = f(P)f(R)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ⊆ by the first two lines; ⊇ since f is onto, so every point of the segment is some fQ

20a2Moves That Keep Shape

From here on, a move f is a similarity with factor k, for a real k > 0, when d(f(P), f(Q)) = k · d(P, Q) for all P and Q. An isometry is a similarity with factor 1.

The stretch from the origin by k, (x, y) ↦ (kx, ky), is a similarity with factor k: its displacements are k times the old ones, and |ku| = k|u| by 18a’s distance-laws. A similarity keeps shape and changes size: every length in a figure is multiplied by the same k. A stretch from a fixed centre is called a dilation, and k its scale factor.

The keeps-segments proof runs again with every distance multiplied by k, so a similarity also sends segments onto segments.

f(x, y) = (3x + 1,\ 3y - 2):\ \ f(Q) - f(P) = 3(Q - P) @@ the 1 and −2 cancel in the displacement
d(fP, fQ) = |3(Q - P)| = 3\,d(P, Q) @@ a similarity with factor 3

20a3All Circles Are Similar

All-circles-similar technique: for any two circles there is a similarity that sends the first onto the second. Slide the first centre to the second, and stretch by the ratio of the radii.

f(P) = O' + \tfrac{r'}{r}(P - O) @@ circles with centres O, O′ and radii r, r′
d(fP, fQ) = \tfrac{r'}{r}\,d(P, Q) @@ the O′ cancels; distance-laws
d(P, O) = r \Rightarrow d(fP, O') = \tfrac{r'}{r} \cdot r = r' @@ f(O) = O′: the first circle lands inside the second
g(Q) = O + \tfrac{r}{r'}(Q - O') \text{ undoes } f @@ so f is a move, onto: every point of the second circle is f of a point of the first
\htmlClass{new}{f(\text{first circle}) = \text{second circle}} \quad \blacksquare

So there is one circle, in every size and place. The same is true of squares, and of equal-sided triangles; it is false of rectangles, as 20a4 asks.

The accepted names

An isometry is also a rigid motion, or a congruence transformation. A similarity with a fixed centre is a dilation, or homothety.

Who said it first

In 20a.4.5, the Greek geometer is Euclid, and his proof is Elements I.4. The French geometer is Jacques Peletier du Mans (1557); the Jesuit is Christopher Clavius, in his edition of Euclid (1574). David Hilbert made the side-angle-side fact an axiom of congruence in 1899.
