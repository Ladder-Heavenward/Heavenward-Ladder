∞·crypt · arrow newsflash

What Can a Key Not Hide?

What can a key not hide? Five cryptography annexes, from rung 4 to rung 43, each broke a cipher or proved one safe by working with its letters, bits, wheels or points. This page reads the five again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. A key is an arrow, and a cipher is a family of arrows. What the family keeps, no key in it can hide, however many keys there are. Shifts compose to shifts, so two locks are one. Every scrambled alphabet keeps the shape of the tallies. The wheel machine is a sandwich, and a sandwich keeps whatever is said with arrows alone. The pad is the one case where the picture of message and secret text falls into two pieces. And a secret split among five is a product, in many ways at once.

Keys closed under ∘: encrypting twice is encrypting once. tally(g · m) = tally(m) ∘ g⁻¹, so any h with h(t ∘ g⁻¹) = h(t) is the same for the secret text and the message. R ↦ S⁻¹RS keeps ∘, do-nothing and undoing, so it keeps “own undo” and “no letter to itself”. With a uniform key, P(c | m) = 2⁻ⁿ: the joint chance of message and secret text is its two marginals side by side. For any three different hours, a curve of degree at most 2 is its three values there; hour 0 is one of the hours.

infcrypt.1Two Locks Are One Lock

4·crypt3 found that shifting twice is shifting once, and that undoing a shift is one more shift. It used both to run a message back. Read as arrows, the two facts say something about the whole family of keys. Write Ek for the arrow from the alphabet to itself that encrypts with key k.

Closed-keys technique: if for every two keys k and m there is a key j with Em ∘ Ek = Ej, then encrypting twice, with any two keys, is encrypting once with one key. An enemy who tries every key has tried every pair of keys as well.

E_m \circ E_k = E_j \text{ for some key } j @@ the family is closed under ∘
\text{a secret text made with } k \text{ then } m \text{ is the secret text made with } j @@ the same arrow, so the same output on every message
\text{trying every } j \text{ runs every } E_j^{-1},\ \text{and } E_j^{-1} = (E_m \circ E_k)^{-1} \text{ is among them} @@ one of the tries undoes both locks
\htmlClass{new}{\text{pairs of keys add no tries}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and so for three keys, or any row of them (47a)
  
E_m \circ E_k = E_{(k + m) \bmod 26} @@ the shifts-add technique (4·crypt3)
E_0 = \text{do-nothing},\qquad E_k^{-1} = E_{26 - k} @@ the key-back technique (4·crypt3)
\htmlClass{new}{k \mapsto E_k \text{ keeps adding as composing: the clock of 26, acting on the alphabet}} @@ a map that keeps composing (47b)

Shift by 3, then by 5, then by 11, and the enemy still has 25 keys to try, not 25³. The scrambled alphabets of 4·crypt5 are closed too: a matching after a matching is a matching. What hides is the arrangement: the letters. Nothing in the lines used that the alphabet is an alphabet. They used only how the keys compose. A family of keys that is not closed is the first thing a stronger cipher needs.

infcrypt.2What Every Key Keeps

10·crypt had 26! keys, too many to try, and broke the cipher anyway by counting letters. Its tallies-travel technique said that the secret text holds g(x) exactly as often as the message holds x. Read as arrows, that is a square that closes for every key, and the break follows from the square alone.

Kept-by-every-key technique: if h is a function of tables with h(t ∘ g⁻¹) = h(t) for every matching g, then h of the secret text’s tally equals h of the message’s tally, whatever the key. The list of tallies put in order is such an h, and so is the number of pairs of places that hold equal letters.

h(\mathrm{tally}(g \cdot m)) = h(\mathrm{tally}(m) \circ g^{-1}) @@ the square: tallies-travel (10·crypt3)
\htmlClass{new}{h(\mathrm{tally}(g \cdot m)) = h(\mathrm{tally}(m))} \quad \blacksquare @@ h does not see the labels
t \circ g^{-1} \text{ takes the same values as } t,\ \text{each as often} @@ g⁻¹ is a matching: so the tallies in order are kept
\textstyle\sum_x \binom{t(x)}{2} \text{ is a sum over all the labels} @@ a sum does not care about the order of its terms: so the pairs of equal letters are kept

What hides is the arrangement, and here that is the key itself. Whatever can be worked out from the message with the labels rubbed off can be worked out from the secret text, by someone with no key. 26! counts the ways to put the labels back. It says nothing about what was never hidden. A cipher that hides the shape of the tallies must have keys that do not all respect the tally, and 11·crypt’s keyword is the first step toward one.

infcrypt.3A Sandwich Keeps What Arrows Say

19·crypt2 found the wheel machine’s shuffle to be E = S⁻¹RS: in through the wheels, across the pairing wheel R, and back out. 19·crypt3 then proved two things about E, each by its own lines: it is its own undo, and it sends no letter to itself. Both proofs carried a property of R through the sandwich. Arrows say what a sandwich carries.

Sandwich-map technique: for a fixed shuffle S, the map c(X) = S⁻¹XS on shuffles keeps composing and do-nothing, and it has an undo, Y ↦ SYS⁻¹. So X is its own undo exactly when c(X) is, and x ↦ S⁻¹x matches the letters X sends to themselves with the letters c(X) sends to themselves.

c(X)\,c(Y) = S^{-1}X\,S\,S^{-1}\,Y S = S^{-1}XY S = c(XY) @@ the inner S S⁻¹ is do-nothing
c(e) = S^{-1}S = e,\qquad S\,c(X)\,S^{-1} = X @@ do-nothing kept; the undo of c
XX = e \iff c(X)c(X) = c(XX) = c(e) = e @@ leftward: apply the undo of c
X x = x \iff S^{-1}X\,S\,(S^{-1}x) = S^{-1}x @@ multiply by S⁻¹; leftward by S
\htmlClass{new}{c \text{ keeps own-undo, and the count of letters sent to themselves}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for R: own undo, and count 0 (19·crypt3)

So no setting of the wheels, and no board of plugs in front of them, could change those two facts: the plugs only make the sandwich thicker, with SP for S. What hides is the arrangement, the wiring of every wheel and the day’s setting. The designers changed S daily and kept R. Whatever is said of R with ∘ and = alone is then said of the machine on every day, and 20·crypt’s break begins from exactly that.

infcrypt.4The Picture That Falls in Two

9·crypt4 proved that for each message m and each secret text c of the same length, exactly one key sends m to c. It concluded that the secret text fits every message equally well. ∞ג‎ has the picture for that conclusion. Let the key be drawn by n fair coin tosses, apart from the message, so each of the 2ⁿ keys has chance 2⁻ⁿ.

Pad-falls-apart technique: with a key drawn uniformly and apart from the message, P(c | m) = 2⁻ⁿ for every message m and every secret text c. So the joint chance of message and secret text is its two marginals side by side, and every event about the secret text is independent of every event about the message.

P(c \mid m) = P(\text{the key is } m \oplus c) = 2^{-n} @@ the one-key-each technique (9·crypt4); the key is uniform and apart from m
P(m, c) = P(m)\,2^{-n} @@ the chance of “and”, from the given chance (30f)
P(c) = \textstyle\sum_m P(m)\,2^{-n} = 2^{-n} @@ add over the messages: their chances total 1
\htmlClass{new}{P(m, c) = P(m)\,P(c)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the falls-apart technique (∞ג‎): the picture is in two pieces

In boxes: the arrow from message to secret text is “discard the message, then draw n fresh bits”. No wire joins the two sides. This is stronger than hiding the labels. In the last three footings the key hid an arrangement and kept a shape. Here the arrow keeps nothing of the message at all, whatever the chances of the messages were.

c_1 \oplus c_2 = m_1 \oplus m_2 @@ the key-cancels technique (9·crypt6)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a plain function of the secret texts equals a plain function of the messages}} @@ the two sides are joined by a wire: the picture does not fall in two

infcrypt.5One Product, Many Ways

43·crypt proved two things about a secret split among five. Any three shares fix the curve, and so the secret. Any two shares fit exactly one curve for each possible secret, and so say nothing. Its proof of the second already used a turn of thought: the secret is the value at hour 0, and 0 is an hour like the others. In arrows the two results are one.

Many-products technique: for any three different hours a, b and c, the set C with the arrows ea, eb, ec is a product of three copies of the clock: for any three values there is exactly one curve with those values at a, b and c.

\text{values } (u, v, w) \mapsto \text{the one curve with } e_a = u,\ e_b = v,\ e_c = w @@ the three-points-fix technique (43·crypt2)
\text{so } (e_a, e_b, e_c) : C \to \text{clock}^3 \text{ is a matching} \quad \blacksquare @@ exactly one curve for each triple: the demand on a product (47d)
a, b, c \text{ three shares’ hours}:\quad e_0 = e_0 \circ (e_a, e_b, e_c)^{-1} \circ (e_a, e_b, e_c) @@ the secret is an arrow out of the three shares: they open it (43·crypt3)
\htmlClass{new}{a = 0,\ b, c \text{ two shares’ hours}:\quad \text{every } (s', v, w) \text{ is the values of exactly one curve}} @@ so two shares leave every secret open, each with one curve (43·crypt4)

What hides is the arrangement: which hours came. The three-points-fix technique was proved once, for three hours with no names. The dealer’s plan is to hand out arrows of one product such that every three of them, the hidden e₀ among them, make the product again. With p² equally likely dealings and one curve for each secret, the chance of each secret given two shares is 1/p, as 43·crypt4 counted.

The accepted names

A family of arrows from a set to itself that is closed under ∘, has do-nothing and has undos is a group of permutations, and a map k ↦ Ek that keeps composing is a group action, or a homomorphism; the clock of 26 is the cyclic group of order 26, and the shift cipher is the Caesar cipher. That a cipher whose keys form a group gains nothing from encrypting twice is a standard remark; whether a cipher’s keys form a group is a question asked of every block cipher. A map that respects an action is equivariant, an instance of a natural transformation; an h that the action does not change is an invariant; the count of pairs of equal letters, scaled, is the index of coincidence. The sandwich S⁻¹XS is conjugation, an inner automorphism; what it keeps of a shuffle is its cycle type; a shuffle that is its own undo is an involution, and one that sends no letter to itself is fixed-point-free, or a derangement. The pad is the one-time pad, and the falling apart of the picture is perfect secrecy: the secret text is independent of the message. The split secret is secret sharing with a threshold of three; the curve through three points is Lagrange interpolation; the demand “exactly one for each triple” is the universal property of the product.

What the arrows let hide

In four of the five footings the arrows hide arrangement: the letters, the labels on the tallies, the wiring, the hours that came. In the fourth the arrow hides the message itself, and that is what sets the pad apart from every cipher that only moves labels. In each footing the plan is one statement about arrows: a family closed under ∘; a square that closes for every key; a map that keeps composing; a picture in two pieces; a product. No dated claim is made on this page.
