15·econ · economics

What is a payment next year worth today?

What is a payment next year worth today? Less than the same payment now: money in hand can be lent out and come back with interest. People have argued about how much less for as long as there has been lending. Every loan, lease, pension and lottery prize turns on it. It is hard for two reasons. A deal is a stream of payments spread over years, and a stream is many numbers at once. And the rate that turns later money into money now is a guess about the years to come. This page shows that interest is a step that multiplies, so a stream of equal payments is 15i's geometric sum. Then it runs the sum backwards to find the true rate hidden in a deal.

At a yearly rate, money grows by the factor one plus the rate each year, so a payment due later is worth itself divided by that factor once for each year. A run of equal payments is worth a geometric sum of such pieces now. Equal payments forever are worth one payment divided by the rate. A deal's true rate is the rate at which its payments are worth its price; halving traps it.

Interest is a step that multiplies

A credit union in Salinas pays a rate i a year on savings: each year it adds i times what is in the account. Start with B₀ dollars, and write Bₙ for the balance after n years.

B_{n+1} = B_n + iB_n = (1 + i)B_n @@ a year's interest is i times what is there
\htmlClass{new}{B_n = (1 + i)^n B_0} @@ induction (4c)
1000 \cdot 1.04^{10} \approx 1480.24 @@ $1000 at 4% for ten years
(1 + i)^n \ge 1 + ni @@ power-of-one-plus (15i): interest on interest never loses to interest on the start alone

A bank that quotes 6% a year and adds it monthly adds 0.5% each month. After a year, 1.005¹² ≈ 1.0617: the account has grown 6.17%, not 6%. Power-of-one-plus says this always happens, since (1 + i/12)¹² ≥ 1 + i. The 6% has the accepted name the annual percentage rate; the 6.17% is the effective annual rate.

Money later, counted now

What is $1000 due in a year worth today? The amount V that, saved at i, grows to $1000 in a year: V(1 + i) = 1000. Run the multiplying step backwards.

V(1 + i)^n = A @@ V now grows to A in n years
\htmlClass{new}{V = \tfrac{A}{(1 + i)^n} = A(1 + i)^{-n}} @@ divide by (1 + i)ⁿ, positive
\tfrac{10\,000}{1.05^{6}} \approx 7462.15 @@ $10 000 due in 6 years, at 5%

A payment A due in n years is worth A/(1 + i)ⁿ today. Its accepted name is the present value, and i is then called the discount rate. Present values are amounts on the same day, so they add. The price of a stream of payments is the sum of their present values.

A stream of equal payments: 15i's sum

A loan of L is paid back by n equal payments M, one at the end of each month, at a monthly rate j. The loan is fair when the payments are worth L today. Write v = 1/(1 + j).

V = \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} M v^k = Mv\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} v^k @@ sums-are-linear (15g)
= Mv\,\tfrac{1 - v^n}{1 - v} @@ finite-ratio-sum (15i), ratio v ≠ 1
\htmlClass{new}{V = M\,\tfrac{1 - (1 + j)^{-n}}{j}} @@ v/(1 − v) = 1/j
M = \tfrac{Lj}{1 - (1 + j)^{-n}} @@ set V = L and solve for M

A $600 000 loan for a house in Salinas, over 30 years at 6% a year, has n = 360 and j = 0.005. Then M ≈ $3597.30 a month. The 360 payments add to about $1 295 029, so about $695 029 of it is interest. The first payment is mostly interest: 0.005 × 600 000 = $3000 of it, and only $597.30 comes off the loan.

Payments that never stop have an endless sum. If |v| < 1, endless-ratio-sum gives it a total.

\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} A(1 + i)^{-k} = \tfrac{A}{1 + i}\cdot\tfrac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{1 + i}} @@ endless-ratio-sum, ratio 1/(1 + i) < 1 for i > 0
\htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac{A}{i}} @@ $5000 a year forever, at 4%: $125 000

Payments that grow make a geometric sum too. In November 2022, a Powerball jackpot won in California was offered two ways. One was about $2.04 billion in 30 yearly payments, the first at once and each 5% bigger than the last. The other was about $997.6 million in cash. The first payment A solves A(1.05³⁰ − 1)/0.05 ≈ 2.04 billion, so A ≈ $30.7 million. At a rate i, the 30 payments are worth A(1 − r³⁰)/(1 − r) today, with r = 1.05/(1 + i). At i = 5%, r = 1 and they are worth 30A ≈ $921 million, less than the cash. The two offers are worth the same at a rate near 4.4% a year.

The true rate of a deal

A dealer in Salinas sells a $20 000 pickup for $2000 down and 48 monthly payments of $450. That lends the buyer $18 000. At what monthly rate j are 48 payments of $450 worth $18 000? Write A(j) = Σₖ₌₁⁴⁸ (1 + j)⁻ᵏ. The rate solves 450 A(j) = 18 000, that is, A(j) = 40.

A \text{ is continuous on } [0, 0.01] @@ continuous sums and quotients (11b)
A \text{ falls as } j \text{ rises} @@ powers-keep-order: each (1 + j)⁻ᵏ falls; so at most one rate works
A(0) = 48 \gt 40,\ \ A(0.01) \approx 37.97 \lt 40 @@ in-between-value (12a): some j in between has A(j) = 40
A(0.005) \approx 42.58,\ \ A(0.0075) \approx 40.19 @@ halving-traps (12e): the trap is [0.0075, 0.01]
A(0.00875) \approx 39.06,\ \ A(0.008125) \approx 39.62 @@ the trap is [0.0075, 0.008125]
\text{ten halvings}:\ \ \htmlClass{new}{j \approx 0.0077} @@ the trap is less than 0.00001 wide
(1.0077)^{12} \approx 1.0964 @@ about 9.6% a year

A deal's true rate is the rate at which its payments are worth its price. Its accepted name is the internal rate of return. On a loan, the 12j ≈ 9.24% is the rate lenders in the United States must print, the annual percentage rate.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

1a set beside the first proofs a second kind of care, the ledger: every line is paid for, and nothing is claimed that was not paid for. A loan is a ledger. Each month the lender adds interest, jB, and takes off a payment, M: B₍ₖ₊₁₎ = (1 + j)Bₖ − M. The books close at 0 after the last payment exactly when the payments are worth the loan today.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every sentence in it is true.

“No interest” is a sentence. The ledger asks where the interest went. Often it is inside the price, and the cash price shows it.

A furniture store in Marina sells a $1200 sofa for 12 monthly payments of $100, “with no interest”, or for $1100 cash. Where on the ledger is the interest?

Who said it first

Ramsey wrote that to count later enjoyments as worth less than earlier ones is a practice that is ethically indefensible, and that it arises merely from a weakness of the imagination.

Frank Ramsey, “A mathematical theory of saving” (1928); paraphrase

Leonardo of Pisa’s Liber Abaci (1202) already worked out what payments to come were worth, for merchants. Richard Witt printed tables of compound interest in London in 1613, and Edmond Halley priced annuities paid for life in 1693. Irving Fisher’s The Theory of Interest (1930) set the rate as a price between now and later. In 15·econ.4.4, the report of 2006 is Nicholas Stern’s, for the British government, and the reply of 2007 is William Nordhaus’s.

Accepted names: compound interest; present value; discount rate and discounting; an annuity for equal payments; amortisation for paying a loan down; a perpetuity for payments forever; the effective annual rate; the annual percentage rate (APR); the internal rate of return. The guess of 15·econ.3.1(d) is the rule of 72.
