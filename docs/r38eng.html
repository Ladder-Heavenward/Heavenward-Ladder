Why Does a Rocket Nozzle Widen?

Why does a rocket nozzle widen? A garden hose nozzle narrows, and water squirts faster. A rocket nozzle first narrows to a throat, then flares out wide like a bell, and the gas leaving the bell is far faster than at the throat. Narrowing speeds slow flow, but widening speeds fast flow. This page keeps the same mass flowing through every section, finds that below the speed of sound (37) narrowing speeds the flow, finds with 28’s partials that above it widening does, finds that the throat runs at exactly the speed of sound, and finds the thrust from momentum (14·phys).

The same mass passes every section each second: ρAv is fixed. Speeding up thins the gas: dρ/ρ = −M² dv/v, with M the speed over the speed of sound. So dA/A = (M² − 1) dv/v. Below M = 1, narrowing speeds the gas; above, widening does; at the throat M = 1 exactly. Thrust is the momentum leaving each second plus the pressure difference across the exit.

38eng1The Same Mass Flow Through Every Section

Randy designs rocket engines. Hot gas from the burning chamber streams steadily through the nozzle. Whatever passes one section each second must pass every other.

\rho\,A\,v = \text{the same at every section} @@ ρ density, A area, v speed
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} + \dfrac{dA}{A} + \dfrac{dv}{v} = 0} @@ logs of a product, then slopes (17, 28c)

From here on, on this page, a is the speed of sound in the gas (37·phys), and M = v/a its Mach number.

38eng2Below the Speed of Sound, Narrowing Speeds the Flow

Gas speeding up is pushed by falling pressure, and falling pressure thins the gas. How much it thins for a given pressure change is set by the speed of sound: dp = a² dρ (37·phys).

v\,dv = -\dfrac{dp}{\rho} @@ the push of the pressure slope (28·phys)
= -a^2\,\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} = -M^2\,\dfrac{dv}{v}} @@ slow gas barely thins; fast gas thins a lot

At low speed M² is tiny, the gas hardly thins, and like water in a hose it must speed up where the passage narrows.

38eng3Above It, Widening Does

\dfrac{dA}{A} = -\dfrac{dv}{v} - \dfrac{d\rho}{\rho} = (M^2 - 1)\,\dfrac{dv}{v} @@ putting the two together
M < 1:\ dA < 0 \text{ to speed up} @@ narrow
\htmlClass{new}{M > 1:\ dA > 0 \text{ to speed up}} @@ widen: the gas thins faster than it speeds

Above the speed of sound, each bit of speed thins the gas by more than its own share, so to keep ρAv fixed the area must grow.

38eng4The Throat Runs at Exactly Mach 1

Where the area is least, dA = 0. So either the speed is not changing, or M = 1. In a working nozzle the gas speeds up the whole way, so at the throat it moves at exactly the speed of sound.

\dfrac{A}{A_{\text{throat}}} = \dfrac{1}{M}\left[\dfrac{2}{\gamma + 1}\left(1 + \dfrac{\gamma - 1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma + 1}{2(\gamma - 1)}} @@ from the steps above, added up
\htmlClass{new}{M = 2:\ \dfrac{1}{2}\,(1.5)^3 \approx 1.69} @@ the bell’s mouth 1.69 times the throat for twice the speed of sound

Throat technique: a nozzle that takes gas from rest past the speed of sound must narrow to a throat, where the gas moves at exactly Mach 1, and then widen; the area ratio beyond the throat fixes the exit speed. In a test stand, pressure taps along a nozzle read the falling pressures this area ratio gives at each section, and the throat reading sits at the value for Mach 1.

38eng5Thrust: Momentum Out per Second Plus a Pressure Term

The engine pushes the gas back; the gas pushes the engine forward by the same amount (14·phys). The push is the momentum carried out each second, plus the extra push of the gas’s pressure across the exit over the outside air’s.

F = \dot m\,v_e + (p_e - p_a)\,A_e @@ momentum out each second, plus the pressure term
\dot m = 250 \text{ kg/s},\ v_e = 3\,000 \text{ m/s}:\ \dot m v_e = 750 \text{ kN} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a bell sized so } p_e \approx p_a \text{ wastes nothing}} @@ too short leaves pressure unused; too long over-thins

Thrust is the momentum the gas carries away each second, plus a pressure term that is best near zero; a nozzle is sized so its exit pressure matches the outside air. That is why engines for the upper stages, working in near-vacuum, have much bigger bells than those that start on the ground.

Accepted names

A nozzle that narrows then widens is a de Laval nozzle; flow held at Mach 1 at the throat is choked; the area ratio is the area-Mach relation, and γ the ratio of specific heats. A bell whose exit pressure matches the outside is ideally expanded.

Who said it first

Gustaf de Laval used the narrowing then widening nozzle in his steam turbines in the 1880s (not yet revisited for the date).
