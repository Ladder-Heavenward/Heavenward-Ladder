1e · proof

Every and some

“Every cat is an animal.” “Some cat is asleep.” These are the words that let a statement reach past the things you have named to everything, or to something unnamed. They carry most of the weight in mathematics, and they are where careful readers and careless ones part company. This step gives each its mark and its techs. It proves that the order of the words matters and which way the swap is licensed. It shows how a “not” passes through each, which is where the rung's gap appears for the last time. It ends with the illegal moves that sound most like good sense.

To speak of everything, speak of anything: prove it of a thing about which you supposed nothing. To speak of something, show it. An every may be applied to whatever you have; a some may be given a fresh name and nothing more.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1b, 1c and 1d (¬, ⊥, ∧, ∨, ⇒, suppositions, and their techs), and on 0a's statements-with-a-gap. With this step the rung's claim is complete: every joining word has its techs. 1h drills reading, writing and negating these statements; 1i makes the counterexample a proof shape; rung 2's principles are all written with ∀ and ∃; rung 7 licenses the direction withheld here.

1e1The words, then the marks

Stands on 0a7 (a statement named whole) and 1d1 (the word, then the mark).

0a built statements-with-a-gap: P is “… is grey”, and P(t) fills the gap with the name t. With a letter in the gap, P is “x is grey”. Now suppose, instead of a name, you want to say that the statement is true whatever fills the gap, or that something fills it. English does this with “every” and “some”, and here are their marks:

∀x P(x) and ∃x P(x) are statements, each made from a statement with a gap.

Read ∀x P(x) as “for every x, P of x” or “for all x”. Read ∃x P(x) as “there is an x with P of x” or “for some x”. The marks are named for their shapes. ∀ is a turned A, said “turn A”: the A of “all”, turned half a turn. ∃ is a turned E, said “turn E”: the E of “exists”, turned the same half turn.

From here on, a gap is marked by a letter such as x, not by “…”. The dots were 0a's first way of showing a gap. The letter does the same job, and it is what turn A and turn E grab: in ∀x P(x), the ∀ grabs the x that marks P's gap. 0a8 asked you to read “some” as the gap until this step. From here “some” has its own mark, and that foreclosure completes.

One of the inventors of these marks compared them to a microscope set beside the eye. The eye is far more versatile, and for most of life it is the better tool. But where sharp distinctions are needed, the microscope sees what the eye cannot.

1e2“Some” means an example exists

Stands on 1e1 (the words, then the marks) and 1c5 (or, and its mark).

“Some” on the ladder means that an example exists. It never hints “but not all”. For example: “some cat is asleep” is so even if every cat is. “Some” is generous in the way 1c5's “or” is generous: content with one, content with all.

What every and some do to 1f's tables, when the things in question can be listed, is answered in 1h.

No key for these marks? Write for every x, P(x) and for some x, P(x), as 4d21 wrote end.

1e3A letter that names nothing

Stands on 1e1 (the words, then the marks) and 0a2 (a letter for a thing).

The letter after the sign is not a name. It is a placeholder, a letter that marks the gap so that the sign can refer to it. It may be changed for any letter not already in use. For example, ∀x P(x) and ∀r P(r) say exactly the same thing. The accepted word is that the letter is bound by the sign.

Compare a name, which 0a said names the same thing throughout an argument. A bound letter names nothing, and outside its sign it means nothing. The only rule about choosing the letter is the obvious one. Do not bind a letter that is already doing work as a name in the same proof, or the reader cannot tell which is which.

1e4Two gaps

Stands on 1e3 (a letter that names nothing) and 0a6 (four shapes of a claim).

A statement can have a gap in more than one place, and 0a5 gave the bracket for filling several. R(u, v) is the statement R with u in its earlier gap and v in its later gap. Every and some can now bind either gap, or both.

So let R be “x is the mother of y”. Then R(u, v) says u is the mother of v. R(v, u) says v is the mother of u, which is a different statement. With both gaps bound you can write ∀x ∃y R(y, x): for every x there is some y who is x's mother. Everyone has a mother. The order of the signs will turn out to matter, in 1e13.

1e5Speak of everything by speaking of anything

Stands on 1e4 (two gaps) and 1b4 (what a line under a supposition may cite).

Make an every: open with “let n be anything”, where n is a letter not in use; if you reach P(n) using nothing about n but that it is a thing, you may write ∀x P(x). “Nothing about n” means two things. Nothing given mentions n, and no supposition still standing at that point mentions n. The thought: an argument that worked for n without using anything about n would work for any thing in n's place. You already do this. Pick any cat at all, use nothing special about it, and show that it has whiskers. Then you have shown that every cat has whiskers.

For example, here is “everything is the same as itself”, ∀x (x = x). Let n be anything. By the self-sameness axiom, filled with n, n = n. Nothing was supposed about n. So, speaking of everything, ∀x (x = x).

1e6Use an every

Stands on 1e5 (speak of everything by speaking of anything).

Use an every: from ∀x P(x), you may write P(t) for any name t you have. A promise about everything covers the thing in your hand. This uses an every. For example: every dog barks; Rex is a dog; so, using the every with Rex, if Rex is a dog, Rex barks. Carry that if through, and Rex barks.

1e7Show an example

Stands on 1e6 (use an every) and 1e2 (“some” means an example exists).

Show an example: from P(t), for some name t, you may write ∃x P(x). This makes a some. For example: Tibbles is asleep; so, showing Tibbles as the example, something is asleep. You have thrown away the news of which thing it was, as add an option throws away which disjunct.

1e8Use a some

Stands on 1e7 (show an example) and 1b4 (a supposition's report).

Use a some: from ∃x P(x), say “call such a thing b”, with b a letter not in use anywhere in the argument, and suppose P(b); reach some R under that supposition that does not mention b; drop it; R stands. This uses a some. You were promised that something has P, not told which. The fresh name is a name for “whatever it is”, and nothing may be supposed of it but P.

For example: something in the yard is wet. Call such a thing b, and suppose b is in the yard and b is wet. Keep right: b is wet. Show an example, b: something is wet. That does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b.

Notice the symmetry. When you make an every, the thing is out of your hands: anything. When you use one, you choose. When you make a some, you choose the example. When you use one, the example is out of your hands. The picture in 1e13 turns this choosing into a game against a sceptic.

1e9Every grandparent is a parent

Stands on 1e5 to 1e8, and 1d4 (prove an if).

Here is an argument that uses every tech in this step and most of the rung: every grandparent is a parent. What “grandparent” means is a some: a grandparent is someone with a child who has a child. What “parent” means is an every around an if: anyone with a child is a parent.

What “parent” means: for every x, if x has a child, x is a parent. What “grandparent” means: for every x, if x is a grandparent, some child of x has a child.

In marks, let C(y, x) say y is a child of x, P(x) that x is a parent, and G(x) that x is a grandparent:

That is the plan: the foot is an every around an if, so the argument must open “let n be anything” and then “suppose n is a grandparent”. Now forward.

Let n be anything. Suppose, until we drop it, that n is a grandparent. Use the every in “grandparent” with n, and carry the if through (1d): some child of n has a child. Use that some. Call such a child b, a fresh name, and suppose nothing of b but this: b is a child of n, and b has a child. Keep the left part (1c): b is a child of n. So n has a child (show an example: b). Use the every in “parent” with n, and carry the if through: n is a parent. “n is a parent” does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b.

Now drop the supposition: if n is a grandparent, n is a parent (1d). Nothing was supposed about n. So, speaking of everything: for every x, if x is a grandparent, x is a parent.

It is a lot of steps for something obvious, and in practice neighbouring steps merge. But every step is now something you could defend to a sceptic, and the places where sceptics strike are marked. The some was used honestly. What was carried out of the call, “n is a parent”, does not mention b. And the every was made honestly. The letter n was brought in as anything, and nothing given and no supposition said anything about n in particular. The supposition “n is a grandparent” was dropped before the every was made.

1e10Every A is B; some A is B

Stands on 1e9 (every grandparent is a parent) and 1d12 (free ifs).

Most everyday every-and-some sentences carry a restriction: “every cat is an animal” is not about everything, it is about cats. Let A(x) be “x is a cat” and B(x) be “x is an animal”. The marks have a standard way of writing each, and they differ in the joining word inside:

Every goes with ⇒; some goes with ∧. Swap them and the statement changes meaning. ∀x (A(x) ∧ B(x)) says everything is a cat and an animal, which the shoe on your foot refutes. ∃x (A(x) ⇒ B(x)) is shown by the same shoe: the shoe is not a cat, so 1d's free-if technique makes its if free, and the statement says nothing about cats at all. 1h works through both with proofs. For this step, carry the pairing: every with if, some with and.

1e11“Is a” is not “is”

Stands on 1e10 (every A is B) and 0b9 (a sameness, or a claim).

0b9 and 1a7 kept two jobs of “is” apart: the sameness “Tibbles is the grey cat” and the claim “Tibbles is a cat”, C(t). The word has a third job, and only the first is =. “Every cat is an animal” is an every around an if.

“Every A is B” is not A = B: it runs one way, from A to B, and = runs both ways. Every cat is an animal, but Rex is an animal and not a cat. Read as a sameness, the statement would turn about by 0b's turn-about technique into “every animal is a cat”, which Rex refutes.

There is a second reason “every A is B” is not A = B. = joins two names, and “cat” and “animal” are not names here. They are claims with a gap, “… is a cat” and “… is an animal”. The nearest an every comes to a sameness is an every each way: every A is B and every B is A. Then A and B pass exactly the same things:

Even an every each way is a claim about what passes, not two names for one thing. 2a is where it becomes a sameness, of the collections the claims gather.

1e12Some, if every

Stands on 1e11 (“is a” is not “is”) and 1e7 (show an example).

Some cat is asleep if every cat is, as long as there is a cat. Pick a cat. The every says that cat is asleep. So a cat is asleep. In marks, reading c as “c, a cat”:

The arrow runs one way only: one sleeping cat does not put every cat to sleep. And “as long as there is a cat” is needed. With no cats at all, “every cat is asleep” is true for free, by 1d's free-if technique, and “some cat is asleep” is false.

1e13Order matters

Stands on 1e8 (use a some) and 1e4 (two gaps).

With an every and a some together, the order of the signs changes the meaning entirely. Let M(y, x) say y is x's mother.

The former is so and the latter totally false. The difference is who chooses when. Read the signs, left to right, as a game between you and a sceptic. At each ∀ the sceptic picks a thing. At each ∃ you pick. You win if the statement left at the end is true. In ∀x ∃y the sceptic names a person and then you name the mother; your pick may depend on theirs. In ∃y ∀x you must commit to a y before you know which x is coming.

1e14The licensed swap

Stands on 1e13 (order matters).

Committing early is harder, so the early-commitment form is the stronger, and the techs get the other from it. With M: if one person is everyone's mother, then everyone has a mother. Licensed-swap technique: if one thing serves every x, then every x has a thing that serves it. In marks, from ∃y ∀x M(y, x) you may write ∀x ∃y M(y, x). The same holds for any two-gap statement, M among them.

Start from ∃y ∀x M(y, x). Use the some: call such a thing b, a fresh name, and suppose ∀x M(b, x). Let n be anything. Use the every with n: M(b, n). Show an example, b: ∃y M(y, n). Nothing was supposed about n, so speak of everything: ∀x ∃y M(y, x). That does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b.

The same b served every n. Run the other way, you would need a b chosen before n is known, and nothing hands you that. From “everyone has a mother” no tech produces a mother shared by all.

“Every student read a book” has two readings in English. One is each student some book or other. The other is a single book read by all. Speech leaves it to context. The marks refuse the ambiguity by writing the signs in order, leftmost outermost.

Signs of the same kind do swap freely. ∀x ∀y and ∀y ∀x say the same, and so do ∃x ∃y and ∃y ∃x, because the same player picks both.

1e15Not some

Stands on 1e14 (the licensed swap), 1c11 (neither) and 1b7 (refute).

A not can sit in front of an every or a some. This step and the next two move it inside. Each sign turns into its partner as the not passes: every into some, some into every. There are three moves to know.

Not-some runs both ways. Counterexample runs one way. The way back from “not every” waits for rung 7.

“Nobody in the room is asleep” and “everyone in the room is awake” say the same thing. Not-some technique: “nothing has P” and “everything lacks P” each give the other. In marks, from ¬∃x P(x) you may write ∀x ¬P(x), and back.

Not-some is 1c11's neither technique on a bigger scale. A some is an or across everything. An every is an and across everything. And the neither technique said that “not either” is “neither”. Here is the argument, each way.

One way. Start from ¬∃x P(x). Let n be anything. Suppose P(n). Show an example, n: ∃x P(x). That clashes with what we have. Drop the supposition, by refute: ¬P(n). Nothing was supposed about n, so speak of everything: ∀x ¬P(x).

The other way. Start from ∀x ¬P(x). Suppose, until we drop it, ∃x P(x). Use the some: call such a thing b, and suppose P(b). Use the every with b: ¬P(b). Clash: totally false. ⊥ does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b. Drop the supposition ∃x P(x), by refute: ¬∃x P(x).

1e16Not every: the counterexample

Stands on 1e15 (not some).

Rex is not grey, so not everything is grey. Rex is the thing that fails, and one is enough.

Counterexample technique: if something fails P, then not everything has P. In marks, from ∃x ¬P(x) you may write ¬∀x P(x). The thing that fails is called a counterexample to the every. You already do this. “Not every swan is white,” you say, and point at a black one.

Start from ∃x ¬P(x): something fails. Suppose, until we drop it, ∀x P(x). Use the some: call the thing that fails b, and suppose ¬P(b). Use the every with b: P(b). Clash: totally false. ⊥ does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b. Drop the supposition ∀x P(x), by refute: ¬∀x P(x).

1e17The way back waits

Stands on 1e16 (not every) and 1b14 (the step you cannot take).

From “not every” to “some fails”, no argument on this rung gets there. Try it. The goal is a some, so the last move is show an example. That needs a name t, with ¬P(t) in hand. And “not every” hands you no name.

Knowing that not everyone in a crowd passed does not point you at anyone. Over things you can list and look at, you would simply look. The ladder cannot look. Over the endless ranges that rung 3 opens up, there may be nothing to look at. Rung 7 lays down the principle under which the step is sound.

1e18Each one, some one, or the crowd

Stands on 1e17 (the way back waits).

Every and some speak of members one at a time: each one, or at least one. A crowd has statements of its own, and they are a different size. A statement about the flock as a whole and a statement about one bird are two sizes of statement, and one can be so while the other is not. A flock has a direction; one bird has a flight path. “The flock is heading south” is about the flock as a whole, an average. “The flock is heading south” is almost never false. “This bird is flying due south right now” is about one, and it is almost never checked.

Say which size your statement lives at: each one, some one, or the crowd as a whole.

Two mistakes come from mixing the sizes. One treats a crowd as one big individual with a will of its own. The other treats one individual as a fair sample of its crowd.

“Every bird in the flock is heading south” and “the flock is heading south”: can one be so while the other is not? Which way round?

1e19Illegal moves

Stands on 1e5 to 1e17.

Seven moves look like uses of this step and are not. One generalises from the thing you looked at. One makes an every while a supposition about its letter is still standing. One gives a some's thing a name already in use. One carries a statement that mentions the fresh name out of the call. One swaps “every … some” to “some … every”. One goes from “not every” to “some fails”, which is the gap. And one writes a some with ⇒. What makes a case “anything” is not that it is typical; it is that the argument used nothing about it.

P(\text{Tibbles}),\;\text{ so } \forall x\, P(x)\,? @@ generalising from a special case: “Tibbles is grey, so everything is grey.” Tibbles is a name, not “anything”; Tibbles is the thing you looked at
\begin{aligned}&\text{let } a \text{ be anything; suppose } a \text{ is a cat; }\\ &\quad  \dots\; a \text{ purrs;}\\ &\text{so } \forall x\,(x \text{ purrs})\,?\end{aligned} @@ the every was made while a supposition about a was still open. Drop the supposition (prove an if) and then generalise: ∀x (x is a cat ⇒ x purrs)
\exists x\, P(x),\;\text{ so } P(\text{Ann})\,? @@ a some's thing gets a fresh name, never one already in use. “Someone took my pen; so Ann took it”
\begin{aligned}&\exists x\, P(x); \text{ call it } b;\; \dots\; Q(b);\\ &\text{so } Q(b) \text{ outside the call}\,?\end{aligned} @@ the line carried out mentions b. Only lines that do not mention the fresh name stand outside; show an example before dropping: ∃x Q(x)
\forall x\,\exists y\, R(x,y),\;\text{ so } \exists y\,\forall x\, R(x,y)\,? @@ the swap in the unlicensed direction: “everyone has a mother, so someone is everyone's mother”
\neg\,\forall x\, P(x),\;\text{ so } \exists x\,\neg P(x)\,? @@ the gap: no tech on this rung. Rung 7
\exists x\,(A(x) \Rightarrow B(x)) \text{ for “some } A \text{ is } B\text{”}\,? @@ wrong joining word: a some goes with ∧. With ⇒, the shoe shows it, by 1d's free-if technique

The first illegal move, generalising, and the gap are the ones that sound most like sense. Generalising from the case in front of you is how everyone reasons in life, and it is not a tech. It is the induction over cases of 1a6, and it never earns a line, from one case or from a thousand. And the fresh name for a some is the rule that catches the mistake in “someone took my pen; so Ann took it”. The pen was taken by someone. The someone gets a name of its own, and that name is Ann only if a line says so.

1e20Running it backwards

Stands on 1e5 to 1e8, and 1i12 (reading the foot).

Wanting “every grandparent is a parent”, open with n, then suppose n is a grandparent. The foot named both moves: its turn A says “let n be anything”, and its arrow says “suppose”. What is left is the middle, and it is found backward.

Wanting ∀x P(x), you know the opening line: “let n be anything”, and the goal becomes P(n). Wanting ∃x P(x), you need a witness: a name t with P(t) reachable. The search is a search for t. Having ∃x P(x) and wanting R, open the call: “call it b”. Then work with P(b) toward an R that does not mention b. (If it must mention b, show an example and carry the some out.) Having ∀x P(x), ask what to apply it to: the names you have, or the “anything” you opened. Most arguments on the ladder open with a “let … be anything” and a “suppose”, in that order, because most claims are an every around an if. 1i makes that the standard skeleton.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • \forall is read “for all” or “for every”, and \exists “there exists” or “for some”; by shape they are the turned A and the turned E; “such that” is shortened to s.t.

Accepted names

Which sign comes first, and so which letters it reaches, has the accepted name scope.

Who said it first

The turned E is from Giuseppe Peano’s notation; the turned A came later, from Gerhard Gentzen. The microscope is Frege’s. Accepted names: ∀ and ∃ are quantifiers, ∀ the universal quantifier and ∃ the existential quantifier.

Frege compared his new marks to a microscope set beside the eye. The eye is far more versatile, he wrote, and for most of life it is the better tool; but where sharp distinctions are needed, the microscope sees what the eye cannot.
