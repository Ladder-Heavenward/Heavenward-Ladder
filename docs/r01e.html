1e · support

Tables

The proof steps of this rung gave each joining word its techs. This step gives each a table: for every way its letters could come out, what the whole comes to. Each word becomes a machine, and any statement built from them, however tangled, has a table of its own. The tables are a check on the techs, and the check comes out exactly right. The agreement fails at precisely the gap the rung left on purpose, and that failure is the best evidence that the gap is real. The tables also give a meaning to “these statements say the same thing”, which 1f turns into a rulebook. They end with marks moved on paper by rules, the step that turned reasoning into a calculus.

List every way the letters can come out; push each row through the machines from the inside out; read the final column. Same column, same statement.

1e1Marks for the Ways a Statement Can Come Out

A statement is so or not so. The rung's techs never needed to say which, because a proof earns a line without looking at the world. A table does look, in a way. It asks: “suppose this letter came out so, and that one not so; what then of the whole?” For that it wants marks for the ways a statement can come out, and here they are:

T is short for “true” and F for “false”. In this step they mean only this: true in the row, false in the row.

  • A table is a listing of rows.
  • A row is a way the letters come out, written as a string of T and F in the order of the letters. For P and Q the rows are TT, TF, FT and FF.
  • Rows are named by their letters, never counted. Example: the row TF is the row where P is true and Q is false. With P “it rains” and Q “the street is wet”, the row TF is the rainy day with a dry street.
  • A column of the table is what a statement does, row by row.
  • A table of this kind is called a truth table.

From here on, the upright T and F under a statement in a table are these marks.

1e2The Machines

A joining word makes a statement out of others, and the new statement's row depends only on theirs. So each word is a machine: feed in T or F for each part, and it hands back T or F. ¬ works on a statement by itself; the others join P and Q. Here they are, each described in words before the table:

  • Not, ¬P: swaps T and F. T becomes F, F becomes T.
  • And, P ∧ Q: T only when both parts are T.
  • Or, P ∨ Q: T when either part is T, or both. The generous or, as 1c said. (Latin had a word for each sense, vel for this and aut for “but not both”; ∨ is usually said to come from vel.)
  • If, P ⇒ Q: F in exactly the row where P is T and Q is F, and T otherwise.
  • Exactly When, P ⇔ Q: T when P and Q match, both T or both F.
  • The Totally False, ⊥: F in every row. It is never true, so no row makes it T.

Learn each machine by the row that stands out. ∧ is T only in the row TT. ∨ is F only in the row FF. ⇒ is F only in the row TF. ⇔ is T in the rows TT and FF, where the letters agree.

1e3Why the If Machine Is Built That Way

1d1 said it: an if is broken only by its condition met and its promise failing. 1d's street-cleaning truck is the row FT: no rain, wet street, promise intact.

The rows where P is F bother everyone at the start. Why should “if P then Q” count as true when P is false? 1d already answered, by techs, before any table existed. The free-if technique is the move that fixes the rows: from ¬P, you may write P ⇒ Q for any Q; and from Q, you may write P ⇒ Q for any P. So in the rows FT and FF, where ¬P is true, the if must be true. In the rows TT and FT, where Q is true, the if must be true. That fixes T in the rows FT, FF and TT. The remaining row, TF, is fixed by carry-through: if P and P ⇒ Q were both true there, Q would be true. But in that row Q is false, so the if is false. The ⇒ table is not a convention someone chose. The techs of 1d leave it no other way to be.

1e4The Tables Agree With the Techs

Each tech of the rung, run on lines that are T in a row, gives a line that is T in that row. No table lets a tech lead from T to F. That is what it means for the tables to agree with the techs, and here is the check, tech by tech.

Be clear about what this table claims. It checks single techs, one row at a time: put statements that are T into any one tech, and what comes out is T in the same row. That is all it says right now. It is enough to check any argument in front of you, one move after another. It does not yet say anything about every argument at once. That would be a claim about arguments as things you can collect and count, and those are built at 4a. There this same table grows into a claim about every proof there is: whatever the techs reach from true statements is true.

1e5Where Tables and Techs Part Company

Now the place where tables and techs part company, which is the place the rung flagged. The ¬ table gives ¬¬P the same column as P: swap, and swap again, and you are back. Yet 1b has no tech from ¬¬P to P. The table has quietly assumed something: that P sits in a row at all, T or F, settled. For a statement you can settle by looking, that costs nothing. For a statement nobody can settle, giving P a row is an assumption, not a calculation, and the techs make no such assumption. Keep the thought that a row is an assumption; it comes back in 1e11.

1e6Rows: Every Way the Letters Can Come Out

A statement made of letters and machines is a compound statement. Its table has a row for each way of making its letters T or F. Bring in another letter and every old row comes back twice: once with the newcomer T and once with it F. The rows for P, Q and R are the rows for P and Q, each with an R of T and again with an R of F. To list them without missing any, let the letters change at different speeds. The last letter changes at every row. The letter before it changes only after the last has been through T and F. The earliest stays T until all the others have been through all their ways together. Then it turns F while they go through them again. The machine in 1e16 lists the rows for P, Q and R that way.

1e7One Row, Worked by Hand

Work out the compound statement from the inside out, the way you unpack “the sister of the mother of Ann”: find Ann's mother, then her sister. Innermost brackets before outer ones. 1e9Statements That Cannot Fail

A tautology is a statement whose final column is all T: it is true whatever its letters do. A contradiction, in this step, is a statement whose column is all F. Example: 1b's ⊥, the plainest contradiction there is. The word is the same as the everyday word for what clash finds. Everything else is true in some rows and false in others. The most important tautology in this step is carry-through, written as a statement:

You do not need every row to check it. An ⇒ can only fail where its right side is F and its left side is T. So hunt for the bad row: make Q F and see whether the left side can still be T. With Q F, P ⇒ Q is F when P is T, and P itself is F otherwise. Either way the left side is F. No bad row, so it is a tautology.

1e10The Hunt Catches a 1d Mistake

Now swap the roles in carry-through, which is 1d's affirming the consequent:

Hunt: make P F. Can the left side be T? Yes. Q T makes P ⇒ Q come out F ⇒ T, which is T. So the left side is T ∧ T, and the whole is T ⇒ F, which is F. That row, P F and Q T, is the street-cleaning truck: the street is wet, the promise is kept, it did not rain. The table finds the convicting row for you, and the machine in 1e16 will hunt it for you too.

1e11A Tautology That Needs a Caution

Another tautology needs a caution:

Every row gives T: in the row where P is T, the left disjunct is T; where P is F, the right is. The table says “tautology”, and for any statement you can settle by looking, the table is right. “The cat is on the mat or the cat is not on the mat”: you go and look. But notice what the table took for granted. It gave P a row in which P is T and a row in which P is F, as if P were settled. No tech of the rung proves P ∨ ¬P. The last move would be add an option, which needs P in hand or ¬P in hand, and for a bare letter you have neither.

Take a statement nobody can check, such as “it was raining on this very spot on a morning ten thousand years ago”. For it, the table's row is a guess about a world it cannot see.

Rung 7 lays down, as a principle, that every statement has a row, and by that principle P ∨ ¬P is so for all P. Until then the table is a tool for statements you can settle. That includes every statement about things you can list and look at, and that is where you will use it. The tables are honest; it is the rows that need a licence.

1e12Earned, and Valid

Since 1a6, a statement an argument reaches by changes that keep it true, each with its technique, is earned. Whether a statement is earned depends only on the steps and their reasons. Whether it is so is another matter. “Tibbles is a dog”, simply given, is earned, and it is not so.

The tables give another idea, a different one. Call an argument, given lines above and a foot below, valid when no row makes every given line T and the foot F. Whatever the letters do, true given lines never come with a false foot. A statement on its own is valid when its column is all T, which is the tautology of 1e9. Validity is checked on the tables, by the bad-row hunt. Earning is checked on the argument, step by step. The next step asks whether earned and valid always agree.

1e13Earned Is Valid, and Not Back

From earned to valid, they do agree. Every tech of the rung keeps T in every row: that was the check in 1e4. So take any argument in front of you and walk down it, step by step. The given lines are T in some row; each move gives a statement that is T in that row too; and so on, to the foot. That argument has no row with the given lines T and the foot F, so it is valid. You can do this for every argument you write. The word for techs like these is sound: each tech of this rung is sound. Saying “everything earned is valid” about every argument at once, including ones nobody has written, waits for 4a, where arguments become objects.

The other way round, they do not agree, yet. P ∨ ¬P, with no given lines at all, is valid: its column is all T. But no tech of the rung earns it, as 1e11 showed. So on this rung valid does not mean earned, and the gap is exactly the statements nobody can settle by looking. Rung 7 closes that gap, and much later the ladder asks how far the gap between valid and earned can be closed in general.

Keep these words apart from here on. So is about the world: things are as the statement says. Valid is about the tables: no row breaks the argument. Earned is about the argument: every step has its reason. An earned statement is always valid. A valid argument with a given line that is not so can have a foot that is not so. And a valid statement is not always one you can earn.

1e14Same Column, Same Statement

When do compound statements say the same thing? The table's answer: when no row tells them apart. A statement is a thing the ladder can name, as 0a named one A. So 0b's = can stand between statements, and the table gives it a test:

Read it “A and B are equally true or false”: in every row, both are T or both are F. They have the same column. Its accepted name: A is equivalent to B. Between statements, = says that they agree in every row. The statements may be spelled quite differently and still be one statement by the table's lights. Put another way, A ⇔ B is a tautology, since ⇔ is T exactly where its sides match. The if family shows why the mark matters. Put these side by side:

P ⇒ Q, ¬P ∨ Q and ¬Q ⇒ ¬P all read T F T T. The if, the if as an or, and 1d's turned-about if are the same statement by the table's lights. Q ⇒ P, the arrow turned about without the denials, reads T T F T. It disagrees in the rows TF and FT, so it is a different statement. The rows where it differs are exactly where 1d's illegal moves bite. 1f names all of these.

1e15A Sameness Is Not yet a Licence

A caution that 1f will take up. The table says ¬P ∨ Q = P ⇒ Q. By techs, from ¬P ∨ Q you can prove P ⇒ Q: by cases, with the free-if technique in each case. But from P ⇒ Q, no tech proves ¬P ∨ Q. The last move would be add an option, needing ¬P or Q in hand, and an if hands you neither.

So between statements, having equality means that your truth tables match. That is a fact about the tables, and the swap axiom is true of that equality, as it is of every sameness, whether or not any technique makes use of it. Using the swap is a move, and a move needs a licence. 1f's rulebook says, technique by technique, which directions the techs license for statements you cannot settle, and rung 7 is where every direction is earned.

1e16From a Column to a Statement

Going forward, a statement gives a column. Can every column be reached? Yes, by a recipe. For each row where you want T, write the “and” of what that row says: a letter where the row has T, its denial where it has F. That piece is T on that row and nowhere else. Then join all the pieces with “or”. Here it is for “P or Q but not both”, whose column is F T T F: cake but not ice cream, or ice cream but not cake.

From here on, an arrow → between pieces of a working is short for “gives” or “becomes”. The slides write, for example, “row TF → P ∧ ¬Q”. It says what the working makes of the piece on its left. It is a statement about the working, not a line of one, and it is not 1d's ⇒.

We want the column F, T, T, F, down the rows TT, TF, FT, FF. It has T in two rows, TF and FT. The row TF says P is T and Q is F, so its piece is . That piece is T in the row TF and nowhere else. The row FT gives , T there only.

1e17Marks Moved on Paper

Write the ways a statement can come out as marks, and write the joining words as signs between the marks. Then the marks can be moved about by rules, on paper, without thinking about what the letters mean. One such law: saying a thing again adds nothing, so the column of P ∧ P is the column of P. Boole drew a familiar principle from that law. Here is the principle, checked by rows instead. P ∧ ¬P is F in the row where P is T, since the right side fails. It is F in the row where P is F, since the left side fails. A contradiction, for any P.

This is the hinge to the next step. With statements written as marks, you can rewrite them by techniques, without ever drawing a table; the tables are the check that each technique is honest. A warning to carry over: a table assumes every letter is already settled. With it, ¬¬P and P have the same column for free, and 1e5 said why that is not a licence. 1f will mark which techniques need that licence and which do not.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

The two marks keep apart the two things this step compares: \vdash is the techs, \models is the tables. Whenever A \vdash B, also A \models B. The other way round is true everywhere on this rung except at the gap: \neg\neg P \models P, and not \neg\neg P \vdash P.

  • Boole wrote T and F with two other marks, which rung 3 brings in as names. A statement whose column is all T is also called a tautology, and one whose column is all F a contradiction.

Who said it first: Boole's marks

In the middle of the century before last, George Boole did something that looks small and was not: he wrote the ways a statement can come out as marks, wrote the joining words as signs between the marks, and then moved the marks about by rules, on paper, without thinking about what the letters meant. Here is how he put the ambition:

“The design of the following treatise is to investigate the fundamental laws of those operations of the mind by which reasoning is performed; to give expression to them in the symbolical language of a Calculus, and upon this foundation to establish the science of Logic and construct its method; …”

Boole, An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854), ch. I, §1

Boole's marks for the ways a statement can come out were not T and F. He borrowed the marks that this ladder has not yet built, the ones rung 3 will bring in as names for its earliest objects, and he was emphatic that in his logic they were not what rung 3 will make them, only marks: they were marks whose whole meaning was given by their tables, exactly as T and F are here. This page uses T and F for them, because the ladder does not write a mark before the line that brings it in, and the line for Boole's marks belongs to rung 3. Nothing is lost: every table in this step is one of Boole's, with the marks renamed.

His signs, in T and F: his product of P and Q is T when both are T, which is ∧. His “taking away” from the always-T mark gives the swap of T and F, which is ¬. And his sum he allowed only between pieces that are never both T, which is why he wrote “or” as “P, or else not-P and Q”: the pieces cannot overlap, and the column is ∨'s. Then the law he put at the foundation of everything:

Saying a thing again adds nothing; the column of P ∧ P is the column of P. Boole wrote this law with his product and called it the fundamental law of thought, and he drew from it what logicians had always called the principle of contradiction:

The principle that a thing cannot both have a quality and lack it, he says, is not an axiom to be laid down but a consequence of the fundamental law, which he states as: the product of a thing with itself is the thing.

Boole, An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854), ch. III

And here is that consequence, in T and F, each move a row check: P ∧ ¬P is F in the row where P is T (the right side fails) and F in the row where P is F (the left side fails). A contradiction, for any P. Boole's marks, like the table, assume every letter is already settled, so the hinge paragraph's warning holds for them too.

The machine “not both” building all the others was shown by Henry Sheffer, early in the century before this one; Charles Peirce had found it earlier and left it unpublished. Its accepted names are the Sheffer stroke, or NAND.

Accepted names

A statement earned from given lines is derived from them; one earned from no given lines, from the axioms and the techs alone, is provable. “Equivalent” is from the Latin for “of equal worth”.

Statements with the same column in every row are equivalent, or logically equivalent. Many books write this sameness of statements with a three-bar mark, A ≡ B, to keep it apart from the = between names. Others, as in most statements of the swap-the-denial rewrites (De Morgan's), use the plain =. This ladder uses the plain =.
