44j · technique

Solving cubics and quartics by hand

The formulas of 44a are short to write and long to use. This step uses them. First, look for a whole-number answer, since a cubic with one is a line times a quadratic and needs no cube root. Then shift out the square term, and read the sign of the number under the square root: it says at once whether there are one or three real answers. With one, the cube roots are of real numbers. With three, the cube roots are of numbers in the plane, and the step trades them for a cosine of a third of an angle, which a table or a calculator gives directly. The quartic is the same, one floor deeper: shift, find one answer of its cubic, and split into two quadratics. Each method is checked by putting the answers back.

Try the divisors of the last coefficient first. Shift out the square term and compute D. If the number under the square root is positive, the cubic formula gives one real answer with real cube roots. If it is negative, use cosines of thirds of an angle. A quartic shifts, solves its cubic for one m, and splits into two quadratics.

44j1Whole answers first

Whole-answer technique: if a cubic or quartic has whole coefficients and top coefficient 1, every rational answer is a whole number dividing the last coefficient.

x = \tfrac uv \text{ in lowest terms},\ \ u^n + c_{n-1}u^{n-1}v + \cdots + c_0 v^n = 0 @@ multiply by vⁿ
v \mid u^n @@ every other term has a factor v
v = \pm 1 @@ u and v share no prime (4j)
\htmlClass{new}{x = u \text{ whole, and } u \mid c_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ every term but c₀ has a factor u
  
x^3 - 7x + 6:\ \ \text{divisors of } 6:\ \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \pm6 @@ the whole-answer technique
x = 1:\ \ 1 - 7 + 6 = 0 @@ a root
x^3 - 7x + 6 = (x - 1)(x^2 + x - 6) @@ 43c’s division
\htmlClass{new}{x = 1,\ 2,\ -3} \quad \blacksquare @@ x² + x − 6 = (x − 2)(x + 3)

Here D = 9 − 343/27 = −100/27 < 0: the cubic formula would have reached 1, 2 and −3 through cube roots of numbers in the plane. Try divisors first.

44j2One real answer: the formula

One-real technique: if D > 0, the cubic y³ + py + q has one real answer, ∛(−q/2 + √D) + ∛(−q/2 − √D), with real cube roots, and two answers that are flips of each other. It is the cube-formula technique with u, v real; the others, ωu + ω²v and its flip, are not real since u ≠ v.

p = 6,\ q = -20,\ \ D = 100 + 8 = 108 @@ already shifted: no square term
u^3 = 10 + \sqrt{108} = 10 + 6\sqrt3,\ \ v^3 = 10 - 6\sqrt3 @@ −q/2 ± √D
(1 + \sqrt3)^3 = 1 + 3\sqrt3 + 9 + 3\sqrt3 = 10 + 6\sqrt3 @@ a guess, checked
u + v = (1 + \sqrt3) + (1 - \sqrt3) = 2 @@ uv = 1 − 3 = −2 = −p/3
\htmlClass{new}{x^3 + 6x - 20 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x + 10):\ \ x = 2,\ -1 \pm 3i} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide, then 8i

The guess at the cube root is a whole-answer check in disguise. When no guess comes, the cube roots are computed as decimals, and the answer is as exact as the decimals.

44j3Three real answers: thirds of an angle

Third-angle technique: if D < 0, put k = 2√(−p/3) and cos θ = (3q / 2p)·√(−3/p); then the three answers of y³ + py + q = 0 are k cos(θ/3), k cos(θ/3 − 120°) and k cos(θ/3 − 240°).

y = k c:\ \ k^3c^3 + pkc + q = 0 @@ substitute
k^2 = -\tfrac{4p}{3}:\ \ k^3c^3 + pkc = \tfrac{k^3}{4}(4c^3 - 3c) @@ pk = −(3/4)k³
4c^3 - 3c = -\tfrac{4q}{k^3} = \cos\theta,\ \ |\cos\theta| < 1 @@ D < 0 says exactly that (44a3)
\htmlClass{new}{c = \cos\varphi,\ 3\varphi = \pm\theta + 360^\circ j} \quad \blacksquare @@ cos 3φ = 4cos³φ − 3cosφ (21d); three values of φ, the rest repeat
  
x = y + 2:\ \ y^3 - 3y + 1 @@ the shift-out technique: p = 9 − 12, q = −1 + 18 − 18
k = 2,\ \ \cos\theta = \tfrac{3}{-6}\cdot 1 = -\tfrac12,\ \ \theta = 120^\circ @@ D = 1/4 − 1 < 0
y = 2\cos 40^\circ,\ 2\cos(-80^\circ),\ 2\cos(-200^\circ) \approx 1.5321,\ 0.3473,\ -1.8794 @@ θ/3 = 40°
\htmlClass{new}{x \approx 3.5321,\ 2.3473,\ 0.1206} \quad \blacksquare @@ add 2; they add to 6 and multiply to 1, as the coefficients say (44b1)

The same technique on x³ − 7x + 6 gives cos θ ≈ −0.8417, θ ≈ 147.32°, k ≈ 3.0551, and 3.0551 cos 49.107° = 2.000. A cosine table reached the whole answer 2 without any number of the plane.

44j4A quartic by hand

Quartic-by-hand technique: shift out the cube term, find one answer m of q² = 4(2m − p)(m² − r), and split (y² + m)² − (√(2m − p) y − q/(2√(2m − p)))² into two quadratics. Try the whole answers of the cubic in m first.

x = y - 1:\ \ y^4 + y^2 + 4y - 3 @@ the shift by b/4 = 1
p = 1,\ q = 4,\ r = -3:\ \ 16 = 4(2m - 1)(m^2 + 3) @@ the cubic in m
m = 1:\ \ 4 \cdot 1 \cdot 4 = 16 @@ a whole answer
(y^2 + 1)^2 = y^2 - 4y + 4 = (y - 2)^2 @@ 2m − p = 1, m² − r = 4
(y^2 - y + 3)(y^2 + y - 1) = 0 @@ a difference of squares
\htmlClass{new}{x = \tfrac{-1 \pm i\sqrt{11}}{2},\ \ \tfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt5}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 8i twice, then x = y − 1

The cubic in m factors as −4(m − 1)(2m² + m + 7), so m = 1 is its only real answer. Any answer m with 2m − p ≠ 0 works; a non-real one would give the same four roots by a longer road.

The accepted names

The whole-answer technique is the rational root theorem for monic polynomials. The third-angle technique is the trigonometric solution of the cubic. The one-real technique is Cardano’s formula in the case of one real root, and the quartic-by-hand technique is Ferrari’s method.

Who said it first

François Viète gave the solution by thirds of an angle around 1591, in a work printed in 1615. René Descartes stated the test of trying the divisors of the last coefficient in La Géométrie (1637). The worked quartic is in the shape Lodovico Ferrari solved, published by Gerolamo Cardano in 1545.

A proof that is not allowed

“x³ + 6x − 20 = 0 has u³ = 10 + 6√3 and v³ = 10 − 6√3. The calculator gives ∛(10 + 6√3) ≈ 2.732 and ∛(10 − 6√3) ≈ 0.732, so x ≈ 3.464.” Not allowed: 10 − 6√3 is negative, about −0.392, and its real cube root is −0.732. The check uv = −p/3 = −2 catches it: 2.732 × 0.732 is +2.
