5f · proof

Between any two, another; yet holes

Two techniques about the fractions that pull against each other. First, they are crowded: between any two there is a third, so no fraction has a next one, as every counting number did. The fraction between them is found by adding the two and multiplying by the reciprocal of 2, and this step gives that reciprocal its name: one half. Second, for all the crowding, they have holes: no fraction multiplied by itself gives 2. That is rung 4's no-double-square technique, about numbers, read as a statement about fractions. Write the fraction as a numerator over a denominator, clear the denominator, and no-double-square refuses what is left. Along the way the step proves the techs with order that the first technique needs, and gives the square of a number its name.

To put a fraction between two others, add them and multiply by the reciprocal of 2. To show no fraction multiplied by itself gives 2, write it as a numerator over a denominator, clear the denominator, and land on a statement about whole numbers that rung 4 has already refuted.

5f1Half

The fraction between two others will be their sum multiplied by the reciprocal of 2. That reciprocal is used so often that it gets a mark and a word.

From here on, names , the reciprocal of the rational 2. Read it “one half”, or “a half”. From here on the words half, halve and halving are legal. Half of w is , which is w/2 by 5c's line for dividing, and halving w is multiplying it by ½.

So ½ = 1/2: by 5c's reciprocal the reciprocal of 2/1 is 1/2, and the two readings of the slash agree (slashes-agree). Three small techniques get used at once. 2 · ½ = 1, by reciprocal on ℚ. 1 + 1 = 2 as rationals, since 1 + 1 = S(1) = 2 among the numbers (4f's plus-one) and 5b and 5c carry sums up unchanged (ℕ-inside-ℤ, ℤ-inside-ℚ). And w + w = 2w, for every rational w. 2w = (1 + 1)w = 1 · w + 1 · w = w + w, by spread on ℚ with turn-about for times on ℚ, and times-one on ℚ. Finally, ½ is positive, straight from 5d's line: ½ = 1/2, and 1 · 2 = 2 is a positive integer.

5f2Techniques with order

5d defined w < b as “b − w is positive” and proved the three order rules. Two techs follow from them, and they are what every argument with order is made of. First a small technique. Negative-of-a-sum technique: −(w + c) = (−w) + (−c). 5b's middle-swap used only regrouping and turning about. So it may be used for rationals, by regroup for plus on ℚ and turn-about for plus on ℚ. Then (w + c) + ((−w) + (−c)) = (w + (−w)) + (c + (−c)) = 0 + 0 = 0, by middle-swap, negative on ℚ and plus-zero on ℚ. One-negative says (−w) + (−c) is therefore the negative of w + c. ∎

Keep-order technique for plus on ℚ: if w < b, then w + c < b + c. Adding the same rational to both keeps their order.

Keep-order technique for times on ℚ: if w < b and c is positive, then wc < bc. Multiplying both by the same positive rational keeps their order. The column proves both.

a \lt b:\ \ b - a \text{ positive} @@ given; ﹀ <
(b + c) - (a + c) = (b + c) + ((-a) + (-c)) @@ ﹀ −; the negative-of-a-sum technique
\phantom{(b + c) - (a + c)} = (b + (-a)) + (c + (-c)) = b - a @@ middle-swap, by regroup for plus on ℚ and turn-about for plus on ℚ; negative on ℚ, plus-zero on ℚ; ︿ −
a + c \lt b + c @@ ︿ <: the keep-order technique for plus on ℚ
0 \lt c:\ \ c \text{ positive} @@ given, for keep-order for times on ℚ; positive is “0 < c”, 5d
bc - ac = bc + (-a)c @@ ﹀ −; the negative-out technique on ℚ (5e), turned about
\phantom{bc - ac} = (b + (-a))c = (b - a)c @@ spread on ℚ with turn-about for times on ℚ, turned about; ︿ −
(b - a)c \text{ positive},\ \text{so } ac \lt bc \quad \blacksquare @@ positive-product on ℚ; ︿ <: the keep-order technique for times on ℚ

Notice what keep-order for times on ℚ needs: c positive. With c = −1, from 1 < 2 it would give −1 < −2, which any-two-compare on ℚ refutes, since (−1) − (−2) = 1 is positive. For a negative c the order turns round, and a question below proves it.

5f3Comparing by cross-multiplying

And a way to compare two fractions without subtracting, when their denominators are positive. Compare-by-cross-multiplying technique: if q and s are positive integers, then p/q < r/s exactly when ps < qr.

r/s - p/q = (rq + s(-p))/(sq) = (rq - sp)/(sq) @@ ﹀ −, + on ℚ; negative-out on ℤ, turn-about for times on ℤ
p/q \lt r/s \;\Leftrightarrow\; (rq - sp)(sq) \text{ positive} @@ ﹀ <; 5d's line for positive
sq \text{ positive} @@ positive-product on ℤ, since q and s are
x(sq) \text{ positive} \;\Leftrightarrow\; x \text{ positive} @@ three-way on ℤ on x: 0 gives 0; −x positive gives −(x(sq)) positive (positive-product on ℤ, negative-out on ℤ), against three-way on ℤ
p/q \lt r/s \;\Leftrightarrow\; rq - sp \text{ positive} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{ps \lt qr} \quad \blacksquare @@ turn-about for times on ℤ; 5d's line for <, with integers read as rationals (5c)

So 3/7 < 1/2, since 3 · 2 = 6 < 7 = 7 · 1. Every fraction has a member with a positive denominator: if the denominator is the negative of a positive, scaling with k = −1 turns both entries round. So compare-by-cross-multiplying compares any two fractions.

5f4Between any two, another

From here on, as in 4i, a chain of order such as is short for . Read it “c is between w and b”.

Another-between technique: if w < b, then w < (w + b)/2 < b. In words: add the two and halve, and you land strictly between. Because of another-between, no two rationals are next to each other. You already do this on a ruler: between any two marks, halfway is another mark.

a \lt b @@ given
a + a \lt b + a @@ the keep-order technique for plus on ℚ, adding a
2a \lt \htmlClass{new}{a + b} @@ a + a = 2a; turn-about for plus on ℚ
(2a)\tfrac12 \lt (a + b)\tfrac12 @@ the keep-order technique for times on ℚ: ½ is positive
(2a)\tfrac12 = a(2 \cdot \tfrac12) = a \cdot 1 = a @@ turn-about for times on ℚ, regroup for times on ℚ; reciprocal on ℚ; times-one on ℚ
a \lt \htmlClass{new}{(a + b)/2} @@ the swap axiom; (a + b) · ½ is (a + b)/2, by 5c's line and the half line
a + b \lt b + b = 2b @@ the keep-order technique for plus on ℚ from a < b, adding b; b + b = 2b
(a + b)/2 \lt (2b)\tfrac12 = b @@ keep-order for times on ℚ with ½; as above
a \lt (a + b)/2 \lt b \quad \blacksquare @@ join; ︿ the chain

So between 1/3 and 1/2 sits (1/3 + 1/2)/2 = (5/6)/2 = 5/12; between 1/3 and 5/12 sits 3/8; between 1/3 and 3/8 sits 17/48; and the halving never runs out. Compare 4h's next-is-not-past technique: among the counting numbers nothing lies strictly between a number and its next one. Among the fractions there is no next one at all. Given any fraction w, any candidate b for “the next fraction after w” is refuted by another-between, which puts (w + b)/2 in between. In the same way there is no least positive fraction: half of a positive fraction is positive (positive-product on ℚ) and less than it (another-between, with 0 < w). The accepted word for this is dense, from the Latin densus, “crowded”.

5f5The square of a number

4j read the high mark ² on numbers: p² = p · p. Multiplying exists for every rational, so the same reading can be extended, and with it a word that until now has been talked around.

From here on, for a rational r, names . Read it “r squared”; r² is the square of r. Here “square” names this product of a number with itself and nothing else. The shape that shares the name is not built, and waits for geometry (rung 18).

For integers and numbers read as rationals, this agrees with 4j and with 4g's power: the product is carried up unchanged (ℕ-inside-ℤ, ℤ-inside-ℚ). Two short checks follow. (−r)² = r², by 5e's two-negatives. And r² is 0 or positive. If r = 0 it is 0 by anything-times-zero. If r is positive, r² is positive by positive-product on ℚ. If −r is positive, r² = (−r)² is positive by positive-product on ℚ again. So squares are never the negative of a positive.

5f6Yet holes

Now the second technique. No-fraction-squares-to-2 technique: for every rational r, r² ≠ 2. Write r by a member, clear the denominator, and the statement becomes one about integers; 5b's member-with-a-0 turns it into one about numbers; and there 4j's no-double-square already refuses it.

\text{assume } r^2 = 2,\ \ r \in \mathbb Q @@ aiming to refute
r = p/q @@ use the some: (p, q) a fraction pair
r^2 = (pp)/(qq) @@ ﹀ ²; ﹀ · on ℚ
(pp)/(qq) = 2/1 @@ the assumption, the swap axiom; 2 names 2/1 (5c)
(pp) \cdot 1 = (qq) \cdot 2 @@ the cross-multiplying technique: the denominator is cleared
pp = 2(qq) @@ times-one on ℤ; turn-about for times on ℤ
p = [m, 0] \lor p = [0, m];\ \ q = [n, 0] \lor q = [0, n],\ \ n \ne 0 @@ the member-with-a-0 technique, twice; n = 0 would make q = 0
pp = [mm, 0],\ \ qq = [nn, 0] @@ square-is-positive on ℤ's computation (5d), in each case; it covers m = 0 too
[mm, 0] = [2(nn), 0] @@ the swap axiom; the ℕ-inside-ℤ technique, with 2 read as [2, 0]
\htmlClass{new}{mm = 2(nn)},\ \ n \ne 0 @@ the ℕ-inside-ℤ technique: a sameness of numbers
\exists p\,\exists q\,(q \ne 0 \land p^2 = 2q^2) @@ 4j's reading of ²; show an example, with m and n
\bot @@ clash with 4j's no-double-square technique
\forall r \in \mathbb Q\;(r^2 \ne 2) \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; speak of everything

All the hard work was done on 4j, by the least-member search. This step only had to carry the question down. It went from fractions to integers, by clearing the denominator (cross-multiplying), and from integers to numbers, by the members with a 0 in them (member-with-a-0). That is the shape of many proofs about fractions: clear the denominators, and ask the whole numbers.

The oldest logic book that has come down to us uses this as its standing example of leading a supposition to an impossibility. Two particular magnitudes have no common measure: suppose they have one, and it follows that odd numbers are equal to even ones.

One more hole is already in hand, from 5c12: the way around a round thing divided by the way across, measured at about 22/7 and never proved. Now that the fractions are finished, that number, if there is one, gets its name: π, the Greek letter read “pie”. It is one of the most influential numbers there is. 22/7 and 223/71 are guesses on either side of it, and whether it is a fraction at all is settled much higher up the ladder, where what it takes is built to measure a way around rather than look at one.

5f7Crowded and holed at once

Put the two techniques side by side. The fractions with square below 2 and those with square above 2 crowd in on the place where a fraction with square 2 would be, and none is there. A number whose square is 2 is called a square root of 2, so no fraction is a square root of 2. The crowding comes in from both sides. 7/5 has square 49/25, below 2 = 50/25. 17/12 has square 289/144, above 2 = 288/144. 41/29 and 99/70 come in after them, from below and from above. Each of these misses by one: p² − 2q² is −1 or 1, and no-fraction-squares-to-2 says it is never 0. How near they come is a question of size, and sizes wait for rung 8. What can be said now, with order alone, is this. No-greatest-below technique: for every positive rational r with r² < 2, there is a rational s with r < s and s² < 2.

r = p/q,\ \ p, q \text{ positive numbers} @@ a member with positive denominator (scaling with k = −1 if needed); then the numerator is positive too (honest-positive, three-way on ℤ)
pp \lt 2(qq) @@ r² < 2/1, by compare-by-cross-multiplying
s = (2p + 2q)/(p + 2q) @@ a fraction pair: its denominator is a number other than 0
r \lt s \;\Leftrightarrow\; p(p + 2q) \lt q(2p + 2q) @@ the compare-by-cross-multiplying technique
\phantom{r \lt s} \;\Leftrightarrow\; pp + 2pq \lt 2pq + 2qq @@ spread for times over plus, turn-about for times, regroup for times, turn-about for plus
\phantom{r \lt s} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{pp \lt 2qq} @@ the keep-order technique for plus and its converse (4h), with turn-about for plus: true
s^2 \lt 2 \;\Leftrightarrow\; (2p + 2q)(2p + 2q) \lt 2((p + 2q)(p + 2q)) @@ ﹀ ²; the compare-by-cross-multiplying technique
\phantom{s^2 \lt 2} \;\Leftrightarrow\; 4pp + 8pq + 4qq \lt 2pp + 8pq + 8qq @@ spread for times over plus, turn-about for times, regroup for times, regroup for plus, turn-about for plus
\phantom{s^2 \lt 2} \;\Leftrightarrow\; 2pp \lt 4qq @@ keep-order for plus's converse, taking 8pq, then 2pp, then 4qq off both sides
\phantom{s^2 \lt 2} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{pp \lt 2qq} @@ keep-order for times with 2, and its converse by any-two-compare: true
r \lt s \text{ and } s^2 \lt 2 \quad \blacksquare @@ join

From 7/5 the step gives (14 + 10)/(7 + 10) = 24/17, whose square 576/289 is below 2 = 578/289. From 24/17 it gives 41/29. The same step from a fraction with square above 2 gives a smaller one, still above (a question asks for the proof). So the fractions below have no greatest member, the fractions above have no least, and nothing sits between the two crowds. That is what “a hole” means: a way of splitting the fractions into a lower part and an upper part with no fraction at the split.

One of the builders of the number system pointed out that the rational numbers, for all that between any two there is another, are not complete. There are endlessly many splittings of them into a lower and an upper class with no rational number at the split. The first example is the splitting by whether the square is below or above 2. Filling those gaps was the starting point of his theory of number.

5f9Running it backwards

Wanting a fraction between two: add and halve. Or cross-multiply to find a simpler one (between 3/7 and 1/2 sits 4/9, since 3 · 9 < 7 · 4 and 4 · 2 < 9 · 1). Wanting to compare: give both a positive denominator and cross-multiply (compare-by-cross-multiplying). Wanting to show no fraction does something: write it as p over q, clear the denominator, and ask the whole numbers, as no-fraction-squares-to-2 did. Wanting to show a set of fractions has no greatest member: from any member, build a larger one that is still a member, as no-greatest-below did. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Aristotle, Prior Analytics I.23; paraphrase
