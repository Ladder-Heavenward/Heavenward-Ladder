21b · proof

cos and sin as Coordinates

Walk a given distance around a circle of radius one. Start at the point due east of the centre and go anticlockwise. Where do you end up? Your position has two parts: how far east of the centre you are, and how far north. Those two numbers are called the cosine and the sine of the distance you walked. Walk once all the way round and you are back where you started, so they repeat. For a short walk, inside the first quarter, they are the two short sides of a right triangle, and that is how builders and surveyors use them.

The cosine and sine of an angle are how far across and how far up you are after walking that far around a circle of radius one. They repeat with every full turn, and in any right triangle they give the sides from the longest side and one angle.

21b1The Upper Half

From here on, for 0 ≤ θ ≤ π, cos θ is the one x with L(x) = θ, and sin θ = √(1 − cos² θ). They are read “cos theta” and “sine theta”.

(cos θ, sin θ) is the point of the circle reached by walking θ around it from (1, 0), anticlockwise. On the upper half that point is (x, √(1 − x²)) with L(x) = θ, and that is the definition.

L(1) = 0 \Rightarrow \cos 0 = 1,\ \sin 0 = 0 @@ no walk at all
L(0) = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \Rightarrow \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0,\ \sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1 @@ 21a3
L(-1) = \pi \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{\cos \pi = -1,\ \sin \pi = 0} @@ π itself

21b2The Lower Half

From here on, for π < θ < 2π, cos θ = cos(2π − θ) and sin θ = −sin(2π − θ).

Walking on past π, you trace the lower half, which is the flip of the upper half over the floor. After θ you are 2π − θ short of a whole turn, so you stand at the flip of the point at 2π − θ. The flip keeps the across and changes the sign of the up.

21b3Every Real θ

From here on, for every real θ, cos θ = cos(θ − 2πk) and sin θ = sin(θ − 2πk), where k is the whole number with 0 ≤ θ − 2πk < 2π.

Whole-turns technique: adding a whole turn changes nothing: cos(θ + 2π) = cos θ and sin(θ + 2π) = sin θ. A negative θ is a walk clockwise. Its accepted name: cos and sin are periodic, with period 2π.

\theta + 2\pi - 2\pi(k + 1) = \theta - 2\pi k @@ the same remainder in [0, 2π)

21b4Arcs You Can Find Exactly

Thirds-of-the-half technique: the points (1/2, √3/2) and (−1/2, √3/2) cut the upper half circle into three arcs of π/3 each. The flip across the line through (0, 0) and A = (1/2, √3/2) swaps (1, 0) with B = (−1/2, √3/2) and keeps A, so it swaps the first two arcs. The flip across the upright axis swaps the first and last.

u = (\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}):\ F_u(1, 0) = 2(\tfrac12)u - (1, 0) = (-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) @@ 20c3; and F_u(A) = A, since A is on the line
\text{arc}(1, 0)\text{ to }A = \text{arc } A\text{ to }B = \text{arc } B\text{ to }(-1, 0) @@ flips keep lengths of paths
3 \cdot \text{arc} = \pi \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac12,\ \sin\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}} @@ the three arcs make the half
\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \sin\tfrac{\pi}{6} = \tfrac12 \quad \blacksquare @@ the flip across y = x: arc π/3 from (0, 1) is arc π/2 − π/3 from (1, 0)

And 21a4 gave the eighth: cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2. These five, with the quarters, are the arcs whose points can be written with square roots. Most arcs cannot be: their cos and sin live in a cloud, like π.

21b5The Opening Is the Cosine

18f gave every angle an opening, e₁ · e₂, and a size, by halving the quarter turn. Now it has a second measure: the arc θ it cuts on the circle of radius 1 about its corner. 21j shows the two measure one thing. Opening-is-cosine technique: the opening of an angle of size θ is cos θ.

\text{slide the corner to } O,\ \text{turn } e_1 \text{ onto } (1, 0) @@ moves keep lengths of paths, so the arc θ is kept
e_2 = (\cos\theta,\ \pm\sin\theta) @@ the point at arc θ, above or below the floor
\htmlClass{new}{e_1 \cdot e_2 = \cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ (1, 0) · e₂, and turns keep dot products (20d.4.4)

Next-and-facing technique: in a triangle with a right angle at C, longest side c and an angle θ at A, the side next to θ is c cos θ and the side facing θ is c sin θ. Put A at O and C on the floor: B is c times the point at arc θ.

The accepted names

cos θ and sin θ are the cosine and sine of θ; the circle of radius 1 is the unit circle. In a right triangle the sides next to and facing θ are the adjacent and opposite sides, and the longest is the hypotenuse: sin = opposite/hypotenuse, cos = adjacent/hypotenuse, often remembered as SOH-CAH-TOA. The opening of 18f, now cos θ, makes its two-sides-and-the-angle technique the law of cosines (21h).

Who said it first

In 21b.4.5, the chord tables are those of Hipparchus (about 140 BC) and Ptolemy's Almagest (about 150 AD). Āryabhaṭa (499 AD) tabulated the half-chord, jyā, a word that also means “bowstring”: the arc is the bow (Latin arcus) and the chord its string (Greek chordē). Arabic writers kept the word as jība; read as jaib, “a fold”, it was translated into Latin in the twelfth century as sinus, from which comes sine.
