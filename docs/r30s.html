30s · technique

Is the Difference Real?

A coin tossed ten thousand times lands heads a little more than half the time. A team wins a little more than half its games. The gardener’s peas come out a little off three to one. Every record differs a little from what a plain claim predicts, because chance never lands exactly on its average. The question is whether this difference is the ordinary wobble of chance or a sign that the claim is wrong. This step turns the question into a number: if the claim were true, how often would chance alone throw up a difference at least this large? A small number is a surprise, and a surprise counts against the claim. Then come the two ways a test can be wrong, and how large a sample must be to catch a difference that is really there. Last, counts in a table, where the peas get their test.

Measure the difference in widths. If the claim is true, the bell says how often chance alone reaches that far: the surprise chance. A test rejects the claim when the surprise is small, and so wrongly rejects a true claim at a rate fixed in advance. Missing a real difference is the other error, and only a larger sample makes it rare. For counts in cells the squared misses over the expected counts make one test number, and with two cells it is exactly the bell test squared.

30s1The Surprise Chance, and the Bell Test

A test starts from a claim to be tried, written H₀: the coin is fair, the share is 3/4, two averages are equal. From the record it makes a test number Z, the difference from what the claim predicts, measured in widths worked out as if the claim were true. A seen value z is then judged by the surprise chance: the chance, if the claim is true, that chance alone gives a test number at least as far from 0 as z.

Bell-test technique: suppose that when the claim is true, Z heads in chance to the bell. Then the surprise chance of a seen z heads to the bell’s area outside −|z| to |z|. And the rule “reject the claim when |Z| ≥ 1.96” rejects a true claim with a chance that heads to 0.05.

\text{surprise chance of } z = P(|Z| \ge |z| \mid \text{claim true}) @@ ﹀ “surprise chance”
\to \text{the bell’s area outside } -|z| \text{ to } |z| @@ the central limit technique (30j3), with the claim’s average and width
P(\text{reject} \mid \text{claim true}) = P(|Z| \ge 1.96 \mid \text{claim true}) @@ the rule
\htmlClass{new}{\to 1 - 0.950 = 0.05} \quad \blacksquare @@ 30q3: the area inside is 0.950
  
n = 10\,000,\ \ k = 5067,\ \ \text{claim } p = 0.5 @@ 14·stat’s coin
\text{width of the count} = \sqrt{n \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5} = 50 @@ the sample-average technique (30q1), as a count, worked as if the claim were true
z = (5067 - 5000)/50 = 1.34 @@ the difference in widths
\text{surprise chance} \approx \text{the bell’s area outside } \pm 1.34 \approx 0.18 @@ the bell-test technique
\htmlClass{new}{|z| < 1.96:\ \text{the record does not reject a fair coin}} \quad \blacksquare @@ chance alone goes this far about one time in five or six

Not rejecting is not proving. The record allows a fair coin, and it allows a coin with chance 0.505 just as well. 14·stat asked whether the coin is fair. The answer the record can give is weaker: it does not show that it is unfair.

The test above is two-sided: a difference either way counts against the claim. When the story against the claim points one way only, a lake grown clearer or apples grown heavier, a one-sided test counts differences in that direction alone. It rejects when Z ≥ 1.645, since the standard bell’s area above 1.645 is 0.05 (the bell-series technique, 30m3).

30s2Small Samples, and Two Samples

For bell-shaped readings with σ unknown, the test number is T = (X̄ − μ₀)/(S/√n), and the trusted law of 30q4 replaces the bell, with its table for n − 1.

Small-sample-test technique: if every reading is bell-shaped and the claim μ = μ₀ is true, then the rule “reject when |T| ≥ t”, with t from the table for n − 1, rejects with chance exactly 0.05. It is the small-sample-cover technique turned around: |T| ≥ t exactly when μ₀ lies outside X̄ ± tS/√n, and that happens with chance 0.05 when μ₀ is the true μ.

\bar x = 5.625,\ \ s \approx 0.6714,\ \ n = 8,\ \ \text{claim } \mu_0 = 5.0 @@ the readings of 30q2, against an average of 5.0 m in earlier years
t_{\text{seen}} = 0.625/0.2374 \approx 2.63 @@ the difference in estimated widths
2.63 \ge 2.365 @@ the table for 7 degrees
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the claim } \mu = 5.0 \text{ is rejected at } 0.05} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same answer as 30q4: 5.0 lies outside 5.06 to 6.19

Two-sample technique: for independent samples of sizes n₁ and n₂ with spreads σ₁² and σ₂², the difference X̄₁ − X̄₂ has spread σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂. By the independent-spreads-add technique (30g3), with Var(−Y) = Var Y by the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2), and the sample-average technique for each. So the claim of equal averages is tested with Z = (X̄₁ − X̄₂)/√(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂), which heads to the bell when the claim is true and the samples are large.

30s3Two Kinds of Error, and Power

A test can go wrong in two ways. It can reject a claim that is true: an error of the first kind. Its chance is the level, 0.05 above, fixed in advance. It can keep a claim that is false: an error of the second kind. The chance of rejecting a false claim is the power of the test. It depends on how false the claim is, and on n.

Test-power technique: for the bell test of the claim p = p₀ from n tosses, when the true chance is p, the power is about the bell’s area above 1.96 − d plus its area below −1.96 − d, where d = (p − p₀)√n / √(p₀(1 − p₀)). Here “about” takes the width at p₀ for the width at p, which differ little when p is near p₀.

Z = \frac{\hat p - p_0}{w_0} = \frac{\hat p - p}{w_0} + d,\ \ w_0 = \sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n} @@ split the difference at the true p
\frac{\hat p - p}{w_0} \approx \text{bell} @@ the central limit technique (30j3), with w₀ for the true width
P(Z \ge 1.96) \approx \text{the bell’s area above } 1.96 - d @@ shift by d
\htmlClass{new}{\text{power} \approx \text{area above } 1.96 - d + \text{area below } -1.96 - d} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two ways to reject
  
p_0 = 0.5,\ \ p = 0.51,\ \ n = 10\,000 @@ a coin a little off
d = 0.01 \times 100/0.5 = 2 @@ the test-power technique
\text{power} \approx \text{area above } -0.04 + \text{area below } -3.96 \approx 0.516 @@ about a coin toss
\text{power } 0.8 \text{ needs } 1.96 - d = -0.84,\ \ d = 2.80 @@ the bell’s area above −0.84 is 0.80
\htmlClass{new}{n \approx (2.80 \times 0.5/0.01)^2 \approx 19\,600} \quad \blacksquare @@ d grows like √n

The two errors pull against each other. Rejecting only past 2.576 makes the first kind rarer, 1 in 100, and the second kind more common. For a fixed level, only a larger sample buys power, and the cost grows with the square of the precision wanted: half the difference takes four times the tosses.

30s4Counts in Cells, and the Peas

Counts fall into cells: round or wrinkled; round and yellow, round and green, wrinkled and yellow, wrinkled and green. A claim gives each cell an expected count, its chance times n. One test number gathers all the cells: X² = the sum, over the cells, of (seen − expected)² / expected.

Two-cell technique: with two cells, of chances p₀ and 1 − p₀, X² = Z², where Z is the bell test number of 30s1. So “reject when X² ≥ 3.841” is the bell test, since 1.96² ≈ 3.841.

\text{seen } k,\ n - k;\ \ \text{expected } np_0,\ n(1 - p_0) @@ the two cells
(n - k) - n(1 - p_0) = -(k - np_0) @@ the two misses are equal and opposite
X^2 = (k - np_0)^2\Big(\tfrac{1}{np_0} + \tfrac{1}{n(1 - p_0)}\Big) @@ take out the common square
\tfrac{1}{np_0} + \tfrac{1}{n(1 - p_0)} = \tfrac{1}{np_0(1 - p_0)} @@ add the fractions: p₀ + (1 − p₀) = 1 on top
\htmlClass{new}{X^2 = \frac{(k - np_0)^2}{np_0(1 - p_0)} = Z^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ 30s1’s Z, squared

Held in trust: when the claim is true and every expected count is large, X² over c cells has a chance law heading to one that depends only on the number of degrees, c − 1. For a two-way table with r rows and k columns, tested for independence with expected counts row total × column total / n, the degrees are (r − 1)(k − 1). Its accepted name is the χ² distribution. Its proof waits much higher up the ladder; the two-cell technique is its first case, proved. What it gives: a table. The 0.05 values are 3.841 for 1 degree, 5.991 for 2, 7.815 for 3.

5474 \text{ round},\ 1850 \text{ wrinkled};\ \text{expected } 5493,\ 1831 @@ the claim 3 : 1, with n = 7324 (5·bio)
X^2 = \tfrac{19^2}{5493} + \tfrac{19^2}{1831} \approx 0.066 + 0.197 = 0.263 @@ the misses are −19 and +19
0.263 \approx (-0.513)^2 @@ the two-cell technique: z from 30s1
315,\ 108,\ 101,\ 32;\ \ \text{rows } 423,\ 133;\ \text{columns } 416,\ 140 @@ round or wrinkled against yellow or green, 556 seeds (5·bio)
\text{expected } \tfrac{423 \cdot 416}{556} \approx 316.49,\ 106.51,\ 99.51,\ 33.49 @@ independent shape and colour (30f2): row share times column share, times n
X^2 \approx 0.116 < 3.841 @@ 1 degree: (2 − 1)(2 − 1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither count rejects its claim}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 3 : 1 stands, and shape and colour pass as independent

Why row total × column total / n: if shape and colour are independent, the chance of round and yellow is the chance of round times the chance of yellow (30f2). The record’s shares, 423/556 and 416/556, stand in for those chances. The expected counts then have the same row and column totals as the table, by the two-ways-to-the-total technique (4·stat), so only one cell is free: one degree.

The accepted names

The claim to be tried is the null hypothesis, and what a rejection points to is the alternative hypothesis. The test number is the test statistic, and the surprise chance is the p-value. The bell test is the z-test; the small-sample test is Student’s one-sample t-test; the two-sample technique gives the two-sample z-test (with S’s for σ’s and small samples, Welch’s t-test). Rejecting at 0.05 is “significant at the 5% level”, and 0.05 is the significance level, α. An error of the first kind is a type I error, or false positive; of the second kind, a type II error, or false negative, with chance β; the power is 1 − β. X² is Pearson’s chi-squared statistic; with a claimed list of chances it is the goodness-of-fit test, and with expected counts from row and column totals it is the test of independence (or of homogeneity) for a contingency table. The trusted law is the χ² distribution with c − 1 or (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom. The rule of thumb that every expected count be at least 5 is Cochran’s. Testing many claims and reporting the surprising ones is the multiple comparisons problem.

Who said it first

John Arbuthnot in 1710 used the excess of boys over girls born in London in 82 straight years to argue against pure chance, an early test. Karl Pearson introduced the chi-squared test in 1900, and Ronald Fisher corrected its degrees of freedom for tables in 1922. Fisher’s Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) spread the p-value and the 5% level. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson set tests up as decisions with two kinds of error and power in 1928 and 1933; Fisher rejected their view, and the quarrel lasted until his death in 1962. Fisher’s paper of 1936, Has Mendel’s work been rediscovered?, found Mendel’s counts closer to his ratios than chance usually allows; Allan Franklin, A. W. F. Edwards, Daniel Fairbanks, Daniel Hartl and Teddy Seidenfeld revisited the question in Ending the Mendel–Fisher Controversy (2008). Gregor Mendel published the pea counts in 1866.
