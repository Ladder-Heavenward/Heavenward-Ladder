24·logi · logistics

Why Does a Small Change in Sales Shake the Factory?

Why does a small change in sales shake the factory? A shop sells a few more bottles of juice one week. A month later the factory that makes them is running overtime, and a month after that it is laying people off. Nobody made a mistake: each link in the chain followed a sensible rule. This page follows one small rise in sales up a chain of shop, wholesaler and factory, watches it grow at every link, writes a smoothed forecast as a rate statement of rung 24 to measure the swing, and finds what calms it.

Each link keeps a stock of so many weeks of its forecast, and orders what it sold plus the change in that stock target. A rise in the forecast raises the target at once, so the order jumps by more than the sales. The next link forecasts from these orders, and jumps again. A smoothed forecast F′ = (D − F)/τ makes the jump 1/τ as steep; sharing the shop’s sales and shortening the stock target calm it too.

24logi1A Chain and a Rule

Claire’s shop sells juice and orders it from Randy’s warehouse, which orders from Mike’s factory. Each link follows the same rule: forecast next week’s demand as this week’s, keep a stock of two weeks of forecast, and order what was sold plus whatever the stock target rose by.

F = \text{this week’s demand} @@ the simplest forecast, 17·logi’s a = 1
\text{target} = 2F @@ two weeks of stock
\htmlClass{new}{\text{order} = \text{demand} + \Delta\text{target}} @@ replace what was sold, and move the stock to the target

From here on, on this page, a link’s demand is what the link below asked it for that week: for the shop, its sales; for the warehouse, the shop’s orders; for the factory, the warehouse’s orders.

24logi2A Rise of 2 at the Shop

For weeks the shop sold 10 a week, and every link ordered 10. In week 3 sales rise to 12, and stay there.

\text{week 3: } F = 12,\ \text{target } 20 \to 24 @@ the target rises by 4
\text{order} = 12 + 4 = 16 @@ the stock must be filled
\text{week 4: target stays } 24:\ \text{order } 12 @@ only replacement
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the shop’s orders: } 10,\ 10,\ 16,\ 12,\ 12} @@ a rise of 2 became a jump of 6

The jump is not waste: the shop really does want more stock now that it sells more. But the warehouse sees only the orders, and cannot tell a jump that will last from one that will not.

24logi3Up the Chain

Randy’s warehouse uses the same rule on the shop’s orders.

\text{week 3: demand } 16,\ \text{target } 20 \to 32 @@ the forecast is 16
\text{order} = 16 + 12 = 28 @@ its own rise of 12 on top
\text{week 4: demand } 12,\ \text{target } 32 \to 24:\ \text{order} = 12 - 8 = 4 @@ the stock is cut back
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the factory sees } 10,\ 28,\ 4,\ 12} @@ a rise of 2 at the shop, a swing from 28 to 4 at the factory

Chain technique: when each link orders its demand plus the change in a stock target set from its own forecast, every swing grows as it climbs the chain. Each link adds its own jump to the jump it was sent, and the far end of the chain sees the largest swing of all.

24logi4A Smoother Forecast as a Rate Statement

Forecasting from this week alone is jumpy. Let the shop smooth instead, moving its forecast toward demand at a rate that is the gap divided by a time τ: 17·logi’s nudge, made into a rate statement.

F\prime = (D - F)/\tau,\ \ F(0) = 10,\ D = 12 @@ the forecast heads for the demand
F = 12 - 2e^{-t/\tau} @@ the shift-to-rest technique (24h)
\text{order} = D + 2F\prime = 12 + \tfrac{4}{\tau} e^{-t/\tau} @@ demand plus the target’s rate
\htmlClass{new}{\text{largest order} = 12 + 4/\tau} @@ at the moment of the rise

Smoothing technique: a forecast smoothed over a time τ makes the jump in orders 1/τ as tall, and spreads it over a time near τ. With τ of four weeks the shop orders at most 13, not 16. The price is 17·logi’s: on a steady rise, a smoother forecast trails further behind.

24logi5What Calms the Chain

Say the shop shares its sales with the warehouse each week, and the warehouse sets its stock target from the shop’s sales instead of from the shop’s orders.

\text{week 3: target from sales: } 20 \to 24 @@ not 20 → 32
\text{order} = 16 + 4 = 20 @@ the shop’s 16, plus its own rise
\text{week 4: order } 12 @@ nothing to cut back
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the factory sees } 10,\ 20,\ 12} @@ not 10, 28, 4

The chain calms when every link forecasts from the true sales, keeps fewer weeks of stock, and smooths its forecast. A stock target of k weeks makes the shop’s jump 2k, so each week cut from the delivery time cuts the jump. None of the cures is free: shared data needs trust, short deliveries cost more, and smoothing lags.

24logi6Moves to Watch Out For

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

\text{the factory’s swings mean demand swings}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> the shop’s sales rose once, by 2 (24logi3)
\text{order exactly what was sold, always}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> calm upstream, but the stock never follows a lasting rise (24logi2)
\text{largest order} = 12 + 4\tau\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-a">out of arrangement</span> the jump is 4/τ: a longer τ makes it smaller (24logi4)
\text{smooth more: no cost}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> a smoother forecast trails a rise further (24logi4)
\text{set the target, then forecast}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-s">out of sequence</span> the target comes from the forecast (24logi1)

Accepted names

Swings growing up a supply chain are the bullwhip effect, or the Forrester effect. The rule of 24logi1 is an order-up-to policy, the stock target the order-up-to level, and the weeks of stock are set by the lead time. Sharing the shop’s sales is information sharing or point-of-sale data sharing. A classroom game that makes players feel the effect is the beer distribution game.

Who said it first

Jay Forrester showed the swings growing up a chain in Industrial Dynamics, 1961. Hau Lee, V. Padmanabhan and Seungjin Whang named the bullwhip effect and its causes in Management Science, 1997.
