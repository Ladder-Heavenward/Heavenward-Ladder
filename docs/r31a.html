31a · proof

Length and Angle From One Product

Rung twenty seven found that anything which adds and stretches by the rules of arrows is a space of arrows, and functions do. It did not give them lengths or angles. Lists of numbers got theirs from the dot, one reading of two arrows at once. This step asks what the dot needed, and finds three demands and nothing more: it is straight in each slot, it does not care which arrow goes first, and an arrow read against itself gives a number above zero unless the arrow is zero. Any reading that meets the three is a product, and every product hands out lengths and angles. The total of one function times another, taken over an interval, is a product. So a function has a length, two functions have an angle between them, and two of them can stand at right angles. The bound that kept a correlation between minus one and one comes back, proved once for every product at the same time, and with it the triangle. For turning numbers the dot fails its third demand, and one small change mends it.

A product is a reading of two arrows that is straight, does not care about order, and is positive on every arrow but zero. It gives every arrow a length and every two arrows an angle. The product bound and the triangle hold for all of them, so functions, lists and chances get lengths and angles from one proof.

31a1What One Product Must Do

Let V be a space of arrows over the reals (27a). From here on, a product on V is a two-slot reading ⟨f, g⟩ (27h2), a real number for each pair, with three demands. It is straight in the first slot: ⟨sf + th, g⟩ = s⟨f, g⟩ + t⟨h, g⟩. It does not care about order: ⟨f, g⟩ = ⟨g, f⟩. And it is positive: ⟨f, f⟩ > 0 for every f ≠ 0. The first two give straightness in the second slot as well. The size of f is ‖f‖ = √⟨f, f⟩, read “the size of f”; the doubled bars keep it apart from 25c’s size of one number. Two arrows are at right angles, f ⊥ g, when ⟨f, g⟩ = 0, the mark ⊥ keeping 27i2’s meaning.

27i1’s dot is a product. So is a weighted dot on lists, ⟨x, y⟩ = 2x₁y₁ + 5x₂y₂: every weight above 0 keeps the third demand. The new one is on the continuous functions on an interval [u, v]: ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ᵤᵛ f(x)g(x) dx. Areas add and stretch (15), which gives the first demand, and fg = gf gives the second. The third needs a line of proof.

f \text{ continuous on } [u, v],\ f \ne 0 @@ some input c has f(c) ≠ 0
f(c)^2 > 0 \Rightarrow f(x)^2 > \tfrac12 f(c)^2 \text{ on } [c - \delta,\ c + \delta] \cap [u, v] @@ sign kept near an input (12a3), run on f² − ½f(c)²
\textstyle\int_u^v f^2 \ge \int_{\text{that piece}} f^2 \ge \tfrac12 f(c)^2 \cdot \delta' @@ f² ≥ 0 everywhere; δ′ > 0 the piece’s length
\htmlClass{new}{\langle f, f\rangle = \textstyle\int_u^v f^2 > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the third demand

Square-sum technique: if f and g are at right angles, the square of the size of f + g is the sum of the squares of their sizes.

\|f + g\|^2 = \langle f + g,\ f + g\rangle @@ the size, squared
= \langle f, f\rangle + \langle f, g\rangle + \langle g, f\rangle + \langle g, g\rangle @@ straight in each slot
= \|f\|^2 + 2\langle f, g\rangle + \|g\|^2 @@ order does not matter
\htmlClass{new}{f \perp g \Rightarrow \|f + g\|^2 = \|f\|^2 + \|g\|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ ⟨f, g⟩ = 0
  
\langle 1, x\rangle = \textstyle\int_{-1}^{1} x\,dx = 0 @@ an odd function over an even interval: 1 ⊥ x
\|1\|^2 = 2,\ \ \|x\|^2 = \textstyle\int_{-1}^{1} x^2\,dx = \tfrac23 @@ areas (16)
\htmlClass{new}{\|1 + x\|^2 = \tfrac83 = 2 + \tfrac23} \quad \blacksquare @@ the square-sum technique, checked by hand: the integral of (1 + x)² from −1 to 1 is 8/3
Anexact, yet rigorous · counting, and the triangle

30g’s box found that independent errors stand at right angles under the co-spread, so their widths add like the sides of a right triangle. That was one product. The square-sum technique is the same triangle for every product at once, with nothing about chance in it.

Parts at right angles add their squared sizes, so their sizes combine like the sides of a right triangle, for any product.

So a measured curve that is a sum of two parts at right angles has a size that is the root of the two sizes squared and added. A signal of size 3 and a hum of size 4, at right angles over the interval, make a reading of size 5.

A tide gauge in Astoria records a steady rise and a daily swing. Over a whole number of days, could the rise and the swing stand at right angles? What would that let you do with the size of the record?

31a2Products With Turning Numbers

Rung 32 and the steps after it need functions whose values are turning numbers, such as e^(ix) (26a). The dot fails them. For the list (1, i), 1·1 + i·i = 1 − 1 = 0, and an arrow that is not zero would have size 0. The mend is to turn the second slot back with the conjugate of 25e, w̄: ⟨z, w⟩ = z₁w̄₁ + ⋯ + zₙw̄ₙ. Then ⟨z, z⟩ = |z₁|² + ⋯ + |zₙ|², the sum of 25c’s sizes squared, which is above 0 unless z = 0.

So over the turning numbers a product is straight in the first slot; swapping the slots turns it to its conjugate, ⟨g, f⟩ = ⟨f, g⟩ with a bar over it; and ⟨f, f⟩ > 0 for every f ≠ 0. On functions on [u, v] with turning values, ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) ḡ(x) dx. Over the reals the bar does nothing, so the first footing is the case with no turning.

Turned-product technique: in the second slot a product is straight up to conjugates, ⟨f, sg + th⟩ = s̄⟨f, g⟩ + t̄⟨f, h⟩, and ⟨f, f⟩ is always a real number.

\langle f, sg + th\rangle = \overline{\langle sg + th,\ f\rangle} @@ swapping turns to the conjugate
= \overline{s\langle g, f\rangle + t\langle h, f\rangle} @@ straight in the first slot
= \bar s\,\overline{\langle g, f\rangle} + \bar t\,\overline{\langle h, f\rangle} @@ the conjugate of a sum and of a product (25e)
= \bar s\langle f, g\rangle + \bar t\langle f, h\rangle @@ swap back
\htmlClass{new}{\langle f, f\rangle = \overline{\langle f, f\rangle},\ \text{so it is real}} \quad \blacksquare @@ swap f with itself: a number equal to its conjugate is real
  
\langle e^{ix}, e^{2ix}\rangle = \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} e^{ix}\,\overline{e^{2ix}}\,dx = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{-ix}\,dx @@ the conjugate of e^(iθ) is e^(−iθ) (26a)
= \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}(\cos x - i\sin x)\,dx = 0 - 0 @@ each part totals 0 over a whole turn (21)
\htmlClass{new}{e^{ix} \perp e^{2ix},\ \ \|e^{ix}\|^2 = \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dx = 2\pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ |e^(ix)|² = 1

31a3The Product Bound

18b1 bounded the dot of two arrows in the plane by their lengths, and 8·stat used the same bound to keep a correlation between −1 and 1. Both proofs moved the numbers place by place. This proof never looks inside an arrow. It uses only the three demands.

Product-bound technique: for any product, |⟨f, g⟩| ≤ ‖f‖ ‖g‖, with equality exactly when one of f and g is a multiple of the other.

g = 0:\ \ \text{both sides are } 0 @@ ⟨f, 0⟩ = 0 by straightness; and 0 is 0 times f
g \ne 0:\ \ t = \tfrac{\langle f, g\rangle}{\|g\|^2},\ \ h = f - tg @@ take away the part of f along g
\langle h, g\rangle = \langle f, g\rangle - t\langle g, g\rangle = 0 @@ straight in the first slot: h ⊥ g
\|f\|^2 = \|h\|^2 + |t|^2\|g\|^2 \ge |t|^2\|g\|^2 = \tfrac{|\langle f, g\rangle|^2}{\|g\|^2} @@ f = h + tg, the square-sum technique; ‖tg‖² = t t̄ ‖g‖² (31a2)
\htmlClass{new}{|\langle f, g\rangle| \le \|f\|\,\|g\|} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply by ‖g‖², take roots; equality exactly when ‖h‖ = 0, so h = 0 and f = tg

Three bounds of the ladder are now one. With the dot it is 18b1. With the deviations of two lists it is 8·stat’s correlation bound. With the area product it is new: (∫ᵤᵛ fg)² ≤ ∫ᵤᵛ f² · ∫ᵤᵛ g².

\langle x, x^2\rangle = \textstyle\int_0^1 x^3\,dx = \tfrac14 @@ areas (16)
\|x\|^2 = \tfrac13,\ \ \|x^2\|^2 = \textstyle\int_0^1 x^4\,dx = \tfrac15 @@ the same
\tfrac{1}{16} = 0.0625 \le \tfrac{1}{15} \approx 0.0667 @@ the product bound, squared
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\langle x, x^2\rangle}{\|x\|\,\|x^2\|} = \tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{4} \approx 0.968} \quad \blacksquare @@ close to 1: on [0, 1], x² nearly points along x

31a4The Triangle, and the Angle

Product-triangle technique: for any product, ‖f + g‖ ≤ ‖f‖ + ‖g‖; so ‖f − h‖ ≤ ‖f − g‖ + ‖g − h‖, and the size of a difference is a distance.

\|f + g\|^2 = \|f\|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}\,\langle f, g\rangle + \|g\|^2 @@ as in 31a1; ⟨f, g⟩ + ⟨g, f⟩ is twice the real part (31a2)
\le \|f\|^2 + 2\|f\|\,\|g\| + \|g\|^2 @@ the real part of a number is at most its size (25c); the product bound
= (\|f\| + \|g\|)^2 @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{\|f + g\| \le \|f\| + \|g\|} \quad \blacksquare @@ take roots; then put f − g and g − h in place of f and g

Over the reals, the product bound puts ⟨f, g⟩/(‖f‖ ‖g‖) between −1 and 1 for f, g ≠ 0. So exactly one θ in [0, π] has cos θ = ⟨f, g⟩/(‖f‖ ‖g‖), by 21m, as 27i1 found for lists. That θ is the angle between f and g. The angle between x and x² on [0, 1] is arccos(√15/4), about 14.5°. Their distance is ‖x − x²‖ = √(1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5) = √(1/30), about 0.18.

One more line is true for every product, and it will tell products apart from other sizes on 31g: ‖f + g‖² + ‖f − g‖² = 2‖f‖² + 2‖g‖². Add the two expansions and the middle terms cancel.

The accepted names

A product with the three demands is an inner product (or scalar product), and a space of arrows with one is an inner-product space (or pre-Hilbert space). The size ‖f‖ is the norm the inner product induces. Functions at right angles are orthogonal. The conjugate in the second slot makes the product Hermitian, or sesquilinear (one and a half times linear). The square-sum technique is the Pythagorean theorem for inner products. The product-bound technique is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, called in Russia the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality. The product-triangle technique is the triangle inequality, and the last line of 31a4 is the parallelogram law, here an accepted name for a proved identity.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the bound for sums in 1821. Viktor Bunyakovsky proved it for integrals in 1859, and Hermann Schwarz again, for double integrals, in 1888. David Hilbert built the space of square-summable lists between 1904 and 1910, and Erhard Schmidt gave it its geometry, with lengths, angles and right angles, in 1908. John von Neumann laid down the three demands as axioms in 1929. Pascual Jordan and von Neumann showed in 1935 that a size comes from a product exactly when the parallelogram line is true for it.

A proof that is not allowed

“The angle between x and x² is about 14.5°, so draw them in the plane at that angle and read off the triangle.” Not allowed as a proof: two functions do span a plane, and inside it the drawing is faithful, but only because the product bound has already been proved. The drawing comes after the bound, never before it.
