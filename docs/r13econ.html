13·econ · economics

What Does One More Cost?

What does one more cost? Every business that makes things asks it each day. Should the kitchen make another batch? Should the farm stand put out one more crate? The bill for the day answers a different question: what everything cost, on average. The next one can cost far less than the average, or far more. Before the first jar of salsa is made, the rent and the kitchen are already paid. Late in a long day, the next jar means overtime. The question is hard because one jar is a whole jar: costs move in steps, and rung 13's slope needs a function of a real number. Economists took the slope anyway, and called it the marginal cost. This page builds it, sets it against the average, and shows why a factory runs at a loss on its first units.

The cost of the next unit is about the slope of the cost. For a cost made of a fixed part, a steady part and a part that grows with the square, it is exactly the slope half a unit on. The average cost, the total over the count, falls while the next unit costs less than the average, and rises while it costs more. A fixed cost makes the first units lose money. One more adds to profit exactly when the revenue it brings is more than its cost.

The Words

Take a small salsa maker in Salinas. The numbers are made up for this page, but their shape is the usual one. Each day she pays $600 before the first jar: rent, the kitchen, the licence, the van. Each jar then takes about $2 of tomatoes, peppers, glass and labour. And the more jars a day, the more each extra one strains the kitchen: overtime, rush deliveries, a slower line. Say that strain adds $0.004 q² over the day.

From here on, on this page, q is the number of units made in a day, here jars. The cost C(q) is what the day costs, in dollars, as a function of q, read for every real q ≥ 0 though only whole q are made. The fixed cost F = C(0) is paid whatever q is. The average cost is A(q) = C(q)/q for q > 0 (not 12·eng's support A). The salsa maker's cost is C(q) = 600 + 2q + 0.004q².

From here on, on this page, the marginal cost at q is the slope C′(q): the rate at which cost grows per unit, at q. Its unit is dollars per jar. For the salsa maker, C′(q) = 2 + 0.008q, by polynomial-slope. The marginal cost is what one more costs, read as a rate.

The next jar, exactly

The jar that takes the day from q to q + 1 costs C(q + 1) − C(q). That is a chord of width 1, and 13k says the slope is close to it when the step is small beside the scale. For a cost of this page's kind there is more. The chord is exactly a slope, taken half a jar along.

Next-one technique: for a cost F + kq + bq², the next unit costs exactly the marginal cost half a unit along: C(q + 1) − C(q) = C′(q + ½). It differs from C′(q) by exactly b.

C(q + 1) - C(q) = k + b\big((q + 1)^2 - q^2\big) @@ F leaves in the subtraction (13g's count of h, with h = 1)
= k + b(2q + 1) @@ square-of-a-sum (5i)
C'(x) = k + 2bx @@ polynomial-slope (13d)
C'\big(q + \tfrac12\big) = k + b(2q + 1) \quad \blacksquare @@ the same: the chord's slope is the slope at its middle
\htmlClass{new}{C(q + 1) - C(q) = C'(q) + b} @@ so C′(q) misses by exactly b
  
C'(200) = 2 + 1.6 = 3.60 @@ the 201st jar costs C′(200.5) = 3.604

Early in the day, the next jar is cheap and the average is dear: the $600 is spread thin over few jars. Late in the day it is the other way about. The next jar carries all the strain, and the average still carries the cheap jars of the morning.

The next one against the average

Marginal-against-average technique: the average cost falls while the next unit costs less than the average, and rises while it costs more. Precisely: for q > 0, A′(q) = (C′(q) − A(q))/q. So A′(q) has the sign of C′(q) − A(q), and A′(q) = 0 exactly where C′(q) = A(q).

A(q) = \tfrac{C(q)}{q} @@ the average cost, q > 0
A'(q) = \tfrac{q\,C'(q) - C(q) \cdot 1}{q^2} @@ quotient for slopes (13i); the slope of q is 1
= \tfrac1q\Big(C'(q) - \tfrac{C(q)}{q}\Big) @@ split the fraction
\htmlClass{new}{A'(q) = \tfrac{C'(q) - A(q)}{q}} \quad \blacksquare @@ q > 0, so the sign is that of C′(q) − A(q)

It is the rule of any average. A student whose next mark is below her average pulls the average down. So by slope-sign-near (13k), where C′ < A the average is lower just to the right: it is falling. Where C′ > A it is rising. For the salsa maker the two meet where 2 + 0.008q = 600/q + 2 + 0.004q, that is 0.004q² = 600, at q = √150 000 ≈ 387. There the average is about $5.10, and on each side of 387 it is higher. That a level place between a fall and a rise is a bottom is held in trust here, until 14f proves it.

Why the first units lose money

Say each jar sells for $6, whatever the number. Then the revenue is R(q) = 6q, and the day's profit is P(q) = R(q) − C(q) (P is profit on this page, not 13·eng's power). At q = 0 the profit is −600: the fixed cost is lost. Each early jar earns $6 and costs about $2, so the loss shrinks, but it takes many jars to pay back $600.

P(q) = 6q - 600 - 2q - 0.004q^2 = -0.004q^2 + 4q - 600 @@ revenue less cost; collect
P(q) = 0 \iff q^2 - 1000q + 150\,000 = 0 @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by −250
q = 500 \pm \sqrt{100\,000} \approx \htmlClass{new}{183.8} \text{ or } \htmlClass{new}{816.2} @@ the quadratic formula (8i)

A fixed cost makes the first units lose money, because it must be paid before any of them is sold. Below 184 jars a day the salsa maker loses. From 184 to 816 she gains. Past 816 the overtime eats the gain, and she loses again. The quantities where profit is 0 have the accepted name the break-even points.

Real factories add a second reason. The first units of a new product take longer to make, because the workers have not yet learned how. In 1936 an engineer studying aircraft reported that each doubling of the number built cut the work per plane to about 80% of what it had been. That is a law, measured, and it works in many trades over a range of doublings.

The revenue of one more

A price is not always fixed. A farm stand near Castroville sells artichokes. To sell more in a day it must lower its price, and the lower price applies to every artichoke sold, not only the last. Say it sells q a day at p(q) = 4 − 0.01q dollars each.

From here on, on this page, the revenue is R(q) = q · p(q), and the marginal revenue is its slope R′(q): the rate at which revenue grows per unit sold, at q.

R'(q) = p(q) + q\,p'(q) @@ product for slopes (13c), with q′ = 1
= (4 - 0.01q) + q(-0.01) = 4 - 0.02q @@ polynomial-slope
R'(100) = 2,\ \ p(100) = 3 @@ the 101st artichoke sells for about $3, but adds only about $2

The gap is q · p′(q). When the price must fall to sell more, one more sale brings in less than its price, because it lowers the price of all the others. Past q = 200, R′ is below 0: one more sale loses revenue.

Profit-of-one-more technique: making one more adds to profit just past q when the marginal revenue is above the marginal cost there, and takes from it when it is below. P′(q) = R′(q) − C′(q) by slopes-add, and slope-sign-near says the rest. For the salsa maker at $6, P′(q) = 4 − 0.008q, above 0 up to 500 jars a day and below 0 past it.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

Jars are whole. Between 200 jars and 201 there is no 200.5th jar, and no cost at 200.5. Yet the slope at 200.5, taken on a cost read for every real q, is exactly what the 201st jar costs. The slope lives in a middle that no day ever reaches. It still measures the step from one whole day's work to the next.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

The day's bill is a before and after: from nothing made to q made. The marginal cost is the rate in the middle. A business that reads only its average cost can keep making jars that lose money, or stop making jars that pay.

At 500 jars a day the average jar cost $5.20 and sells for $6. Does the 501st jar make money?

Running It Backwards

Handed a marginal cost and a fixed cost, find a cost whose slope it is, and check it: 2 + 0.008q and 600 give 600 + 2q + 0.004q². Whether it is the only one is 14e's question. Handed the place where the average is level, read the marginal cost there: it equals the average. Handed a price list, read what one more brings in from its differences.

Who said it first

Antoine Augustin Cournot wrote cost as a function of the quantity and took its slope in 1838. The book of 1776 is Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, whose puzzle has the accepted name the paradox of value, or the water and diamonds paradox. The three economists of the 1870s are William Stanley Jevons (1871), Carl Menger (1871) and Léon Walras (1874); their turn has the accepted name the marginal revolution. Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) put marginal cost and marginal revenue at the centre of the subject. The engineer of 1936 is T. P. Wright, and his rule is the learning curve.

Smith observes that nothing is more useful than water, yet scarce anything can be had in exchange for it; a diamond has scarce any use, yet a great quantity of other goods may be had for it.

Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), Book I, chapter 4; paraphrase

Accepted names

The fixed cost and the variable cost; the average cost, also unit cost; marginal cost and marginal revenue; the break-even point. An average cost that falls as more is made shows economies of scale. Where the average is level, the accepted name is the efficient scale.
