Does Every Gas Follow the Same Law?

Does every gas follow the same law? Hydrogen is the lightest substance there is. A molecule of carbon dioxide weighs about twenty-two times as much. Air, steam, helium and the propane in a camp stove are as different as substances get. Yet squeeze any of them and it pushes back harder. Warm any of them in a closed tank and it pushes harder still. Through the seventeen and eighteen hundreds, experimenters measured gas after gas and kept finding the same numbers. People asked the question because that seemed too good to be true. What makes it hard is that the law has four quantities in it at once: how hard the gas pushes, how much room it fills, how hot it is, and how much of it there is. This page reads the law as one function with several inputs, in rung 9's sense. It changes one input at a time and holds the rest. It finds that pressure and volume, at one temperature, run each other backwards. And it shows why equal volumes of any two gases hold the same count of molecules: the law has no input for which gas it is.

Pressure times volume, over temperature counted from the coldest possible point, is the amount of gas times one fixed number, whatever the gas. Hold all the quantities but one, and the law becomes a function of the one that is left. At one temperature, pressure and volume multiply to a fixed number, so each is the other run backwards. The amount is a function of pressure, volume and temperature alone, so the same three give the same amount, for any gas at all.

9chem1Four Quantities

5·chem said that the pressure of a gas is how hard it pushes on its container. Chemists measure it in kilopascals, kPa, a thousand pascals by 5j's kilo. The open air at sea level pushes with about 101.3 kPa. A tyre gauge reads only the pressure above the open air's. So a tyre whose gauge reads 220 kPa holds air at about 220 + 101.3 = 321.3 kPa. That full figure is the one the law below uses. American gauges read psi, and 1 psi is about 6.895 kPa.

The volume of a gas is the room it fills: all of its container, since a gas spreads to fill whatever holds it. It is measured in litres, L. The amount is 5·chem's count of molecules in moles, mol. One mole is the mole number of molecules, about 6.022 · 10²³. The fourth quantity is temperature, and it needs a new zero.

9chem2A Zero for Temperature

In the 1840s a careful experimenter in Paris warmed air at a fixed pressure and measured its volume. From the freezing point of water to its boiling point, 0 °C to 100 °C, the air grew by 0.3665 of its volume at 0 °C, evenly in between. Hydrogen, carbon dioxide and the other gases he tried grew by nearly the same share. So at a fixed pressure the volume is a linear function of the temperature in °C (9e).

(0,\ V_0),\ \ (100,\ 1.3665\,V_0) @@ two measured pairs: temperature in °C, volume
V(t) = V_0\,(1 + 0.003665\,t) @@ two-points-one-line: step 0.3665 V₀/100, start V₀
V(t) = 0 \text{ at } t = -\tfrac{1}{0.003665} \approx \htmlClass{new}{-272.9} @@ solve for t (5g)

No gas was ever measured there. Every gas turns to liquid long before. This is a guess by extending a line past every case seen, and the line is measured, not proved. But the guess has held up: every gas points at nearly the same place, and later measurements put it at −273.15 °C. Nothing can be colder.

From here on, on this page, T names temperature in kelvins, K: T = t + 273.15, where t is the temperature in °C. A kelvin is the same size as a degree Celsius; only the zero has moved. So 20 °C is 293.15 K, and 0 K is the coldest possible point.

Counted from that zero, the volume at a fixed pressure is directly proportional to the temperature (5j): V = cT for a fixed c. Double the temperature in kelvins, and a gas at a fixed pressure fills twice the room. Doubling the figure in °C means nothing of the kind: 10 °C to 20 °C is 283.15 K to 293.15 K, a rise of only about 3.5%.

9chem3One Law, Three Inputs, One Output

Four measured regularities, each found with the other quantities held, fit together into one. It is a law in 5e's sense: a measured regularity, with a range. In that range, at pressures near the open air's and well away from turning to liquid, real gases follow it to within about one part in a hundred.

The gas law: pressure times volume is the amount of gas times a fixed number times the temperature in kelvins, whatever the gas. In marks, PV = nRT. Here R ≈ 8.314 kPa·L/(mol·K) is the gas constant, measured once and the same for every gas.

From here on, on this page, a function of several inputs is a function whose inputs are lists (4i). P(n, T, V) is P applied to the list (n, T, V), as 4i wrote h(n, y) for h((n, y)). For positive n, T and V, the law gives the function P(n, T, V) = nRT/V.

9a's definition needs no change: a function is its pairs, and here each pair is a list of three inputs and one output. Hand the law an amount, a temperature and a volume, and it hands back exactly one pressure. Solved for each letter in turn (5g), it gives three more functions of three inputs: V(n, T, P) = nRT/P, T(n, P, V) = PV/(nR), and n(P, V, T) = PV/(RT).

n = 1 \text{ mol},\ \ T = 293.15 \text{ K},\ \ P = 101.3 \text{ kPa} @@ 20 °C, open air at sea level
V = \tfrac{1 \cdot 8.314 \cdot 293.15}{101.3} \text{ L} @@ the law, solved for V; kPa, mol and K cancel (5j)
\approx \htmlClass{new}{24.06 \text{ L}} @@ 5·chem's “about 24 L”, now with its reason

9chem4Hold the Rest

An experiment changes one thing at a time. A function of several inputs does the same when all inputs but one are held.

Hold-the-rest technique: fix every input but one, and a function of several inputs becomes a function of the one that is left. Let f be a function of lists (x, y, z), and fix y₀ and z₀. Each x gives one list (x, y₀, z₀), and that list has exactly one output (9a). So the pairs (x, f(x, y₀, z₀)) have exactly one output for each x: a function. ∎

The gas law gives a different function of one input for each choice held. Each is a familiar shape from rung 9.

9chem5Pressure Runs Volume Backwards

Hold n and T, and write k for the fixed product nRT, a positive number. Then the law gives g(V) = k/V: the pressure as a function of the volume, on the positive reals. 5j called this an inverse proportion: double one and the other halves.

Squeeze-and-push technique: at a fixed amount and temperature, the function from volume to pressure, run backwards, is the function from pressure to volume, by the same law. In marks, g ∘ g = id on the positive reals, so g is a bijection and g⁻¹ = g.

g(g(V)) = \tfrac{k}{k/V} @@ composing (9b): put g(V) in for the input of g

g \circ g = \mathrm{id} @@ same pairs (9a), for every positive V
g \text{ is a bijection, and } \htmlClass{keep}{g^{-1} = g} \quad \blacksquare @@ there-and-back, read with g in both places; running-backwards (9d)

Its graph is 9e's one over x, stretched up by k. It falls as V grows, and it never meets either axis. In 1661 an experimenter in Oxford and his assistant trapped air in the short closed leg of a tube shaped like the letter J, and poured mercury into the long open leg to squeeze it. They read the room the air filled, in equal spaces marked on the tube, and the pressure, as inches of mercury. Five of their readings, with the products:

9chem6Equal Volumes Hold Equal Counts

5·chem met a claim from 1811: equal volumes of any two gases, at the same temperature and pressure, hold the same number of molecules. There it was a law, measured in the ratios gases combine in. Here it follows from the gas law in one line, because of what a function is.

Equal-counts technique: two samples of gas with the same pressure, volume and temperature hold the same amount, whatever the gases are. The amount is n(P, V, T) = PV/(RT), a function of the list (P, V, T). One list has exactly one output (9a). No input names the gas. ∎ The same amount is the same count of molecules: n times the mole number.

P = 101.3 \text{ kPa},\ \ T = 273.15 \text{ K}:\ \ \tfrac{V}{n} = \tfrac{RT}{P} \approx \htmlClass{new}{22.42} \text{ L/mol} @@ the law at 0 °C, for any gas

Anexact, yet rigorous · connections, not families

9a met a principle of the second kind of care. A thing can be defined by its family, or by what it does to whatever passes through it. 9·crypt judged a key by what it does to messages, not by where it came from. Here the families are as different as families get: the lightest element, a gas from burning, a gas you breathe.

Define a thing twice: by its family, and by what it does to whatever passes through it.

The gas law defines a gas the second way. It asks what the gas does when it is squeezed or warmed, and nothing else. On that, near the open air's pressure, every gas agrees. The family comes back where the law stops. Carbon dioxide's molecules pull on each other more than hydrogen's do. So a mole of carbon dioxide measures 22.26 L against the law's 22.42 L, and hydrogen does not fall short. The connections tell you where the family will matter.

A jug of helium and a jug of propane, at the same temperature and pressure, hold the same number of molecules. One floats a party balloon in Portland and the other sinks to the floor of a camp tent. What is different about them, and what is not?

9chem7Where the Law Stops

The gas law is a law, with a range, and it fails in a known direction past the edge. Squeeze carbon dioxide at 20 °C, and up to about 5730 kPa it acts much as the law says. At that pressure it begins to turn to liquid. Then the pressure stops rising while the volume keeps falling, and the law's function k/V no longer describes it. Cool any gas far enough and the same happens. Helium holds out longest, down to about 4.2 K. Each gas leaves the law where its own molecules start to pull on each other. That is where its family matters again.

\text{launch: } 101.3 \text{ kPa},\ \ 15\ ^\circ\text{C} = 288.15 \text{ K} @@ the weather service launches balloons from Oakland twice a day
\text{at } 30 \text{ km: } 1.2 \text{ kPa},\ \ {-46}\ ^\circ\text{C} \approx 227 \text{ K} @@ the standard atmosphere's figures
\tfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \tfrac{P_1}{P_2} \cdot \tfrac{T_2}{T_1} \approx \tfrac{101.3}{1.2} \cdot \tfrac{227}{288.15} \approx \htmlClass{new}{66} @@ n held: PV/T is fixed (the law, twice)

The gas inside would fill some 66 times the room it filled on the ground. No rubber stretches that far for long, and the balloon bursts at about that height. The instruments drop back on a parachute.

9chem9Running It Backwards

Who said it first

The J tube was Robert Boyle's, with Robert Hooke as his assistant, published in 1662 in a defence of his book on the spring of the air; the readings above are from Boyle's table. In France the same regularity is credited to Edme Mariotte (1676). Jacques Charles found around 1787 that gases at a fixed pressure grow evenly with warming, and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac measured it carefully and published it in 1802. The air's share of 0.3665 is Henri Victor Regnault's, from the 1840s. Gay-Lussac also found the whole-number ratios of combining volumes (1808). Amedeo Avogadro, then teaching at Vercelli in Piedmont, made the equal-volumes claim in 1811; John Dalton, in Manchester, refused it. Stanislao Cannizzaro's pamphlet at the Karlsruhe congress of 1860 settled the matter in Avogadro's favour. Émile Clapeyron put the regularities together into one law in 1834. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, set the scale from the coldest point in 1848.

Avogadro proposed that the number of particles in any gas is always the same for equal volumes, or always proportional to the volumes, and that the particles of a simple gas such as oxygen are themselves made of parts that can separate.

Amedeo Avogadro, Essay on a manner of determining the relative masses of the elementary molecules of bodies, Journal de physique 73 (1811); paraphrase

Accepted names

The gas law is the ideal gas law, and a gas that followed it exactly would be an ideal gas. With n and T held it is Boyle's law (or Mariotte's); with n and P held, Charles's law; with n and V held, Gay-Lussac's law. The equal-counts technique is Avogadro's law, and the mole number is Avogadro's. The kelvin scale is the absolute or thermodynamic temperature scale, and 0 K is absolute zero. A gauge's reading is gauge pressure; the full figure is absolute pressure. P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ is the combined gas law. 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C and 101.3 kPa is the molar volume at standard temperature and pressure.
