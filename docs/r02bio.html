2·bio · biology

Is a whale a fish?

Is a whale a fish? A whale lives in the sea, swims with fins and a tail, and looks like a fish. For most of history, people who fished called it one, and some old books and one old court did too. Yet a whale breathes air, feeds its young on milk, and is born the way a calf is. What makes the question hard is that it is not about the whale at all. The whale is what it is. The question is about the group called fish, and what makes something a member of it. A group can be made by a condition, as a list of features a member must have. Or a group can be made by descent, as an ancestor and everything that came from it. The two ways give different sets. This page treats each kind of living thing as a set and each kind inside a bigger kind as a part. It sifts the fish by their features. It builds a group that keeps an ancestor and every one of its descendants, and proves that such a group never lets a descendant out. Then it shows that fish, without the land animals that came from fish, is not that kind of group.

A kind of living thing is a set, and a kind inside a bigger kind is a part of it. Asking whether a whale is a fish asks whether the whales are part of the fish, and so it asks which set the word fish names. Sifted by gills and fins, the fish leave the whale out. Taken as an ancestor with everything that came from it, any group that holds both a salmon and a lungfish holds the whale as well. So fish, as the word is used, is a group that leaves some descendants out.

2bio1A kind is a set

Stands on 2a10 (subsets chain), 2a12 (in, and part of) and 2a14 (one in at the bottom, part of all the way up).

Off the San Juan Islands lives a family of orcas known as the Southern Residents. They travel in pods called J, K and L. One orca, known to researchers as J35, swims with J pod. Read each kind as the set of its animals. Then J35 is a member of J pod. J pod is part of the Southern Residents, they are part of the orcas, the orcas are part of the whales, and the whales are part of the mammals.

That is 2a14’s shape: one ∈ at the bottom, then ⊆ all the way up. The chain technique for part of carries J35 to the top: J35 is a mammal.

Now the question. “A whale is a fish” is not about J35 alone. It says that whatever whale you take, it is a fish. “A whale is a fish” is a claim about parts: every member of the whales is a member of the fish. In marks it is “the whales ⊆ the fish”. Whether that is so depends on which set the word fish names. The page tries the ways a set of living things gets made.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth, again

The Southern Resident orcas are listed as endangered, in Canada and in the United States. That is a claim about the set: about how few of them there are, and whether there will be more. It is not a claim about any one orca. J35 is not endangered in the way the Southern Residents are. She is not few.

A kind is nothing but its members, and what is true of the kind need not be true of each member.

The orcas of the Southern Residents eat mostly Chinook salmon. Is that a claim about every orca, or about the set?

2bio2A kind made by a condition

Stands on 2·bio1, 2b5 (the sift) and 2g4 (combining sifts).

The plainest way to make a kind is a sift. Take a set to sift from: let a be the animals living now. Write the features a member must have as a condition. Here is the fish of a field guide:

A Chinook salmon passes: it has a backbone, gills and fins. A lingcod passes. An orca fails, since it breathes with lungs. So no whale is in f, and the whales are not part of f.

Now take an older condition, close to the one people who fished used for centuries: lives in the water, has a backbone, and swims with fins or flippers. Call its sift f′. Every whale passes it, and so does the harbor seal. With f′, the whales are part of the fish.

Neither sift is wrong. Each is a set, fixed by its members, and 2a’s same-members axiom says f and f′ are different sets, since the orca is in one and not the other. A kind made by a condition is only as fixed as its condition: change the condition and the same word names another set.

2bio3A kind made by an ancestor

Stands on 2·bio2, 2e11 (relations), 2e12 (transitive), 2b5 (the sift) and 2b’s low letters.

Biologists make kinds another way, by descent. Let l be the set of living things that have ever lived. Let d be the relation “is descended from” on l: x d y when x is a child of y, or a child of a child of y, and so on back. 2e12 named this kind of relation transitive. If x is descended from y, and y from z, then x is descended from z.

Pick an ancestor a. The group of a and all its descendants is a sift from l:

The low a is 2b’s low letter: it makes a new name that recalls a, and claims nothing. From here on, on this page, a whole branch is a group ga, for some a in l. It holds an ancestor and every one of its descendants.

Keep-every-descendant technique: a whole branch never lets a descendant of its members out. If y ∈ ga and x d y, then x ∈ ga.

Here is why. Suppose y ∈ ga and x d y. Unpack the sift: y = a, or y d a. Take the cases. If y = a, swap a in for y, and x d a. If y d a, then x d y and y d a, and d is transitive, so x d a. In both cases x d a. Add an option: x = a or x d a. Pack the sift: x ∈ ga.

2bio4Fish, and the branch that holds them

Stands on 2·bio2, 2·bio3, 2a9 (⊆) and 2c8 (∖).

Now the facts of descent, as biologists have pieced them together, laid down as givens because the ladder cannot dig up a fossil. The Chinook salmon and the lungfish share an ancestor; call it o. Neither of the two is descended from the other. Every ancestor the two share is o, or an ancestor of o. And the land animals with a backbone, the salamanders, the birds, the mammals and so the whales, are descended from o too, through the lungfish’s side of the tree.

Take any whole branch ga that holds the salmon and the lungfish. Each of the two is a, or is descended from a. Neither is descended from the other, so neither is a, and a is an ancestor they share. So a is o or an ancestor of o, and o is in ga. An orca is descended from o. By the keep-every-descendant technique, the orca is in ga.

So every whole branch that holds the salmon and the lungfish holds the orca. The fish by gills, f, holds the salmon and the lungfish, which has gills as well as lungs. And f leaves the orca out. So f is no whole branch. It is a branch with some of its descendants taken away: the land animals that came from fish, and the whales that went back to the sea.

Every whole branch that holds a salmon and a lungfish also holds the whale; so if fish is to be a whole branch, a whale is a fish, and so are you. If fish is a sift by gills and fins, a whale is not one. The answer is in the choice of set.

The mammals, by contrast, are a whole branch: an ancestor and all its descendants, whales and seals and bats and people. So “a whale is a mammal” is true in both senses at once. That is why biologists say it with no hesitation, and say “a whale is a fish” only with a long footnote.

The basement · there if you want it

Accepted names

A whole branch is a clade, or a monophyletic group. A branch with some descendants taken away, like f, is paraphyletic. A group made by features that draws members from separate branches, like the warm-blooded animals, is polyphyletic. A drawing of descent like the picture is a cladogram or phylogenetic tree. The land animals with a backbone and their descendants are the tetrapods. The lungfish’s side of the tree is the lobe-finned fish, and the salmon’s side the ray-finned fish. Sorting living things into kinds inside kinds is taxonomy, and sorting them by descent is cladistics.

Who said it first

Aristotle set the whales and dolphins apart from the fish, since they breathe air and bear live young. Carl Linnaeus put the whales among the mammals in 1758. In New York in 1818, in Maurice v. Judd, a jury decided for a tax on fish oil that a whale is a fish. In Moby-Dick (1851), Ishmael sides with the jury: a whale is a spouting fish with a horizontal tail. Willi Hennig argued from 1950 that only whole branches should be named; the long quarrel with Ernst Mayr and others over that rule, in the 1970s and 1980s, is the battle of 2·bio.6.5. The Southern Resident orcas were listed as endangered in Canada in 2003 and in the United States in 2005.
