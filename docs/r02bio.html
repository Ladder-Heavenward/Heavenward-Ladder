Is a Whale a Fish?

Is a whale a fish? A whale lives in the sea, swims with fins and a tail, and looks like a fish. For most of history, people who fished called it one, and some old books and one old court did too. Yet a whale breathes air, feeds its young on milk, and is born the way a calf is. What makes the question hard is that it is not about the whale at all. The whale is what it is. The question is about the group called fish, and what makes something a member of it. A group can be made by a condition, as a list of features a member must have. Or a group can be made by descent, as an ancestor and everything that came from it. The two ways give different sets. This page treats each kind of living thing as a set and each kind inside a bigger kind as a part. It sifts the fish by their features. It builds a group that keeps an ancestor and every one of its descendants, and proves that such a group never lets a descendant out. Then it shows that fish, without the land animals that came from fish, is not that kind of group. Last, it puts the same question to a flower: is a petal a leaf?

A kind of living thing is a set, and a kind inside a bigger kind is a part of it. Asking whether a whale is a fish asks whether the whales are part of the fish, and so it asks which set the word fish names. Sifted by gills and fins, the fish leave the whale out. Taken as an ancestor with everything that came from it, any group that holds both a salmon and a lungfish holds the whale as well. So fish, as the word is used, is a group that leaves some descendants out. A petal is the same puzzle in a plant: sifted by looks it is no leaf, and sifted by where it grows it is one.

2bio1A Kind Is a Set

Off the San Juan Islands lives a family of orcas known as the Southern Residents. They travel in pods called J, K and L. One orca, known to researchers as J35, swims with J pod. Read each kind as the set of its animals. Then J35 is a member of J pod. J pod is part of the Southern Residents, they are part of the orcas, the orcas are part of the whales, and the whales are part of the mammals.

That is 2d14’s shape: one ∈ at the bottom, then ⊆ all the way up. The chain technique for part of carries J35 to the top: J35 is a mammal.

Now the question. “A whale is a fish” is not about J35 alone. It says that whatever whale you take, it is a fish. “A whale is a fish” is a claim about parts: every member of the whales is a member of the fish. In marks it is “the whales ⊆ the fish”. Whether that is so depends on which set the word fish names. The page tries the ways a set of living things gets made.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth, again

The Southern Resident orcas are listed as endangered, in Canada and in the United States. That is a claim about the set: about how few of them there are, and whether there will be more. It is not a claim about any one orca. J35 is not endangered in the way the Southern Residents are. She is not few.

A kind is nothing but its members, and what is true of the kind need not be true of each member.

The orcas of the Southern Residents eat mostly Chinook salmon. Is that a claim about every orca, or about the set?

2bio2A Kind Made by a Condition

The plainest way to make a kind is a sift. Take a set to sift from: let n be the animals living now. Write the features a member must have as a condition. Here is the fish of a field guide:

A Chinook salmon passes: it has a backbone, gills and fins. An orca fails, since it breathes with lungs. So no whale is in f, and the whales are not part of f.

Now take an older condition, close to the one people who fished used for centuries: lives in the water, has a backbone, and swims with fins or flippers. Call its sift f′. Every whale passes it, and so does the harbor seal. With f′, the whales are part of the fish.

Neither sift is wrong. Each is a set, fixed by its members, and 2d’s same-members axiom says f and f′ are different sets, since the orca is in one and not the other. A kind made by a condition is only as fixed as its condition: change the condition and the same word names another set.

2bio3A Kind Made by an Ancestor

Biologists make kinds another way, by descent. Let l be the set of living things that have ever lived. Let d be the relation “is descended from” on l: x d y when x is a child of y, or a child of a child of y, and so on back. 2h12 named this kind of relation transitive. If x is descended from y, and y from z, then x is descended from z.

Pick an ancestor p. The group of p and all its descendants is a sift from l:

The low p is 2e’s low letter: it makes a new name that recalls p, and claims nothing. From here on, on this page, a whole branch is a group gp, for some p in l. It holds an ancestor and every one of its descendants.

Keep-every-descendant technique: a whole branch never lets a descendant of its members out. If y ∈ gp and x d y, then x ∈ gp.

Here is why. Rows hold y ∈ gp and x d y. Unpack the sift: y = p, or y d p. Set in the cases. If y = p, swap p in for y, and x d p. If y d p, then x d y and y d p, and d is transitive, so x d p. In both cases x d p, said once. Add an option: x = p or x d p. Pack the sift: x ∈ gp.

2bio4Fish, and the Branch That Holds Them

Now the facts of descent, as biologists have pieced them together, laid down as givens because the ladder cannot dig up a fossil. The Chinook salmon and the lungfish share an ancestor; call it o. Neither of the two is descended from the other. Every ancestor the two share is o, or an ancestor of o. And the land animals with a backbone, the salamanders, the birds, the mammals and so the whales, are descended from o too, through the lungfish’s side of the tree.

Take any whole branch gp that holds the salmon and the lungfish. Each of the two is p, or is descended from p. Neither is descended from the other, so neither is p, and p is an ancestor they share. So p is o or an ancestor of o, and o is in gp. An orca is descended from o. By the keep-every-descendant technique, the orca is in gp.

So every whole branch that holds the salmon and the lungfish holds the orca. The fish by gills, f, holds the salmon and the lungfish, which has gills as well as lungs. And f leaves the orca out. So f is no whole branch. It is a branch with some of its descendants taken away: the land animals that came from fish, and the whales that went back to the sea.

Every whole branch that holds a salmon and a lungfish also holds the whale; so if fish is to be a whole branch, a whale is a fish, and so are you. If fish is a sift by gills and fins, a whale is not one. The answer is in the choice of set.

The mammals, by contrast, are a whole branch: an ancestor and all its descendants, whales and seals and bats and people. So “a whale is a mammal” is true in both senses at once. That is why biologists say it with no hesitation, and say “a whale is a fish” only with a long footnote.

2bio5Is a Petal a Leaf?

In spring the western trillium flowers on the forest floor of the Pacific Northwest. One stem holds up a ring of three broad green leaves. Above them sits one flower: three green sepals, then three white petals that turn pink as the flower ages. Is a petal a leaf? The whale’s question comes back, put to a plant. It is not about the petal. It asks which set the word leaf names.

Sift first by looks. Let o be the parts that grow out from the stems of plants. Here is leaf by looks:

The trillium’s leaves pass. Its petals fail, since they are white, so the petals are not part of ℓ. But its sepals pass: they are green and flat, and no one calls a sepal a leaf.

The Pacific dogwood tests ℓ from the other side. What look like its four to eight big white petals are bracts: leaves that grow just under a tight cluster of small flowers. Being white, they fail ℓ. So ℓ takes in a part no one calls a leaf, and leaves out a part botanists do call one.

Botanists sift by where a part grows. A shoot grows at its tip, and its leaves come off the sides of the tip, one after another. A flower is a shoot tip that stops growing. Its last side parts come in rings, close together: sepals, then petals, then stamens, then the carpels in the middle. Let s be the side parts of shoots:

Leaves, bracts, sepals and petals all pass. Which of them a side part grows into is set by three groups of genes, which botanists label A, B and C. Each group is switched on in two neighbouring rings. A alone makes a sepal. A with B makes a petal. B with C makes a stamen. C alone makes a carpel. So the petals are a sift of s:

The sift-is-part technique of 2e8 gives p ⊆ s with no further work. The leaves pass s’s condition too. Now a given, because the ladder cannot grow a flower. Botanists have switched on the genes a petal needs in the young leaves of thale cress, a small weed of the mustard family, and the leaves grew more like petals than leaves. A leaf and a petal are one kind of side part, with different switches on.

If leaf names the green, flat parts, a petal is no leaf and a sepal is one; if it names the side parts of a shoot, a petal is a leaf with its switches set for a flower. Botanists keep the word leaf for the side parts along a stem, and say that a petal is a changed leaf.

2bio7Reading a Family Tree of Kinds

Kinds are grouped by ancestry (2bio3). On a family tree of kinds, two kinds are nearer kin when the branching that joins them lies closer to the tips. A group is a whole branch only if it keeps every descendant of its first ancestor.

\text{whale and hippo branch apart most recently} @@ nearest kin
\text{then cow; shark branched off long before all three} @@ 
\text{“fish” = shark and its kind, but not the land animals descended from the same first fish} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so “fish” is not a whole branch; “whale and hippo and their first ancestor’s other descendants” is}} @@

Tree-reading technique: read kinship by how recent the branching that joins two kinds is, not by how alike they look; a group is a whole branch only if it keeps every descendant of its first ancestor. Shown in a lab: the DNA of whales, compared letter by letter with that of other hoofed animals, is nearest that of hippos, as the tree predicts, though a whale looks far more like a shark.

Accepted names

A whole branch is a clade, or a monophyletic group. A branch with some descendants taken away, like f, is paraphyletic. A group made by features that draws members from separate branches, like the warm-blooded animals, is polyphyletic. A drawing of descent like the picture is a cladogram or phylogenetic tree. The land animals with a backbone and their descendants are the tetrapods. The lungfish’s side of the tree is the lobe-finned fish, and the salmon’s side the ray-finned fish. Sorting living things into kinds inside kinds is taxonomy, and sorting them by descent is cladistics.

A side part of a shoot is a lateral organ, and the growing tip it comes from is the shoot apical meristem. The rings of a flower are whorls; the sepals together are the calyx, the petals the corolla. A leaf that grows under a flower is a bract. The A, B and C switches are floral organ identity genes, and the rule that says which mix makes which part is the ABC model. Thale cress is Arabidopsis thaliana, the plant most flower genes were found in.

Who said it first

Aristotle set the whales and dolphins apart from the fish, since they breathe air and bear live young. Carl Linnaeus put the whales among the mammals in 1758. In New York in 1818, in Maurice v. Judd, a jury decided for a tax on fish oil that a whale is a fish. In Moby-Dick (1851), Ishmael sides with the jury: a whale is a spouting fish with a horizontal tail. Willi Hennig argued from 1950 that only whole branches should be named; the long quarrel with Ernst Mayr and others over that claim, in the 1970s and 1980s, is the battle of 2·bio.7.5. The Southern Resident orcas were listed as endangered in Canada in 2003 and in the United States in 2005.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe argued in 1790, in The Metamorphosis of Plants, that the parts of a flower all come from one organ, the leaf. Enrico Coen and Elliot Meyerowitz set out the ABC model in 1991. In 2001 two teams, Takashi Honma and Koji Goto in Japan, and Soraya Pelaz and colleagues in California, switched on the flower genes in leaves of thale cress and turned the leaves into petal-like parts.
