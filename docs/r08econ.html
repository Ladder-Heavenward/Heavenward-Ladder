8·econ · economics

What does a price really say?

What does a price really say? A price is a number on a label, but the number alone says little. $6.49 is cheap for a big box and dear for a small one. A dollar buys more pesos than Canadian dollars. A wage of $16 an hour in 2024 is more money than $12 in 2019, but is it more food? People have asked this whenever prices moved fast, as they did in California after 2020. What makes it hard is that every price is a ratio, so every comparison is a ratio of ratios, and ratios do not add the way the numbers on labels seem to. This page reads a price as a ratio, carries it through chains of units, and shows why a rise and an equal fall do not cancel. Then it uses rung 8's roots to find the one steady yearly rate behind several years of uneven ones, and asks what a wage buys.

A price is dollars per unit: compare per unit. Change currency with a chain of ones. A change of some percent multiplies by one plus that percent, so changes multiply and never simply add: a rise and an equal fall leave a little less than you started with. Over several years the steady rate is the root of the total factor, of the same order as the number of years. A wage's worth is the wage divided by the price level.

A price is a ratio

A label says $4.29 for a 12 oz jar of salsa and $9.99 for a 32 oz jar. Neither number can be compared to the other as it stands. Each is a ratio of dollars to ounces, and 5i says to compare ratios by a unit rate. Divide the price by the amount, and compare the prices of one unit.

\tfrac{\$4.29}{12 \text{ oz}} = \$0.3575 \text{ per oz} @@ the small jar
\tfrac{\$9.99}{32 \text{ oz}} \approx \$0.3122 \text{ per oz} @@ the big jar, rounded as in 5g
\htmlClass{new}{0.3122 \lt 0.3575} @@ the big jar is cheaper per ounce, by about 13%

Gas is priced per gallon in California and per litre across the border. A US gallon is 3.785 litres, so $4.60 a gallon is 4.60/3.785 ≈ $1.22 a litre. The price did not change. Only its unit did.

Exchange rates are chains of ones

In 2025 a US dollar bought about 1.39 Canadian dollars, and a Canadian dollar about 13.2 Mexican pesos. Each rate is a ratio that is the same amount written two ways, so it can be written as a one: 1.39 CAD / 1 USD. 5i's chain of ones carries money through as many currencies as you like, and the units cancel as the numbers do.

250\ \text{USD} \cdot \tfrac{1.39\ \text{CAD}}{1\ \text{USD}} = 347.50\ \text{CAD} @@ dollars cancel
347.50\ \text{CAD} \cdot \tfrac{13.2\ \text{MXN}}{1\ \text{CAD}} = \htmlClass{new}{4587\ \text{MXN}} @@ Canadian dollars cancel

A booth at an airport does not use one rate. It buys at one rate and sells at another, and keeps the gap. Change $100 into Canadian dollars at 1.36 and back at 1.42, and $100 becomes $100 · 1.36/1.42 ≈ $95.77. A round trip through two rates that are not each other's reciprocals does not come home.

Up 10%, then down 10%

5g made a percent change a multiplication: up p percent multiplies by 1 + p/100. Write r = p/100 for short. So a rise of 10% multiplies by 1.1, and a fall of 10% by 0.9. Two changes in a row multiply, in either order.

Up-and-down technique: a rise by any rate and a fall by the same rate leave less than you started with. Let r be a real with 0 < r < 1.

(1 + r)(1 - r) = 1 - r^2 @@ 5h's difference of squares, on ℝ (8d)
0 \lt r^2 @@ square-never-negative (8f), and r ≠ 0, so the square is not 0
1 - r^2 \lt 1 @@ subtract from both sides (8g)
\htmlClass{new}{(1 + r)(1 - r) \lt 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two changes multiply; the order does not matter

Up 10% then down 10% leaves 1 − 0.01 = 0.99 of the start: a loss of 1%. The bigger the swing, the bigger the loss: up 50% and down 50% leaves 0.75. To undo a fall, you need a bigger rise. After a fall of 25%, the price is 0.75 of what it was, and only a rise of 1/0.75 − 1 = 1/3, about 33%, brings it back.

Inflation, and the steady rate

The Bureau of Labor Statistics keeps a price index: the cost, each month, of a fixed basket of goods city people buy, against a base period. Its yearly averages from 2019 to 2024 are below, with each year's change.

year201920202021202220232024
index255.657258.811270.970292.655304.702313.689
change1.23%4.70%8.00%4.12%2.95%

Over the five years prices rose by the factor 313.689/255.657 ≈ 1.2270. What steady yearly rate would do the same? A rate g each year for 5 years multiplies by (1 + g)⁵. So 1 + g must be a real whose 5th power is 1.2270. 8f proved that such a real exists, and that there is only one positive one. Steady-rate technique: a total factor G > 0 over n years comes from exactly one steady yearly factor, the nth root of G.

G = \tfrac{313.689}{255.657} \approx 1.2270 @@ the five years' factor
1 + g = \sqrt[5]{G} @@ roots-of-every-order gives it; one-root makes it the only positive one
\approx \htmlClass{new}{1.0418} @@ a steady 4.18% a year
\tfrac15(1.23 + 4.70 + 8.00 + 4.12 + 2.95) = 4.20 @@ the plain average of the five rates is a little higher

The steady rate is a real number, and there is no reason for it to be a fraction. It exists because the line has no holes. The plain average of the yearly rates is not the steady rate. For two years the gap is easy to see. Two rises u and v give (1 + u)(1 + v). The plain average gives (1 + (u + v)/2)². The difference is ((u − v)/2)², which is never negative, by square-never-negative. Uneven years grow less than their average rate says.

What a wage buys

California's minimum wage was $12.00 an hour in 2019 for larger employers, and $16.00 in 2024. Is $16 in 2024 more than $12 in 2019? Bring the 2019 wage to 2024 prices with the index's factor, and compare like with like.

\$12.00 \cdot 1.2270 \approx \$14.72 @@ $12 of 2019, in 2024 prices
\tfrac{16.00}{14.72} \approx 1.087 @@ the 2024 wage against it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 8.7\% \text{ more}} @@ not 33%, as 16/12 says

A wage's worth is the wage divided by the price level, not the wage alone. The wage rose by a third, and what it buys rose by under a tenth. The basket is the country's, not Salinas's. Rents on the Central Coast rose faster than the basket, so for many the gain was smaller still.

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

Price indexes were first worked out to measure what money had lost, in England in the 1700s; William Fleetwood priced a basket of goods across six centuries in 1707 to settle a question about a college fellowship. The United States index for city consumers has been published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics since 1919. The review of 8·econ.4.4 is the Boskin Commission's report to the Senate Finance Committee in December 1996, which put the index's overstatement at about 1.1 percentage points a year, from new goods, better goods and people switching to cheaper ones.

Accepted names

A price per unit is a unit price. The index is the Consumer Price Index, CPI-U for city consumers. A rise in the price level is inflation. A wage in money is nominal; divided by the price level it is real. The steady yearly rate is the compound annual growth rate, and the nth root of a product of n factors is their geometric mean. The fact that uneven years grow less than their average rate is a case of the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means. The gap between a booth's buying and selling rates is the spread. Adjusting a price for what the thing does is hedonic adjustment.
