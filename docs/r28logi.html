28·logi · logistics

Where should the warehouse go?

Where should the warehouse go? A grocery chain with stores in Missoula, Great Falls, Bozeman and Billings sends trucks from one warehouse to all four, every week, all year. Each kilometre of each trip costs fuel, a driver’s time and wear on the truck. Put the warehouse in the wrong town and the chain pays for it on every trip for decades. The question is old. In the seventeenth century it was asked for three points: find the one point whose distances to all three add up to the least. What makes it hard is that there are two inputs, east and west, north and south, and moving the warehouse toward one store moves it away from others. There is no formula for the answer in general. And the answer depends on what you add up. Add the squares of the distances and the best point is an average, found in one line. Add the distances themselves, which is what the trucks pay for, and the best point is somewhere else. This page finds both with the flat maps of rung twenty-eight: the uphill arrow of the total, the point where it is zero, and a walk downhill to reach it.

On one road, the total distance is lowest at the middle store, where no more than half the trucks lie on either side; the total of squared distances is lowest at the average. In the plane the same split holds. The total of squared distances has an uphill arrow that points straight away from the weighted average of the stores, so the average is its bottom. The total of distances has an uphill arrow made of one unit arrow from each store, weighted by its trucks: each store pulls the warehouse toward itself with the same force, however far away it is. The best point is where those pulls balance, and the total bends up, so there is only one such point. It has no formula, but a step downhill can be taken as a fresh weighted average, with each store weighted by its trucks over its distance, and each such step never raises the total. Sometimes the best point is a store itself: when that store’s trucks outweigh the pull of all the others. And near the bottom the total is so flat that a whole region does nearly as well.

28logi1One road: the middle store

Stands on 13 (slope), 14d (the slope-sign-order technique) and 14a (the candidates technique).

Start on one road. Interstate 5 runs north and south through five cities of Puget Sound and beyond. Measured north from Seattle as the crow flies, Bellingham is at 127 km, Everett at 41, Seattle at 0, Tacoma at −39 and Olympia at −63. Each gets one truck a day. Where on the line should the warehouse be?

F(p) = \textstyle\sum_i w_i\,(p - s_i)^2,\ \ F'(p) = 2\textstyle\sum_i w_i\,(p - s_i) @@ squared distances, with wᵢ trucks to the store at sᵢ
F'(p) = 0 \iff p = \tfrac{\sum w_i s_i}{\sum w_i} @@ the weighted average; F′ rises, so this is the bottom (14d)
G(p) = \textstyle\sum_i w_i\,|p - s_i|,\ \ G'(p) = (\text{trucks south of } p) - (\text{trucks north of } p) @@ away from the stores, each |p − sᵢ| has slope +1 or −1
G \text{ falls while more than half the trucks lie north, and rises while more than half lie south} @@ the slope-sign-order technique (14d)
\htmlClass{new}{G \text{ is lowest at a store with no more than half the trucks on either side of it}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the only candidates where G′ changes sign are the stores (14a)

Middle-store technique: on one road, the total distance is lowest at the middle store, where no more than half of the trucks lie on either side; the total of squared distances is lowest at the average. Here the average is 66/5 = 13.2 km north of Seattle, and the middle store is Seattle itself.

Move the northern store from Bellingham to Vancouver, 186 km north of Seattle. The average moves 12 km north, to 25.0. The middle store does not move at all. Squares let a far store pull hard; distances let it pull no harder than a near one.

28logi2Two inputs: the average point

Stands on 28·logi.1, 28a (the one-knob technique), 28g (the uphill technique) and 18a (distance).

Now the plane. Put Helena at (0, 0) and measure east and north in kilometres, as the crow flies. Missoula is at (−150, 31), Great Falls at (56, 102), Bozeman at (76, −101) and Billings at (271, −90). The chain sends trucks in proportion to each city’s people in the 2020 count: 7, 6, 5 and 12 a week.

F(p) = \textstyle\sum_i w_i\,|p - s_i|^2 = \sum_i w_i\big((x - a_i)^2 + (y - b_i)^2\big) @@ stores sᵢ = (aᵢ, bᵢ), the warehouse p = (x, y)
F_x = 2\textstyle\sum_i w_i\,(x - a_i),\ \ F_y = 2\textstyle\sum_i w_i\,(y - b_i) @@ the one-knob technique (28a)
\nabla F(p) = 2W\,(p - m),\ \ W = \textstyle\sum w_i,\ \ m = \tfrac1W \textstyle\sum w_i s_i @@ the uphill arrow (28g) points straight away from m
F(p) = W\,|p - m|^2 + F(m) @@ expand, and use Σ wᵢ(sᵢ − m) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{F \text{ is lowest at the weighted average } m \text{, and only there}} \quad \blacksquare @@ W|p − m|² is above 0 for p ≠ m

Average-point technique: the total of squared distances, weighted by trucks, is lowest at the weighted average of the stores, and it grows as W times the square of the distance from there. For the four stores, W = 30 and m = (97.3, −25.2), about 100 km east and 25 km south of Helena. Each store pulls on the uphill arrow with 2wᵢ times its distance: Missoula, the farthest, pulls hardest.

28logi3Distance itself: the pulls balance

Stands on 28·logi.2, 28c (the roads-add technique), 28g (the uphill technique), 18b (the triangle inequality in the plane) and 14g (the bends-up technique).

The trucks pay for distance, not its square. Write G(p) = Σ wᵢ|p − sᵢ|, the truck-kilometres a week, one way.

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}|p - s| = \tfrac{x - a}{|p - s|},\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}|p - s| = \tfrac{y - b}{|p - s|} @@ the one-knob technique and the chain technique of rung 13, for p ≠ s
\nabla G(p) = \textstyle\sum_i w_i\,e_i,\ \ e_i = \tfrac{p - s_i}{|p - s_i|} @@ eᵢ: the unit arrow from store i toward p
G(tp + (1 - t)q) \le t\,G(p) + (1 - t)\,G(q),\ \ 0 \le t \le 1 @@ the triangle inequality (18b), store by store: G bends up along every line
\varphi(t) = G(p + t(q - p)),\ \ \varphi'(0) = \nabla G(p)\cdot(q - p) @@ the roads-add technique (28c)
\nabla G(p) = 0 \Rightarrow \tfrac{\varphi(t) - \varphi(0)}{t} \ge \varphi'(0) = 0 @@ chord slopes from 0 of a function that bends up never fall below its slope at 0 (14g)
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla G(p) = 0 \Rightarrow G(q) \ge G(p) \text{ for every } q} \quad \blacksquare @@ t = 1

Balanced-pulls technique: each store pulls the warehouse toward itself with a force equal to its trucks, however far away it is, and a point where all the pulls add to zero is the best point. With squares, a store’s pull grows with its distance. With distance, it does not. That is why far Missoula moves the average more than it moves the best point.

At the average m = (97.3, −25.2) the pulls do not balance: ∇G(m) ≈ (−1.2, 1.7), so the walk goes the other way, east and south. The best point is about (111.8, −44.3). There the unit arrows from Missoula, Great Falls, Bozeman and Billings, weighted 7, 6, 5 and 12, add to zero.

28logi4Stepping downhill by reweighting

Stands on 28·logi.2 (the average-point technique), 28·logi.3 and 28o (gradient descent).

Where the pulls balance has no formula. So walk downhill (28o): p ↦ p − η∇G(p), for a step size η. One step size turns each step into an average.

\nabla G(p) = \textstyle\sum_i \tfrac{w_i}{d_i}\,(p - s_i),\ \ d_i = |p - s_i| @@ 28·logi.3
\eta = \Big(\textstyle\sum_i \tfrac{w_i}{d_i}\Big)^{-1}:\ \ p' = p - \eta\nabla G(p) = \tfrac{\sum (w_i/d_i)\,s_i}{\sum w_i/d_i} @@ the step lands on an average, store i weighted by wᵢ/dᵢ
H(q) = \textstyle\sum_i \tfrac{w_i}{d_i}\,|q - s_i|^2,\ \ H(p') \le H(p) = G(p) @@ the average-point technique: p′ is H’s bottom
\tfrac{d_i'^2}{d_i} \ge 2d_i' - d_i,\ \ d_i' = |p' - s_i| @@ (dᵢ′ − dᵢ)² ≥ 0, divided by dᵢ
2G(p') - G(p) \le H(p') \le G(p) @@ multiply by wᵢ and add
\htmlClass{new}{G(p') \le G(p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ gather

Reweighting technique: re-averaging the stores, each weighted by its trucks over its distance from the warehouse, is a step downhill that never raises the total distance. A near store gets more weight, a far store less: the opposite of squares.

p_0 = (97.3,\ -25.2),\ \ G = 5196.1 @@ the average point
p_1 = (103.3,\ -33.9),\ \ G = 5177.9 @@ one reweighting
p_2 = (106.9,\ -38.9),\ \ G = 5171.8 @@ two
p_4 = (110.2,\ -43.0),\ \ G = 5169.4 @@ four
\htmlClass{new}{p_{15} = (111.8,\ -44.3),\ \ G = 5169.2} @@ settled to the first decimal

The best point is in ranch country about 60 km north of Livingston, far from any interstate. Its total is 5169 truck-kilometres a week, one way, against 5400 at Bozeman and 5829 at Helena.

28logi5When a store is the answer

Stands on 28·logi.3, 28c (the roads-add technique) and 18b (the triangle inequality in the plane).

At a store the distance to that store has a sharp point, and no uphill arrow. Test the slopes in each direction instead.

\varphi(t) = G(s_k + tu),\ \ |u| = 1,\ \ t \ge 0 @@ walk out of store k in the direction u
\varphi(t) = w_k\,t + \textstyle\sum_{i \ne k} w_i\,|s_k + tu - s_i| @@ store k’s own term is wₖt
\text{slope at } t = 0^+:\ \ w_k + R\cdot u,\ \ R = \textstyle\sum_{i \ne k} w_i\,\tfrac{s_k - s_i}{|s_k - s_i|} @@ the other terms have uphill arrow R at sₖ (28·logi.3, 28c)
w_k + R\cdot u \ge w_k - |R| @@ the dot bound (18b)
\htmlClass{new}{|R| \le w_k \Rightarrow G \text{ is lowest at store } k} \quad \blacksquare @@ every direction climbs at first, and G bends up along every line (28·logi.3)

Heavy-store technique: a store is itself the best place for the warehouse exactly when its own trucks are at least the size of the combined pull of all the other stores on it. When |R| > wₖ, walking from the store along −R lowers the total. In Montana the others pull on Billings with size 17.2, against its 12 trucks: not Billings. With 18 trucks a week or more, Billings would be the answer. Along Interstate 5, the four other cities pull on Seattle with size 0.64, less than its one truck: Seattle is the best point in the plane too.

28logi6A flat bottom

Stands on 28·logi.3, 28·logi.4 and 28b (the best flat map).

At the best point the uphill arrow is zero, so the best flat map of G there is the zero map: walk a little way and G changes by far less than the distance walked. That is good news and bad news.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, at the bottom

The trucks a week are a guess from head counts. Roads are not straight lines. Next year Bozeman may grow faster than Billings. Each of these moves the best point by kilometres. But near the bottom G barely changes: every point within about 30 km of the best point has a total within 1% of the best. The average point, 24 km away, costs 0.5% more. Bozeman, 67 km away, costs 4.5% more.

Near a flat bottom, report the region within a stated margin of the best, not one point, and choose inside it by what the total left out.

So the point the reweighting walk settles on is less an answer than the centre of a region of near-answers. Inside that region, the choice belongs to things the total left out: an interstate, a rail line, land for sale, workers to hire.

Livingston sits on Interstate 90, about 60 km south of the best point. Is it inside the 1% region, and what would you need to know before you build there?

Flat bottoms are why the question of where to build is easier than it looks, and why it is argued over all the same.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The point that makes the total distance least is the geometric median, and for three points the Fermat point; with weights, finding it is the Weber problem. The middle store is the weighted median. The reweighting walk is the Weiszfeld algorithm. A function whose chords lie on or above it is convex. The uphill arrow is the gradient. The pulls are the forces in a Varignon frame: strings through holes in a table at the stores, tied at one knot, each pulled down by weights in proportion to its trucks; the knot comes to rest at the best point.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat posed the three-point problem in the early seventeenth century, and Evangelista Torricelli solved it by geometry. Thomas Simpson added weights in 1750. Alfred Weber made it a question of where industry should go in 1909. Endre Weiszfeld published the reweighting walk in 1937, and Harold Kuhn showed in 1973 when it settles. The heavy-store test is in Kuhn’s work too. The questions of clustering in 28·logi.8.5 belong to economic geography, from Alfred Marshall in 1890 to Paul Krugman in 1991.
