26d · proof

A Complex Slope

A slope on the line is what the rise over the run settles at as the run shrinks. In the plane the run is a complex number, and it can shrink toward zero from any direction at all. A complex slope asks for one answer from every direction at once. That is a great deal to ask. The powers, the polynomials, one over z and the growth function all give it. The flip, which looks as plain as any function, gives it nowhere. The rest of the rung shows how much follows from asking so much.

A function has a complex slope at a point when its rise over run settles at one number, whichever way the run shrinks. Powers, polynomials, one over z and the growth function have one. The flip has none.

26d1A Slope From Every Direction

From here on, f has a complex slope f′(z₀) at z₀ when f is defined on a disc round z₀ and (f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀))/h heads to f′(z₀) as the complex number h heads to 0: for every ε > 0 some δ > 0 makes |(f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀))/h − f′(z₀)| < ε whenever 0 < |h| < δ. Accepted name: f is complex differentiable at z₀; on an open set, holomorphic.

Plane-slope technique: sums, products, quotients and chains of functions with complex slopes have complex slopes, by rung 13’s rules; so (zⁿ)′ = nzⁿ⁻¹, every polynomial has a complex slope everywhere, and (1/z)′ = −1/z² for z ≠ 0.

\text{13's proofs of the sum, product, quotient and chain rules} @@ they use only the field techniques and limits by size
|\cdot| \text{ in } \mathbb{C} \text{ multiplies and obeys the triangle} @@ 25c1, 25c2: every step of those proofs holds in ℂ
\frac{(z+h)^n - z^n}{h} = nz^{n-1} + h\,(\ldots) @@ share out (4k); the rest has a factor h
\frac{1/(z+h) - 1/z}{h} = \frac{-1}{z(z+h)} \to -\frac{1}{z^2} @@ the quotient, with |z + h| ≥ |z|/2 for small h (25c2)
\htmlClass{new}{(z^n)' = nz^{n-1},\ \ (1/z)' = -1/z^2} \quad \blacksquare

26d2The Growth Function Has a Slope

Plane-exp-slope technique: exp has a complex slope at every z, and exp′(z) = exp z.

\frac{e^{z+h} - e^z}{h} = e^z\,\frac{e^h - 1}{h} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b1)
\frac{e^h - 1}{h} - 1 = \sum_{k \ge 2} \frac{h^{k-1}}{k!} @@ the sum for e^h (26a2), less its first two pieces
\Big|\sum_{k\ge2} \frac{h^{k-1}}{k!}\Big| \le |h|\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{|h|^{k-2}}{k!} \le |h|e^{|h|} @@ the triangle (25c2), endlessly: sums that settle in size
\htmlClass{new}{\exp'(z) = e^z} \quad \blacksquare @@ |h|e^(|h|) heads to 0 with h

So e^(iθ), as a function of the real θ, has slope ie^(iθ) by the chain rule: its velocity is always a quarter turn from its position. That is moving round a circle, at speed 1.

26d3The Flip Has No Slope

Two-ways technique: if f has a complex slope at z₀, the rise over run along the floor (h = t) and along the upright (h = it) head to the same number; so the slope along the upright is i times the slope along the floor. The flip fails it everywhere.

h = t \in \mathbb{R},\ t \to 0:\ \ \frac{f(z_0+t) - f(z_0)}{t} \to f'(z_0) @@ a direction is a special case of every direction
h = it,\ t \to 0:\ \ \frac{f(z_0+it) - f(z_0)}{it} \to f'(z_0) @@ the same, along the upright
f(z) = \overline{z}:\ \ \frac{\overline{t}}{t} = 1,\ \ \frac{\overline{it}}{it} = \frac{-it}{it} = -1 @@ the flip of a real is itself; the flip of it is −it
\htmlClass{new}{\overline{z} \text{ has no complex slope anywhere}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 ≠ −1

The flip is as smooth as can be in each direction, and still fails. A complex slope is not a slope in two directions; it is one number that serves every direction at once. Rung 28 builds slopes in many directions for any function. Here, the demand for a single answer will turn out to fix almost everything about the function.

The accepted names

A function with a complex slope on an open set is holomorphic (or analytic, or complex differentiable). The two-ways technique, written for f = u + iv, is the pair of Cauchy–Riemann equations u_x = v_y and u_y = −v_x.

Who said it first

Jean le Rond d’Alembert met the two-ways equations in fluid flow in 1752, and Leonhard Euler used them in 1777. Augustin-Louis Cauchy made the complex slope the centre of a theory from 1814 on, and Bernhard Riemann took it as the definition in his 1851 thesis.
