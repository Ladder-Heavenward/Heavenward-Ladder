Why Did Chips Stop Getting Faster?

Why did chips stop getting faster? For thirty years every new chip ran a faster clock than the last, and from about 2005 the clock stopped near three or four billion ticks a second, while the count of switches on a chip kept doubling. The reason is heat. Each time a switch of this rung flips, a small store of charge on its gate is filled from the supply and later emptied to the ground, and the energy of that charge ends up as warmth. A chip flips billions of switches billions of times a second, so its power is the count of flips times the energy of each. For a long time shrinking every length and the voltage together kept the heat on each square millimetre the same, so each new chip could be faster for free. Then the voltage could not shrink any further, because a switch that is off must still hold back its leak, and the leak of a silicon switch is set by the warmth of the room. With the voltage stuck, a faster clock means a hotter chip. So the extra switches went into many slower cores in place of one fast one, and the question became how much of a job can be split between them.

Filling a gate from the supply spends C V², half kept and half warmed, and emptying it warms the rest: power α C V² f. Shrinking every length and the voltage by the same factor keeps the heat per area fixed. A switch that is off leaks tenfold more for each 60 mV less of voltage, so the voltage stopped near 1 V. Then many slower cores at lower voltage beat one fast one, as far as the job can be split.

39eng1The Heat of One Flip

The gate of a switch, with the wire that leads to it, is a store of charge C: a charge q on it sets a voltage q/C (7·phys’s energy per charge). The pair inverter of 39e fills the next gate from the supply through its upper switch and empties it to the ground through its lower one.

Switch-heat technique: filling a store C from 0 to V through any switch costs the supply C V²; the store keeps ½ C V² and the switch warms by ½ C V²; emptying it warms the lower switch by the ½ C V² kept. A chip of N switches, a share α of them flipping on each of f ticks a second, turns α N C V² f into heat each second.

\text{supply: } Q V = C V \cdot V = C V^2 @@ every bit of charge leaves the supply at V (7·phys)
\text{store: } \textstyle\int_0^{CV} \tfrac{q}{C}\,dq = \tfrac{(CV)^2}{2C} = \tfrac12 C V^2 @@ the bit of charge dq goes up by q/C, the store’s voltage so far: the area under a line
\text{upper switch: } C V^2 - \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 CV^2;\ \text{lower: } \tfrac12 CV^2 @@ energy is never lost (7·phys); emptying warms what was kept
C = 10^{-16}\text{ F},\ V = 0.7\text{ V}:\ \ C V^2 \approx 4.9 \cdot 10^{-17}\text{ J} @@ a switch of the 2020s
\htmlClass{new}{N = 10^{10},\ \alpha = 0.1,\ f = 3\text{ GHz}:\ \ 147\text{ W}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0.1 × 10¹⁰ × 4.9·10⁻¹⁷ × 3·10⁹

The half lost in the upper switch does not depend on its resistance: a smaller resistance passes a bigger current for a shorter time, and the heat comes out the same. 147 W from a chip the size of a thumbnail is more heat per area than a stove’s ring, and the fan and the block of copper above it are there to carry it off.

39eng2Shrinking Everything at Once

Make every length of a switch smaller by a factor s, and the voltage too. The store C is plate area over gap, so it shrinks by s. The current that fills it shrinks by s as well, so the time to fill it, C V over the current, shrinks by s: the clock can run s times faster. And s² switches fit where one did.

Even-shrink technique: shrinking every length and the voltage by the same factor s keeps a chip’s heat per area the same, while the clock runs s times faster and s² times more switches fit in the area.

\tfrac{C}{s} \cdot \big(\tfrac{V}{s}\big)^2 \cdot s f = \tfrac{C V^2 f}{s^2} @@ the heat of one switch, by the switch-heat technique
s^2 \text{ switches per area} \times \tfrac{C V^2 f}{s^2} = C V^2 f @@ the heat per area
s = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41:\ \ 2\times \text{ the switches},\ 1.41\times \text{ the clock} @@ one step of the industry, about every two years
\htmlClass{new}{\text{heat per area unchanged}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the speed came free

That is the doubling of the count of switches every two years, kept up from the 1970s, and the rising clock that rode on it, from about a million ticks a second in 1975 to about three billion in 2004.

39eng3The Leak Floor

A switch that is off is a junction held below its opening voltage. 26·then1’s sixty-millivolt technique says that its current falls tenfold for each 60 mV below the opening, at room warmth, and 39·phys5 gave the reason: the current over a barrier goes as e^(−qV/kT), and kT/q × ln 10 ≈ 59.6 mV at 300 K. That number has no length in it.

Leak-floor technique: at 300 K a switch can be shut by a voltage V_t below its opening only to a leak 10^(−V_t/60 mV) of its on current, whatever its size; so the voltage cannot shrink below a floor set by the leak a chip can bear.

\tfrac{I_\text{off}}{I_\text{on}} = 10^{-V_t/(59.6\text{ mV})} @@ the sixty-millivolt technique (26·then1)
\tfrac{I_\text{off}}{I_\text{on}} = 10^{-6} \Rightarrow V_t \ge 6 \times 59.6 \approx 0.36\text{ V} @@ a billion switches off, a thousand on: the leak of the off ones matches the on
V \approx V_t + \text{ margin} \approx 0.7 \text{ to } 1\text{ V} @@ the on switch must be well on, to fill its store fast
\text{V fixed, shrink by } s:\ \ s^2 \cdot \tfrac{C}{s} \cdot V^2 \cdot s f = s^2 \cdot C V^2 f @@ the even-shrink count, with V not shrinking
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a faster clock doubles the heat per area each step}} \quad \blacksquare @@ s² = 2

Even with the clock held, the heat per area rises by s each step. Around 2004 chips reached about 100 W per square centimetre, the most that air and copper could carry off, and the clock stopped near 3 to 4 GHz. Since then the voltage has crept down only from about 1.2 V to 0.7 V, and parts of every chip sit switched off at any moment so as not to melt it.

The shrinking went on by changing the shape of the switch. On 30 June 2022 mass production began of chips, sold as 3 nanometres, whose switches have the gate wrapped all the way round the channel it shuts.

39eng4Many Slow Cores

A core is one processor of 39j. At the top of its range, a core runs a clock roughly in proportion to its voltage: a lower voltage fills each store more slowly. So lowering both together cuts the heat of a core as the cube.

Cores-for-clock technique: with the clock in proportion to the voltage, N cores, each at clock f/N^(1/3) and voltage V/N^(1/3), give off the heat of one core at f and V, and do N^(2/3) times its work, if the work can be split; with a share p split, the speedup is S(N)/N^(1/3), where S is 17·comp’s.

N \cdot C \big(\tfrac{V}{N^{1/3}}\big)^2 \tfrac{f}{N^{1/3}} = C V^2 f @@ the switch-heat technique, N cores
N \cdot \tfrac{f}{N^{1/3}} = N^{2/3} f @@ ticks a second, all cores together
N = 8:\ \ \text{clock } \tfrac f2,\ \ 4\times \text{ the work} @@ every part split
p = 0.95:\ \ S(8) = \tfrac{1}{0.05 + 0.95/8} \approx 5.93,\ \ \tfrac{5.93}{2} \approx 2.96 @@ the speed-up-limit technique (17·comp)
\htmlClass{new}{8 \text{ half-speed cores: about } 3\times \text{ at the same heat}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the unsplit share decides

The cube rule holds only above the leak floor of 39·eng.3: once V is near the floor, the voltage cannot follow the clock down, and extra cores buy work at extra heat. So phones mix a few fast cores with several slow ones, and graphics chips run thousands of slow cores on work that splits almost perfectly, such as the pixels of a picture.

The accepted names

The switch-heat count is dynamic power, α C V² f, with α the activity factor; the half lost in filling is the same for any resistance, a standard result for charging a capacitor. The even-shrink technique is Dennard scaling, and its end around 2005 the end of Dennard scaling, or the power wall. The doubling of the count of switches is Moore’s law (a measured trend, not a law of nature). The tenfold step of 60 mV is the subthreshold swing, and the leak subthreshold leakage; parts switched off to save heat are dark silicon. Many cores on one chip is a multicore processor, and a mix of fast and slow cores is heterogeneous computing. The crosswalk items here are AP Computer Science Principles 4.3 (parallel and distributed computing, speedup) and the first course on digital circuits, power and scaling.

Who said it first

Gordon Moore saw the count of parts on a chip doubling each year in 1965 and revised it to every two years in 1975. Robert Dennard and his colleagues at IBM gave the rules for shrinking a switch in 1974. Gene Amdahl gave the speed-up limit in 1967. Intel cancelled a planned faster single core in 2004 and turned to two cores; the clocks of the fastest sold chips have stayed near 3 to 5 GHz since.

A proof that is not allowed

“Chips doubled their switches every two years from 1975 to 2005, and they still do, so the clock will keep rising when better cooling arrives.” Not allowed: a trend is a measurement, and the leak-floor technique says why the voltage stopped. A count that held for thirty years is no proof of the next ten, and the clock rode on a shrinking voltage, not on the count of switches.
