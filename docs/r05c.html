5c · proof

Fractions as Ratios

The integers can take away anything from anything. They cannot divide: no integer multiplied by 2 gives 1. This step proves that, then runs 5b’s move again with multiplying in place of adding. A fraction is one of 2h’s classes of integer pairs, sorted by cross-multiplying. The one new thing is a refusal: a pair whose second entry is 0, and the step shows exactly why it must go. Adding, multiplying, the integers inside, reciprocals and dividing follow as in 5b, and every division by a number other than 0 has an answer.

Write the missing factor as a pair, the amount to reach on top and the multiplier below. Count two pairs as one when the top of each times the bottom of the other gives the same integer. Never let a bottom be 0. Work on members, and check that the class of the answer stays put.

5c1The Gap in the Integers

Ask for an integer x with 2x = 1, the 2 and the 1 being integers as 5b's naming line reads them. By member-with-a-0, x is n or −n for a number n, and either way the answer would land somewhere it cannot. Gap-in-ℤ technique: no integer x has 2x = 1.

\text{case } x = [n, 0]:\ \ [2, 0]\,[n, 0] = [2n, 0] @@ the naming line; the ℕ-inside-ℤ technique
\qquad [2n, 0] = [1, 0],\ \text{so } 2n = 1 @@ the assumption, the swap axiom; ℕ-inside-ℤ
\qquad 2 \mid 1 \text{ and } 1 = 2 \cdot 0 + 1:\ \ 1 \text{ even and odd};\ \ \bot @@ ︿ ∣ (4j); times-zero, zero-plus; even-or-odd's “not both”; clash
\text{case } x = [0, n]:\ \ [2, 0]\,[0, n] = [2 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot n,\ 2n + 0 \cdot 0] = [0, 2n] @@ ﹀ · on ℤ; times-zero, zero-times, plus-zero

4j asked “does d divide n?” of numbers, and often the answer was no: 4 ∤ 10. Gap-in-ℤ says that moving to the integers does not help. Multiplying runs backwards only sometimes.

5c2Cancelling in the Integers

The construction ahead leans on one technique about the integers that 5b did not prove: a factor other than 0 can be cancelled from both sides. For numbers this is 4h's cancel for times. For integers, pick the member of the factor that has a 0 in it (member-with-a-0), and the whole thing comes down to cancel for times. Cancel technique for times on ℤ: if xz = yz and z ≠ 0, then x = y.

z = [e, 0] \ \lor\ z = [0, e],\ \text{with } e \ne 0 @@ the member-with-a-0 technique; e = 0 would make z = 0
\text{case } z = [e, 0]:\ \ xz = [ae, be],\ \ yz = [ce, de] @@ ﹀ · on ℤ; times-zero, plus-zero, zero-plus

\text{case } z = [0, e]:\ \ xz = [be, ae],\ \ yz = [de, ce] @@ ﹀ · on ℤ; times-zero, plus-zero, zero-plus

And with it, the no-zero-product technique on ℤ: if x ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0, then xy ≠ 0. Suppose xy = 0. By times-zero on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ, 0y = 0, so xy = 0y, with y ≠ 0. Then cancel for times on ℤ gives x = 0, which clashes with x ≠ 0. Refute. ∎ Both techniques fail without “other than 0”: 3 · 0 = 5 · 0, and 3 ≠ 5.

5c4Fraction Pairs and Cross-Multiplying

From here on, a fraction pair is a pair (p, q) of integers with q ≠ 0: a member of . Its left entry p is its top, the amount to reach, and its right entry q its bottom, the multiplier. Their accepted names are the numerator and the denominator, and from here on the ladder uses those.

From here on, for fraction pairs (p, q) and (r, s), means:

Read it, as on 5b, “(p, q) is equivalent to (r, s)”: the relation on fraction pairs that goes with this statement. Between fraction pairs, ∼ always means this one.

5b's ∼, the cross-sum between pairs of numbers, keeps its meaning there, and 2h's use for whichever sameness relation is in hand stands elsewhere.

Cross-multiply: the numerator of each times the denominator of the other. (1, 2) ∼ (2, 4), since 1 · 4 = 2 · 2. (2, 3) and (3, 4) are not equivalent: 2 · 4 is 8 and 3 · 3 is 9.

The oldest geometry book that has come down whole proves this test for proportion. When four numbers are proportional, the product of the first and the fourth equals the product of the second and the third. Conversely, when those products are equal, the four numbers are proportional. The sameness that defines a fraction in this step is that test.

5c5An Equivalence, and the Refused Pair

Same-fraction technique: the relation ∼ on fraction pairs is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The three properties are 2h11’s again, as 2i13 reads them off dots. Reflexive: pq = qp is turn-about for times on ℤ. Symmetric: from ps = qr, the turn-about technique and turn-about for times on ℤ on each side give rq = sp. Transitive needs a cancellation, and the cancelled factor is a denominator.

ps = qr,\ \ \ ru = st @@ given (p, q) ∼ (r, s) and (r, s) ∼ (t, u); ﹀ ∼
(ps)u = (qr)u @@ the swap axiom: each side times u
(pu)s = q(ru) @@ regroup for times on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ on the left; regroup for times on ℤ on the right
(pu)s = q(\htmlClass{new}{st}) @@ the swap axiom, with the second given
(pu)s = (qt)\htmlClass{keep}{s} @@ regroup for times on ℤ, turn-about for times on ℤ
\htmlClass{new}{pu = qt} @@ the cancel technique for times on ℤ: s is a denominator, so s ≠ 0
(p, q) \sim (t, u) \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ ∼

Look at the line that cites cancel for times on ℤ. It needs s ≠ 0, and s is the denominator of the middle pair. Now suppose the pair (0, 0) had been let in. It is equivalent to everything. (1, 2) ∼ (0, 0), since 1 · 0 = 2 · 0. And (0, 0) ∼ (3, 4), since 0 · 4 = 0 · 3. Transitivity would then glue (1, 2) to (3, 4), and 1 · 4 = 4 is not 2 · 3 = 6. A relation that is equivalent to everything through one bad member sorts nothing. Refusing a denominator of 0 is not fussiness: it is what lets the relation make classes at all. This is the first face of “you cannot divide by 0”, met before a single technique of arithmetic has been written down; 5e meets the second.

5c6The Fractions

From here on, for a fraction pair (p, q), , also written , names the class of (p, q) under ∼: 2h's [(p, q)]. Read it “p over q”. From here on, p/q names the class of (p, q), and never the pair itself.

Cross-multiplying technique: p/q = r/s exactly when ps = qr. The column is same-class's from 5b, word for word, with same-fraction in place of same-difference. The pair is in its own class. Classes-never-overlap makes two classes sharing a member one. And a class's members are the pairs equivalent to it. ∎ So 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = (−1)/(−2), and 2/3 ≠ 3/4. The accepted name for two writings of one fraction, such as 1/2 and 2/4, is equivalent fractions.

Scaling technique: if k ≠ 0, then (kp)/(kq) = p/q. First, (kp, kq) is a fraction pair, since kq ≠ 0 by no-zero-product on ℤ. Then (kp)q = (kq)p, each side being k, p and q multiplied in some order (regroup for times on ℤ, turn-about for times on ℤ), and cross-multiplying finishes. ∎ So every class holds its members' multiples, and in the picture below a class is a trail of dots heading back toward the place where (0, 0) would be.

From here on, names the set of the classes of fraction pairs:

Its members are the rationals, also called fractions. Read it “Q”, or “the rationals”; the letter is for quotient. A fraction is a statement that names a ratio of integers, with equivalent ratios counted as one.

5c8A Fake Sum

Here is a function that looks simpler and fails: add numerators and denominators, (p, q), (r, s) ↦ (p + r, q + s). From (1, 2) and (1, 3) it gives (2, 5). From (2, 4) and (1, 3), a member of the same two fractions, it gives (3, 7). And 2 · 7 = 14 is not 5 · 3 = 15. It names no operation on fractions. (Even its first answer is odd: it would make 1/2 “plus” 1/2 equal to 2/4, which is 1/2 again.)

5c9The Integers Inside

ℤ-inside-ℚ technique: for integers x and y, x/1 = y/1 exactly when x = y; x/1 + y/1 = (x + y)/1; and (x/1)(y/1) = (xy)/1. By cross-multiplying, x/1 = y/1 is x · 1 = 1 · y, which is x = y by times-one on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ. The sum is (x · 1 + 1 · y)/(1 · 1) and the product (xy)/(1 · 1), which times-one on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ tidy. ∎

From here on, where a rational is meant, an integer x names the rational x/1. So 0 names 0/1, 1 names 1/1, −2 names (−2)/1, and every number names a rational too, through 5b's naming line. By ℤ-inside-ℚ, sameness, sums and products come out the same whichever reading is taken.

As on 5b, this is a matching, in 4k's word, between ℤ and the part of ℚ made of the classes x/1, carrying + to + and · to ·. The naming line lets you forget the difference. One fact gets used constantly: p/q = 0 exactly when p = 0. By cross-multiplying, p/q = 0/1 is p · 1 = q · 0, which is p = 0 by times-one on ℤ and times-zero on ℤ. A fraction is 0 exactly when its numerator is. The same sentence holds for dividing. Any function f from ℤ into a system where dividing by anything but 0 always has an answer, if it keeps sums and products and sends only 0 to 0, extends to ℚ in exactly one way that keeps them: send x/y to f(x)/f(y).

5c10Reciprocals

The new numbers can now answer the question that made them. First, the plainest case, “what must w be multiplied by to make 1?”. It needs a technique that is also useful on its own. Cancel technique for fractions: if w ≠ 0 and wb = wb′, then b = b′.

(pu)/(qv) = (pu')/(qv') @@ ab = ab′; ﹀ · on ℚ
(pu)(qv') = (qv)(pu') @@ the cross-multiplying technique
(uv')(pq) = (vu')(pq) @@ middle-swap (for ·) and turn-about for times on ℤ, on each side
uv' = vu' @@ the cancel technique for times on ℤ: pq ≠ 0 by no-zero-product on ℤ
\htmlClass{new}{b = b'} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cross-multiplying technique

Reciprocal technique: if w ≠ 0, there is exactly one rational b with wb = 1; for w = p/q it is q/p. Since p ≠ 0, (q, p) is a fraction pair. And (p/q)(q/p) = (pq)/(qp), which is 1/1 by cross-multiplying, since (pq) · 1 = (qp) · 1 by turn-about for times on ℤ. That is one; cancel for fractions says there is no other. ∎

From here on, for a rational a other than 0, names the one rational b with ab = 1 (the reciprocal technique). Read it “the reciprocal of a”, or “a inverse”. It is also called the multiplicative inverse of a. The small high −1 is one mark, not 4g's power: 4g's high place holds numbers, and −1 is not one.

The word “inverse” here is a new use: 1f's inverse of an if, ¬P ⇒ ¬Q, keeps its meaning; said of a number, it means the reciprocal.

So (2/3)⁻¹ = 3/2, ((−2)/5)⁻¹ = 5/(−2) = (−5)/2, and 2⁻¹ = (2/1)⁻¹ = 1/2. 0 is left out of the line on purpose, and the reason is not that nobody has thought of a reciprocal for 0: 5e proves there cannot be one.

5c11Dividing

From here on, for rationals a and b with b ≠ 0, , also written , names . Read it “a over b”, or “a divided by b”; it is the quotient of a by b, and forming it is dividing a by b.

From here on, quotient and dividing are also said of rationals. 4j's quotient, the q of a split with remainder, and 4j's “d divides n”, d ∣ n, keep their meanings for numbers.

The slash now has two readings when w and b are integers: the class of the pair (w, b), from the fraction line, and w · b⁻¹, from this one. 5c12A Quotient Found by Measuring

Wrap a string once round the rim of a can, and lay it out straight. Then measure straight across the rim, through the middle. Divide the first length by the second. Do it again with a bicycle wheel, and again with a dinner plate. Each time the quotient comes out at about 3 and 1/7, which is 22/7.

So here is a guess, named so that it can be talked about. Round-quotient guess: for every round thing, the way around divided by the way across is one and the same number, about 22/7. That number, if there is one, gets its name on 5f, once the fractions are finished. Here it stays a guess: we can’t prove anything about it yet.

“About” is the honest word here. Measuring is looking, and looking earns no line (1a). So some questions stay open. Is the quotient exactly the same for every round thing? Is 22/7 too big or too small? Another guess, 223/71, sits just 1/497 away: over one denominator, they are 1562/497 and 1561/497. Is the quotient between them? Is it a fraction at all? The ladder does answer them; for now they stay open.

5c14Running It Backwards

Who said it first

Euclid proves that when four numbers are proportional, the product of the first and the fourth equals the product of the second and the third; and, conversely, that when those products are equal the four numbers are proportional. The sameness that defines a fraction in this step is his test for proportion.

Euclid, Elements VII.19; paraphrase after Heath's translation (1908)

Accepted names

Two writings of one fraction, such as 1/2 and 2/4, are equivalent fractions.
