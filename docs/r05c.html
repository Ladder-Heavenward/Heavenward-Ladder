5c · proof

The rules of arithmetic

The fractions are built. This step proves twelve techniques for them, the twelve techniques of arithmetic: nine about adding and multiplying, three about which numbers are positive. Each is proved the same way. Write each fraction by a member, work the two sides out on pairs with the integer techniques that 5a carried over from rung 4, and cross-multiply. Along the way the fractions get negatives and subtraction, and a meaning for “positive” that does not depend on the member chosen. Then order: one fraction is less than another when the difference is positive. Any two compare. And, as for the counting numbers, whether one fraction is less than another can always be settled.

To prove a rule for fractions: write each one by a member, compute both sides on pairs with the integer techniques, and cross-multiply. Only the class matters, so two different pairs on the same trail are the same fraction.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 5a (carry-over, member-with-a-0, same-class, ℕ-inside-ℤ, pair-is-the-difference, middle-swap, gap-in-ℕ) and 5b (cross-multiplying, scaling, honest-fractions, ℤ-inside-ℚ, reciprocal, no-zero-product on ℤ, cancel for times on ℤ), and through them on 4e–4g. 5d takes the twelve techniques as its starting point and draws what they force. 5e uses the order rules. The technique pages 5f to 5i use the rules as their whole licence.

The list, in this order, is the one a well-known calculus book sets out at its start as the properties of numbers. There it is the starting point. Here it is a technique, twelve of them, about objects built on this ladder. By the end of this step ℚ obeys all twelve.

Two hundred years ago, a teacher of algebra laid down what he called the permanence of equivalent forms. Rules found to be true for whole numbers should be kept when the letters are allowed to stand for more general quantities. Later writers made permanence the guide for extending the number system.

This ladder does not assume permanence. The negatives and the fractions were built from pairs, and whether the old rules survive the building is a question with an answer, proved below one rule at a time.

5c3The adding rules

Stands on 5c2 and the techniques of 4e carried to ℤ by 5a.

Write w = p/q, b = r/s and c = t/u, by members, throughout. Order first. Turn-about for plus on ℚ: w + b = (ps + qr)/(qs), and b + w = (rq + sp)/(sq). Turn-about for times on ℤ turns rq into qr, sp into ps and sq into qs. Turn-about for plus on ℤ swaps the two parts of the numerator. The result is the same pair, so the same fraction. ∎ Brackets next.

(w + b) + c = \tfrac{ps + qr}{qs} + \tfrac tu = \tfrac{(ps + qr)u + (qs)t}{(qs)u} @@ ﹀ + on ℚ, twice

p(su) + q(ru + st) = psu + qru + qst @@ regroup for times on ℤ; spread on ℤ; regroup for times on ℤ, regroup for plus on ℤ
(qs)u = q(su) @@ regroup for times on ℤ

5c4The multiplying rules

Stands on 5c3 and 5b (the reciprocal technique).

These are shorter, because the rule for · works on numerators and denominators separately. Turn-about for times on ℚ: wb = (pr)/(qs) and bw = (rp)/(sq), the same pair by turn-about for times on ℤ twice. Regroup for times on ℚ: (wb)c = ((pr)t)/((qs)u) and w(bc) = (p(rt))/(q(su)), the same pair by regroup for times on ℤ twice. Times-one on ℚ: w · 1 = (p · 1)/(q · 1) = p/q by times-one on ℤ, and 1 · w by turn-about for times on ℚ. And 1 ≠ 0: 1/1 = 0/1 would need 1 · 1 = 1 · 0, which is 1 = 0 in ℤ, and times-one on ℤ says 1 ≠ 0 there. Reciprocal on ℚ: for w ≠ 0, w · w⁻¹ = 1 is 5b's reciprocal technique, and w⁻¹ · w = 1 follows by turn-about for times on ℚ. ∎ (all four)

5c5The spreading rule

Stands on 5c3, 5c4 and 5b (scaling).

Spread on ℚ is the only rule that ties adding to multiplying. It is also the only one where the two sides come out as different pairs. They are different members of one class, and 5b's scaling, which scales a member, shows it.

w(b + c) = \tfrac pq \cdot \tfrac{ru + st}{su} = \tfrac{p(ru + st)}{q(su)} @@ ﹀ + on ℚ; ﹀ · on ℚ
wb + wc = \tfrac{pr}{qs} + \tfrac{pt}{qu} = \tfrac{(pr)(qu) + (qs)(pt)}{(qs)(qu)} @@ ﹀ · on ℚ, twice; ﹀ + on ℚ
\phantom{wb + wc} = \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{q}\,(p(ru + st))}{\htmlClass{new}{q}\,(q(su))} @@ regroup for times on ℤ and turn-about for times on ℤ in each product; spread on ℤ turned about, twice
\phantom{wb + wc} = \tfrac{p(ru + st)}{q(su)} @@ the scaling technique: q is a denominator, so q ≠ 0
w(b + c) = wb + wc \quad \blacksquare @@ the techniques turn-about and chain

So the nine rules of adding and multiplying are proved for the fractions. Not one was assumed. Each rests on a technique of the integers, which rests on a technique of rung 4, proved by induction from the next-one gesture.

5c6Positive integers

Stands on 5a (member-with-a-0, ℕ-inside-ℤ, gap-in-ℕ) and 4g (no-zero-product).

The last three rules need a meaning for positive. Start with the integers, where 5a's member-with-a-0 has done most of the work.

From here on, for an integer x, the statement “x is positive” means x = n for some number n ≠ 0: in 5a's naming, x = [n, 0] with n ≠ 0.

Three-way technique on ℤ: for every integer x, exactly one of these is true: x = 0; x is positive; −x is positive. Member-with-a-0 gives x = [n, 0] or x = [0, n] for exactly one number n. If n = 0, x = 0. If n ≠ 0, the first form makes x positive and the second makes −x = [n, 0] positive. At most one is true, since member-with-a-0's n is unique and both forms meet only at [0, 0]. ∎ The positive-sum technique on ℤ and positive-product on ℤ (positives are closed): if x and y are positive, so are x + y and xy. By ℕ-inside-ℤ, [m, 0] + [n, 0] = [m + n, 0] and [m, 0][n, 0] = [mn, 0]. Here m + n ≠ 0 by gap-in-ℕ, since m ≠ 0, and mn ≠ 0 by 4g's no-zero-product. ∎

One more technique, used three times below. Square-is-positive technique on ℤ: if x ≠ 0, then xx is positive.

x \ne 0:\ \ x = [m, 0] \ \lor\ x = [0, m],\ \ m \ne 0 @@ the member-with-a-0 technique; m = 0 would make x = 0
[m, 0]\,[m, 0] = [mm, 0] @@ the ℕ-inside-ℤ technique
[0, m]\,[0, m] = [0 \cdot 0 + mm,\ 0 \cdot m + m \cdot 0] = [mm, 0] @@ ﹀ · on ℤ; times-zero, zero-times, plus-zero, zero-plus
mm \ne 0 @@ the no-zero-product technique (4g)
xx = [mm, 0] \text{ is positive} \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases; ︿ positive

5c7Positive fractions

Stands on 5c6 and 5b (cross-multiplying).

From here on, for a rational a, the statement “a is positive” means a = p/q for some fraction pair (p, q) whose product pq is a positive integer.

Why the product? A fraction p/q answers “what must q be multiplied by to make p?”. That answer should be positive when p and q are on the same side of 0, both positive or both the negative of a positive. pq says so in one integer. So 2/3 is positive, and so is (−2)/(−3), the same fraction. But a definition that says “for some member” must be checked against the other members, or a fraction could be positive by one member and not by another. Honest-positive technique: if p/q = r/s and pq is positive, then rs is positive.

pq \text{ positive},\ \ ps = qr @@ given; the cross-multiplying technique
(rs)(qq) = (qr)(sq) @@ middle-swap (for ·), turn-about for times on ℤ inside
\phantom{(rs)(qq)} = (\htmlClass{new}{ps})(sq) @@ the given, by the swap axiom
\phantom{(rs)(qq)} = (pq)(ss) @@ turn-about for times on ℤ inside; middle-swap (for ·)
(pq)(ss) \text{ positive} @@ square-is-positive on ℤ (s ≠ 0); positive-product on ℤ
rs = 0 \ \lor\ rs \text{ positive} \ \lor\ -(rs) \text{ positive} @@ the three-way technique on ℤ
\text{case } rs = 0:\ \ (rs)(qq) = 0,\ \text{yet positive};\ \ \bot @@ turn-about for times on ℤ, times-zero on ℤ; three-way on ℤ's exactly one; clash
\text{case } -(rs) \text{ positive}:\ \ (-(rs))(qq) = -((rs)(qq)) \text{ positive};\ \ \bot @@ square-is-positive on ℤ (q ≠ 0), positive-product on ℤ; negative-out on ℤ; both y and −y positive, against three-way on ℤ
\htmlClass{new}{rs \text{ positive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remaining option (the remaining-option technique, twice)

Notice the case split. It is on three-way on ℤ's three-way or, which is a proved statement, not on rung 7's principle that every statement is true or false. Every split in this step is of that kind.

5c9Order

Stands on 5c8, 5c2 (subtraction) and 4g (less-is-adding).

From here on, for rationals a and b, the statement means b − a is positive. Read it “a is less than b”. 4g's line for ≤, > and ≥ is read with this <, and the order words of 4g are given the same meanings for rationals.

Two checks that the line says what it should. First, w is positive exactly when 0 < w. That is because w − 0 = w + (−0) = w + 0 = w (−0 = 0/1 = 0, and plus-zero on ℚ). Second, it agrees with 4g on the numbers. Order-agrees technique: for numbers m and n, m < n in 4g's sense exactly when m < n as rationals. If m < n, less-is-adding gives n = m + c with c ≠ 0. Then n − m = [n, m] by pair-is-the-difference. That is [c, 0] by same-class, since n + 0 = m + c. So n − m is c, positive. Conversely, suppose n − m = [n, m] is positive. Then it is [k, 0] with k ≠ 0, so n = m + k by same-class, and m < n by less-is-adding. ∎

Any two rationals compare. That needs one small technique from the rules alone, used again on 5d. One-negative technique: if w + x = 0, then x = −w.

a + x = 0 @@ given
x = x + 0 = x + (a + (-a)) @@ plus-zero on ℚ; negative on ℚ
\phantom{x} = (x + a) + (-a) @@ regroup for plus on ℚ, turned about
\phantom{x} = (\htmlClass{new}{a + x}) + (-a) @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ, inside
\phantom{x} = 0 + (-a) = \htmlClass{new}{-a} \quad \blacksquare @@ the given, by the swap axiom; plus-zero on ℚ

Any-two-compare technique on ℚ: for rationals w and b, exactly one of w < b, w = b, b < w. The rules give (b − w) + (w − b) = b + ((−w) + w) + (−b) = b + (−b) = 0, by regroup for plus on ℚ, negative on ℚ and plus-zero on ℚ. So w − b = −(b − w) by one-negative. Now three-way on ℚ on b − w: exactly one of b − w = 0, b − w positive, −(b − w) positive. The second is w < b. The third is w − b positive, which is b < w. And b − w = 0 exactly when b = w. If b − w = 0, then b = b + ((−w) + w) = (b − w) + w = 0 + w = w. And if b = w, then b − w = 0 by negative on ℚ. ∎

5c10Order is settled

Stands on 5c9, 4g (settled-order) and 1g.

4g's settled-order technique said that for numbers, m < n or not, and m = n or not, can always be decided. So 1g's techniques work both ways for statements built from them. The same goes for fractions. Settled-order technique on ℚ: for rationals w and b, w = b ∨ w ≠ b, and w < b ∨ ¬(w < b).

a = p/q,\ \ b = r/s @@ use the somes
a = b \;\Leftrightarrow\; ps = qr @@ the cross-multiplying technique
[e, f] = [g, h] \;\Leftrightarrow\; e + h = f + g @@ the same-class technique: a sameness of integers is a sameness of numbers
\htmlClass{new}{a = b \ \lor\ a \ne b} @@ settled-order (4g) settles the sameness of numbers; carry it up through the two exactly-whens
[e, f] \text{ positive} \;\Leftrightarrow\; f \lt e @@ [e, f] = [k, 0] with k ≠ 0 is e = f + k (same-class), which is f < e (less-is-adding)

\htmlClass{new}{a \lt b \ \lor\ \neg(a \lt b)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a < b is “b − a positive”, settled by the line above

So, from here on, sameness and order of rationals are settled in 1g's word. 1g's techniques may be used both ways for statements built from them with ¬, ∧, ∨ and ⇒. And a case split on “w < b or not”, or on “w = 0 or not”, is licensed for rationals, as it was for numbers, before rung 7.

5c12Running it backwards

Stands on 5c1 to 5c10.

Wanting a rule for fractions: write each by a member, compute both sides on pairs, and if the pairs differ, cross-multiply or find the common factor and use scaling. Wanting to know whether a fraction is positive: multiply its numerator by its denominator and ask three-way on ℤ. Wanting to compare two fractions: subtract and ask whether the difference is positive. Wanting a case split on order: settled-order on ℚ licenses it. From here on, wanting any move with fractions: name the rule that licenses it, and check its small print. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The list of twelve techniques, in this order, is the one Michael Spivak sets out at the start of his Calculus as the properties of numbers.

George Peacock laid down what he called the permanence of equivalent forms: rules found to be true for whole numbers should be kept when the letters are allowed to stand for more general quantities. Hermann Hankel later made permanence the guiding principle for extending the number system.

Peacock, A Treatise on Algebra (1830); Hankel, Theorie der complexen Zahlensysteme (1867)

Accepted names

A set of numbers whose adding and multiplying obey the first nine rules is called a field; one that obeys all twelve is an ordered field. So ℚ is an ordered field. The turn-about rules are the commutative laws, the regroup rules the associative laws, spread the distributive law, and three-way is trichotomy.
