11f · technique

Finding the Breaks

11a to 11e proved that most of any function built from formulas is continuous: polynomials everywhere, quotients wherever the bottom is not 0, chains wherever the links are, pieces wherever they arrive at one height. So the places where a function can fail to land where it was headed are few, and you can list them before looking. This step is the field guide. It names the places a break can hide, the three kinds of break, and what each kind allows. A removable break can be mended by choosing one value; a jump or an endless break cannot.

List the suspects; at each, find the limit from the left, the limit from the right and the value; name the break by what fails; mend it only if the two sides agree.

11f1Breaks, and Where They Hide

11a's definition asks about inputs. A picture of a graph shows gaps at reals that are not inputs too: 1/x at 0, |x|/x at 0, 9a's H at 1. The field guide needs one word for all of them.

From here on, f has a break at a real w when f is defined near w and is not continuous at w: either w is an input and f is discontinuous there, or w is not an input, so that f has a hole at w in 10a's second meaning. 11a, 11b and 11e used the word ahead of this line, each time pointing here; this is its line.

So 1/x is continuous (11b's one-over-x-is-continuous) and has a break at 0; u is not continuous and has a break at its input 0. The word says where a picture tears, whether or not the tear is at an input. By the techniques of 11b to 11e, for a function built from formulas a break can only be at one of a few kinds of place, the suspects:

  • where the bottom of a fraction is 0 (continuous-quotients covers every other input of a quotient);
  • where a function given in pieces changes formula, including where an absolute value changes sign (glued-functions decides these);
  • where a chain's inside reaches the edge of the outside's domain, as the inside of a root reaching 0 (chain-at-an-edge decides these, one side at a time).

Everywhere else the earlier steps have already done the work. That turns “where is this continuous?” from a question about every real into a short list.

11f2Three Kinds of Break, Each With Its Line

At a suspect w, compute three things, as 10f7 computed them one side at a time: the limit from the left, the limit from the right, and the value f(w), if there is one. The kind of break is read off from them.

From here on, a break of f at w is removable when f has a limit at w: lim_{x→w} f(x) exists, and either w is not an input or the limit is not f(w).

From here on, a break of f at w is a jump when both one-sided limits exist and differ: lim_{x→w⁻} f(x) ≠ lim_{x→w⁺} f(x). Its size is 10e's |L⁺ − L⁻|.

From here on, a break of f at w is endless when, as x → w⁺ or as x → w⁻, f(x) passes every bound or passes below every bound (10f's lines).

A break is also called a discontinuity, and the three kinds are called removable, jump and infinite discontinuities: an endless break is an infinite discontinuity. Mending is called removing the discontinuity.

Endless is said here of a break. 8·cb5's endless decimal keeps its meaning.

No break is of two kinds. A removable break has a limit, so its sides agree (two-sides-agree): not a jump, which is 10e3's sides that disagree. An endless break has a side with no limit (10f's passing-every-bound, side by side), so it is neither removable nor a jump. And some breaks are of none of the three kinds. 9a's q, at every real, has sides that settle nowhere and stay bounded; so does x · q(x) at every w ≠ 0. The field guide names the three kinds that formulas make, and leaves the wild ones as what they are.

f(x) = \tfrac{x^2 - x - 2}{x^2 - 4} @@ suspects: the bottom is 0 at 2 and at −2 (difference-of-squares, zero-product). Everywhere else continuous (rational-functions-are-continuous)
= \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(x - 2)}(x + 1)}{\htmlClass{new}{(x - 2)}(x + 2)} @@ factor top and bottom (5i's sum-and-product, difference-of-squares)
x \to 2:\ \ \tfrac{x + 1}{x + 2} \to \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac34} @@ cancel where x ≠ 2 (agreeing-near), then substitute (rational-functions-are-continuous): both sides head to ¾
2 \notin \operatorname{dom} f:\ \ \text{removable} @@ a hole with a limit. Mend it with the value ¾ (below)
x \to -2:\ \ \text{top} \to -1,\ \ \text{bottom} \to 0 @@ the cancelled form near −2; a nonzero top over a bottom heading to 0 (10f)
x \to -2^+:\ \tfrac{-1}{\text{small positive}} \text{ passes below every bound} @@ x + 2 > 0 just right of −2, x + 1 < 0
x \to -2^-:\ \tfrac{-1}{\text{small negative}} \text{ passes every bound} @@ x + 2 < 0 just left: \htmlClass{new}{an endless break}

Note the order of moves. Cancelling at 2 (10f2's factor-and-cancel) tells you the two sides agree; it does not make f continuous at 2. f still has no value at 2, and cancelling does not give it one. The cancelled expression (x + 1)/(x + 2) is a different function, which agrees with f wherever f is defined and also has a value at 2.

11f3Mending a Removable Break

From here on, when f has a removable break at w with lim_{x→w} f(x) = L, the function mended at w is (f|_D) ∪ {(w, L)}, where D = dom f ∖ {w}: f with its value at w replaced by L, or given the value L if it had none (9d's restriction, then 2f's Pairing and Union). To mend f at w is to pass to that function.

Mended-function technique: if f has a removable break at w with limit L, the function mended at w is continuous at w; it is continuous wherever f was; and L is the only value at w that makes a function agreeing with f off w continuous at w. The mended function agrees with f on every neighbourhood of w with w left out, so it heads to L (agreeing-near), and its value is L: continuous. At any other input b where f was continuous, the two agree on the neighbourhood of b of reach |b − w| (agreeing-near again). And a function agreeing with f off w heads to L at w, so if it is continuous at w its value is the limit, L (one-limit). ∎

A jump or an endless break cannot be mended by any value. The second demand of 11a, a limit, fails (two-sides-differ; passing-every-bound), and no value at a single input changes a limit (agreeing-near). So the kind of break says what can be done. For the hunt above: mend f at 2 with ¾; leave −2 alone.

H(x) = \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}:\ \text{mend at } 1 \text{ with } \htmlClass{new}{2} @@ removable: 9a's H heads to 2 at 1. The mended function is x + 1 on ℝ (equal-functions)
H_3:\ \text{the value } 3 \text{ at } 1 \text{ replaced by } 2 @@ 11a's stray value: removable, mended by the same L
u \text{ at } 0:\ \ 0 \text{ and } 1 \text{ from the sides} @@ a jump of size 1: no value mends it. u(0) = 1 makes it continuous from the right only
\tfrac{1}{x} \text{ at } 0:\ \text{endless on each side} @@ no limit: no value mends it, and 1/x is continuous all the same (one-over-x-is-continuous)

An older usage called a function continuous when one formula gave it everywhere, and discontinuous when it was pieced together from several. Later work wrote some pieced functions as a single formula, and the old line began to dissolve.

11f4Functions in Pieces

A function given in pieces is continuous inside each piece, and the only suspects are where the formula changes. For polynomial pieces that is 11d5, on every interval. At each, glued-functions (11e) decides: the left limit comes from the left formula, the right limit from the right formula, the value from whichever formula claims the input.

f(x) = x^2 + \htmlClass{new}{k}\ (x \lt 1),\ \ 3x - 1\ (x \ge 1) @@ one suspect: 1. The right formula claims the input 1
\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1 + k @@ the left formula is a polynomial: substitute (polynomials-are-continuous, side by side)
\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 2 = f(1) @@ the right formula, also the value
1 + k = 2 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{keep}{k = 1} @@ glued-functions: a joint exactly when the heights agree
k \ne 1:\ \text{a jump of size } |1 - k| @@ continuous from the right, since the right formula gives the value

Changing k changes every output of the left piece by the same amount, so its arrival height moves while its rate of change does not. You are moving the left piece's arrival until it meets the right piece's. The picture lets you do exactly that. With two unknowns the same method gives one condition, so many answers. For f(x) = mx + 1 left of 2 and x² − b from 2 on, a joint at 2 needs 2m + 1 = 4 − b. Then (m, b) = (1, 1), (0, 3) and (3/2, 0) all close it. A second condition of another kind would pin down one pair; rung 13 supplies the usual one.

The round-down is a staircase of pieces, one for each integer. It rests on one technique about reals. Floor technique: for every real x there is exactly one integer n with n ≤ x < n + 1. The column below finds such an n, with counting-passes-any-real and a least number. Its last line shows that no second integer fits.

From here on, for a real x, ⌊x⌋ names the one integer n with n ≤ x < n + 1 (the floor technique). Read it “the floor of x”: x rounded down, the greatest integer not above x. So ⌊2.7⌋ = 2, ⌊−0.5⌋ = −1 and ⌊3⌋ = 3.

-x \lt M,\ \ M \in \mathbb N @@ counting-passes-any-real (8e), for the real −x; so x + M > 0 (order-and-arithmetic-agree)
k \text{ the least number with } x + M \lt k @@ counting-passes-any-real gives some number above x + M; least-member-for-any-set (7·cb4) gives the least
k \ne 0;\ \ k - 1 \le x + M \lt k @@ x + M > 0; k − 1 is a number (zero-or-a-next) and not above x + M, or it would be less than the least
n = (k - 1) - M:\ \ \htmlClass{keep}{n \le x \lt n + 1} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: take M from each part. n is an integer
n \le x \lt n' + 1 \text{ and } n' \le x \lt n + 1 \Rightarrow n = n' @@ n < n′ + 1 gives n ≤ n′ for integers (4h's next-is-not-past, read in ℤ), and back; at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ's antisymmetry ∎

⌊x⌋ agrees with the constant n − 1 on (n − 1, n) and with n on [n, n + 1). So at every integer n its limit from the left is n − 1, its limit from the right is n, and its value is n: a jump of size 1, continuous from the right. At every real that is not an integer it agrees with a constant on a neighbourhood: continuous. ⌊x⌋ is continuous on each [n, n + 1) and not on any interval that holds an integer and reals below it.

11f5Absolute Values in Fractions, and the Edge of a Domain

\tfrac{x^2 - 9}{|x - 3|} \text{ near } 3 @@ the inside x − 3 changes sign at 3: a suspect, and 3 is not an input
x \gt 3:\ \ \tfrac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{\htmlClass{new}{x - 3}} = x + 3 \to \htmlClass{new}{6} @@ |x − 3| = x − 3 on the right (8h); cancel; substitute
x \lt 3:\ \ \tfrac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{\htmlClass{new}{-(x - 3)}} = -(x + 3) \to \htmlClass{new}{-6} @@ |x − 3| = −(x − 3) on the left
\text{a jump of size } |6 - (-6)| = 12 @@ no value mends it
\sqrt{x - 1} \text{ at } 1 @@ not defined near 1: no inputs on the left, so not a break, and two-sided continuity is not asked
\lim_{x \to 1^+} \sqrt{x - 1} = 0 = \sqrt{1 - 1} @@ chain-at-an-edge's round on one side, or root-at-0 with the displacement x − 1: continuous from the right; so continuous on [1, ∞)

So at the edge of a domain the right question is one-sided, and for a root the answer is usually yes. That is the form rung 12 needs: “continuous on [c, d]” checks the ends from the inside only (11a).

11f6A Whole Hunt, With One Invented Move

f(x) = (x² − 1)/(|x| − 1). The suspects: where the bottom is 0, at 1 and −1; and where the absolute value changes formula, at 0. One invented move makes the whole thing plain: x² = |x|² for every x (8h's size-squared, or two-negatives for negatives).

\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{|x| - 1} = \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{|x|^2} - 1}{|x| - 1} @@ x² = |x|²: a licensed rewrite you will not find in a list of techs
= \tfrac{\htmlClass{new}{(|x| - 1)(|x| + 1)}}{|x| - 1} @@ difference-of-squares, with |x| playing the letter
= \htmlClass{keep}{|x| + 1} \quad (|x| \ne 1) @@ cancel, at every input: the inputs are the reals other than 1 and −1
x = \pm 1:\ \text{both sides head to } 2,\ \text{no value} @@ two removable breaks; mend both with 2
x = 0:\ \text{a joint of } |x| + 1 @@ pieces −x + 1 and x + 1 arrive at 1, the value: rates −1 and 1. Not a break (11e)

Three suspects, two holes, and one joint that was never a break. The right rewrite turned three calculations into one.

11f7Endless Breaks, Side by Side

At an endless break, the two sides can go the same way or opposite ways, and the count of factors on the bottom decides. 1/(x − 1)² passes every bound on both sides of 1: the square is positive on each side. 1/(x − 1) passes every bound on the right of 1 and below every bound on the left. And take (x − 1)/(x³ − x), whose bottom is x(x − 1)(x + 1). It has a removable break at 1 (it heads to ½: cancel, then substitute). It has endless breaks at 0 and −1, each with its sides going opposite ways. Cancelling first is what tells a hole from an endless break. A factor that cancels completely leaves a hole. A factor left on the bottom, with a top that is not 0 there, leaves an endless break.

11f8Careful Words

The verdicts of 11f1 stand: 1/x and |x|/x are continuous, with breaks at a real that is not an input; u is not, because its jump is at its input 0. In everyday use a break is called a discontinuity even at a real that is not an input. So “1/x has a discontinuity at 0” is said, and means “1/x has a break at 0”. Read by 11a's line, which asks only at inputs, 1/x is still continuous, and both say the same about the picture: no value at 0 could mend it.

11f10Running It Backwards: Breaks to Order

To build a function with given breaks, place each with the factor or the piece that makes it. Say you want a hole at 1 heading to 4, and an endless break at −2. Put x − 1 on top and bottom for the hole, and x + 2 on the bottom only for the endless break. Then put a constant c on top, chosen so that the cancelled form c/(x + 2) is 4 at 1: c = 12. So 12(x − 1)/((x − 1)(x + 2)) = (12x − 12)/(x² + x − 2). A jump needs pieces or an absolute value: (3/2) · |x − 2|/(x − 2) is −3/2 on the left of 2 and 3/2 on the right, a jump of size 3 at 2. Every break of the three kinds you can name, you can now build.

Who said it first

The older usage is Leonhard Euler’s; the single formulas for pieced functions are Joseph Fourier’s series. q is Dirichlet’s function. In 11f.4.4, the philosopher is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and the model of 1913 is Niels Bohr’s.

Euler called a function continuous when one formula gave it everywhere, and discontinuous when it was pieced together from several. Fourier’s series later wrote some pieced functions as a single formula, and the line Euler drew began to dissolve.

Euler, Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748), vol. II; Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822); paraphrase

Accepted names: a break is a discontinuity (including, loosely, a point outside the domain); the three kinds are removable, jump and infinite discontinuities; ⌊x⌋ is the floor function.
