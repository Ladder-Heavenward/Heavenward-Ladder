17·then · newsflash

Can a bending curve be straightened?

Can a bending curve be straightened? A straight line is the one curve the eye can judge: whether points lie on it, what its slope is, where it crosses. Exponentials and powers bend, and a bend is hard to read. But the log turns each into a line. Plot the log of an exponential against time and it is straight, with the rate for its slope. Plot the log of a power against the log of its input and it is straight, with the exponent for its slope. This page reads both kinds of plot, and the scales of sound and earthquakes built the same way. It ends with an argument, open since 1932, about the exponent that ties an animal's size to how fast it burns its food.

Steady growth plots straight when its logarithm is read against time, and the slope is the rate. A power law plots straight when both sides are read by their logarithms, and the slope is the power. Either log works; changing base rescales both axes alike or one axis only, and keeps lines straight. Survivors: straight means a fixed death rate. A dose’s effect climbs an S against the logarithm of the dose. A measure of diversity built from logarithms of each kind’s share is greatest when every kind is equally common.

Two straightenings

Semi-log technique: for positive y, y = Aekt for some A > 0 and k if and only if ln y is a linear function of t. Its slope is k, and its value at t = 0 is ln A.

Log-log technique: for positive x and y, y = Axᵖ for some A > 0 and p if and only if ln y is a linear function of ln x. Its slope is p, and its value at ln x = 0 is ln A.

\ln(Ae^{kt}) = \ln A + kt @@ log-of-a-product; ln and exp undo each other
\ln y = b + kt \Rightarrow y = e^b e^{kt} @@ adding-becomes-multiplying; A = e^b
\ln(Ax^p) = \ln A + p\ln x @@ log-of-a-product; real-exponent for real p
\ln y = b + p\ln x \Rightarrow y = e^b x^p \quad \blacksquare @@ adding-becomes-multiplying; x^p = e^(p ln x)

Take a graph whose upright axis is marked at distances log y, and whose other axis is plain. It has the accepted name a semi-log plot. One with both axes marked in logs is a log–log plot. The marks read 1, 10, 100, … at equal spacing, as on 17h's rulers. With log₁₀ in place of ln, the slope on a semi-log plot is k / ln 10. On a log–log plot it is still p: change-of-base divides both logs by the same ln 10.

Scales built of logs

L = 10\log_{10}\tfrac{I}{I_0} \text{ dB} @@ sound level, in decibels, dB; I₀ is about the faintest sound heard
I \times 2 \Rightarrow L + 10\log_{10} 2 \approx L + 3 \text{ dB} @@ log-of-a-product for log₁₀
\text{magnitude } +1 \Rightarrow \text{amplitude} \times 10,\ \text{energy} \times 10^{1.5} \approx 31.6 @@ earthquakes; a magnitude 9 off the Cascadia coast releases about 31.6² ≈ 1000 times the energy of a 7

In 1932 a study compared the heat produced by animals from rats to steers. It found that the heat varies not with the surface area, as had been thought, but more nearly with the three-quarters power of the body weight.

Survivors on a log scale

Follow a thousand newborns of one species and count how many are still alive at each age. Plotted with the count on a log scale, the accepted name of the curve is a survivorship curve. By semi-log, a stretch that is straight means a fixed fraction die in each year of age, whatever the age. Ecologists sort the shapes into three kinds. Type I is flat and then falls steeply: most live to old age, as with people and elephants. Type II is straight: the same chance of dying at every age, as with many songbirds. Type III falls steeply at first and then flattens: nearly all die young, and the few that survive live long, as with oysters and Chinook salmon.

\ln N = \ln 1000 - 0.4\,t @@ straight on the semi-log plot, slope −0.4 a year
N = 1000e^{-0.4t},\ \ \tfrac{N(t + 1)}{N(t)} = e^{-0.4} \approx 0.67 @@ semi-log; a third die each year, at every age

Dose on a log scale

Give a poison to groups of test animals at a range of doses d, in mg per kg of body weight, and record the fraction f(d) that die. The graph of f has the accepted name a dose–response curve, and the dose that kills half has the accepted name the LD50 (lethal dose, 50%). Toxicologists plot f against log dose, because the doses that matter run over factors of a thousand, and there the curve is usually a symmetric S.

Dose-response technique: let f(d) = 1/(1 + (D/d)ⁿ) with D, n > 0. Then f(D) = ½, so D is the LD50. And in u = ln d, f is 17·bio's logistic curve, rising fastest at u = ln D, with slope n/4 there.

(D/d)^n = e^{n(\ln D - u)} @@ real-exponent; u = ln d
f = \tfrac{1}{1 + e^{-n(u - \ln D)}} @@ logistic-solution's form with K = 1, r = n, its middle moved to ln D
f(D) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 1} = \tfrac12 @@ the LD50
\htmlClass{new}{\text{steepest at } u = \ln D,\ \ \tfrac{df}{du} = \tfrac n4} \quad \blacksquare @@ fastest-growth: rK/4
  
\text{LD50} \approx 190\ \text{mg/kg},\ n = 3:\ \ f(95) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 2^3} \approx 0.11 @@ half the LD50 still kills about one in nine, in this model
f(380) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 2^{-3}} \approx 0.89 @@ twice the LD50: one step of ln 2 either side of the middle

A smaller LD50 means a more toxic substance. The same curve with "shows the effect" in place of "dies" gives the ED50, the effective dose. A drug is safer the farther its ED50 sits below its LD50 on the log axis.

Counting what lives in a place

The variety of life in a place has the accepted name biodiversity: of genes within a species, of species within a place, and of places, from tide pools to old-growth forest. Two plots can hold the same number of species, the accepted name species richness, and still differ: one shared evenly, the other nearly all one species. A measure of information from the study of signals, used on the fractions pᵢ of each species, rewards both richness and evenness. Its accepted name is the Shannon diversity index.

For S species with fractions p₁, …, pS, all positive and summing to 1, H = −Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ. H is a name, for one number.

Even-diversity technique: 0 ≤ H ≤ ln S, with H = ln S exactly when every pᵢ = 1/S, and H = 0 exactly when S = 1.

0 \lt p_i \le 1 \Rightarrow -p_i\ln p_i \ge 0 @@ log-rises: ln pᵢ ≤ 0; equal to 0 only at pᵢ = 1
H - \ln S = \textstyle\sum p_i \ln\tfrac{1}{Sp_i} @@ ln S = Σ pᵢ ln S, since Σ pᵢ = 1; log-of-a-product, log-of-a-quotient
\le \textstyle\sum p_i\Big(\tfrac{1}{Sp_i} - 1\Big) = \tfrac SS - 1 = 0 @@ log-bounds: ln y ≤ y − 1, equal only at y = 1
\htmlClass{new}{H \le \ln S},\ \text{equal iff each } Sp_i = 1 \quad \blacksquare @@ the equality case of log-bounds, term by term
  
\begin{aligned}&\text{Douglas fir 25, western hemlock 25, red }\\ &\quad \text{cedar 25, bigleaf maple 25}:\ H = \ln 4 \\ &\quad \approx 1.39\end{aligned} @@ even-diversity's top for 4 species
\text{fir 85, hemlock 5, cedar 5, maple 5}:\ H \approx 0.59 @@ the same richness, less than half the index
\tfrac{H}{\ln S} \approx 0.42 @@ its accepted name is evenness

Who said it first

Kleiber, comparing the heat produced by animals from rats to steers, found that it varies not with the surface area, as had been thought, but more nearly with the three-quarters power of the body weight.

Max Kleiber, Body size and metabolism (1932); paraphrase

The three-quarter power is Kleiber’s law. The diversity index is Claude Shannon’s measure of information, used on species. In 17·then.4.4, the older rule is Max Rubner’s; the three of 1997 are Geoffrey West, James Brown and Brian Enquist; the three of 2001 are Peter Dodds, Daniel Rothman and Joshua Weitz.
