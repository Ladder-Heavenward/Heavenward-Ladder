4j · technique

Divides, Remainders and Primes

Multiplying two numbers always gives a number. The question this step asks runs the other way: given a number, which numbers make it a product of themselves times something? That question gets a mark, and its rules. Then the split of 4i gets its usual names, quotient and remainder. It also gets a second proof by the least-member search of 4h, which first needs a technique about which statements can be searched at all. Then come even and odd, and the primes. Then an algorithm for what two numbers share, and what it gives when run backwards. Then the technique that a prime dividing a product divides a factor. Last, three of the oldest techniques there are. Every number other than 0 is a product of primes in exactly one way. The primes never run out. And no number multiplied by itself is two times another number, other than 0, multiplied by itself.

To learn whether one number divides another, split the second by the first and look at the remainder. To find what two numbers share, replace the pair by the smaller and the remainder, again and again: the common divisors never change. A prime that divides a product divides one of the factors, and that is why a number splits into primes in only one way.

4j1Divides

From here on, for numbers d and n, means this. Read it “d divides n”; d is a divisor of n, and n is a multiple of d.

From here on, means . Read it “d does not divide n”.

d ∣ n is a statement, so or not so, never a number.

Example: 3 ∣ 12, since 12 = 3 · 4. 5 ∣ 0, since 0 = 5 · 0 (times-zero). 1 ∣ n and n ∣ n for every n (one-changes-nothing). But 0 ∣ n only when n = 0, since 0 · k = 0 (zero-times).

And 4 ∤ 10: 4 · 2 = 8 < 10 < 12 = 4 · 3. By keep-order for times, any k at most 2 gives at most 8, while any k at least 3 gives at least 12. An older word for it is “measures”: 3 measures 12 because four 3s make 12 exactly.

4j2Three Rules That Run Forward

Four rules do most of the work, each proved by unpacking ∣ and using a technique. Here are the first three.

Chain technique for divides: if d divides m and m divides n, then d divides n. For n = m · k = (d · j) · k = d · (j · k), by regroup for times.

Divides-a-sum technique: a number that divides each of two numbers divides their sum. In marks: d ∣ x and d ∣ y give d ∣ x + y. For d · j + d · k = d · (j + k), by spread for times over plus.

Divides-a-multiple technique: a number that divides m divides every multiple of m. In marks: d ∣ m gives d ∣ m · c, by regroup for times.

Example: 2 ∣ 4 and 4 ∣ 12 give 2 ∣ 12. 3 ∣ 6 and 3 ∣ 9 give 3 ∣ 15.

4j3The Way Back

The fourth rule runs the other way and needs care, because there is no taking away on this rung: everything is done with + and ·.

Divides-the-rest technique: a number that divides x, and divides x + y, divides y.

Example: 3 ∣ 6 and 3 ∣ 6 + 9 give 3 ∣ 9.

Plan: unpack both givens, split on d = 0, and otherwise cancel d · j from both sides.

d \mid x,\ \ d \mid x + y @@ given; aiming at d ∣ y
x = d \cdot j,\ \ x + y = d \cdot k @@ ﹀ ∣; use the somes
\text{case } d = 0:\ \ x = 0,\ \ 0 + y = 0,\ \ y = 0 = 0 \cdot 0 @@ zero-times; the swap axiom, zero-plus; times-zero
\text{case } d \ne 0:\ \ d \cdot j \le d \cdot k @@ x ≤ x + y (at-most-is-adding); the swap axiom
\qquad j \le k @@ k < j would give d · k < d · j by keep-order for times (with turn-about for times), against the line above: any-two-compare
\qquad k = j + m @@ at-most-is-adding, its some used
\qquad d \cdot j + y = d \cdot j + d \cdot m @@ the swap axiom; spread for times over plus
\qquad y = \htmlClass{new}{d \cdot m} @@ cancel for plus, on the left
d \mid y \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ ∣; reason by cases (settled-order settles d = 0)

Notice what divides-a-sum does not say. It runs from the parts to the sum, not back: 3 ∣ 1 + 2, and 3 divides neither 1 nor 2. Divides-the-rest is the one way back, and it needs d to divide one of the parts already.

4j4Size and Divisors

Two more. Divisor-not-past technique: a divisor of a number other than 0 is at most that number. In marks: if d ∣ n and n ≠ 0 then d ≤ n.

For n = d · k with k ≠ 0, and d ≤ d · k by 4i's times-does-not-lessen.

Divides-both-ways technique: two numbers that divide each other are the same number. In marks: d ∣ e and e ∣ d give d = e.

Example: 4 ∣ 12 and 4 ≤ 12. 3 ∣ 3 and 3 ∣ 3, so 3 = 3.

Proof. e = d · j and d = e · k give d = d · (j · k). If d = 0 then e = 0 too. Otherwise cancel for times cancels d, leaving 1 = j · k. So neither is 0 and neither is past 1 (times-does-not-lessen), so j = 1 and e = d.

4j5Which Statements Can Be Searched

The search of 4h's least-member technique asks, at each number in turn, “is it in the set?”. So least-member is for settled sets. 4h's list of settled sets covered sifts of ℕ by conditions built from =, <, + and · with the joining marks.

But d ∣ n has a some inside it, ∃k, and a some over all the numbers cannot be checked number by number: there is always another number to try. A some with a bound can be.

From here on, is short for , and for ; likewise with < in place of ≤. (4h already wrote ∀k < n in this way; this line makes it official.)

Bounded-some technique: a some or an every kept below a bound is settled, when its condition is settled at each number. In marks: if φ(k) is settled at every number k, then for every m, ∃k ≤ m φ(k) is settled, and so is ∀k ≤ m φ(k).

Example: “some k ≤ 3 has k · k = 4” is settled: check k = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 2 · 2 = 4.

Plan: induct on the bound m, adding one more number to check at each step.

\psi(m):\ \big(\exists k \le m\ \varphi(k)\big) \lor \neg\big(\exists k \le m\ \varphi(k)\big) @@ the condition; φ settled at every number is given
\exists k \le 0\ \varphi(k) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \varphi(0) @@ k ≤ 0 means k = 0, since ¬(k < 0) by the empty-set technique: the base, as φ(0) is settled
k \le S(m) \;\Leftrightarrow\; k \le m \lor k = S(m) @@ the next-membership technique; ﹀ ≤
\exists k \le S(m)\ \varphi(k) \;\Leftrightarrow\; \big(\exists k \le m\ \varphi(k)\big) \lor \varphi(S(m)) @@ the line above, inside the some
\text{an or of two settled statements is settled} @@ cases on each: one is true, or neither is, and then neither (1c) gives the denial
\htmlClass{new}{\psi(S(m))} @@ the hypothesis; φ(S(m)) settled; the exactly-when above, each way
\forall m \in \mathbb N\;\psi(m) \quad \blacksquare @@ the induction technique. The every goes the same way, with ∧ in place of ∨

4j6Divides Is Settled

Settled-divides technique: whether d divides n is always settled, because the some inside it can be kept below n. In marks: d ∣ n ⇔ ∃k ≤ n (n = d · k), and so d ∣ n ∨ d ∤ n.

Example: 4 ∣ 10? Check k = 0 to 10: 4 · 2 = 8 and 4 · 3 = 12, and no k gives 10. So 4 ∤ 10.

Leftward is keeping a part. Rightward: if n = 0, then k = 0 will do. If n ≠ 0, then k ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0, and k ≤ d · k = n by times-does-not-lessen. The sameness n = d · k is settled by settled-order, so bounded-some settles the bounded some.

From here on, every case split in this step is on a settled statement, and the line says which.

4j7Division With Remainder

4i split a number by b by counting up, the last place rolling over. Here is the other road, the older one: take as many copies of b out of the number as fit, and look at what is left. “As many as fit” is a least member: the least thing that can be left.

From here on, when a = b · q + r and r < b, q is the quotient and r the remainder of a by b. (4i's one-split makes them “the”.)

Division-with-remainder technique: dividing by a number other than 0 leaves a remainder less than that number. In marks: if b ≠ 0, then for every u there are q and r with u = b · q + r and r < b. Its accepted name is in the cellar. You already do this. Share seventeen sweets among five children: three each, and two left over, fewer than five.

Example: 17 = 5 · 3 + 2: quotient 3, remainder 2.

Plan: take the least possible remainder, then refute that it could be b or more.

X = \{r \in \mathbb N : \exists q \le a\ (a = b \cdot q + r)\} @@ b ≠ 0 and a kept fixed; a sift of ℕ
X \text{ is settled} @@ the bounded-some technique, with settled-order for each sameness
a \in X @@ q = 0: a = b · 0 + a (times-zero, zero-plus), and 0 ≤ a
r \text{ least in } X;\ \ a = b \cdot q + r,\ \ q \le a @@ the least-member technique; use the some
\text{suppose } b \le r:\ \ r = b + c @@ aiming to refute; at-most-is-adding, its some used
\qquad a = b \cdot q + b + c = \htmlClass{new}{b \cdot S(q) + c} @@ the swap axiom, regroup for plus; times-a-next turned about
\qquad S(q) \le b \cdot S(q) \le a,\ \text{so } c \in X @@ times-does-not-lessen; at-most-is-adding; ︿ the sift
\qquad c \lt r @@ r = c + b with b ≠ 0 (turn-about for plus): the less-is-adding technique
\qquad r \le c,\ \text{so } r \lt r;\ \ \bot @@ r is least; chain for less; no-self-membership; clash
\htmlClass{new}{r \lt b} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; then any-two-compare: b ≤ r is refuted, so r < b

Look at the line “X is settled”. Without it least-member could not be cited. With only 4h's list it could not be shown, since the condition has a some in it. Bounded-some is what lets the search run.

17 = 5 · 2 + 7 is a true sameness but not this split, since 7 < 5 fails: too few 5s were taken out.

The remainder is 0 exactly when b ∣ u. For u = b · k is the split u = b · k + 0, and one-split makes it the only one. So every question “does b divide u?” becomes “what is the remainder?”.

4j8Even and Odd

From here on, the statement “n is even” means 2 ∣ n, and “n is odd” means n = 2 · d + 1 for some number d.

Example: 6 = 2 · 3 is even, and 7 = 2 · 3 + 1 is odd. Since 2 · d = d + d (turn-about for times, times-a-next, one-changes-nothing), these are 4h's two kinds, d + d and S(d + d).

Even-or-odd technique: every number is even or odd, and not both. You know this from pairing socks: either every sock has a partner, or one is left over.

Division-with-remainder with b = 2 gives n = 2 · q + r with r ∈ 2 = {0, 1}: remainder 0 is even, remainder 1 is odd. Both at once would be two splits by 2 with remainders 0 and 1, and one-split says 0 = 1, against next-never-empty. So “even” is settled, and odd is its denial.

  • Odd-times-odd technique: an odd number times an odd number is odd.
  • Even-square technique: if a number times itself is even, the number is even.
(2 \cdot a + 1) \cdot (2 \cdot b + 1) = 4 \cdot a \cdot b + 2 \cdot a + 2 \cdot b + 1 @@ spread for times over plus, turn-about for times, regroup for times, one-changes-nothing
\phantom{(2 \cdot a + 1) \cdot (2 \cdot b + 1)} = \htmlClass{new}{2 \cdot (2 \cdot a \cdot b + a + b) + 1} @@ spread for times over plus turned about: the odd-times-odd technique, odd times odd is odd
p \cdot p \text{ even};\ \ p \text{ even or odd} @@ given; the even-or-odd technique
\text{case } p \text{ odd}:\ \ p \cdot p \text{ odd};\ \ \bot @@ the odd-times-odd technique; even-or-odd's “not both”; clash
\htmlClass{new}{p \text{ even}} \quad \blacksquare @@ everything permitted in that case; reason by cases: the even-square technique

And an even number times anything is even, by divides-a-multiple.

4j9Primes

From here on, the statement “p is prime” means 1 < p and every divisor of p is 1 or p:

Example: 2, 3, 5 and 7 are prime; 4 = 2 · 2, 6 = 2 · 3 and 9 = 3 · 3 are not.

Being prime is settled. By divisor-not-past every divisor of p is at most p, so the every is bounded, and bounded-some applies.

1 is not prime, on purpose: 1 < 1 fails. The reason for leaving it out comes in 4j18, where it would spoil “exactly one way”.

The oldest definition: a prime number is one that is measured by a unit alone.

4j10Every Number Above 1 Has a Prime Divisor

Prime-divisor technique: every number above 1 has a prime divisor; its least divisor above 1 is prime.

Example: the divisors of 15 above 1 are 3, 5 and 15. The least, 3, is prime.

Plan: take the least divisor above 1 by the search, then show any divisor of it is 1 or itself.

4j11Common Divisors

From here on, the statement “g is a greatest common divisor of a and b” means g ∣ a, g ∣ b, and every common divisor of a and b divides g:

Example: 6 is a greatest common divisor of 12 and 18: 6 ∣ 12, 6 ∣ 18, and the common divisors 1, 2, 3, 6 all divide 6.

“Greatest” here is in the sense of ∣, and that is the stronger sense: when g ≠ 0, divisor-not-past puts every common divisor at most g. There is at most one, by divides-both-ways.

That there is one at all is the greatest-common-divisor technique in 4j12, and it rests on a single technique. Split-keeps-divisors technique: if u = b · q + r, then u and b have exactly the same common divisors as b and r.

a = b \cdot q + r @@ given
d \mid a,\ d \mid b \;\Rightarrow\; d \mid b \cdot q \;\Rightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{d \mid r} @@ divides-a-multiple; divides-the-rest, since d divides b · q and b · q + r
d \mid b,\ d \mid r \;\Rightarrow\; d \mid b \cdot q \;\Rightarrow\; \htmlClass{new}{d \mid a} @@ divides-a-multiple; divides-a-sum. So the two pairs share their common divisors

4j12The gcd Algorithm

Greatest-common-divisor technique, the gcd algorithm: any two numbers have a greatest common divisor.

Plan: replace the pair (u, b) by (b, r); the second number shrinks, so strong induction carries it.

\begin{aligned}&\varphi(b):\ \forall a\ \exists g\ (g \text{ is}\\ &\quad\text{a greatest common divisor of } a \text{ and } b)\end{aligned} @@ the condition
\text{case } b = 0:\ \ g = a @@ a ∣ a, a ∣ 0, and any common divisor of a and 0 divides a
\text{case } b \ne 0:\ \ a = b \cdot q + r,\ \ r \lt b @@ the division-with-remainder technique
\qquad g \text{ a greatest common divisor of } b \text{ and } r @@ the hypothesis, used with r, and with b for a
\qquad \htmlClass{new}{g \text{ is one of } a \text{ and } b} @@ the split-keeps-divisors technique: the same common divisors
\forall b\;\varphi(b) \quad \blacksquare @@ the strong induction technique; settled-order settles b = 0

From here on, names the greatest common divisor of a and b. Read it “the gcd of a and b”.

Example: the algorithm on 1071 and 462.

1071 = 462 \cdot 2 + \htmlClass{new}{147} @@ division-with-remainder; the remainder takes the smaller number's place
462 = 147 \cdot 3 + \htmlClass{new}{21} @@ division-with-remainder again
147 = 21 \cdot 7 + 0 @@ remainder 0: the pair is now (21, 0)
\gcd(1071, 462) = \gcd(462, 147) = \gcd(147, 21) = \htmlClass{new}{21} @@ split-keeps-divisors at each line; gcd(21, 0) = 21

Three divisions, and neither number was broken into primes. The algorithm measures the larger number by the smaller as often as it goes, then goes on with the smaller and what is left over. On this ladder, each round of that is one use of division-with-remainder.

The oldest account sets the problem of finding the greatest common measure of two numbers not prime to one another. It adds that whatever measures both numbers also measures their greatest common measure.

Plus-only combination technique: for any two numbers, a multiple of one is a multiple of the other with their gcd added. In marks: for any numbers u and b there are numbers x and y with u · x = b · y + gcd(u, b) or b · y = u · x + gcd(u, b).

\psi(b):\ \forall a\ \exists x\,\exists y\,\big(a \cdot x = b \cdot y + g \lor b \cdot y = a \cdot x + g\big),\ \ g = \gcd(a, b) @@ the condition

4j14A Prime That Divides a Product

Prime-divides-a-factor technique: a prime that divides a product of two numbers divides one of them. In marks: if p is prime and p ∣ u · b, then p ∣ u or p ∣ b.

Example: 3 ∣ 4 · 6 = 24, and 3 ∣ 6.

Plan: split on whether p divides u. If not, their gcd is 1, so plus-only combination gives a line with 1 added; multiply it by b.

p \text{ prime},\ \ p \mid a \cdot b @@ given
p \mid a \lor p \nmid a @@ the settled-divides technique
\text{case } p \nmid a:\ \ \gcd(p, a) = 1 @@ the gcd divides p, so is 1 or p; p would divide a
\qquad p \cdot x = a \cdot y + 1 \ \lor\ a \cdot y = p \cdot x + 1 @@ the plus-only combination technique
\qquad p \cdot x \cdot b = a \cdot b \cdot y + b \ \lor\ a \cdot b \cdot y = p \cdot x \cdot b + b @@ the swap axiom, times b; spread for times over plus, turn-about for times, regroup for times
\qquad p \mid p \cdot x \cdot b,\ \ p \mid a \cdot b \cdot y @@ divides-a-multiple, twice
\qquad \htmlClass{new}{p \mid b} @@ divides-the-rest in each case: p divides one side and the other piece
p \mid a \lor p \mid b \quad \blacksquare @@ add an option; reason by cases

The second line is where primeness was used, and without it the technique is false. 6 ∣ 4 · 9 = 36, yet 6 divides neither 4 nor 9: its 2 went to the 4 and its 3 to the 9.

4j15Products of Lists

From here on, for a list p of numbers of length k, names P(k), where P is fixed by counted-recursion with this start and step, the step when S(i) ≤ k:

Past the last entry the step changes nothing. It is the product of the list; the empty list's product is 1.

Here P is a letter for the function of this line, and P(k) is its value at k by 4d's line. It is not 0a's P(a), a statement with a in its gap. Capitals for statements were 0a's habit, not a rule, and the letter is free once the line is done. The product of a list is also a new use of 4g's word; 4g's product a · b of two numbers keeps its meaning.

Example: 2 · 3 · 5 = (1 · 2 · 3) · 5 = 30.

Two techniques about these products follow, each by induction along the list. Entry-divides technique: each entry of a list divides the product of the list. It is proved by divides-a-multiple at each step.

Prime-divides-a-list technique: a prime that divides the product of a list divides one of its entries. In marks: a prime that divides p₁ · ⋯ · pₖ divides some entry. It is proved by prime-divides-a-factor at each step, the empty product 1 having no prime divisor (divisor-not-past).

A product of a list of numbers none of which is 0 is not 0, by no-zero-product at each step.

4j16The Primes Never Run Out

The oldest proof does not say that there are endlessly many primes. It says that the primes are more than any multitude of primes you care to set out: hand it a list, and it finds one not on it.

Endless-primes technique: for any list of primes p₁, ⋯, pₖ there is a prime that is none of them.

Plan: multiply the list and add 1. A prime divisor of that number cannot be on the list, since it would then divide 1.

p_1, \cdots, p_k \text{ primes};\ \ N = p_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot p_k + 1 @@ given; the product by counted-recursion
p_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot p_k \ne 0,\ \text{so } 1 \lt N @@ no-zero-product along the list; next-is-not-past gives 1 ≤ the product; keep-order for plus
q \text{ prime},\ \ q \mid N @@ the prime-divisor technique
\text{suppose } q = p_i,\ \ 1 \le i \le k @@ aiming to refute
\qquad q \mid p_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot p_k @@ the entry-divides technique
\qquad q \mid 1,\ \text{so } q \le 1 @@ divides-the-rest; divisor-not-past
\qquad 1 \lt q:\ \ \bot @@ q is prime; any-two-compare; clash
\htmlClass{new}{q \ne p_i \text{ for every } i} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; speak of everything: q is a prime not on the list

A common misreading is that N itself is prime. It need not be: 2 · 3 · 5 · 7 · 11 · 13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 · 509. But 59 and 509 are both new, which is all the technique says.

4j17Every Number Splits Into Primes

To say “one way” the order must be fixed, since 2 · 3 and 3 · 2 are the same product by turn-about for times. The order that suits counted-recursion's step, which sets each new entry at the end, is from the greatest prime to the least.

From here on, a prime list for n is a list of primes, each at least the next ( whenever S(i) ≤ k), whose product is n.

Example: 5, 3, 2 is a prime list for 30: each entry is prime, each is at least the next, and the product is 30.

Prime-list technique: every number other than 0 has a prime list.

Plan: take the least prime divisor and put it at the end. Then build the rest of the list for what is left, by strong induction.

\varphi(n):\ 1 \le n \Rightarrow n \text{ has a prime list} @@ the condition
n = 1:\ \ \text{the empty list} @@ its product is 1
1 \lt n:\ \ p \text{ the least divisor of } n \text{ above } 1 @@ the prime-divisor technique: p is prime
\qquad n = m \cdot p,\ \ 1 \le m,\ \ m \lt n @@ ﹀ ∣, turn-about for times; m ≠ 0; m = m · 1 < m · p by keep-order for times
\qquad m \text{ has a prime list } q_1, \cdots, q_j @@ the hypothesis, used with m
\qquad p \le q_i \text{ for each } i @@ q_i ∣ m ∣ n (entry-divides, chain for divides), and p is least
\qquad \htmlClass{new}{q_1, \cdots, q_j, p} \text{ is a prime list for } n @@ the product's step: (q₁ · ⋯ · q_j) · p = m · p = n
\forall n\;\varphi(n) \quad \blacksquare @@ the strong induction technique; any-two-compare and settled-order settle the cases

4j18In Only One Way

One-prime-list technique: a number has only one prime list.

Plan: the last entry of a prime list is pinned down by n alone, and then the rest is a prime list for a smaller number. Its accepted name, together with prime-list, is in the cellar.

\varphi(n):\ \text{any two prime lists for } n \text{ are one list} @@ the condition
n = 1:\ \ \text{both lists are empty} @@ a list with an entry has a product at least that entry (times-does-not-lessen), so at least 2
1 \lt n:\ \ \text{last entries } p \text{ and } p' @@ both lists have entries
\qquad p \text{ is the least prime that divides } n @@ a prime dividing n divides an entry (prime-divides-a-list), is that entry, and every entry is at least the last
\qquad \htmlClass{new}{p = p'} @@ the same for p′; a least member is unique (4h)
\qquad \text{the rests have one product } m,\ \ m \lt n @@ cancel for times, since p ≠ 0; keep-order for times
\qquad \text{the rests are one list} @@ the hypothesis, used with m
\qquad \text{the lists are one list} @@ same length, same entry at every place: same-outputs
\forall n\;\varphi(n) \quad \blacksquare @@ the strong induction technique

So 360 has exactly one prime list, 5, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2. Every other way of writing 360 as a product of primes is a rearrangement of it, which turn-about for times and regroup for times allow. Gathered into powers by power-of-a-next, 360 = 2³ · 3² · 5.

Now the reason 1 is not prime. If it were, 6 = 3 · 2 = 3 · 2 · 1 = 3 · 2 · 1 · 1 would have as many lists as you liked, and the technique would need an exception.

With prime lists in hand, gcds have a second route. 84 = 7 · 3 · 2 · 2 and 36 = 3 · 3 · 2 · 2 share 3 · 2 · 2 = 12, and 12 = gcd(84, 36). For small numbers that is quick. For large numbers finding the primes is very slow and the gcd algorithm is very fast, which is why the algorithm is how a gcd is found in practice.

Writing a number as a product of primes, as 360 = 2³ · 3² · 5, has the accepted name prime factorisation.

4j19Twice a Square Is Never a Square

The last technique uses the high mark: p² = p¹ · p = p · p, by power-of-a-next and power-one.

No-double-square technique: twice a square, other than 0, is never a square. In marks: there are no numbers p and q with q ≠ 0 and p² = 2 · q².

Example: 2 · 1² = 2, 2 · 2² = 8 and 2 · 3² = 18, and none of 2, 8, 18 is a square.

Plan: take the least q that works and make a smaller one, which cannot be. The set of q's that work has a some in it, “for some p”. So first that some is given a bound: p ≤ p · p (times-does-not-lessen, or p = 0) = 2 · q².

\text{assume } p^2 = 2 \cdot q^2,\ \ q \ne 0 @@ aiming to refute; the somes used
X = \{q \in \mathbb N : q \ne 0 \land \exists p \le 2 \cdot q^2\ (p^2 = 2 \cdot q^2)\} @@ a sift of ℕ, settled by bounded-some and settled-order
q \in X @@ p ≤ p² = 2 · q² (times-does-not-lessen, or p = 0); ︿ the sift
m \text{ least in } X;\ \ p'^2 = 2 \cdot m^2 @@ the least-member technique; use the some
p' \text{ is even}:\ \ p' = 2 \cdot r @@ 2 ∣ p′ · p′; the even-square technique
2 \cdot (2 \cdot r^2) = 2 \cdot m^2,\ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{m^2 = 2 \cdot r^2} @@ the swap axiom, regroup for times, turn-about for times; cancel for times, cancelling 2 ≠ 0
r \ne 0 @@ r = 0 gives m² = 0, against m ≠ 0 (no-zero-product)
r \lt m @@ m² = r² + r² with r² ≠ 0, so r² < m² (less-is-adding); m ≤ r would give m² ≤ r² (keep-order for times twice)
r \in X @@ m ≤ m² = 2 · r² (times-does-not-lessen): m is the witness
m \le r \text{ and } r \lt m;\ \ \bot @@ m is least; chain for less; no-self-membership; clash
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\exists p\,\exists q\,(q \ne 0 \land p^2 = 2 \cdot q^2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; the somes close

Look at the shape. A solution (p, q) made a solution (q, r) with a smaller second number, which would make another, and so on down. The numbers have no room for that, because the least member is at the bottom. Rung 5 reads this technique as a statement about fractions.

This way of arguing has an old name, descent. From a supposed case, produce a smaller one, and the supposed case falls, since the numbers cannot go down forever.

4j21Running It Backwards

Wanting d ∣ n, find the k. Wanting d ∤ n, find the remainder and show it is not 0, or squeeze n strictly between two multiples of d with keep-order for times.

Wanting a least member, first show the set settled, bounding every some (bounded-some).

Wanting gcd(u, b), run the algorithm forwards; wanting the plus-only combination line, run it backwards from the foot. Wanting a prime list, take least prime divisors one at a time and set each at the end.

Wanting to show a number has no divisor above 1 but itself, check only up to the number, which bounded-some allows. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The older word “measures”, the older road to the remainder, the oldest definition of a prime, the gcd algorithm, the prime-divides-a-factor technique and the endless-primes proof are all Euclid's, in the Elements. The gcd algorithm is Euclid's algorithm, and prime-divides-a-factor is Euclid's lemma. The plus-only combination technique carries Bézout's name; Bachet had it for numbers a century and more before him. Descent, as a name for the argument, is Fermat's (infinite descent).

Euclid's definition: a prime number is one that is measured by a unit alone.

Euclid, Elements VII, definition 11; paraphrase after Heath's translation (1908)

Euclid sets the problem of finding the greatest common measure of two numbers not prime to one another, and adds, as a corollary, that whatever measures both numbers also measures their greatest common measure.

Euclid, Elements VII.2 and its porism; paraphrase after Heath's translation (1908)

Euclid does not say that there are endlessly many primes. He says that the primes are more than any multitude of primes you care to set out: hand him a list, and he will find one not on it.

Euclid, Elements IX.20; paraphrase after Heath's translation (1908)

Fermat, describing his own methods in a letter late in life, called this way of arguing infinite descent: from a supposed case, produce a smaller one, and the supposed case falls, since the numbers cannot go down forever.

Fermat, letter to Carcavi (1659); paraphrase

Accepted names

Division-with-remainder is the division algorithm. The plus-only combination technique is a form of Bézout's identity. A prime list is a prime factorisation, and prime-list with one-prime-list is the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, or unique factorisation.
