How does a crank turn a wheel into a push?

How does a crank turn a wheel into a push? A wheel turns round and round, but a pump needs a push in a straight line, back and forth. An arm fixed to the wheel’s axle, with a rod hung from its end, does the change. Saws, pumps and engines have used it for close to two thousand years. What makes it hard is that the end of the arm goes round at a steady speed, while the far end of the rod does not move steadily at all. It speeds up, slows down, and stops dead at each end of its stroke. This page shows, with the cos and sin of rung 21, where the piston is for each angle of the crank, how fast it moves, why it pauses at each end, and what the rod adds to a plain back and forth.

The end of the crank goes round a circle. The piston sits where the rod, of fixed length, reaches the piston’s line. So the piston’s place is the crank’s along part plus a root that the rod adds. Its speed is the crank end’s speed times the sine of the angle, times a factor near one. At each end of the stroke the sine is zero, the crank end moves straight across the piston’s line, and the piston stops.

21eng1Where the piston is

A crank is an arm of length r fixed to an axle. Its end, the pin, goes round a circle. A rod of length l, longer than r, joins the pin to the piston, which can only slide along one line through the axle. Put the axle at the origin and the piston’s line along the x-axis. With the crank at angle θ, the pin is at (r cos θ, r sin θ) (21b), and the piston at (x, 0).

(x - r\cos\theta)^2 + (r\sin\theta)^2 = l^2 @@ the rod’s length, by distance (18a)
x - r\cos\theta = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta} @@ the piston on the far side of the pin; l > r keeps the root real
\htmlClass{new}{x(\theta) = r\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add r cos θ
x(0) = l + r,\ \ x(\pi) = l - r @@ the two ends: a stroke of 2r

Place technique: the piston sits at r cos θ + √(l² − r² sin² θ), and moves 2r from end to end. The 12.9-litre diesel in many trucks built by Kenworth, of Kirkland, Washington, has a stroke of 162 mm, so r = 81 mm. Take a rod of l = 260 mm, a little over three times the crank.

x(90^\circ) = \sqrt{260^2 - 81^2} = \sqrt{61\,039} \approx 247.06 \text{ mm} @@ cos 90° = 0, sin 90° = 1
\text{mid-stroke} = \tfrac{341 + 179}{2} = 260 \text{ mm} @@ the ends, l + r and l − r
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at a quarter turn the piston is } 12.94 \text{ mm past the middle}} @@ toward the axle

21eng2How fast the piston moves

Let the crank turn at a steady ω radians a second, so θ = ωt. Take the slope of x in t.

x = r\cos\theta + (l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta)^{1/2} @@ 21·eng.1
x' = -r\omega\sin\theta + \tfrac{-2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\omega}{2\sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta}} @@ the slopes of sin and cos (21g) and the chain rule, in radians (21j)
\htmlClass{new}{x' = -r\omega\sin\theta\,\Big(1 + \tfrac{r\cos\theta}{\sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta}}\Big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ take out −rω sin θ

Speed technique: the piston’s speed is the pin’s speed rω, times sin θ, times a factor near 1 that the rod adds.

\omega = 1500 \cdot \tfrac{2\pi}{60} = 50\pi \approx 157.08 \text{ rad/s} @@ a turn is 2π radians
r\omega = 0.081 \times 157.08 \approx 12.72 \text{ m/s} @@ the pin’s speed
x'(90^\circ) \approx -12.72 \text{ m/s} @@ cos 90° = 0: the factor is 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on average}: \tfrac{2 \times 0.162 \text{ m}}{0.04 \text{ s}} = 8.1 \text{ m/s}} @@ two strokes a turn, a turn every 0.04 s

The piston is fastest a little before the quarter turn, near 74°, at about 13.3 m/s. The rod’s factor is above 1 there, since cos θ is positive.

21eng3Why the piston pauses at each end

At θ = 0 and θ = π, sin θ = 0, so x′ = 0. There the crank lies along the piston’s line, and the pin moves square to the crank (21q), straight across the line. None of its motion is along the line, so the piston stops.

Pause technique: the piston stops at each end of its stroke, because there the pin moves straight across the piston’s line. Near the far end it is slow, and in the middle quick. The rod makes the two halves of the stroke take different shares of the turn.

r\cos\theta + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta} = l @@ the piston at mid-stroke
(l - r\cos\theta)^2 = l^2 - r^2 + r^2\cos^2\theta @@ square both sides; sin² θ = 1 − cos² θ (21c)
\cos\theta = \tfrac{r}{2l} = \tfrac{81}{520} \approx 0.1558 @@ the l² and r² cos² θ cancel
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \approx 81.0^\circ:\ \text{far half in } 81^\circ,\ \text{near half in } 99^\circ} @@ arccos (21m); the same on the way back

The pause is why an engine carries a heavy wheel: at each end a push on the piston gives no turn, and the wheel carries the crank through. An oil pump in the Kern County fields nods its head several times a minute. At each end of its stroke the long rod down the well comes to rest gently, not with a jerk.

21eng4A slot instead of a rod

Anexact, yet rigorous · the triangle: a motion split into parts

The pin moves round its circle at speed rω. Split that motion along the piston’s line and across it: −rω sin θ along, and rω cos θ across. A rod passes on the along part, and some of the across part too, through its tilt.

Split a slanted change into parts along directions you choose, and say which directions you chose.

A slotted yoke, a bar with a slot square to the piston’s line that the pin slides in, passes on the along part only. Then x = r cos θ exactly, and the piston swings like 21i’s wave.

Turn the yoke’s slot so that it is no longer square to the piston’s line. Which part of the pin’s motion does the piston take now, and along which directions did you split it?

x_{\text{slot}} = r\cos\theta @@ the along part only: a wave of amplitude r (21i)
x_{\text{rod}} - (l + r\cos\theta) = \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta} - l @@ 21·eng.1, measured from the slot’s middle
= -\tfrac{r^2\sin^2\theta}{l + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta}} @@ times the sum over the sum
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at most } \tfrac{r^2}{l + \sqrt{l^2 - r^2}} = \tfrac{6561}{507.06} \approx 12.94 \text{ mm, at a quarter turn}} @@ largest where sin² θ = 1

Slot technique: with a slot the piston swings exactly as r cos θ, and a rod pulls it toward the axle by at most r²/(l + √(l² − r²)), less as the rod grows. A long rod makes the crank nearly a slot.

The accepted names

The crank, rod and piston together are a slider-crank mechanism; the rod is the connecting rod, the pin the crankpin, and the piston’s pin the wrist pin or gudgeon pin. The two ends of the stroke are top dead centre and bottom dead centre. l/r is the rod ratio. The slot is a Scotch yoke, and the rod’s pull is the second-order term of the piston’s motion. The heavy wheel is a flywheel. The average piston speed, 8.1 m/s here, is the mean piston speed. An oil pump of this kind is a pumpjack, or nodding donkey.

Who said it first

A stone carving at Hierapolis, in what is now Turkey, shows a water-driven sawmill with a crank and rod from the third century. Ismail al-Jazari (1206) drew a crank and rod in a water-raising machine. In 21·eng.6.5, James Pickard patented the crank for steam engines in 1780, and James Watt built the sun-and-planet gear (1781) to go round it; Watt argued that the crank was as old as the foot-driven lathe. The patent ran out in 1794, and engines took the crank back.
