Why Does a Pendulum Keep Time?

Why does a pendulum keep time? A weight on a string, pushed a little, swings back and forth, and each swing takes the same time as the last, even as the swings die down. For about three hundred years the best clocks in the world were built on that one fact. What makes it surprising is that a smaller swing covers less ground, and still takes as long. What makes it hard is that the fact is not quite true. This page shows, with rung twenty-four’s model equations and rung twenty-three’s series, why a spring keeps perfect time, why a pendulum keeps nearly perfect time for small swings, how much a wider swing loses each day, and how the same rules on rates bring a falling body to a steady speed.

A weight on a spring is pulled back in proportion to how far it is from the middle, and the rule that says so has only one kind of answer: a wave of fixed period, whatever its size. Its energy stays the same, and that fixes how wide it swings. A pendulum is pulled back by the sine of its angle, which is nearly the angle for small swings, so it nearly obeys the spring’s rule. For wider swings the pull falls short, and each swing takes a little longer, by about a sixteenth of the square of the swing. A body falling through air gains speed until the drag matches its weight, and that balance is a state that holds.

24phys1A Spring Keeps Time

A mass m on a spring is pulled back toward the middle with a force −κx, where x is how far it sits from the middle and κ is the spring’s stiffness (13k). Force is mass times acceleration (13·phys). So mx″ = −κx, which is 24h’s model equation y″ = −n²y with n = √(κ/m).

x'' = -n^2x,\ \ n = \sqrt{\kappa/m} @@ the spring law and force is mass times acceleration
x = a\cos nt + b\sin nt @@ 24h: every solution, with a = x(0), b = x′(0)/n
x = C\cos(nt - \varphi),\ \ C = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} @@ the sum technique (21d): a = C cos φ, b = C sin φ
x\big(t + \tfrac{2\pi}{n}\big) = x(t) @@ cos repeats after 2π
\htmlClass{new}{\text{period } 2\pi\sqrt{m/\kappa}, \text{ whatever } C \text{ and } \varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ C and φ come from the start; n from the spring and the mass

Even-time technique: a mass on a spring swings with period 2π√(m/κ), however wide its swing and however it was started. C is the amplitude, n the turn rate, n/2π the frequency, and φ the phase: where in its swing it was at t = 0. A 0.5 kg mass on a spring of stiffness 20 N/m swings once in 0.99 s. This φ (little phi) is not 2e's condition.

Frequency is what an ear hears as pitch. A siren that sends out crests f times a second, coming toward you at speed v, starts each crest v/f closer than the last. Sound runs at c ≈ 343 m/s, so the crests arrive c/(c − v) times as often. A 700-a-second siren at 25 m/s is heard at about 755 a second coming, and 652 going: the drop in pitch as it passes.

24phys2Energy Fixes the Size

16·phys found that a spring stretched by x stores ½κx², and a moving mass carries ½mv². Add them.

E = \tfrac12 m x'^2 + \tfrac12 \kappa x^2 @@ moving, plus stored (16·phys)
E' = m x' x'' + \kappa x x' = x'\,(m x'' + \kappa x) @@ the chain rule
E' = 0 @@ mx″ = −κx (24·phys.1)
E \text{ is constant} @@ zero-slope-is-constant (14d)
\htmlClass{new}{C = \sqrt{2E/\kappa},\ \ \text{top speed } nC} \quad \blacksquare @@ at the ends x′ = 0; at the middle x = 0

Swing-size technique: the energy of a swinging spring never changes, so the swing’s width and its top speed are fixed by it. Real springs lose a little energy each swing to the air and the metal. The swing shrinks, and by the even-time technique the period does not.

24phys3A Pendulum, Swinging a Little

A weight on a string of length L swings at angle θ from straight down. Its path is an arc, and its distance along the arc is Lθ. Gravity pulls it along the arc by g sin θ, back toward the bottom.

L\theta'' = -g\sin\theta @@ the pull along the arc, per unit of mass
\sin\theta = \theta\,\big(1 + \text{a part that heads to } 0\big) @@ 21f: sin θ / θ → 1
\theta'' \approx -\tfrac{g}{L}\,\theta @@ for small swings
\htmlClass{new}{T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the even-time technique, with n² = g/L

Small-swing technique: a pendulum swinging a little takes 2π√(L/g) for each swing there and back, whatever its weight. A pendulum whose swing there and back takes 2 s, one tick each way, is 0.994 m long. The pendulum hung in Griffith Observatory in Los Angeles is about 12.2 m long; it should take about 7.0 s.

24phys4A Wide Swing Runs Slow

Now let the swing be wide, released at angle A. Energy, per unit of mass, says how fast the weight moves at each θ. Then the time is an area, and the binomial series opens it.

\tfrac12 L^2\theta'^2 = gL(\cos\theta - \cos A) @@ speed Lθ′; the weight has dropped L(cos θ − cos A) since the top (16·phys)
\cos\theta - \cos A = 2\big(k^2 - \sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big),\ \ k = \sin\tfrac{A}{2} @@ cos θ = 1 − 2 sin²(θ/2) (21d)
\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} = k\sin\varphi:\ \ dt = \sqrt{\tfrac{L}{g}}\,\tfrac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\varphi}} @@ substitution (16d); φ runs 0 to π/2 as θ runs 0 to A
T = 4\sqrt{\tfrac{L}{g}}\int_0^{\pi/2}\big(1 - k^2\sin^2\varphi\big)^{-1/2}\,d\varphi @@ a quarter swing, four times
(1 - u)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac{u}{2} + \tfrac{3u^2}{8} + \cdots,\ \ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\varphi\,d\varphi = \tfrac{\pi}{4} @@ the binomial series (23h); sin² φ = (1 − cos 2φ)/2
\htmlClass{new}{T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{L}{g}}\,\big(1 + \tfrac{k^2}{4} + \cdots\big) \approx T_0\big(1 + \tfrac{A^2}{16}\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ term by term (23e); k ≈ A/2 for small A

Slow-swing technique: a pendulum released at A radians takes longer than the small-swing time by about a share A²/16. At 5° that is 0.05%, about 41 seconds a day. At 10° it is about 165 seconds a day. At 90° the swing takes 18% longer.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with an exception rate

A pendulum’s even time is a law with a range. Inside its range it is excellent: under 2°, it holds to better than one part in ten thousand. Outside it, it fails by an amount the slow-swing technique states before anyone measures.

Keep a law where its exception rate is known, and keep the case inside the range where it is small.

So clockmakers did not give up the law. They kept the swing small and, above all, the same from day to day. A clock whose swing stays at 4° runs slow by a fixed share, which is set right once by moving the weight. A clock whose swing drifts between 4° and 6° gains and loses by turns, about 33 seconds a day apart.

A clock in a Portland shop gains its swing from a spring that weakens over the week, so its swing shrinks from 6° to 4°. Would you expect it to run fast or slow by Sunday, and by how much a day?

24phys5A Fall That Settles

A small body falling through still air feels its weight and a drag. For small, slow bodies a measured law makes the drag grow in proportion to the speed: per unit of mass, v′ = g − kv.

v' + kv = g @@ weight, less a drag in proportion to speed
\big(e^{kt}v\big)' = g\,e^{kt} @@ the integrating factor (24g)
v = \tfrac{g}{k}\big(1 - e^{-kt}\big) @@ from rest, v(0) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{v \to \tfrac{g}{k}:\ \text{the state left alone, and it holds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope of g − kv in v is −k < 0 (24k)

Steady-fall technique: a falling body gains speed until the drag matches its weight, and that speed is a state that holds. For a skydiver the drag grows as the square of the speed: v′ = g − cv². The state left alone is v* = √(g/c), and the slope there is −2cv* < 0, so it holds too. Belly down, skydivers settle near 55 m/s, so c ≈ 0.0032 per metre.

The accepted names

A swing obeying x″ = −n²x is simple harmonic motion; n is the angular frequency, often written ω, and κ the spring constant. The constancy of the period is isochronism. The area in the slow-swing technique is a complete elliptic integral of the first kind. A drag in proportion to speed is Stokes drag; one growing as the square of speed is quadratic drag, and the steady speed is terminal velocity. The change of pitch of a moving siren is the Doppler shift, named again here.

Who said it first

Galileo Galilei claimed that swings of every width take the same time (letter of 1602; Two New Sciences, 1638). Marin Mersenne’s measurements in the 1630s found wide swings slower. Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock (1656) and showed in Horologium Oscillatorium (1673) that only a weight running on a cycloid keeps exactly even time. Robert Hooke gave the spring law (1678). George Gabriel Stokes found the drag on a slow sphere (1851). Christian Doppler proposed the shift for light and sound (1842), and Christophorus Buys Ballot tested it with trumpeters on a moving train (1845). In 24·phys.7.5, the water clock is Galileo’s and the 60° swings are Mersenne’s.
