Why buy both insurance and lottery tickets?

Why buy both insurance and lottery tickets? A family in Boise pays 650 dollars a year to insure a house against fire, though the fire company’s own tables say the average loss is about 400. On the way home one of them buys a two-dollar lottery ticket whose prizes, counted with their chances, are worth well under a dollar. In both deals the buyer pays more than the average comes to. In the first, the buyer pays to be rid of a risk. In the second, the buyer pays to take one on. What makes the question hard is that one person does both in one afternoon, and both look sensible to most people who do them. Averages alone cannot explain either. This page values a gamble by what it is worth to the person holding it, proves that a careful person pays to shed risk, shows that the same rule forbids the lottery ticket, and then turns to the company on the other side, which can take on ten thousand fires because their spreads add as squares of arrows at right angles.

A gamble is worth to a person the sure amount with the same average worth, and when worth bends down that sure amount is below the gamble’s average: so a careful person pays to shed risk. The same bend forbids a fair or worse lottery, so buying both needs another story. An insurer pools many policies, and their spread per policy falls as one over the root of their number, down to a floor set by how much they co-spread.

31econ1What a gamble is worth to you

Let W be what a person will have, a chance quantity, and let u(w) be what having w is worth to that person, rising with w. Value W by its average worth E u(W), and call the sure amount c with u(c) = E u(W) the gamble’s sure worth. Most people value the hundred-thousandth dollar less than the first, so u bends down: its slope falls as w rises.

Bends-down technique: if u bends down, then E u(W) ≤ u(E W), so the sure worth of any gamble is at most its average; and a person will pay up to E W − c to swap the gamble for a sure E W.

m = \operatorname{E}W @@ the average
u(w) \le u(m) + u'(m)(w - m) \text{ for every } w @@ a curve that bends down lies under its tangent line (13k)
\operatorname{E}u(W) \le u(m) + u'(m)\operatorname{E}(W - m) = u(m) @@ average both sides; E(W − m) = 0 (30g)
u(c) = \operatorname{E}u(W) \le u(m) @@ the sure worth
\htmlClass{new}{c \le \operatorname{E}W} \quad \blacksquare @@ u rises, so a smaller worth is a smaller amount
  
W = 300\,000,\ \text{or } 100\,000 \text{ with chance } \tfrac1{500} @@ a fire takes 200 000 dollars
\operatorname{E}W = 300\,000 - 400 @@ the average loss is 400
u = \ln:\ \ \operatorname{E}\ln W = \tfrac{499}{500}\ln 300\,000 + \tfrac1{500}\ln 100\,000 @@ worth by logs (17a)
c = e^{\operatorname{E}\ln W} \approx 299\,342 @@ the sure worth
\htmlClass{new}{\text{pays up to } 300\,000 - 299\,342 = 658} \quad \blacksquare @@ 258 above the average loss, to be rid of the risk

Near the average, a small gamble with spread σ² costs about ½(−u″/u′)σ² below its average: the second slope of u, read along the tangent. For ln that is σ²/(2m). For the house the rule gives 400 + 133, short of 658, because a fire is not small next to the house.

31econ2The lottery ticket

A big lottery sells a two-dollar ticket with a top prize of about 100 million dollars, after tax, at a chance of about 1 in 292 million, and smaller prizes besides. The top prize, counted with its chance, is worth about 34 cents.

\operatorname{E}\ln W = (1 - q)\ln(W_0 - 2) + q\ln(W_0 - 2 + 10^8) @@ q ≈ 1/292 million; W₀ = 300 000
c - (W_0 - 2) \approx 0.006 @@ the top prize’s sure worth: under a cent
\text{price } 2 > \operatorname{E}(\text{prizes}) > \text{sure worth} @@ the bends-down technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{with } u \text{ bending down, the ticket is refused}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and every ticket priced at or above its average

Both-ways technique: if u bends down everywhere, a person never pays more than its average for a gamble and always pays something to shed one; so a person who buys insurance and also buys a ticket priced above its average cannot be valuing both by one u that bends down everywhere. The first half is the bends-down technique for the ticket. The second is the same for the house.

There are two ways out, and the evidence does not choose between them. Worth may bend down for small changes and up across a jump in how one lives, so a ticket buys a chance at a different life, while a fire threatens the one in hand. Or people may not value by averages of worth at all, and give a chance of 1 in 292 million far more weight than its size. Either way, the average alone cannot be the rule that decides.

31econ3Ten thousand fires

The company on the other side takes the house’s risk for 658 dollars or less, though its own average cost is 400. It can, because it holds many houses. Treat each policy’s payout, less its average, as an arrow, with products given by co-spread, as 30g’s box and 31a do. The spread of a payout is its arrow’s squared size, and two payouts with correlation ρ are arrows at an angle with cosine ρ (31l1).

Pool-floor technique: if n payouts each have spread σ² and every two have co-spread ρσ², the average payout has spread σ²(ρ + (1 − ρ)/n); it falls towards ρσ² as n grows, and to 0 only when ρ = 0.

\big|\textstyle\sum_i X_i\big|^2 = \sum_i |X_i|^2 + \sum_{i \ne j}\langle X_i, X_j\rangle @@ straight in each slot (31a1)
= n\sigma^2 + n(n - 1)\rho\sigma^2 @@ n squares, n(n − 1) cross products
\operatorname{Var}\big(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum X_i\big) = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{n} + \tfrac{n - 1}{n}\rho\sigma^2 @@ divide by n²
\htmlClass{new}{= \sigma^2\big(\rho + \tfrac{1 - \rho}{n}\big) \ge \rho\sigma^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ n arrows at right angles add only as squares
  
\sigma = 200\,000\sqrt{\tfrac1{500}\cdot\tfrac{499}{500}} \approx 8935 @@ one policy’s spread width
n = 10\,000,\ \rho = 0:\ \ 8935/100 \approx 89 @@ a width of 89 dollars on an average of 400
n = 10\,000,\ \rho = 0.01:\ \ 8935\sqrt{0.0101} \approx 898 @@ a small shared risk
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a pool with } \rho = 0.01 \text{ is no safer than about } 100 \text{ houses}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the floor is √ρ σ ≈ 894

Fires in separate towns hardly co-spread. Fires in one dry valley in one windy summer do, and so do floods along one river and storms along one coast. That is why a company limits how many houses it holds in one place, and why it insures itself with companies that hold risks elsewhere. The arithmetic of right angles is what makes pooling work, and a small angle short of a right angle is what makes it fail.

The accepted names

The worth function is a utility function, and valuing by E u(W) is expected utility theory. The sure worth is the certainty equivalent, and E W − c the risk premium; the bends-down technique is Jensen’s inequality for concave functions, and the small-gamble rule uses the Arrow–Pratt measure of absolute risk aversion −u″/u′. The puzzle of the both-ways technique is the insurance–lottery puzzle. Pooling is the law of large numbers for insurance, and the floor ρσ² is undiversifiable or systematic risk; an insurer’s insurance is reinsurance.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli valued gambles by the log of wealth in 1738, answering a puzzle of his cousin Nicolas. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern set out the rules for average worth in 1944. Milton Friedman and Leonard Savage gave worth a bend upward in its middle in 1948 to explain both purchases, and Harry Markowitz moved the bend to the person’s present wealth in 1952. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave small chances extra weight in 1979. Johan Jensen proved the inequality for bending curves in 1906; Kenneth Arrow and John Pratt measured the bend in 1964 and 1965.
