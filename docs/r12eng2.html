Why Does a Fan Twice as Fast Use Eight Times the Power?

Why does a fan twice as fast use eight times the power? Turn a fan up from half speed to full and it moves twice the air, pushes it twice as hard twice over, and draws eight times the power. Pumps and fans of every size follow the same three powers of speed. This page builds the flow, the push and the power from how fast the blades meet the air, scales a pump up by its size as well as its speed, and uses 12a to show that some speed between two settings gives exactly the flow a building needs.

Every speed in the fan scales with its turning speed n. Air moved per second scales with n; the push, a pressure, scales with the square of a speed, n²; power is flow times push, n³. Making the fan D times bigger multiplies the flow by D³ and the push by D², so the power by D⁵.

12eng21Flow Grows With the Speed

Mike looks after the air handling in a school. Its big fan turns n times a minute. Each turn, the blades sweep a fixed volume of air through the duct. So the air moved per minute, the flow Q, is that volume times the turns a minute.

Q = (\text{volume per turn}) \cdot n @@ a fixed fan
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}} @@ twice the speed, twice the flow

This holds as long as the duct and the fan stay the same: the air leaves the blades at the same angles at every speed, only faster.

12eng22Push Grows as Its Square

The fan pushes the air into the duct with a pressure, its push p. The push comes from speeding the air up: the air’s energy of motion per cubic metre is half its density times its speed squared, and every speed in the fan is proportional to n.

\text{air speed} \propto n @@ every speed scales with the turning
p \propto \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(\text{air speed})^2 \propto n^2 @@ energy of motion per volume
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\right)^2} @@ twice the speed, four times the push

12eng23Power Grows as Its Cube

Power is the work done each second: pushing a volume Q each second against pressure p takes p·Q (pressure times volume is energy, 5j).

P = p \cdot Q @@ push times flow
P \propto n^2 \cdot n = n^3 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{P_2}{P_1} = \left(\dfrac{n_2}{n_1}\right)^3} @@ twice the speed, eight times the power

Fan-scaling technique: for one fan in one duct, flow goes as the speed, push as its square and power as its cube. Run the other way, it pays: turning a fan down to 80% speed cuts its flow to 80% but its power to 0.8³ ≈ 51%. That is why buildings now fit fans with speed controls instead of dampers that choke the air.

12eng24Scaling a Pump Up by Its Size

An engineer testing a small model pump wants to know what a pump of the same shape, D times bigger, will do. Every length scales by D: the volume swept per turn by D³, and each speed at a given turning rate by D.

Q \propto n\,D^3 @@ volume per turn grows as D³
p \propto n^2 D^2 @@ speeds grow as nD
P = p\,Q \propto n^3 D^5 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{D = 2,\ \text{same } n:\ 8 \text{ times the flow, } 32 \text{ times the power}} @@ size matters more than speed

A pump maker tests a model a quarter the size, measures its flow and push at one speed, and multiplies by these powers to predict the full pump before building it. The full pump, tested, lands close to the prediction when the two are truly the same shape.

12eng25Some Speed Gives Exactly the Flow You Need

The school needs 12 cubic metres of air a second. At 600 turns a minute the fan gives 10; at 900 it gives 15. Is there a speed that gives exactly 12?

Q(600) = 10,\ \ Q(900) = 15 @@ the fan’s two settings
Q \text{ is continuous in } n @@ flow changes without jumps as the speed changes
10 < 12 < 15 @@ 
\text{some } n \text{ in } (600, 900) \text{ has } Q(n) = 12 @@ the intermediate value technique (12a)
\htmlClass{new}{Q \propto n:\ n = 600 \cdot \tfrac{12}{10} = 720} @@ and here it can be found exactly

Between two speeds that give too little and too much, some speed gives exactly the flow needed. The technique says a speed exists even when the fan’s real curve is not a clean proportion; a commissioning engineer then finds it by halving the gap, as 12e does, adjusting the drive and reading a flow meter until it says 12.

Accepted names

The three powers of speed are the fan laws or affinity laws. Same shape at a different size is geometric similarity, and scaling by D³ and D⁵ is the affinity law for diameter. The push is the fan’s pressure rise or a pump’s head.

Who said it first

The scaling follows from treating every speed in a machine as proportional to its tip speed; it is taught in every first course on fluid machines, and engineering bodies such as the Air Movement and Control Association publish its derivation.
