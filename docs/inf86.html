Can a Count Reveal No One?

Can a count reveal no one? A table of counts names nobody, and still it tells on people: the count for a town, less the count for the town without one household, is that household’s answer. In 2003 it was proved that anyone given enough counts that are accurate enough can rebuild nearly the whole list behind them. So a count that reveals no one cannot be exact. In 2006 a promise was written down that noise can keep: whatever report comes out, it would have been nearly as likely had any one person’s row been different. The promise has a size, ε. Two arrow facts make it usable. Any map applied after a private release is still private, and along a row of releases the sizes add. Since 2021 the census of the United States has been published under this promise, and what size of ε is small enough is in dispute.

Rebuild: answers within E for every group fix the list up to 4E rows. Coin answers: P(yes | yes) = ¾, P(yes | no) = ¼, ratio 3 = e^ε; p̂ = 2q̂ − ½, width at most 1/√n. ε-private: P(M(D) ∈ S) ≤ e^ε·P(M(D′) ∈ S) for lists differing in one row. Count + noise of density (ε/2)e^(−ε|z|): ε-private, width √2/ε. A map after: still ε. Two releases: ε₁ + ε₂.

inf86.1Exact Counts Tell on People

Let a list hold one bit for each of n people, d₁ to dₙ: 1 for yes, 0 for no. A group is any set S of the people, and its count is the number of yes among them. Suppose the keeper of the list answers the count of every group asked, each answer within E of the truth.

List-rebuild technique: if a list c of n bits agrees with every answer to within E, then c differs from the true list d in at most 4E places.

S = \{i : d_i = 1,\ c_i = 0\}:\quad \text{true count } |S|,\ \text{count under } c \text{ is } 0 @@ the people c gets wrong one way
\big|\,|S| - a_S\big| \le E,\quad |0 - a_S| \le E @@ the answer a_S is within E of the truth, and c agrees with it within E
|S| \le 2E @@ the triangle inequality (8g)
\{i : d_i = 0,\ c_i = 1\} \text{ has at most } 2E \text{ members} @@ the same way
\htmlClass{new}{d \text{ and } c \text{ differ in at most } 4E \text{ places}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the two

So with answers good to within 2, anyone who tries lists until one agrees has all but 8 rows of a list of a million. That search is long. In 2003 it was shown that a short one serves: a small number of groups picked at random gives the list away. A keeper who answers many counts must answer them badly, or the list is out.

inf86.2A Coin for Each Answer

The oldest cure puts the noise in before the list exists. Each person tosses a coin unseen. On heads the person reports the truth. On tails the person tosses again, and reports yes on heads and no on tails.

Coin-answer technique: a report is at most 3 times as likely under one truth as under the other; and if q̂ is the share of yes reports among n people, p̂ = 2q̂ − ½ has for its average the true share p, with width at most 1/√n.

P(\text{yes} \mid \text{truth yes}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34,\quad P(\text{yes} \mid \text{truth no}) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 @@ the branch-product technique (30l)
\tfrac34 \div \tfrac14 = 3 @@ and the same for a report of no
q = P(\text{report yes}) = \tfrac34 p + \tfrac14(1 - p) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{p}{2} @@ the whole-chance technique (30f)
E\,\hat p = 2q - \tfrac12 = p @@ averages add (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{width of } \hat p = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{q(1 - q)}{n}} \le \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sample-average technique (30q1); q(1 − q) ≤ ¼

No single report proves anything about its sender: a yes may be the second coin. Yet with 10 000 people the share is known to about 1 part in 100. The price is in the count, which is off by about √n people, and it is paid because every person adds noise of their own.

inf86.3Noise Sized to One Person

A release M takes a list D and gives a report, with chances that may depend on D. Two lists are neighbours when they differ in one person’s row. M is ε-private when, for all neighbours D and D′ and every set S of reports, P(M(D) ∈ S) ≤ eε·P(M(D′) ∈ S). The coin answers are ε-private with eε = 3. A keeper who holds the true list can do better with one draw of noise. Let c(D) be a count, and let Z have the density (ε/2)·e−ε|z|, a peak at 0 that falls off the same on both sides.

Noised-count technique: the release c(D) + Z is ε-private, and its error has width √2/ε, whatever the length of the list.

\text{density of the report at } t:\ \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,e^{-\varepsilon|t - c|},\quad c = c(D) @@ the noise, shifted by c (30m)
\tfrac{e^{-\varepsilon|t - c|}}{e^{-\varepsilon|t - c'|}} = e^{\varepsilon(|t - c'| - |t - c|)} \le e^{\varepsilon|c - c'|} \le e^{\varepsilon} @@ the triangle inequality (8g); neighbours’ counts differ by at most 1
P(M(D) \in S) = \textstyle\int_S \le e^{\varepsilon}\textstyle\int_S = e^{\varepsilon}\,P(M(D') \in S) @@ integrate the two densities over S (30m)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Var} Z = \textstyle\int z^2\,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}e^{-\varepsilon|z|}\,dz = \tfrac{2}{\varepsilon^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the density-average technique (30m); width √2/ε

With ε = 0.1 the count is off by about 14 people, in a village or in a country. The coin answers of ten million people are off by thousands. The noise here is sized to the most that one person can change, which for a count is 1, and not to the list.

inf86.4Arrows Keep the Promise

Afterwards technique: if M is ε-private and g is any map on reports, then g after M is ε-private.

g(M(D)) \in S \iff M(D) \in g^{-1}(S) @@ g⁻¹(S): the reports that g sends into S
P(M(D) \in g^{-1}(S)) \le e^{\varepsilon} P(M(D') \in g^{-1}(S)) @@ M is ε-private, and g⁻¹(S) is a set of reports
\htmlClass{new}{P(g(M(D)) \in S) \le e^{\varepsilon}\,P(g(M(D')) \in S)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first line, used on both sides

Losses-add technique: if M₁ is ε₁-private and M₂ is ε₂-private, with noise drawn apart, then releasing both is (ε₁ + ε₂)-private.

P(M_1(D) = a,\ M_2(D) = b) = P(M_1(D) = a)\,P(M_2(D) = b) @@ drawn apart (30l2); reports taken from a list, for plainness
\le e^{\varepsilon_1} P(M_1(D') = a) \cdot e^{\varepsilon_2} P(M_2(D') = b) @@ each is private
= e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\,P(M_1(D') = a,\ M_2(D') = b) @@ drawn apart, again
\htmlClass{new}{P((M_1, M_2)(D) \in S) \le e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\,P((M_1, M_2)(D') \in S)} \quad \blacksquare @@ add over the pairs (a, b) in S

Read both with arrows. A private release is an arrow from lists to reports that carries a number, its ε. A map after it costs nothing, and arrows in a row add their numbers, with “at most” in place of “is”. That is the kind of arrow ∞Η found in a distance. So the promise of a long chain of work is read off its pieces, and what was computed from the reports, in what order, and how it was arranged into tables, is out of sight. It also says why the first footing had to come out as it did. Each count answered spends some ε. To answer k counts within a total of ε, each gets ε/k, and its noise grows k times.

inf86.5In Use, and in Dispute

  • The coin answers date from 1965, for surveys on what people hide. The rebuilding is from 2003, and the promise of this page, with noise sized to what one person can change, from 2006.
  • From 2014 a browser gathered habits of use from its users by coin answers made on each device, and from 2016 a maker of telephones used the promise as well.
  • In 2016 the walk downhill of ∞9 was made private. The slope from each example is cut down to a fixed size, the cut slopes are added, bell-shaped noise is added to the sum, and the step is taken. Each step is a private release, and the losses are added along the walk.
  • In 2021 the United States published the first counts of its 2020 census under the promise. Noise was added to a set of counts, and the tables were then made to agree with one another by maps applied afterwards. The total was set in June 2021 at ε = 19.61.

An ε of 19.61 allows a factor of e19.61, over three hundred million, and as a bare promise that is nearly none. Some argue that the loss in fact is far below the sum that the losses-add technique allows. Others argue that the noise already harms the counts of small places. How ε should be chosen, and by whom, has no agreed answer. A second thread joins ∞D: a learner that is private cannot lean on any one example, so it cannot fit by luck, and in 2015 private answers were shown to stay true on fresh draws.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1965 the coin answer was given as a way to ask about what people hide. The census of the United States protected its lists from 1990 to 2010 by swapping rows between households, a method that came with no stated size of promise.

In 2003 came the proof that accurate answers to many counts give a list away, and in 2006 the promise with a size, the noise that keeps it, and the two facts that make it compose.

From 2014 the promise went into use in browsers and telephones, in 2016 into the training of learning machines, and in 2021 into a national census.

The accepted names

The promise is ε-differential privacy, and ε is the privacy loss, or privacy budget; neighbours are neighbouring, or adjacent, datasets, and a release is a mechanism. The rebuilding is a reconstruction attack, and the rule that too many accurate answers give a list away is the fundamental law of information recovery; subtracting two counts is a differencing attack. The coin answer is randomized response, a case of local differential privacy; a trusted keeper adding noise is the central model. The noise density (ε/2)e^(−ε|z|) is the Laplace distribution, the release the Laplace mechanism, and the most one person can change is the sensitivity. The afterwards technique is closure under post-processing; the losses-add technique is basic composition, and sharper sums for many releases are advanced composition. The private walk is differentially private stochastic gradient descent, with gradient clipping. The census method is the TopDown algorithm.

Who said it first

Cynthia Dwork, Frank McSherry, Kobbi Nissim and Adam Smith, “Calibrating noise to sensitivity in private data analysis” (2006). Úlfar Erlingsson, Vasyl Pihur and Aleksandra Korolova, on coin answers in a browser (2014). Martín Abadi and six others, “Deep learning with differential privacy” (2016). John Abowd and thirteen others of the United States Census Bureau, on the method of the 2020 census (2022). Cynthia Dwork and co-authors on private answers and fresh draws (2015).
