16·econ · economics

Who gains from a trade, and how much?

Who gains from a trade, and how much? A trade happens only when both sides want it, so both must gain. But a sale leaves no record of the gain. The receipt shows the price, and the price is the one number on which buyer and seller agreed. The buyer would have paid more, and the seller would have taken less, and how much more and how much less is written nowhere. People asked the question in earnest when governments began to tax trade and to fix prices, because each of those changes who gains and how much. This page reads the gains as areas between a demand curve, a supply curve and a price. Then it finds, by areas, what a tax or a fixed price destroys, and who carries a tax.

The buyers’ gain is the area between what they would have paid and the market price. The sellers’ gain is the area between the price and what they would have taken. Together those gains are greatest at the amount the market trades. A tax shrinks trade to the amount where the gap between what buyers will pay and what sellers will take is the tax, and the gains on the trades it stops are lost to everyone. When the two curves are straight, how much of the tax buyers carry depends on how steep each curve is.

Two curves

The Pajaro Valley, around Watsonville, on the line between Santa Cruz and Monterey Counties, is one of the great strawberry districts of the state. Take a week's trade in flats of its strawberries, a flat being eight baskets. Count q in thousands of flats, and prices in dollars a flat.

Sort the buyers from the keenest down. The demand curve D(q) is the most that the buyer of the q-th flat would pay. Sort the sellers from the cheapest up. The supply curve S(q) is the least that the seller of the q-th flat would take. D falls and S rises. Here is a model with round numbers, not a measurement.

D(q) = 40 - q^2,\ \ S(q) = 4 + 5q,\ \ 0 \le q \le 6 @@ q in thousands of flats; dollars a flat
D(q) - S(q) = 36 - 5q - q^2:\ \ 36 \text{ at } 0,\ -30 \text{ at } 6 @@ continuous, so it is 0 somewhere: in-between-value (12a)
(D - S)' = -5 - 2q \lt 0 @@ it falls, so it is 0 only once
q^* = 4,\ \ p^* = D(4) = S(4) = 24 @@ 36 − 5q − q² = −(q + 9)(q − 4): the market quantity and price

The gains are areas

A buyer who would pay D(q), and pays p*, gains D(q) − p*. The buyers of the flats from q to q + Δq gain about (D(q) − p*) Δq between them. By strip sums, the buyers' gain is ∫₀^{q*} (D(q) − p*) dq: the area between the demand curve and the price. In the same way the sellers' gain is ∫₀^{q*} (p* − S(q)) dq. Their accepted names are consumer surplus and producer surplus.

\text{buyers}: \textstyle\int_0^4 (40 - q^2 - 24)\,dq = 64 - \tfrac{64}{3} \approx 42.67 @@ the table (16f); thousands of dollars a week
\text{sellers}: \textstyle\int_0^4 (24 - 4 - 5q)\,dq = 80 - 40 = 40 @@ the same
\text{total}: \textstyle\int_0^4 (D - S)\,dq \approx 82.67 @@ areas-add: the price cancels; area-between (16g)

The price moves gain from one side to the other. The total does not depend on it: only on how many flats are traded, and between whom.

Total-gain technique: trading exactly the market quantity makes the total gain as large as it can be. Let D and S be continuous, with D − S positive before q* and negative after. Let the q flats traded go to the keenest buyers and come from the cheapest sellers. Then the total gain G(q) = ∫₀^q (D − S) is greatest at q = q*.

G(q) = \textstyle\int_0^q \big(D(s) - S(s)\big)\,ds @@ the buyers' and sellers' gains added, at any one price
G'(q) = D(q) - S(q) @@ area-slope (16b): D − S continuous
G' \gt 0 \text{ before } q^*,\ \ G' \lt 0 \text{ after} @@ the condition
\htmlClass{new}{G \text{ is greatest at } q^*} \quad \blacksquare @@ first-test (14f)

Past q*, each extra flat costs its seller more than it is worth to its buyer. Before q*, a flat left unsold is a gain left on the table.

What a tax destroys

Let the state take t dollars on each flat. Buyers pay p_b, and sellers keep p_s = p_b − t. Buyers take a flat while D(q) ≥ p_b, and sellers offer it while S(q) ≤ p_s. So trade stops at q_t, where D(q_t) − S(q_t) = t.

Lost-gain technique: a tax destroys the gain on the trades it stops, and only that. The buyers' gain, the sellers' gain and the tax raised add to G(q_t). So the gain destroyed is G(q*) − G(q_t) = ∫_{q_t}^{q*} (D − S). Its accepted name is deadweight loss.

\textstyle\int_0^{q_t} (D - p_b) + \int_0^{q_t} (p_s - S) + t\,q_t @@ buyers' gain, sellers' gain, tax raised
= \textstyle\int_0^{q_t} (D - S) + q_t\,(p_s - p_b + t) = G(q_t) @@ areas-add (15e); p_s − p_b + t = 0
\htmlClass{new}{G(q^*) - G(q_t) = \textstyle\int_{q_t}^{q^*} (D - S)} \quad \blacksquare @@ split-in-any-order (16a)
  
36 - 5q - q^2 = 12 \iff (q + 8)(q - 3) = 0 \Rightarrow q_t = 3 @@ D − S = t
p_b = D(3) = 31,\ \ p_s = S(3) = 19 @@ buyers pay $7 more, sellers keep $5 less
\text{buyers } 18,\ \text{sellers } 22.5,\ \text{tax } 36:\ \text{total } 76.5 @@ ∫₀³ (9 − q²) = 18; ∫₀³ (15 − 5q) = 22.5; 12 · 3
\textstyle\int_3^4 (36 - 5q - q^2)\,dq = 36 - 17.5 - \tfrac{37}{3} \approx 6.17 @@ lost-gain: 82.67 − 76.5

The tax raised, 36, is not lost. It moves from the traders to the state. What is lost is the gain on the thousand flats a week that are no longer grown and eaten.

Who carries a tax?

In the strawberry model, buyers carry $7 of the $12, and sellers $5. Who-carries technique: the side whose curve is steeper carries more of a tax. Let D(q) = A − bq and S(q) = c + dq, with b, d > 0, and a tax t < A − c. Then buyers carry bt/(b + d), and sellers dt/(b + d).

A - bq^* = c + dq^* \Rightarrow q^* = \tfrac{A - c}{b + d} @@ no tax
(A - bq_t) - (c + dq_t) = t \Rightarrow q_t = \tfrac{A - c - t}{b + d} @@ with the tax
p_b - p^* = b\,(q^* - q_t) = \tfrac{bt}{b + d} @@ D falls by b for each flat less
\htmlClass{new}{p^* - p_s = \tfrac{dt}{b + d}} \quad \blacksquare @@ S falls by d; the two shares add to t

A steep demand curve means buyers keep buying as the price climbs: they cannot easily walk away. So they carry most of the tax. Nothing in the technique says who hands the money to the state. The same tax, collected from the buyers instead, gives the same q_t, the same p_b and p_s, and the same shares.

A price fixed from above the market

Let a law forbid selling below $31 a flat. Buyers take only 3 thousand flats, since D(3) = 31. Sellers would gladly grow more: S(q) = 31 at q = 5.4. If they grow 5.4 thousand and sell 3, the other 2.4 thousand flats are grown at a cost of ∫₃^{5.4} S(q) dq = 60 thousand dollars, and left in the field. The gain destroyed is then the 6.17 of the stopped trades, plus the 60 spent on fruit nobody eats.

This happened. In 1933, to lift farm prices, the United States paid farmers to plough under about 10 million acres of growing cotton, and bought and slaughtered about 6 million young pigs. 11·econ followed the oranges left to rot.

Who said it first

Dupuit argued that the utility of a public work is not what it collects in tolls, but the sum of what every user would have paid rather than go without it, less what each does pay; and that a toll, by keeping away those who value a crossing at less than the toll, destroys a utility that nobody gains.

Jules Dupuit, On the measurement of the utility of public works, Annales des ponts et chaussées (1844); paraphrase

In 16·econ.4.4, the engineer is Jules Dupuit. Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) gave consumer surplus its place in teaching. The doubts run through Vilfredo Pareto and Lionel Robbins (1932), who argued that gains to different people cannot be compared, to John Hicks's repairs of 1939 to 1946. Arnold Harberger's estimates in the 1950s and 1960s made the lost gain a working number in tax policy. In 16·econ.4.2, a tax on land that cannot pass to its renters is the proposal of Henry George's Progress and Poverty, written in San Francisco (1879).

Accepted names: consumer surplus, producer surplus, deadweight loss, tax incidence (who carries), a price floor; the 1933 law was the Agricultural Adjustment Act.
