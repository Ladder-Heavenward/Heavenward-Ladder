46a · proof

Cubics and Quartics

Rung eight solved every equation of the second degree with one formula, built from the coefficients by the four operations and one square root. This step does the same for the third degree and the fourth. The third needs a cube root as well as a square root, and it needs one more thing that took a long time to accept. When all three answers of a cubic are ordinary numbers on the line, the formula reaches them only by passing through the plane of rung 25, with square roots of negative numbers on the way. The step proves that this happens exactly when the three answers are on the line and different. The fourth degree comes down to the third: one cubic, then two quadratics. After that the old tools run out, and the rest of the rung asks why.

Shift the unknown to take out the square term. Split the unknown into two parts whose cube roots the quadratic formula gives. When the three roots are real and different, the square root in the formula is of a negative number. A quartic is solved by one cubic and two quadratics.

46a1A Formula by Roots, and the Square Term Taken Out

A formula by roots for the equations of a degree is an expression in their coefficients built by +, −, ×, ÷ and taking n-th roots, finitely often, that gives every answer of every such equation. 8i’s quadratic formula is one, with a single square root. The question of this rung is which degrees have one.

Shift-out technique: putting x = y − b/3 into x³ + bx² + cx + d gives a cubic in y with no square term.

(y - \tfrac b3)^3 = y^3 - by^2 + \tfrac{b^2}{3}y - \tfrac{b^3}{27} @@ the binomial technique (4k)
b(y - \tfrac b3)^2 = by^2 - \tfrac{2b^2}{3}y + \tfrac{b^3}{9} @@ the same, for the square
\text{the } y^2 \text{ terms: } -b + b = 0 @@ the only two
\htmlClass{new}{x^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = y^3 + py + q,\ \ p = c - \tfrac{b^2}{3},\ \ q = d - \tfrac{bc}{3} + \tfrac{2b^3}{27}} \quad \blacksquare @@ collect the rest
  
x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6,\ \ x = y + 2 @@ b = −6, so y − b/3 = y + 2
(y + 2)^3 - 6(y + 2)^2 + 11(y + 2) - 6 @@ substitute
= y^3 + 6y^2 + 12y + 8 - 6y^2 - 24y - 24 + 11y + 22 - 6 @@ expand
\htmlClass{new}{= y^3 - y = y(y - 1)(y + 1):\ \ y = 0, \pm 1,\ \ x = 2, 3, 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ p = 11 − 12 = −1, q = 0

The same shift by b/4 takes the cube term out of a quartic. From here on a cubic is y³ + py + q and a quartic y⁴ + py² + qy + r, each with its top coefficient 1.

46a2The Cubic Formula

Look for y as a sum u + v. Then y³ = u³ + v³ + 3uv(u + v), so y³ + py + q = 0 when u³ + v³ = −q and uv = −p/3. Those two say that u³ and v³ are the two answers of a quadratic.

Cube-formula technique: the answers of y³ + py + q = 0 are u + v, ωu + ω²v and ω²u + ωv, where u³ and v³ are the two answers of t² + qt − p³/27 = 0, the cube roots are chosen with uv = −p/3, and ω is a turn by a third.

(u + v)^3 = u^3 + v^3 + 3uv(u + v) @@ the binomial technique
y = u + v,\ \ uv = -\tfrac p3:\ \ y^3 + py + q = u^3 + v^3 + q @@ the 3uv(u + v) and py terms cancel
u^3 + v^3 = -q,\ \ u^3 v^3 = -\tfrac{p^3}{27} @@ so u³ and v³ are the answers of t² + qt − p³/27 (8i, read backwards)
u^3, v^3 = -\tfrac q2 \pm \sqrt{D},\ \ D = \tfrac{q^2}{4} + \tfrac{p^3}{27} @@ the quadratic formula
\omega = \tfrac{-1 + i\sqrt3}{2},\ \ \omega^3 = 1:\ \ (\omega u)(\omega^2 v) = uv @@ the even-spacing technique (21·cb20), n = 3
\htmlClass{new}{y = u + v,\ \ \omega u + \omega^2 v,\ \ \omega^2 u + \omega v} \quad \blacksquare @@ each pair keeps uv = −p/3, so each is an answer; they are three, by 25’s splitting, when they differ
  
y^3 + 3y - 4 = 0:\ \ p = 3,\ \ q = -4 @@ given
D = 4 + 1 = 5,\ \ u^3 = 2 + \sqrt5,\ \ v^3 = 2 - \sqrt5 @@ the cube-formula technique
\big(\tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2}\big)^3 = \tfrac{1 + 3\sqrt5 + 15 + 5\sqrt5}{8} = 2 + \sqrt5 @@ a guess, checked
\htmlClass{new}{y = \sqrt[3]{2 + \sqrt5} + \sqrt[3]{2 - \sqrt5} = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} + \tfrac{1 - \sqrt5}{2} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ uv = (1 − 5)/4 = −1 = −p/3

The formula wrote 1 as a sum of two cube roots of numbers with square roots in them. It is right, and it hides the answer. The other two answers are ω and ω² times those parts, and they are not on the line.

46a3Three Real Roots, Through the Plane

21k.4.5 asked about y³ − 3y − 1 = 0, whose three answers are on the line. Here p = −3 and q = −1, so D = 1/4 − 1 = −3/4. The formula asks for the square root of a negative number.

u^3 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i = \cos 60^\circ + i \sin 60^\circ @@ −q/2 + √D, with √(−3/4) = (√3/2)i
u = \cos 20^\circ + i\sin 20^\circ,\ \ v = \cos 20^\circ - i\sin 20^\circ @@ the multiply-and-add technique (21·cb20), with uv = 1 = −p/3
\htmlClass{new}{y = 2\cos 20^\circ,\ 2\cos 140^\circ,\ 2\cos 260^\circ \approx 1.879,\ -1.532,\ -0.347} \quad \blacksquare @@ u + v, and ω turns u by 120°

Three-real-roots technique: y³ + py + q has three different real roots exactly when D = q²/4 + p³/27 is below 0, so exactly then the cubic formula takes the square root of a negative number.

f(y) = y^3 + py + q,\ \ f'(y) = 3y^2 + p @@ 13’s slopes
\text{three different real roots} \Rightarrow f' = 0 \text{ twice between them} \Rightarrow p < 0 @@ between two roots the slope is 0 somewhere (14)
s = \sqrt{-p/3}:\ \ f(s)f(-s) = q^2 - (s^3 + ps)^2 = q^2 + \tfrac{4p^3}{27} = 4D @@ s² = −p/3, so s³ + ps = s(−2p/3), squared 4p²s²/9 = −4p³/27
\text{three different real roots} \iff f(-s) > 0 > f(s) \iff D < 0 @@ f climbs, falls between −s and s, climbs again; in-between values (12)
\htmlClass{new}{D < 0 \iff \text{three different real roots}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for p ≥ 0, f climbs everywhere and has one real root, and D ≥ 0

Could some cleverer formula reach 2 cos 20° by real roots alone? For this cubic, no. That is proved on 46g; until then it is held in trust.

46a4The Quartic Comes Down to a Cubic

Write the quartic y⁴ + py² + qy + r = 0 as (y² + m)² = (2m − p)y² − qy + (m² − r), true for every m. The left side is a square. The right side is a square of a line in y exactly when its own discriminant is 0. That is one condition on m, of degree 3.

Quartic-to-cubic technique: if m is an answer of q² = 4(2m − p)(m² − r) with 2m − p ≠ 0, the quartic factors into two quadratics, so its four answers come from one cubic and two quadratics.

(y^2 + m)^2 = y^4 + 2my^2 + m^2 @@ expand
\text{so } y^4 + py^2 + qy + r = 0 \iff (y^2 + m)^2 = (2m - p)y^2 - qy + (m^2 - r) @@ add (2m − p)y² − qy + m² − r to both sides
Ay^2 + By + C \text{ is } (\sqrt A\,y + \tfrac{B}{2\sqrt A})^2 \iff B^2 = 4AC @@ 8i: a quadratic is a square exactly when its discriminant is 0
q^2 = 4(2m - p)(m^2 - r) @@ the resolvent cubic in m: an answer m by the cube-formula technique
\htmlClass{new}{(y^2 + m)^2 - (ky - \tfrac{q}{2k})^2 = (y^2 - ky + m + \tfrac{q}{2k})(y^2 + ky + m - \tfrac{q}{2k}),\ k = \sqrt{2m - p}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a difference of squares, then 8i twice
  
y^4 - 2y^2 + 8y - 3 = 0:\ \ p = -2,\ q = 8,\ r = -3 @@ given
64 = 4(2m + 2)(m^2 + 3):\ \ m = 1 @@ 4 · 4 · 4 = 64
(y^2 + 1)^2 = 4y^2 - 8y + 4 = (2y - 2)^2 @@ 2m − p = 4, m² − r = 4
\htmlClass{new}{(y^2 - 2y + 3)(y^2 + 2y - 1) = 0:\ \ y = 1 \pm i\sqrt2,\ \ -1 \pm \sqrt2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the quadratic formula twice

So the second, third and fourth degrees each have a formula by roots. The fifth is the subject of the rest of the rung.

The accepted names

A formula by roots is a solution by radicals. The shift-out technique gives the depressed cubic. The cube-formula technique is Cardano’s formula, or the Cardano–Tartaglia formula; D is a quarter of the cubic’s discriminant with its sign changed, as 46b shows. The three-real-roots case is the casus irreducibilis, the irreducible case. The quartic-to-cubic technique is Ferrari’s method, and the cubic in m is the resolvent cubic.

Who said it first

Scipione del Ferro solved y³ + py = q around 1515 and kept it secret. Niccolò Tartaglia found it again in 1535 and won a public contest with it; he told Gerolamo Cardano in a verse, under a promise of silence. Cardano published it in the Ars Magna (1545), with his student Lodovico Ferrari’s solution of the quartic, after seeing del Ferro’s papers. Rafael Bombelli’s L’Algebra (1572) worked y³ = 15y + 4 through (2 + i)³ = 2 + 11i, the first computation that used the numbers of 25 to reach an ordinary answer. François Viète solved the three-real-roots case by the triple-angle identity around 1591.

A proof that is not allowed

“Put y = u + v. Then u³ + v³ = −q and u³v³ = −p³/27. So u and v are the cube roots of the two answers, and y = u + v, whichever cube roots are taken.” Not allowed: the second line used uv = −p/3, not only its cube. Cubing loses which cube roots belong together, so the last line must keep uv = −p/3.
