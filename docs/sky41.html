∞41 · research front

Can You Compute on a Locked Message?

Can you compute on a locked message? A hospital wants a distant computer to run a test on its patients’ records; a phone wants a server to look something up; neither wants the other side to read what it sent. Locks so far protect a message while it travels or sits. This page asks for a lock that lets anyone add and multiply the locked numbers, so that the owner of the key unlocks the answer to a computation that nobody else ever saw. The question was asked in 1978 and stood open for thirty years. The noisy lock of ∞40 adds for free: add two locked bits and the result is the lock of their sum, with the errors added. Multiplying is harder, and on the simplest lock of this kind, made of whole numbers, it works too, but the errors multiply. After a few products the errors grow past what the key can strip away, and the answer is lost. In 2009 a way round was found: unlock the message under the lock itself, running the unlocking program on locked inputs, which gives back the same message with fresh small errors. From 2011 locks were found that run any program up to a fixed depth with no refresh at all, by shrinking the errors after each product. The price is speed. One refreshed gate took under a tenth of a second in 2016, against billions of plain gates a second on an ordinary chip. Whether a lock can ever compute nearly as fast as plain arithmetic, and whether it can hide which program was run, is open.

In the lock of 44·now2.4, adding two locks gives v − u·s = (r₁ + r₂)·e + (m₁ + m₂)⌊q/2⌋, so it unlocks to m₁ + m₂ on the clock of 2 while the errors stay below q/4. On the whole-number lock c = pk + 2r + m, sums and products of c unlock to sums and products of m while |2r + m| stays below p/2, and a product multiplies the errors: 7 times 5 is 35, and 35 squared is past 500. Unlocking is a program; if the lock can run it plus one more gate, running it on the locked key refreshes the errors forever. Shrinking after each product keeps the errors near N, so d levels need a clock of about N^(d + 1) in place of N^(2^d).

sky41.1Adding Under the Lock

44·now2.4 locked a bit m as u = rA, v = r·b + m⌊q/2⌋, and ∞40.3 showed when the error hides the secret. Here the difference: the locks themselves can be added, by anyone, with no key. On {0, 1} plus on the clock of 2 is the gate “exactly one” (39a).

Sum-under-lock technique: if (u₁, v₁) locks m₁ and (u₂, v₂) locks m₂ with lists r₁, r₂, then (u₁ + u₂, v₁ + v₂) unlocks to the remainder of m₁ + m₂ by 2, provided (r₁ + r₂)·e, together with −1 when both bits are 1, stays below q/4 in size.

(v_1 + v_2) - (u_1 + u_2)\cdot s = (r_1 + r_2)\cdot e + (m_1 + m_2)\lfloor q/2 \rfloor @@ the round-off technique of 44·now2.4, twice, added
m_1 = m_2 = 1:\ \ 2\lfloor q/2 \rfloor = q - 1 \equiv -1 @@ for odd q: two halves make nearly a whole turn
\text{size below } q/4 \Rightarrow \text{nearer } 0 \text{ or } \lfloor q/2 \rfloor \text{ as } m_1 + m_2 \text{ is even or odd} @@ the receiver reads as before
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sum of locks unlocks to } m_1 \oplus m_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ errors add, they do not vanish
  
r_1 = (1, 0, 1),\ m_1 = 1:\ \ (6, 3),\ 8 @@ 44·now2.4’s lock; r₁·e = 1
r_2 = (1, 1, 0),\ m_2 = 1:\ \ (4, 6),\ 14 + 11 + 8 = 33 \equiv 16 @@ r₂·e = 0
(10, 9),\ 24 \equiv 7:\ \ 7 - (30 + 45) = -68 \equiv 0 @@ 1 + 0 − 1 = 0, nearer 0
\htmlClass{new}{1 \oplus 1 = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ read without anyone seeing either bit

Each sum adds the errors. With q = 17 the room is q/4 = 4.25, and every lock here carries an error of size at most 2, so a few sums fit and then no more. In the standard, q = 3329 and the room is 832, but its errors are tuned to fill much of that room already; locks built for computing choose far larger clocks to leave room. Sums alone, though, compute only “exactly one” and its repeats. A program needs “both” as well.

sky41.2Multiplying, and the Errors Multiply

The simplest lock that multiplies is made of whole numbers. The key is an odd whole number p. To lock a bit m, pick any whole k and a small whole r, and send c = pk + 2r + m. To unlock, take the member of c’s class on the clock of p that lies between −p/2 and p/2, and read it on the clock of 2. Call 2r + m the error-and-message.

That is 44b2 twice: the classes of the ideal pℤ add and multiply honestly, by its honest-product technique, and so do the classes of 2ℤ. The only catch is that the small member must not wrap round the clock of p.

Product-under-lock technique: if c₁ and c₂ lock m₁ and m₂ with error-and-messages t₁ = 2r₁ + m₁ and t₂ = 2r₂ + m₂, then c₁ + c₂ unlocks to m₁ ⊕ m₂ while |t₁ + t₂| < p/2, and c₁c₂ unlocks to m₁m₂ while |t₁t₂| < p/2.

c_1 c_2 = p(\ldots) + t_1 t_2 @@ multiply out: every other term has a factor p
|t_1 t_2| < p/2 \Rightarrow t_1 t_2 \text{ is the small member of the class} @@ no wrap
t_1 t_2 = (2r_1 + m_1)(2r_2 + m_2) = 2(\ldots) + m_1 m_2 @@ the same on the clock of 2
\htmlClass{new}{c_1 c_2 \text{ unlocks to } m_1 m_2,\ \text{the error-and-message multiplied}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum is the same with + for ×
  
c_1 = 1001 \cdot 12 + 2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 12\,019,\ \ c_2 = 1001 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 3 + 1 = 5000 @@ t₁ = 7, t₂ = −5: both lock 1
c_1 + c_2 = 17\,019 \to 2 \to 0,\ \ c_1 c_2 = 60\,095\,000 \to -35 \to 1 @@ 1 ⊕ 1 = 0, 1 · 1 = 1
(c_1 c_2)^2 \to (-35)^2 = 1225 > 500 @@ past p/2: it wraps
\htmlClass{new}{1225 - 1001 = 224 \to 0,\ \text{but } 1 \cdot 1 = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second product is read wrong

With “exactly one” as plus and “both” as times, every table of 39f can be built: not-and is 1 + m₁m₂ on the clock of 2. So the lock runs any program, as long as the errors hold. They do not hold long. An error of size N after d levels of products is up to N^(2^d): with N = 16 and a key of 1000 binary digits, 4 · 2ᵈ must stay below 999, and d is at most 7.

sky41.3Unlocking Under the Lock

Unlocking is itself a program. For the whole-number lock with p odd, c − p·(the nearest whole number to c/p) is the small member, and on the clock of 2 that is the last binary digit of c plus the last binary digit of the nearest whole number to c/p. Give the computer, once, a lock of the key’s own digits. It can then run the unlocking program on locked inputs.

60\,095\,000 / 1001 = 60\,034.965\ldots,\ \ \text{nearest } 60\,035 @@ odd
60\,095\,000 - 1001 \cdot 60\,035 = -35 @@ the small member
60\,095\,000 \text{ even},\ 60\,035 \text{ odd} @@ last digits 0 and 1
\htmlClass{new}{0 \oplus 1 = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same as −35 on the clock of 2

Refresh technique: if a lock can run its own unlocking program followed by one more gate, before its errors pass the bound, and a lock of the key is published, then it can run every program of 39f’s gates.

c \text{ locks } m,\ \text{errors near the bound} @@ after a gate
\text{lock each digit of } c,\ \text{run unlocking on them and on the locked key} @@ the program runs on locks; its inputs are fresh, so their errors are small
\text{its output locks } m \text{ with the errors of one unlocking run} @@ the unlocking program’s depth, whatever c’s errors were
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one gate, then refresh: the errors never pile up}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hypothesis covers one unlocking run and one gate

The hypothesis is the hard part. Unlocking needs c/p, a division, which is deep. In 2009 the first lock that met it was built not on whole numbers but in a ring of polynomials, with ideals of 44b in the places of pℤ and 2ℤ: the message lives in the quotient by one ideal, and the key is a short basis of another, so that unlocking is a rounding that can be made shallow. The paper also had to hand the computer hints that make division shallower still, at the cost of an extra belief. And the lock of the key under itself rests on a belief of its own, that publishing it is safe, which nobody has proved.

sky41.4Levels Without Refreshing

From 2011 another road: let the errors grow by one product, then shrink them back. On the clock of q, a lock can be moved to a smaller clock q′ by multiplying it by q′/q and rounding; held in trust, the error is multiplied by q′/q too, plus a small rounding term. Each product squares the error; each shrink divides it back.

Level-count technique: if fresh errors have size N, a product squares the error, and a shrink by N brings N² back to N, then d levels of products need a clock of about N^(d + 2); without shrinking they need N^(2^d) or more.

\text{level } 0:\ N;\ \text{product: } N^2;\ \text{shrink by } N:\ N @@ held in trust: the shrink divides the error
d \text{ shrinks divide the clock by } N^d @@ one shrink per level
\text{the last clock must still exceed } N^2 @@ room for the last product
\htmlClass{new}{q \approx N^{d} \cdot N^{2} = N^{d + 2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clock’s digits grow in a line with d, not as a doubling
  
d = 7:\ \ 4 \cdot 2^7 = 512 \text{ digits without},\ \ 4 \cdot 9 = 36 \text{ with} @@ the two counts
d = 20:\ \ 4 \cdot 2^{20} = 4\,194\,304 \text{ without},\ \ 4 \cdot 22 = 88 \text{ with} @@ a million times shorter
\htmlClass{new}{\text{depth } 20 \text{ in under } 100 \text{ binary digits of clock}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the level-count technique

So for a program of fixed depth, a test of a medical record or the score of a model, no refresh is needed: the clock is chosen once, large enough. These are the locks of 2011 to 2013. A program of unknown depth, or one that loops, still needs the refresh of sky41.3.

sky41.5What It Costs

39k counts turns, and its machine-change technique lets a count be carried from one machine to another up to a constant. The constant is the whole story here. A refreshed gate is one unlocking program, run on locks; an ordinary gate is one tick.

\text{a chip: about } 3 \times 10^9 \text{ ticks a second} @@ 39·eng: the clock that stopped near 2005
\text{a refreshed gate, 2016: under } 0.1 \text{ s} @@ the fastest refresh of its year
0.1 \times 3 \times 10^9 = 3 \times 10^8 @@ ticks per refreshed gate
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a program of a million gates: about a day, against a third of a millisecond}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 10⁶ × 0.1 s ≈ 28 h

The count is in the same family of 39k, a line in the number of gates, and that is why the lock is a lock and not a curiosity. The constant is why it is rare. Since 2016 faster refreshes, locks that hold many bits in one, and chips built for the arithmetic have narrowed the gap, by amounts that depend on the program and are argued. Lookups and programs of fixed depth, like the photo lookup of 2024 told below, are where it runs today.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞41.oq.1Can a lock compute nearly as fast as plain arithmetic? Every known lock that adds and multiplies carries errors, and keeping them down costs either refreshes or wide clocks. Nobody knows a lock without errors that is believed safe, and nobody has proved that errors, and so a large slowdown, are needed.
∞41.oq.2Can a lock hide which program was run? The locks of this page hide the data, not the program, which the computer must know to run. Hiding a program while it still runs, scrambled so that two programs with the same table cannot be told apart, was built in 2020 from beliefs long studied, but the constructions are far too slow to use, and several of their beliefs are not about lattices, so would fall to ∞1. A fast scrambling that survives quantum computers is open.
∞41.oq.3Is publishing a lock of the key under itself safe? Every refreshing lock does it. For the locks in use no proof is known that it is safe, and no attack that uses it either. Can the belief be proved from learning with errors alone, and for which locks?
∞41.oq.4Who should decide when a locked computation is used? In 2024 a phone maker began looking up the places in users’ photos by locked computation, switched on by default. Its defenders said the server learned nothing; its critics said users were not asked, and that data left the phone at all. Whether a strong lock makes consent unnecessary is argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question is older than the answer by thirty years. In 1978 three researchers at MIT asked for locks that let a data bank compute on records it cannot read. They called them privacy homomorphisms. For thirty years no lock was found that does both without breaking.

In 2009 a graduate student at Stanford, working with IBM, announced the first lock that does both, by the refresh of sky41.3, built on ideals in a ring of polynomials. The announcement went round the field’s mailing lists in March and the paper appeared at that year’s main theory meeting. Readers were divided between delight that it was possible at all and doubt that a refresh so deep would ever be practical. A whole-number version followed in 2010, the one of sky41.2, which made the idea teachable on a page.

The years 2011 to 2013 changed the shape. Locks built on ∞40’s learning with errors came first, then the shrinking of sky41.4, which ran fixed depth with no refresh. Patents on the refresh were filed in the United States, and open libraries of code appeared beside them, and the arguments moved to speed. In 2016 a refresh of one gate was brought under a tenth of a second.

Money followed. In 2021 a US defence research agency funded teams to build chips just for this arithmetic. A phone maker used it in 2024 for looking up places in photos, switched on by default; when this became widely known in early 2025, some defended the design and others objected that nobody had been asked. The argument was not about the mathematics but about who decides.

What is argued now: how far the cost can fall, whether trusting a sealed piece of hardware would do the same job more simply, and whether locked computing protects people or chiefly lets companies use data they could not otherwise touch.

The accepted names

A lock that adds and multiplies locked messages is fully homomorphic encryption, FHE; one that runs any program up to a fixed depth is leveled FHE, and one that only adds is additively homomorphic. The 1978 name is privacy homomorphism. The whole-number lock is the DGHV scheme, and its hard problem the approximate common divisor problem. The refresh technique is bootstrapping, the published lock of the key the bootstrapping key, and the belief that publishing it is safe circular security; the hints that make division shallow are squashing, on the sparse subset sum problem. Shrinking to a smaller clock is modulus switching, and the leveled locks of 2011–13 are BGV and its relatives, with BFV and CKKS; the 2016 fast refresh is TFHE. Scrambling a program so that equal tables look alike is indistinguishability obfuscation, iO.

Who said it first

Ronald Rivest, Leonard Adleman and Michael Dertouzos asked the question in 1978. Craig Gentry gave the first scheme in his 2009 thesis at Stanford (STOC 2009). Marten van Dijk, Gentry, Shai Halevi and Vinod Vaikuntanathan gave the scheme over the integers (Eurocrypt 2010). Zvika Brakerski, Gentry and Vaikuntanathan gave leveled FHE without bootstrapping (2011, ITCS 2012). Ilaria Chillotti, Nicolas Gama, Mariya Georgieva and Malika Izabachène gave bootstrapping in under 0.1 s (Asiacrypt 2016). Aayush Jain, Huijia Lin and Amit Sahai built iO from well-founded assumptions (2020). DARPA launched its DPRIVE programme in 2021; Apple’s Enhanced Visual Search is the 2024 photo lookup.

A proof that is not allowed

“The computer only ever adds and multiplies the locked numbers, so it learns nothing.” Not allowed: that the computer learns nothing is a statement about the lock, which rests on ∞40’s belief, and, once the key is published under itself, on a further belief. What the computer does is only half of the claim; what it could do with what it holds is the other half.
