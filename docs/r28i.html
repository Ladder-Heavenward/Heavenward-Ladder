28i · proof

Stretching and Folding

24k found that one number fed back into a simple rule can wander forever without repeating. In the plane there is more room. A map of the plane can shrink every area and still stretch in one direction, folding what it stretches back into a bounded region, like dough kneaded over and over. Points that start together are pulled apart at a steady exponential rate, yet all stay on a thin, folded set. This step reads stretching and shrinking off 28b’s best flat map, finds a map that shrinks area by the same factor everywhere, and measures how fast neighbours part.

Near a point, a map of the plane acts as its best flat map, a 2 × 2 grid. Its determinant is the factor by which areas change; its eigenvalues are the stretches along special lines. Hénon’s map shrinks area by 0.3 each step but stretches in one direction, so neighbours part like e^(0.42n) while the whole set stays bounded.

28i1Areas and Stretches Up Close

(x, y) \mapsto (1 - a x^2 + y,\ b x),\ \ a = 1.4,\ b = 0.3 @@ Michel Hénon, 1976
\text{best flat map: } J = \begin{pmatrix} -2ax & 1 \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix} @@ the partial slopes (28b)
\det J = -b = -0.3 @@ the same at every point
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every small area is multiplied by } 0.3 \text{ each step}} @@ and turned over

Area technique: near a point, a map multiplies small areas by the size of its best flat map’s determinant, and stretches most along the direction its eigenvalue of largest size points. A determinant of size below 1 everywhere means the map squeezes everything toward a set of zero area.

28i2A Saddle That Folds

x = 1 - 1.4x^2 + 0.3x:\ x \approx 0.631,\ y \approx 0.189 @@ the point that stays put
J = \begin{pmatrix} -1.77 & 1 \\ 0.3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}:\ \lambda \approx -1.92,\ 0.16 @@ from tr and det (27e)
\text{one direction stretched 1.92 times, the other squeezed to 0.16} @@ a saddle, 27i’s picture for maps
\htmlClass{new}{\text{stretched points cannot leave: the } x^2 \text{ folds them back}} @@ stretch, fold, repeat

The level point is a saddle: neighbours along one line flee it and those along the other approach. Stretching in one direction while the whole is bounded forces folding, and repeated folding makes a layered set like the leaves of pastry.

28i3How Fast Neighbours Part

\text{two starts } 10^{-10} \text{ apart} @@ measured to ten places
\text{distance after } n \text{ steps} \approx 10^{-10} e^{0.42 n} @@ on average along the path
e^{0.42n} = 10^{10} \iff n \approx 55 @@ solve with ln (17)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after about 55 steps the two paths are unrelated}} @@ ten digits spent in 55 steps

Parting technique: when neighbours part on average like e^(λn) with λ > 0, each digit of the start is spent in about ln 10 / λ steps, here about 5.5, however exactly the rule is known. Weather obeys exact laws of the same kind, which is why forecasts fail past about two weeks.

Accepted names

Hénon’s map settles onto a strange attractor; the parting rate is the largest Lyapunov exponent, about 0.42 for Hénon’s map; stretching and folding is Smale’s horseshoe; sensitive dependence on the start is the butterfly effect.

Who said it first

Edward Lorenz found sensitive dependence in a weather model in 1963; Stephen Smale described the horseshoe in 1967; Michel Hénon published his map in 1976.
