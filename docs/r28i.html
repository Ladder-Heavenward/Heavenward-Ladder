28i · proof

Changing the variables

Some regions are awkward in east and north but simple in other measures. A round pond is hard to cut into rectangles, but easy to describe by distance from the centre and angle. This step proves how a total changes when you change the variables you measure with. A straight map stretches every area by the same factor, the size of its determinant. A curved map stretches small patches by the size of the determinant of its slope grid there, and the total picks up that factor. In round measures the factor is the distance from the centre.

A straight map of the plane multiplies every area by the size of its determinant. For a smooth one-to-one change of variables T, the total of f over T(Q) is the total over Q of f(T) times the size of the determinant of T’s slope grid.

28i1Straight maps scale area

Area-scale technique: a straight map with grid A sends a rectangle of area |Q| to a parallelogram of area |det A| · |Q|.

Q \text{ has corners } c,\ c + s e_1,\ c + t e_2,\ c + s e_1 + t e_2 @@ a rectangle with sides s and t
A(Q) \text{ has corners } Ac,\ Ac + sAe_1,\ Ac + tAe_2,\ Ac + sAe_1 + tAe_2 @@ A is straight (27c); a parallelogram
|A(Q)| = 2 \cdot \tfrac12\,|s a_{11} \cdot t a_{22} - s a_{21} \cdot t a_{12}| @@ two triangles cut by a diagonal; the triangle-area technique (18g) with u = sAe₁, v = tAe₂
\htmlClass{new}{|A(Q)| = |\det A|\,st = |\det A|\,|Q|} \quad \blacksquare @@ det A = a₁₁a₂₂ − a₂₁a₁₂ (27e)

So 27e’s three rules were not only like the rules of a volume. In the plane, |det A| is the area scale of A, proved. The sign says whether A keeps or flips the turning sense.

In space the triangle-area technique has no twin on the ladder yet, so the volume scale is proved another way: by breaking A into row moves. Volume-scale technique: a straight map of space with grid A multiplies the volume of every solid that has one by |det A|.

\det A = 0 \Rightarrow A \text{ sends space into a plane, of volume } 0 @@ the nonzero-det and lost-plus-reached techniques (27e, 27c): A reaches at most a plane
\det A \ne 0 \Rightarrow A = E_1 E_2 \cdots E_k,\ \text{each } E \text{ a row move} @@ the undo-by-rows technique (27j): row moves bring A to I, and each is undone by a row move
E \text{ adds a multiple of one place to another: volume kept} @@ the slices across the other places only slide; the matching-slices technique (16j)
E \text{ swaps two places: volume kept};\ \ E \text{ stretches one place by } t: \text{ volume} \times |t| @@ a box goes to a box (16j’s box volume)
\text{each } E \text{ scales volume by } |\det E| @@ det E is 1, −1 or t (27e)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{volume of } A(K) = |\det E_1| \cdots |\det E_k|\,\text{vol}(K) = |\det A|\,\text{vol}(K)} \quad \blacksquare @@ scales multiply along the chain; the det-product technique (27e)

28i2Small patches of a curved map

A curved map T, smoothly sloped and one-to-one with det DT ≠ 0, is nearly its flat map on a small enough patch. Near-flat technique: for every ε > 0 there is a δ such that on every square Q of side less than δ inside the region, with centre c and A = DT(c), T(Q) lies inside the image under A of Q enlarged about c by the factor 1 + ε, and holds the image of Q shrunk by 1 − ε.

\psi(h) = A^{-1}\big(T(c + h) - T(c)\big),\ \ D\psi(h) = A^{-1}DT(c+h) @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c2)
\text{entries of } D\psi - I \le \eta \text{ for } |h| < \delta @@ DT is continuous on the closed region, so evenly so (28h1); η small
|\psi(h) - h| \le 2\eta\,|h| @@ the segment-bound technique (28e1) for ψ − identity, n = 2
\text{each place of } \psi(h) \text{ differs from that of } h \text{ by at most } 2\sqrt2\,\eta \cdot \tfrac{\text{side}}{2} @@ |h| ≤ √2 · side/2 on the square
T(Q) \subset T(c) + A\big((1+\varepsilon)(Q - c)\big) @@ choose η with 4√2 η ≤ ε
\htmlClass{new}{T(c) + A\big((1-\varepsilon)(Q - c)\big) \subset T(Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines for the undoing of T (28e), whose slope grid at T(c) is A⁻¹ (28e3)

With the area-scale technique this squeezes: (1 − ε)² |det A| |Q| ≤ |T(Q)| ≤ (1 + ε)² |det A| |Q|.

28i3Changing the variables in a total

Over a region D that is not a rectangle, the total is trapped the same way as in 28h: lower sums from the boxes inside D, upper sums from the boxes that meet D. Totals over such regions add over pieces that meet only along their edges, as areas do (15e).

Change-of-variables technique: if T is smoothly sloped and one-to-one on a region holding the rectangle Q, with det DT ≠ 0 there, and f is continuous on T(Q), then ∬T(Q) f = ∬Q f(T(u)) · |det DT(u)|.

Q = \textstyle\bigcup Q_k,\ \ T(Q) = \bigcup T(Q_k) @@ cut Q into small squares; their images meet only along edges (T is one-to-one)
m_k\,|T(Q_k)| \le \textstyle\iint_{T(Q_k)} f \le M_k\,|T(Q_k)| @@ m_k, M_k the inf and sup of f(T(u)) on Q_k
|T(Q_k)| \text{ is within } (2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2)\,|\det DT(c_k)|\,|Q_k| \text{ of } |\det DT(c_k)|\,|Q_k| @@ the near-flat technique (28i2)
\textstyle\iint_{T(Q)} f \text{ lies within } C\varepsilon \text{ of a box sum of } f(T)\,|\det DT| \text{ over } Q @@ add over k: the totals add over pieces; f and det DT are bounded
\text{those box sums head to } \textstyle\iint_Q f(T(u))\,|\det DT(u)| @@ the rectangle-total technique (28h1): the function is continuous
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\iint_{T(Q)} f = \iint_Q f(T(u))\,|\det DT(u)|} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε was as small as we like

Round measures: T(r, θ) = (r cos θ, r sin θ) has det DT = cos²θ · r + r sin²θ = r. So a total over a disc or a ring becomes ∬ f(r cos θ, r sin θ) r dr dθ, with that extra r. Over the disc of radius R, the total of 1 is ∫₀2π ∫₀R r dr dθ = πR².

In space the same lines run word for word: cubes for squares, the volume-scale technique for the area-scale technique, n = 3 in the segment bound, and triple totals built from boxes as double totals were. So ∭T(Q) f = ∭Q f(T) |det DT|, with the 3 × 3 slope grid.

Slanted regions: the region a ≤ x ≤ b, φ₁(x) ≤ y ≤ φ₂(x) is the image of [a, b] × [0, 1] under T(x, t) = (x, φ₁(x) + t(φ₂(x) − φ₁(x))), whose det is φ₂ − φ₁. So such a region is totalled as ∫ab ∫φ₁(x)φ₂(x) f dy dx, by the substitution technique (16d) on the inner total.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

|det DT| is the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, and the change-of-variables technique is the change of variables formula (or substitution rule) for multiple integrals. Round measures are polar coordinates. The trapping of a total over a region by inner and outer boxes is Jordan content, or Jordan measure.

Who said it first

Euler changed variables in double integrals in 1769, and Lagrange used the determinant factor for triple integrals in 1773, in his work on the attraction of ellipsoids. Jacobi gave the general formula with his determinant in 1841. Polar coordinates were used for areas by Newton and by Jacob Bernoulli in the 1690s.
