21f · proof

Sin h / h → 1

Walk a very short way around the circle. The distance you walked along the curve and the height you rose are almost the same, and the shorter the walk, the closer they agree. This step proves it. The height is never more than the walk, and the walk is never more than the steepness of the line to where you stopped. Squeezed between those two, the height divided by the walk settles on exactly one. That one fact is what lets the next step find how fast the sine changes.

For a tiny angle, the height is the arc: the sine of a small angle divided by the angle settles on one. It is fenced between the cosine and one, and the cosine settles on one as well.

21f1The arc, measured upward

Upward-arc technique: for 0 ≤ h < π/2, h = ∫₀^{sin h} ds/√(1 − s²). The flip across y = x carries the arc from (1, 0) to the point at h onto the arc from (0, 1) to (sin h, cos h). That is the graph of √(1 − s²) for s from 0 to sin h, and sin h < 1, so the integral is proper.

F(\text{arc from } (1, 0) \text{ to } P_h) = \text{arc from } (0, 1) \text{ to } (\sin h, \cos h) @@ F(x, y) = (y, x); lengths kept (21d1)
\text{that arc} = \text{graph of } \sqrt{1 - s^2},\ 0 \le s \le \sin h @@ the upper half circle
\htmlClass{new}{h = \int_0^{\sin h} \frac{ds}{\sqrt{1 - s^2}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the arc-length technique (21a1)

21f2Fences for a small arc

Small-arc technique: for 0 ≤ h < π/2, sin h ≤ h ≤ tan h. On [0, sin h] the integrand is at least 1 and at most 1/√(1 − sin² h) = 1/cos h. Each fence is an area of a box.

0 \le s \le \sin h \Rightarrow 1 \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - s^2}} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 h}} = \frac{1}{\cos h} @@ the bottom falls as s rises; cos h > 0 here
\int_0^{\sin h} 1\,ds \le h \le \int_0^{\sin h} \frac{ds}{\cos h} @@ smaller-function-smaller-area (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\sin h \le h \le \tan h} \quad \blacksquare @@ the boxes: sin h · 1 and sin h · (1/cos h)

So the height is never more than the walk, and the walk is never more than the height on the touching line (21c3).

21f3The squeeze

Sine-over-arc technique: sin h / h heads to 1 as h heads to 0. Divide the fences by h and turn them over, and sin h / h sits between cos h and 1. Cos h heads to 1, because sin h ≤ h makes cos h = √(1 − sin² h) at least √(1 − h²).

0 < h < \tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ \ \cos h \le \frac{\sin h}{h} \le 1 @@ from h ≤ sin h / cos h and sin h ≤ h
1 \ge \cos h \ge \sqrt{1 - h^2} \to 1 @@ sin² h ≤ h²
\frac{\sin(-h)}{-h} = \frac{\sin h}{h} @@ reversal: sin is odd (21e1)
\htmlClass{new}{\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the squeeze technique (10d), from both sides

In passing: sin h → 0 and cos h → 1 as h → 0. So sin and cos are continuous at 0.

21f4Its partner

Cosine-gap technique: (1 − cos h) / h heads to 0 as h heads to 0. Multiply top and bottom by 1 + cos h. The top becomes sin² h.

\frac{1 - \cos h}{h} = \frac{1 - \cos^2 h}{h(1 + \cos h)} = \frac{\sin h}{h} \cdot \frac{\sin h}{1 + \cos h} @@ times (1 + cos h)/(1 + cos h); the circle technique
\to 1 \cdot \frac{0}{2} @@ 21f3, and continuity at 0
\htmlClass{new}{\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos h}{h} = 0} \quad \blacksquare

The accepted names

The sine-over-arc technique is the fundamental trigonometric limit; sin h ≈ h for small h is the small-angle approximation. The squeeze technique is also called the sandwich or pinching theorem.

Who said it first

In 21f.4.5, the scientist is Galileo Galilei (letters of 1602, and the Dialogue, 1632), and the correction is Christiaan Huygens's Horologium Oscillatorium (1673): the swing keeps exact time along a cycloid, the tautochrone.
