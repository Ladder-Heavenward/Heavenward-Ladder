13c · proof

The Product Rule

Change the input of a product of two functions by a little. Each factor changes, and 5i's grid of cells splits the new product into four cells. There is the old product, and two cells that each carry one change. And there is a leftover cell that carries both changes at once. Divided by the change in the input, the two one-change cells head to each function times the other's slope. The leftover cell, divided the same way, is a chord slope times a change, and by 13b the change goes to 0 in the limit. So the slope of a product is each function times the slope of the other, added. A second road, by adding and taking away one product, reaches the same rule.

Split the new product into four cells, take away the old product, and divide by the change. Two cells head to each function times the other’s slope. The leftover cell goes to 0 in the limit, because a slope means no gap.

13c1The Change of a Product, in Cells

Let f and g have slopes at w. Each is defined on some open interval around w; on the smaller of the two (8b's min of the reaches) both are, and so is fg, 10c's function x ↦ f(x)g(x). By 13a's line for Δ, f(w + h) = f(w) + Δf and g(w + h) = g(w) + Δg. So the new product is a product of two sums, and 5i's grid-of-cells multiplies it out.

A word about the picture, said plainly. It is often drawn with boxes that grow, and read as geometry, speaking of what the boxes cover. The ladder has not built that; rung 15 builds what the boxes would be read as. Here it is 5i's grid of cells and nothing more. There is a row for each term of the first sum and a column for each term of the second. In each cell is the product of its row and its column. The boxes are writing. Their sizes mean nothing.

The cell at the top left is the old product f(w)g(w). Two cells carry one change each: f(w)Δg and Δf g(w). They are called the strips. The cell at the bottom right carries both changes at once, Δf Δg: call it the leftover cell. The change of the product, Δ(fg) = (fg)(w + h) − (fg)(w), is the new product less the old one: the three cells other than the old product.

With numbers: f(x) = x² + 1 and g(x) = x at w = 2, with h = 0.1. Then f(2) = 5, g(2) = 2, Δf = 5.41 − 5 = 0.41 and Δg = 0.1. The cells are 10, then 5 · 0.1 = 0.5 and 0.41 · 2 = 0.82, and the leftover 0.41 · 0.1 = 0.041. Their sum less the old product is 0.5 + 0.82 + 0.041 = 1.361, and indeed (fg)(2.1) = 5.41 · 2.1 = 11.361, which is 10 + 1.361. Divided by h the three cells are 5, 8.2 and 0.41. Shrink h and the first two head to 5 and 8, and the leftover heads to 0.

13c2The Proof, Every Line Cited

Product technique for slopes (the product rule): if f and g have slopes at w, then fg has a slope at w, and (fg)′(w) = f′(w)g(w) + f(w)g′(w). In words: each function times the slope of the other, added.

\text{call } r:\ f, g \text{ defined on } (w - r, w + r) @@ the lesser of the two reaches (8b's min, lesser-below-both); fg is defined there (10c)
f(w + h) = f(w) + \Delta f,\ \ g(w + h) = g(w) + \Delta g @@ 13a's line for Δ, and adding-keeps-answers: for 0 < |h| < r
(fg)(w + h) = (f(w) + \Delta f)\,(g(w) + \Delta g) @@ 10c's line for fg; the swap axiom
= f(w)g(w) + \htmlClass{new}{f(w)\,\Delta g} + \htmlClass{new}{\Delta f\, g(w)} + \htmlClass{new}{\Delta f\, \Delta g} @@ grid-of-cells (5i), read in ℝ: the four cells
\Delta(fg) = f(w)\,\Delta g + g(w)\,\Delta f + \Delta f\,\Delta g @@ take away the cell f(w)g(w) (adding-keeps-answers); turn-about for times on ℝ
\dfrac{\Delta(fg)}{h} = f(w)\,\dfrac{\Delta g}{h} + g(w)\,\dfrac{\Delta f}{h} + \dfrac{\Delta f}{h}\cdot \Delta g @@ multiply by h⁻¹: spread on ℝ, regroup for times on ℝ, turn-about for times on ℝ; h ≠ 0
\dfrac{\Delta g}{h} \to g'(w),\ \ \dfrac{\Delta f}{h} \to f'(w) @@ the hypotheses: the chord slopes head to the slopes
\Delta g \to \htmlClass{keep}{0} @@ change-heads-to-0 (13b): g has a slope at w. This is where “a slope means no gap” is spent
\dfrac{\Delta f}{h}\cdot \Delta g \;\to\; f'(w) \cdot 0 = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ limits-multiply (10c); anything-times-zero. The leftover cell, divided by h, goes to 0 in the limit
(fg)'(w) = f(w)\,g'(w) + g(w)\,f'(w) \quad \blacksquare @@ constant-factor for the constants f(w) and g(w), limits-add, plus-zero on ℝ; agreeing-near: the chord slope of fg is this sum at every 0 < |h| < r

Look at the leftover cell. For every h ≠ 0 the four cells add up exactly to the new product: nothing is set aside. Divided by h, the leftover cell is (Δf/h) · Δg: a chord slope of f times a change of g. The chord slope heads to f′(w), and the change heads to 0, by 13b. So their product heads to 0. When f and g are both x, Δf = Δg = h and the leftover cell is h · h. Divided by h it is h, which goes to 0 as h does. That is the same h · h piece 13a met in x², and it gets the same treatment. It is not dropped for being small. It goes to 0 in the limit, and the line that sends it there is cited.

Notice where each hypothesis was spent. The slopes of f and g were spent on the two one-change cells. The slope of g was spent a second time, through change-heads-to-0, on the leftover cell. Without continuity of g at w, Δg need not shrink, and the leftover cell need not go to 0.

13c3A Second Road

A well-known modern calculus book proves the rule by one trick, which never draws a grid: add and take away the same product, so that a real is unchanged.

f(w + h)g(w + h) - f(w)g(w) @@ the change of the product
= f(w + h)g(w + h) \htmlClass{new}{- f(w + h)g(w) + f(w + h)g(w)} - f(w)g(w) @@ add and take away f(w + h)g(w): negative on ℝ, plus-zero on ℝ
= \htmlClass{keep}{f(w + h)}\,\big(g(w + h) - g(w)\big) + \big(f(w + h) - f(w)\big)\,\htmlClass{keep}{g(w)} @@ spread on ℝ turned about, twice; regroup for plus on ℝ
\dfrac{\Delta(fg)}{h} = f(w + h)\,\dfrac{\Delta g}{h} + \dfrac{\Delta f}{h}\, g(w) @@ multiply by h⁻¹
f(w + h) \to f(w) @@ slope-means-no-gap (13b) and two-ways-to-write-a-limit: f is continuous at w. The leftover cell is hiding in f(w + h) = f(w) + Δf
(fg)'(w) = f(w)\,g'(w) + f'(w)\,g(w) \quad \blacksquare @@ limits-multiply, constant-factor, limits-add

The two roads spend the same hypotheses. The grid makes the leftover cell visible. The second road folds it into f(w + h), and spends continuity on the line that sends f(w + h) to f(w).

13c4Checks Against What 13a Found

x \cdot x:\ \ 1 \cdot w + w \cdot 1 = 2w @@ product for slopes with slope-of-a-line twice: agrees with slope-of-x²
x \cdot x^2:\ \ 1 \cdot w^2 + w \cdot 2w = \htmlClass{new}{3w^2} @@ product for slopes with slope-of-a-line and slope-of-x²: agrees with slope-of-x³, and x · x² = x³ by power-of-a-next
x \cdot \tfrac1x = 1 \ (x \ne 0):\ \ 1 \cdot \tfrac1w + w \cdot \big(-\tfrac{1}{w^2}\big) = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ product for slopes with slope-of-a-line and slope-of-one-over-x; and the constant 1 has slope 0 (slope-of-a-line), by slopes-decided-near on (w − |w|, w + |w|)
(x^2 + 1)\cdot x \text{ at } 2:\ \ 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 + 5 \cdot 1 = \htmlClass{new}{13} @@ product for slopes, with the slope of x² + 1 at 2 being 4 (its chord slopes are x²'s: the 1s cancel in the top; slope-of-x²) and the slope of x being 1 (slope-of-a-line)
\dfrac{(2.001^3 + 2.001) - 10}{0.001} = 13.006001 @@ one chord slope of x³ + x at 2: exactly 13 + 6h + h² at h = 0.001

The fourth line took the slope of x² + 1 from x²'s, because the 1s cancel in the top of every chord slope. 13d proves in general that adding a constant changes no slope. With g a constant c the rule gives f′(w) · c + f(w) · 0 = c f′(w): a constant factor comes out, which 13d records.

13c5The Tempting Rule

The rule everyone tries first is (fg)′ = f′g′: the slope of a product is the product of the slopes. Test it on the smallest case. x · x is x², whose slope at 3 is 6 (slope-of-x²). The tempting rule gives 1 · 1 = 1. The cells say why. (Δf/h)(Δg/h) heads to f′(w)g′(w), and it is the leftover cell divided by h twice. But the change of the product is divided by h once, and divided once the leftover cell is the one piece that goes to 0. The tempting rule keeps the only piece that vanishes and throws away both pieces that stay.

One of the two inventors, as historians of his manuscripts tell it, tried that guess in his notes of November 1675. He checked it on an example, and found it false. When he published his rules in 1684, in a short paper in the Acta Eruditorum, the product came with the right rule: each factor times the difference of the other, added.

13c7Running It Backwards

Handed the answer 3x²(2x + 5) + x³ · 2, look for two factors whose slopes appear. 3x² is the slope of x³ (slope-of-x³) and 2 is the slope of 2x + 5 (slope-of-a-line), so the product was x³(2x + 5). Handed a product whose slope must be 0 everywhere, as x · (1/x), look for factors that undo each other. Handed a product and a slope to check, compute one chord slope with a small h and compare, as above. A chord slope is not the slope, but it should be near it, and the exact chord slope tells you how near. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The modern book of the second road is Michael Spivak’s Calculus. The inventor who tried the tempting guess in November 1675, and published the right rule in 1684, is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz.

Spivak proves the product rule by adding and subtracting f(w + h)g(w), and points out that the proof needs f(w + h) to approach f(w), which is where the continuity of a function with a derivative is used.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 10

Accepted names: the product technique for slopes is the product rule.
