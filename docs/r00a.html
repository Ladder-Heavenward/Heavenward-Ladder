0a · proof

Names and Statements

Before anything can be proved, there has to be a way of writing down what is being talked about. This step builds exactly that. It also builds the ladder's rule for bringing in a new mark, and uses that rule on its own marks, so that you see it done before you are asked to trust it.

Name the thing with a letter. Cover the name and you have a claim waiting for a name; give the whole statement a letter and you have a statement under a name. 

Marks this rung brings in

Rung 0 brings in a few marks. Each is said in plain words first, and then shown, so that you know it on sight. None of them is used before the line that brings it in, on the page named beside it.

  • A short name for a thing: a letter standing alone, such as x (this step).
  • The place where a name is missing: three dots, … (this step).
  • A claim made about a thing: a capital, with the thing's name in round brackets after it, P(x) (this step).
  • A claim about two things, in order: both names in the brackets, with a comma between them, M(x, b) (this step).
  • Talk about a word, not about its thing: the word between quote marks, “Lena” (this step).
  • Two names for one thing, a statement with two gaps: the equal sign, x = b (0b).

0a1The First Axiom

Everything on this ladder starts from one statement laid down before any other. A statement laid down because there is nothing beneath it is called an axiom.

Possibility axiom: it is possible to represent any statement, or anything that happens, in a logical system. Without it, there would be things you could say or see that no statement could ever hold, and no foreclosure could ever be completed.

Nothing proves it. It is the bet that the climb is worth making: If it happens it can be written down.

0a2A Letter for a Thing

Suppose there is a cat asleep on a mat, and we want to talk about her. We could say “the cat asleep on the mat” each time, which is long. We could use her name, Tibbles, if we know it. Or we could do what this ladder does on every page: give her a letter. “Call the cat t.” From that sentence on, for as long as the page is about her, t is a name of the cat, exactly as “Tibbles” is. Whatever is so of Tibbles is so of t. The letter t is not a different thing; it is the same cat under a shorter name.

Any letter will do. A letter standing alone on this ladder is always a name, never a piece of a word. It can name anything you like: a cat, a colour, a town, a person, a sentence.

Your choice of letter is never wrong, and no page will ever say otherwise. If you would rather call the cat r, call her r. If you would rather use a capital for her, do. What matters is only that a letter names the same thing for the length of an argument.

Names are not unique, and they never were. Nobody is confused by this in ordinary life for more than a second because the conversation tells you which thing is meant. The same thing happens with letters on this ladder. The letter t names the cat on one page, a color on another, a town in a question.

0a3What a Statement Is

A statement is a sentence that says something that is so or not so. Here are four: “Tibbles is asleep.” “Ann lives on Elm Street.” “The gate is painted red.” “Rex is a puppy.” Take the first. Either Tibbles is asleep or she is not; there is no third way. That is what makes it a statement. It does not have to be so. “Tibbles is a dog” is a statement, and it is not so.

Plenty of good English does not look like a statement. “Shut the gate” commands. “Is Tibbles asleep?” asks. As they stand, neither one says how things are. Look closer, though, and each carries a statement inside it. A question keeps a statement open, waiting to be judged: “is Tibbles asleep?” is “Tibbles is asleep”, not yet settled. A command hides a statement of the form “if …, then …”. For example, “shut the gate” hides “if the gate is open, close it”. So the ladder does not throw questions and commands away. It asks each one to say the statement it carries before it stands in an argument.

A name on its own says nothing is so. “Tibbles” claims nothing. But it can be made a statement: “Tibbles is Tibbles”, a statement that is so; why it may be written is 0b’s business. “A grey cat” and “spotted Rex” are descriptions: a name with a claim built in. Said as sentences, they are statements too: “the cat is grey”, “Rex has spots”. Turning a claim from one shape to another is 1a’s transform, and the possibility axiom is why the claim survives the turn.

There is also a famous curiosity, the sentence “this sentence is false”. If it is so, then it is not, and if it is not, then it is. Whatever that sentence is, it cannot be judged once and for all. The ladder sets it aside for now, and comes back to it built in wires (4·comp).

Statements are the material of everything that follows. Only a statement can be so or not so, and that is why the arguments of 1a are built of statements and nothing else. What the ladder means by calling a statement true is 1a’s business too. In this step, so or not so is all we need.

0a4A Claim, Written With a Bracket

Take the statement “Tibbles is grey” and cover the name. What is left, “… is grey”, is a statement with a gap in it. The three dots, …, mark where the name was. They only keep the place and claim nothing, so they need no line of their own. “… is grey” is not yet a statement, because it says nothing until a name fills the gap. But it is ready to become one. Fill the gap with Tibbles and you have “Tibbles is grey”; fill it with Rex and you have “Rex is grey”.

A statement-with-a-gap gets a letter too, by habit a capital. Filling its gap with a name makes a claim about that thing. For example, call “… is grey” P. Fill P's gap with Tibbles and you have the claim “Tibbles is grey”: a statement that says something of her, so or not so.

Here is the ladder's way of writing a filled claim, as a line. That line is also the ladder's way of bringing in any mark at all: a line that says what the mark means, before the mark is ever used.

So if P is “… is grey” and x is Tibbles, P(x) is the statement “Tibbles is grey”. With b naming Rex, P(b) is “Rex is grey”. The brackets are part of the writing, not a thing: they hold the name against the gap it fills.

A statement-with-a-gap can have the gap in any place. “Ann lives on …” is as good as “… lives on Elm Street”. If you call it Q, then Q(e), with e naming Elm Street, is “Ann lives on Elm Street”.

The thing that fills a gap has a name of its own. A member is whatever slots into a gap: here a name or a phrase, in the gap of a statement; on rung 2 the gap will be a set's, and what fills it is a member in the same sense. A gap takes whole statements too, not only names. Cover “Rex is barking” in “Ann hears that Rex is barking”, and “Ann hears that …” waits for a statement. Fill it with “the kettle is on”, and the statement is the member there.

Notice that P alone, with no bracket after it, is still waiting for its name. It is a statement with a gap: not yet so or not so, until a name fills it. An open gap does not make P idle. Once a name fills it, you can judge it, and not before.

0a5Gaps in Several Places

Cover both names in “Ann lives next door to Ben” and you have “… lives next door to …”: a statement with a gap in two places, waiting for two names. It is a different thing from a gap filled twice by the same name, and the bracket needs a little more to say which name goes where. Here is the same bracket, given a further job by a line:

The bracket's job from 0a4 still stands: one name in one gap, as in P(x). A bracket with one name in it does the one-gap job. A bracket with names separated by commas does the several-gaps job.

So let M be “… lives next door to …”, with n naming Ann and b naming Ben. Then M(n, b) is “Ann lives next door to Ben”, and M(b, n) is “Ben lives next door to Ann”. Those are different statements, even though, for neighbours, they stand or fall together.

With “… is older than …” the two orders could not both be so. If Ann is older than Ben, then Ben is not older than Ann: swap the names and the claim flips. The order of the names is part of what is said. And one name may fill both gaps. M(n, n) is “Ann lives next door to Ann”, a perfectly good statement, and not so.

More gaps take more names. Let G be “… gives … to …”, with m naming Mike, k naming the key and n naming Ann. Then G(m, k, n) is “Mike gives the key to Ann”: three names, in the order the gaps are read. G(n, k, m) is “Ann gives the key to Mike”, a different statement.

0a6Four Shapes of a Claim

People say things of a thing in more than one shape, and the ladder takes in each of them. It names the shapes by the kind of word that does the saying. The names are the ladder's choice; grammar books use some of them differently, or not at all.

  • Predicate: the thing is joined to a describing word by “is” (a copula, a joining word). Example: “Rex is spotted”, S(r).
  • Tentative: the thing is joined to something it has by “has” (a possession, a having word). Example: “Rex has spots”, with s naming the spots, H(r, s). It can be read as a claim about Rex, “… has spots”. It can also be read as a claim about the spots, “Rex has …”.
  • Processive: the thing does something (an action). A verb that takes an object takes two names. Example: “Rex chases the ball”, C(r, b). A verb that takes no object gives only one. Example: “Rex barks”, B(r). With no object there is one way to cut it, and no second name to read it as a claim about.
  • Descriptive: the thing has a describing word set in front of it (a modification). Example: “spotted Rex”. It is not a statement; it is a name with a claim built into it.

These are rules for rewriting a sentence so that its claims come out one at a time. That is what lets the ladder handle the grammar people actually use. “Spotted Rex is barking” holds two claims, a descriptive and a processive. Written out, they are “Rex is spotted” and “Rex is barking”: S(r) and B(r). “Rex, the spotted dog who is barking” is a name with both built in. Take it apart and the same two claims come out.

0a7A Statement Named Whole

A capital letter can also name a whole statement on its own, with no gap and no name in a bracket. “Let A be ‘the last apricot was taken’.” From that sentence on, A is a statement in its own right, judged so or not so like any other. A is so if the last apricot was taken, and not so if it was not. There is nothing after A to fill, so the ladder writes it bare.

So capitals do two jobs on this ladder, and the difference is worth keeping straight. P(x) is a statement built by filling a gap: the claim P makes about x. A is a statement named whole: the letter stands for the entire sentence, and nothing in it is waiting for a name. Both are statements, and either can stand wherever a statement can. The bracket is what tells you which you are looking at. A capital with a bracket after it is a statement-with-a-gap with its gap filled. A capital standing alone is either a statement named whole, or a statement-with-a-gap still waiting. The line that brought the letter in says which.

0a8“Something”, Said of a Gap

In everyday speech the gap usually gets a word instead of three dots: “something is grey”, “someone lives on Elm Street”. The word is doing honest work, and it does two different jobs.

First job: “something” asks you to put off your judgment. There is a slot here, and which thing fills it is still to be worked out. That is exactly the gap. The ladder writes it “…”, or, once it needs a name of its own while it is being worked out, with a letter. Example: you say “something is on the mat”, not yet knowing what. You have made no claim; you have left a gap, the way “… is on the mat” leaves one.

Second job: “something is grey” is already a claim you can judge. It is so, because Tibbles is grey: it says that at least one thing fills the slot. Example: the words next door to “something”, “nothing” and “everything”, show this job plainly. “Nothing is grey” and “everything is grey” are each not so, and a bare slot would not care which of those words stood beside it.

The test that tells the two jobs apart is the test for a statement: can you judge it yet? The first job comes first. Until you have more tools, read “some” as the gap. Writing a gap in plain English as “something”, “someone” or “some colour” is correct. In 2b we'll refine how “some” and “every” affect our statements.

0a9Using a Name, and Mentioning It

Most of the time a name is used: it stands for its thing, and the sentence is about the thing. “Lena sings” uses the name, and is about the woman. Sometimes a sentence is about the name itself: how it is spelled, how long it is, what ink it is written in. Then the name is mentioned, not used. English shows it by putting the name between quote marks. “‘Lena’ begins with a capital letter” is about the word, and is so. “Lena begins with a capital letter” would be about the woman, and says nothing sensible. Whole sentences can be mentioned the same way. When this step writes “Tibbles is asleep” in quote marks, it is talking about the sentence, not claiming that Tibbles is asleep. The ladder keeps to that habit, as a line:

From here on, a name or a sentence between quote marks is mentioned: the statement is about the words, not about what they name or claim.

So a quotation is itself a name: the thing it names is the words between the marks. “Lena” and “the woman who sings” are two names for one person, and they are different names. So a statement about a name is not a statement about its thing. 0b will need that fact as soon as it has a mark for sameness.

A language loop. Some names carry a description inside them. For example, a family name can carry a place: “Rosa from Salinas” is called after Salinas, and “Rosa from Salinas is from Salinas” is so. Now try “Ann of Ann”. The description inside the name contains the name, so reading out what it calls never stops: Ann of Ann, of Ann, of Ann, and on without end.

Something said in terms of itself is a loop, and the ladder's word for building a thing out of its own earlier steps is recursion. Nothing in this step can handle one. A name here is a bare label that calls a thing and says nothing about it, so the loop cannot start. The ladder builds the tool for things defined by themselves on 4d, where defining by repeating a step is made safe: every repeat starts from one that came before, and the first starts from something given. Until then, when a loop turns up, notice it, enjoy it, and step around it.

0a10How a Mark Is Brought In

You have now seen the rule in use on its own marks, so it can be said plainly. On this ladder, no mark is written until a line has said what it means. The line always has the same shape: “from here on, this mark means…”. This step brought the bracket in with such a line, and then gave the bracket a further job for several gaps. The boxed lines in 0a4 and 0a5 are those two lines.

The rule is what keeps the no-foreshadowing promise honest. The one exception is foreclosure: a step that needs a tool the ladder has not built yet says it in plain words now, and the tool is built later. It is always flagged where it opens. If you meet a mark on any page, a line on that page or an earlier one gave it its meaning. Marks are only shorthand for words. The words come first, and the mark is always allowed to be read back into them.

Marks overlap, like names, and for a reason worth knowing. There are only so many small marks a hand can scribble that a reader can tell apart. The scribbles run out long before the ideas do. So mathematics reuses its marks, the way English reuses names. It is an economy of scribbles, and that economy is a large part of why mathematics looks the way it does, dense and small.

This ladder handles the reuse in the open. A mark already in use may be given a further job. Then the same kind of line does it, saying plainly that the mark is being reused and what it means in its new place. The bracket's second line, in 0a5, did exactly that reuse. Until a page says otherwise, a mark means what it was last told to mean. So never fret that a sign meant something on an earlier page: it still means that.

Who said it first

Frege called the part of a statement that is left when a name is taken out “unsaturated”: it is incomplete, and needs something put into its gap before it says anything. The name, he said, is complete in itself.
