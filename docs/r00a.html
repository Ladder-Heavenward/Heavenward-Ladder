0a · proof

Names and Statements

Before anything can be proved, there has to be a way of writing down what is being talked about. This step builds exactly that. It also builds the ladder's rule for bringing in a new mark, and uses that rule on its own marks, so that you see it done before you are asked to trust it.

Name the thing with a letter. Cover the name and you have a claim waiting for a name; give the whole statement a letter and you have a statement under a name. 

Marks this rung brings in

Rung 0 brings in a few marks. Each is said in plain words first, and then shown, so that you know it on sight. None of them is used before the line that brings it in, on the page named beside it.

  • A short name for a thing: a letter standing alone, such as x (this step).
  • The place where a name is missing: three dots, … (this step).
  • A claim made about a thing: a capital, with the thing's name in round brackets after it, P(x) (this step).
  • A claim about two things, in order: both names in the brackets, with a comma between them, M(x, b) (this step).
  • Talk about a word, not about its thing: the word between quote marks, “Lena” (this step).
  • Two names for one thing, a statement with two gaps: the equal sign, x = b (0b).

0a1The First Axiom

Everything on this ladder starts from one statement laid down before any other. A statement laid down because there is nothing beneath it is called an axiom.

Possibility axiom: it is possible to represent any statement, or anything that happens, in a logical system. Without it, there would be things you could say or see that no statement could ever hold, and no foreclosure could ever be completed.

Nothing proves it. It is the bet that the climb is worth making: if it happens it can be written down.

0a2A Letter for a Thing

Suppose there is a cat asleep on a bed. The cat is called cat, and that's a name. The cat is called Serena and that's a name too. Finally, in logic we'd call that cat "c" and say c is asleep on the bed, or "s" and say the same because in that case we made c the same as s.

Any letter works. A letter standing alone is always a name. It can name anything you like: a cat, a colour, a town, a person, a sentence, a statement, or a claim. The name is just a hook you use to grab the idea of the name.

Names are not unique, and they never were. Nobody is confused by this in ordinary life for more than a second because you can clear it up. The same thing happens in logic. If there are more than one thing with a shared name, we clear up if we meant c or s, or whatever.

0a3What a Statement Is

A statement is whatever strings ideas into a relationship. The cat is on the bed, the dog is barking. The cat is on the bed, is logically speaking a statement of sameness, the cat isn't the same as the bed, it is the same as on, and on refers to the bed. The dog is barking is a statement of sameness between dog and the function bark, bark-ing.

A statement can be true, but it can also be false, and working that out is the business of logic.

Statements are the material of everything that follows. Only a statement says how things are, and that is why the arguments of 1a are built of statements and nothing else. A statement shows what happened between names, nothing more.

Questions and commands have to be turned into statements to fit the logic, but they're easy. "Is the cat asleep?" turns into "The cat is asleep" and "Open the door" becomes "If the door is closed open it". 

We can write any statement as a single letter O for the 'on the bed' statement, B for the 'barking' statement.

0a4A Claim, Written With a Bracket

Take the cat off  of on the bed and put it somewhere. you're left with ()is on the bed, O (). Now you have a statement talking about the sameness, where anything can be the same. O(c) in the original example, but anything that is the same as 'on the bed' can fit into the slot. O(d) the dog is on the bed, O(b) the blanket is on the bed

Now, let's not move too fast and miss that a statement is a name by our rules, that fact is half of the logic game, and keeping it in mind will never be a problem.

Now, our first procedure box:

We can do a few things with this.

We can take "The cat is on the bed" and make "O" a statement about a cat on a bed.
We take the name out, and leave the sentence "() is on the bed" and we have a statement with a gap O(c). The cat is on the bed so "c is on the bed" is true, so it works.
Maybe we leave the cat in the statement, and take the bed out. "The cat is on the ()" O(b).

The thing that fills a gap has a name of its own. A member is whatever slots into a gap: here a name or a statement, later we get sets that are names with gaps where names and other sets can fill in. It's easier than it sounds.

0a5Gaps in Several Places

We can take out both "() is on the ()" and now we have a statement with two gaps O(c,b), so "c is on the b". O(c,m) the cat is on the mat. O(d,p) the dog is on the porch, we've made O into a machine that makes a relationship between two things O(m,p) the mat is on the porch.

All of this is pretty simple, it's just another way to write, where the idea is carried instead of the sound. One technique makes 4 different things.

The bracket's job from 0a4 still stands: one name in one gap, as in P(x). A bracket with one name in it does the one-gap job. A bracket with names separated by commas does the several-gaps job.

So let M be “… lives next door to …”, with n naming Ann and b naming Ben. Then M(n, b) is “Ann lives next door to Ben”, and M(b, n) is “Ben lives next door to Ann”. Clearly different, turned about from eachother.

With “… is older than …” the two orders say opposite things. If Ann is older than Ben, then Ben is not older than Ann: swap the names and the claim flips. The order of the names is part of what is said. And one name may fill both gaps. M(n, n) is “Ann lives next door to Ann”, a perfectly good statement, though an odd one.

More gaps take more names. Let G be “… gives … to …”, with m naming Mike, k naming the key and n naming Ann. Then G(m, k, n) is “Mike gives the key to Ann”: three names, in the order the gaps are read. G(n, k, m) is “Ann gives the key to Mike”, a different statement.

0a6Four Shapes of a Claim

You'll need to think about the kind of relationship that the statement has, if you want the guess to come out right. This is all technical, but it's simple.

  • Predicate: the thing is joined to a describing word by “is” (a copula, a joining word). Example: “Gomez is spotted”, S(g).
  • Tentative: the thing is joined to something it has by “has” (a possession, a having word). Example: “Gomez has spots”, with s naming the spots, H(g, s). It can be read as a claim about Gomez, “… has spots”. It can also be read as a claim about the spots, “Gomez has …”.
  • Processive: the thing does something (an action). A verb that takes an object takes two names. Example: “Gomez chases the ball”, C(g, b). A verb that takes no object gives only one. Example: “Gomez barks”, B(g). With no object there is one way to cut it, and no second name to read it as a claim about.
  • Descriptive: the thing has a describing word set in front of it (a modification). Example: “spotted Gomez”. It is not a statement; it is a name with a claim built into it.

These are rules for rewriting a sentence so that its claims come out one at a time. That is what lets the ladder handle the grammar people actually use. “Spotted Gomez is barking” holds two claims, a descriptive and a processive. Written out, they are “Gomez is spotted” and “Gomez is barking”: S(g) and B(g). “Gomez, the spotted dog who is barking” is a name with both built in. Take it apart and the same two claims come out.

0a7A Statement Named Whole

B(P(s)) Is just the same technique with another layer of result. Serena is on the pillow, the pillow is on the bed. () on the pillow, () on the bed. 
Serena is on the pillow on the bed B(P(s)). 

A lot of logic will separate this as P(b) and B(s), and that's fine, but the statements are both about the relationship between some system and another, and the whole system "Serena is on the pillow" is on the bed, so B(P(s)) is true, and specific and compact, we appreciate the economy.

0a8“Something”, Said of a Gap

In everyday speech the gap usually gets a word instead of three dots: “something is grey”, “someone lives on Elm Street”. The word is doing honest work, and it does two different jobs.

First job: “something” asks you to put off your judgment. There is a slot here, and which thing fills it is still to be worked out. That is exactly the gap. The ladder writes it “…”, or, once it needs a name of its own while it is being worked out, with a letter. Example: you say “something is on the mat”, not yet knowing what. You have made no claim; you have left a gap, the way “… is on the mat” leaves one.

Second job: “something is grey” is already a whole claim. It says that at least one thing fills the slot. Example: the words next door to “something”, “nothing” and “everything”, show this job plainly. “Nothing is grey” and “everything is grey” say other things again, and a bare slot would not care which of those words stood beside it.

The test that tells the two jobs apart is the test for a statement: can you judge it yet? The first job comes first. Until you have more tools, read “some” as the gap. Writing a gap in plain English as “something”, “someone” or “some colour” is correct. In 2b we'll refine how “some” and “every” affect our statements.

0a9Using a Name, and Mentioning It

Most of the time a name is used: it stands for its thing, and the sentence is about the thing. “Lena sings” uses the name, and is about the woman. Sometimes a sentence is about the name itself: how it is spelled, how long it is, what ink it is written in. Then the name is mentioned, not used. English shows it by putting the name between quote marks. “‘Lena’ begins with a capital letter” is about the word. “Lena begins with a capital letter” would be about the woman, and says nothing sensible. Whole sentences can be mentioned the same way. When this step writes “Serena is asleep” in quote marks, it is talking about the sentence, not claiming that Serena is asleep. The ladder keeps to that habit, as a line:

From here on, a name or a sentence between quote marks is mentioned: the statement is about the words, not about what they name or claim.

So a quotation is itself a name: the thing it names is the words between the marks. “Lena” and “the woman who sings” are two names for one person, and they are different names. So a statement about a name is not a statement about its thing. 0b will need that fact as soon as it has a mark for sameness.

A language loop. Some names carry a description inside them. For example, a family name can carry a place: “Rosa from Salinas” is called after Salinas, and “Rosa from Salinas is from Salinas” says only what her name already says. Now try “Ann of Ann”. The description inside the name contains the name, so reading out what it calls never stops: Ann of Ann, of Ann, of Ann, and on without end.

Something said in terms of itself is a loop, and the ladder's word for building a thing out of its own earlier steps is recursion. Nothing in this step can handle one. A name here is a bare label that calls a thing and says nothing about it, so the loop cannot start. The ladder builds the tool for things defined by themselves on 4d, where defining by repeating a step is made safe: every repeat starts from one that came before, and the first starts from something given. Until then, when a loop turns up, notice it, enjoy it, and step around it.

Who said it first

Frege called the part of a statement that is left when a name is taken out “unsaturated”: it is incomplete, and needs something put into its gap before it says anything. The name, he said, is complete in itself.
