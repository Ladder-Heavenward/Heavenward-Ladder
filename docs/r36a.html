The Shape of a Spectrum

Rung 32 took a periodic function apart into turnings; a spectrum is such a function on an interval, and 17·phys met light as a flow of dots. This step looks at the function as a shape: where it is high, where it is low, how wide its hills are, and whether it is one spike or a spread. The names it gives are the names the rest of the rung uses for what an eye receives.

A spectrum is a function from wavelength to amount, never negative. A spike is a spectrum that is 0 except near one wavelength; a spread is one that is not. Spectra add, and the sum’s amount at each wavelength is the sum of the amounts.

36a1A Spectrum Is a Function

From here on, the wavelength interval is a closed interval W of reals (36b says which), and a spectrum is a function S from W to the reals with S(λ) ≥ 0 for every λ in W. S(λ) is read “the amount at λ”. The accepted name for a graph of S is the spectrum of the light, and the ladder uses the one word for the function and its graph, as 11a5 did for curves.

A spectrum is not a colour. It is the whole function, with a value at every wavelength, and 9e draws it as a curve over W. Two spectra are the same function exactly when they agree at every λ, by 9a: a function is its pairs.

36a2Spikes and Spreads

From here on, a spectrum is a spike at λ₀ when it is 0 everywhere outside a small reach of λ₀ (10a2’s neighbourhood), and a spread when it is not a spike. A spike has one wavelength that matters; a spread has many. Every spectrum lies between: a spike of some reach, or a spread of some shape.

Where a spectrum is greatest is its peak, by 12c’s highest-and-lowest technique on a closed interval, which a spectrum has because 36b will take it continuous. A spread may have several peaks; a spike has one.

Spectrum-peak technique: a spectrum that is continuous on W has a greatest value, and so a peak.

36a3Adding Spectra

From here on, for spectra S and T, S + T is the spectrum with (S + T)(λ) = S(λ) + T(λ), and for a real k ≥ 0, kS has (kS)(λ) = k·S(λ). These are 10c1’s functions made from functions, and a sum or non-negative scaling of spectra is a spectrum, since sums and non-negative multiples of non-negative numbers are non-negative (5d7, 8g2).

Two lights shone together make the spectrum that is the sum of their spectra: that is laid down here as what “together” means for light, and 36·phys says why it is so. A spike added to a spike far from it is a spread with two peaks; the sum of two spreads is a spread.

Sum-of-spectra technique: S + T = T + S, (S + T) + U = S + (T + U), and k(S + T) = kS + kT. Proof: 5d1’s twelve techniques at each λ.

The accepted names

A spectrum in this sense is a spectral power distribution, or spectral density of the light. A spike is monochromatic light, or a spectral line; a spread is broadband light. The peak is the peak wavelength.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton gave the word spectrum its meaning for light in his paper on light and colours of 1672, for the band of colours a prism throws on a wall.

A proof that is not allowed

“S has its peak at 580 and so does T, at the same height. So S = T.” Not allowed: a peak is one value of the function, and 9a needs every value to agree. A spike at 580 and a wide hill with its top at 580 share a peak and are different functions.
