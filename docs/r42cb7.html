42·cb7 · newsflash for 7

Last time on the number too big to write down

Rung seven built a tower of threes so tall that its height alone has thirteen digits, and asked whether a number that could never be written is still a number. It found the last digit, seven, by watching what powers of three do on the clock of four and the clock of ten. That was the first clock the ladder used without the word. Now the clocks have their full arithmetic. The power on the clock of ten needs only its exponent on the clock of four, the count of units. The exponent is itself a tower, and its value on the clock of four needs its own exponent on the clock of two, and then the clock of one, where everything is zero. So a chain of smaller and smaller clocks reaches the bottom, and below some height the tower’s last digits stop changing. Every tower of threes taller than a few dozen ends in the same ten digits. The number rung seven could not write ends in 2464195387, and that is as rigid as any fact on the ladder.

A power of three on the clock of ten to the k needs its exponent only on the clock of the count of units, which is again a power of two times a power of five. The chain of counts reaches one in 3k steps, so the last k digits of a tower of threes freeze once it is that tall. A tower seven trillion threes high ends in 2464195387.

42·cb7.1Clocks inside clocks

7·then found 3↑↑b ends in 7 for b ≥ 2. It worked on the clocks of 4 and 10 by hand. Here is the move it made, in general. On the clock of 10ᵏ, 3 is a unit. By the exponent-clock technique, 3^E on that clock needs only E on the clock of c(10ᵏ), the count of units.

Clock-chain technique: starting from 10ᵏ and taking the count of units again and again, the clocks are all powers of 2 times powers of 5, 3 is a unit of each, and the chain reaches the clock of 1 in exactly 3k steps.

c(2^a 5^b) = 2^{a-1}\cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 5^{b-1} = 2^{a+1}5^{b-1} \text{ for } a, b \ge 1 @@ the unit-count technique (42d2): times ½ and times ⅘
c(2^a) = 2^{a-1} \text{ for } a \ge 1 @@ the same, with no 5
10^k = 2^k 5^k \to 2^{k+1}5^{k-1} \to \cdots \to 2^{2k} @@ k steps, one 5 at a time
\htmlClass{new}{2^{2k} \to 2^{2k-1} \to \cdots \to 1:\ \ 2k \text{ more steps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no clock in the chain has 3 as a factor, so 3 is a unit of each

From the clock of 10¹⁰ the chain is 10¹⁰, 4 · 10⁹, 1.6 · 10⁹, …, 2²⁰, 2¹⁹, …, 2, 1: thirty clocks.

42·cb7.2The tower freezes

Tower-freeze technique: for every k, the last k digits of 3↑↑b are the same for every b ≥ 3k + 1.

m_0 = 10^k,\ m_{i+1} = c(m_i),\ \ m_{3k} = 1 @@ the clock-chain technique
3\uparrow\uparrow b = 3^{\,3\uparrow\uparrow(b-1)} @@ the arrows’ step (7·then)
3\uparrow\uparrow b \bmod m_i \text{ depends only on } 3\uparrow\uparrow(b-1) \bmod m_{i+1} @@ the exponent-clock technique (42d3): 3 is a unit of mᵢ
\text{on the clock } m_{3k} = 1 \text{, every value is } 0 @@ one hour
\htmlClass{new}{3\uparrow\uparrow b \bmod 10^k \text{ is fixed once } b - 1 \ge 3k} \quad \blacksquare @@ down the chain, 3k times: each value is fixed by the one below

Here x mod m names the hour of x on the clock of m, read as its member from 0 to m − 1: it is the remainder of 4j7, and the mark is used only in this sense. The technique gives a height that is enough. The truth is lower: the last digit is 7 from b = 2, the last two are 87 from b = 3, the last three 387 from b = 4. The next footing shows why, with orders in place of counts.

42·cb7.3Three digits by hand

A power comes back after its order (42e1), and the order divides the count. So the chain can use orders, which are smaller. The order of [3] is 100 on the clock of 1000, 20 on the clock of 100, 4 on the clock of 20, and 2 on the clock of 4.

3\uparrow\uparrow(b-3) \text{ is odd},\ \text{so } 3\uparrow\uparrow(b-2) = 3^{\text{odd}} \equiv 3 \pmod 4 @@ order 2 on the clock of 4: an odd exponent gives 3
3\uparrow\uparrow(b-1) = 3^{(\ldots \equiv 3 \bmod 4)} \equiv 3^3 = 27 \equiv 7 \pmod{20} @@ order 4 on the clock of 20
3\uparrow\uparrow b = 3^{(\ldots \equiv 7 \bmod 20)} \equiv 3^7 = 2187 \equiv 87 \pmod{100} @@ order 20 on the clock of 100
3\uparrow\uparrow(b+1) \equiv 3^{87} \pmod{1000} @@ order 100 on the clock of 1000
\htmlClass{new}{3^{87} \equiv 387 \pmod{1000}} \quad \blacksquare @@ square-and-multiply (42h1): 87 = 1010111 in twos

So 3↑↑b ends in 87 from b = 3 on, and in 387 from b = 4 on. 3↑↑3 = 7 625 597 484 987 ends in 987, and 3↑↑4 already ends in 387. Each digit costs about one more level of the tower.

42·cb7.4Ten digits of the tower of rung 7

7·then’s tower 3↑↑↑3 is 3↑↑(3↑↑3), a tower of 7 625 597 484 987 threes. That is far above 31, so its last ten digits are frozen, and a tower of 31 threes has the same ones.

3\uparrow\uparrow 3 \equiv 5\,597\,484\,987 \pmod{10^{10}} @@ the thirteen digits of 7·then, cut to ten
3\uparrow\uparrow 4 \equiv 6\,100\,739\,387,\ \ 3\uparrow\uparrow 5 \equiv 9\,660\,355\,387 @@ down the chain of thirty clocks, by square-and-multiply on each
3\uparrow\uparrow 10 \equiv 6\,464\,195\,387,\ \ 3\uparrow\uparrow 11 \equiv 2\,464\,195\,387 @@ one more digit freezes at each level
3\uparrow\uparrow b \equiv 2\,464\,195\,387 \text{ for every } b \ge 11 @@ the tower-freeze technique, with the true heights of the run
\htmlClass{new}{3\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow 3 \text{ ends in } 2464195387} \quad \blacksquare @@ its height is at least 11

Each line is a few hundred products on clocks below 10¹⁰, work for a machine in a blink. The last digits are rigid, set by clocks. The first digit of 3↑↑↑3 is another matter. It depends on the whole size of the number, which no chain of clocks sees, and nobody knows it. 7·then said a number is a number if a recipe fixes it, written or not. Here part of it is written, and the part is the end.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

x mod m is the modulo operation, the least nonnegative residue. The chain of counts is the iterated totient; the bound by 3k steps is special to 10ᵏ, and in general the iterated totient of n reaches 1 in about log₂ n steps (Pillai, 1929). The tower-freeze technique says power towers stabilise modulo n; its general form is for any base and any n, with Euler’s theorem extended to non-units. The ten digits 2464195387 are also the last ten digits of Graham’s number, a number from Ronald Graham’s work of 1971 on cubes in many dimensions, which is a tower of threes far taller than 3↑↑↑3.

Who said it first

The last digits of Graham’s number were computed as soon as it was published in Martin Gardner’s column (1977); they appear in many sources since. The freezing of towers modulo n is folklore of olympiad number theory, and was written out by Ilan Vardi in his Computational Recreations in Mathematica (1991).

A proof that is not allowed

“3↑↑b ends in 7 for b ≥ 2, so 3↑↑b ends in 87 for b ≥ 2.” Not allowed: 3↑↑2 = 27 ends in 27. A digit freezes at its own height. The page’s third footing shows the two digits wait until b = 3.
