21i · technique

Around the Circle

A point going round and round a circle rises and falls, and its height over time draws a wave. Tides, seasons, sound and the swing of a pendulum all draw waves of that one shape. This step shows that every such wave is the plain wave of the sine, stretched taller or shorter, squeezed or spread along, slid sideways and lifted. Four numbers say how much of each, and four readings of a real wave are enough to find them.

Every wave of the sine’s shape is the sine moved: lifted, stretched up, squeezed across and slid along. How high it swings, how long it takes to repeat, where it starts, and the level it swings about are the four numbers that say how.

21i1How High, and About What Level

Pull the graph of y = sin θ upright by A, then lift it by d. By 20i it becomes y = A sin θ + d. Wave-height technique: y = A sin θ + d swings between d − |A| and d + |A|, evenly about the level d.

-1 \le \sin\theta \le 1 @@ the circle technique (21c)
-|A| \le A\sin\theta \le |A| @@ times A, for A > 0 or A < 0
\htmlClass{new}{d - |A| \le A\sin\theta + d \le d + |A|} \quad \blacksquare @@ add d; both ends are reached, at sin θ = ±1

From here on, the level d is the midline, and the half-height |A| is the amplitude.

21i2How Long Until It Repeats

Squeeze the graph across by b > 0, and y = sin θ becomes y = sin(bθ) (20i4, a sideways pull by 1/b). Period technique: y = sin(bθ) repeats every 2π/b, and no sooner.

\sin\!\big(b(\theta + \tfrac{2\pi}{b})\big) = \sin(b\theta + 2\pi) = \sin(b\theta) @@ a whole turn adds nothing (21b3)
0 < p,\ \ \sin(b(\theta + p)) = \sin(b\theta) \text{ for every } \theta @@ suppose a shorter repeat p
\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2b}:\ \ \sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + bp) = 1 @@ the top of the wave must come back
\tfrac{\pi}{2} + bp = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k @@ sin is 1 only at the top of the circle, a whole number of turns round (21b)
\htmlClass{new}{bp = 2\pi k,\ \ p \ge \tfrac{2\pi}{b}} \quad \blacksquare @@ k ≥ 1, since p > 0

From here on, the shortest repeat is the period. A wave with period T goes round once every T; its turn per unit of θ is 2π/T.

21i3Where It Starts

Slide-inside technique: to read the slide of y = sin(bθ − c), take b out first: sin(bθ − c) = sin(b(θ − c/b)), a slide by c/b, not by c. The squeeze acts on the slide too.

y = \sin(b\theta) \text{ slid by } h @@ graph-slide (20i2): replace θ by θ − h
y = \sin(b(\theta - h)) = \sin(b\theta - bh) @@ share out
\htmlClass{new}{\sin(b\theta - c) = \sin\!\big(b(\theta - \tfrac{c}{b})\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ c = bh, so h = c/b
\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \tfrac{\pi}{2}) @@ the complement (21e): the cosine is the sine slid left a quarter turn

From here on, the slide h is the phase shift. So every wave of this kind is A sin(b(θ − h)) + d, and cos is one of them.

21i4A Share of the Time Is a Share of the Turn

Four readings decide a wave. Wave-from-readings technique: from the highest value M, the lowest m, the time T between two highs, and the time t₀ of one high, the wave is y = A cos(2π(t − t₀)/T) + d, with A = (M − m)/2 and d = (M + m)/2.

d = \tfrac{M + m}{2},\ \ A = \tfrac{M - m}{2} @@ wave-height: the midline is halfway, the amplitude half the gap
b = \tfrac{2\pi}{T} @@ period: one turn per T
h = t_0 @@ cos is highest at 0, so slide the high to t₀
\htmlClass{new}{y = A\cos\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{T}(t - t_0)\big) + d} \quad \blacksquare @@ slide-inside: cos is sin slid, so this is A sin(b(t − h)) + d too

The share (t − t₀)/T of the time since a high is the same share of a whole turn. A quarter of the way through, the wave is at its midline, falling; half way, at its lowest.

The accepted names

A graph y = a sin(b(θ − h)) + d is a sinusoid. Its amplitude is |A|, its period 2π/|b|, its phase shift h, its midline y = d. The number b/2π of turns per unit is its frequency. In physics the turn per unit, b, is the angular frequency, often written ω.

Who said it first

In 21i.6.5, the disc is August Seebeck’s siren (1841), and the reply is Georg Ohm’s “acoustic law” (1843), backed by Hermann von Helmholtz, On the Sensations of Tone (1863). Seebeck’s pitch with no sine wave at its own rate is now called the missing fundamental (Jan Schouten, 1940).
