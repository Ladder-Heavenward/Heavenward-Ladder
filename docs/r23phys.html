How does a lens bend light?

How does a lens bend light? A drop of water on a leaf makes the veins under it look bigger. A curved piece of glass held in the sun gathers its light into a bright spot that can char paper. The front of your own eye does the same job every waking second. People ground lenses for spectacles for centuries before anyone knew why a curved surface gathers light at all. What makes it hard is that the rule for bending is not simple. Light that crosses from air into glass bends by an amount set by the sine of its slant, and the sine is not a straight proportion. Followed exactly, the rays through a round piece of glass do not meet at one point. This page shows, with the quickest path of rung twenty, the sines of rung twenty-one and rung twenty-three’s polynomials that hug a curve, where each ray really crosses, why the rays near the middle still gather to one spot, how big the blur at the edge is, and how the same small-slant reading measures the distance to the stars.

Light crossing into glass keeps one product the same on both sides: how slow the light is, times the sine of its slant. A ray that hits a round glass surface off centre crosses the middle line at a place that depends on how far off centre it came in. The rays near the middle meet very nearly at one spot, because for small slants the sine is the slant itself, and the bend is then in proportion to the height. The rays near the edge cross a little short, by an amount that grows with the square of their height. The same small-slant reading turns the tiny yearly shift of a near star into its distance, with an error far smaller than any measurement.

23phys1Least time, in sines

20·phys found that the quickest path from air into water bends so that the run along the surface over the length of the path, divided by the speed, is the same on both sides. Measure the slant of each part of the ray from the upright to the surface, and call it θ. The run over the length is then sin θ (21b).

From here on, on this page, n for a clear stuff is how many times slower light runs in it than in empty space: n = c/v. Measured, n is about 1.0003 for air (take it as 1), 1.33 for water and about 1.52 for window glass. Each is a law found by induction under the case with predictable variation: a few careful measurements, one rule, and named exceptions such as colour.

\tfrac{\text{run}_1/\ell_1}{v_1} = \tfrac{\text{run}_2/\ell_2}{v_2} @@ the bend technique (20·phys.3)
\text{run}_i/\ell_i = \sin\theta_i @@ cos and sin as coordinates (21b), θ from the upright
\tfrac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \tfrac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2} @@ put the sines in
\htmlClass{new}{n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply both sides by c; n = c/v

Kept-sine technique: light crossing from one clear stuff into another keeps n sin θ the same on both sides. Sunlight meets Lake Washington at 50° from the upright. Below the surface, sin θ₂ = sin 50°/1.33 ≈ 0.576, so the light runs on at about 35.2° from the upright. The bend depends on sines, not on the slants themselves.

23phys2Where each ray crosses

Take a glass rod with a rounded end, as round as a ball of radius R, its centre C at the origin. Light comes from the left, parallel to the rod’s middle line, the axis. A ray at height y meets the glass at P = (−R cos θ₁, R sin θ₁), where sin θ₁ = y/R. Write s = y/R.

\sin\theta_1 = s @@ the upright to a round surface is its radius (21q), so θ₁ is the slant at P
\sin\theta_2 = \tfrac{s}{n} @@ kept-sine technique, from air (n₁ = 1) into glass
\text{inside, the ray dips at } \theta_1 - \theta_2 \text{ below the axis} @@ it turns toward the radius by θ₁ − θ₂
x_F = -R\cos\theta_1 + \tfrac{R\sin\theta_1}{\tan(\theta_1 - \theta_2)} = \tfrac{R\sin\theta_2}{\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)} @@ run down from height y to the axis; the sum technique (21d)
\htmlClass{new}{x_F = \tfrac{R}{\sqrt{n^2 - s^2} - \sqrt{1 - s^2}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide top and bottom by sin θ₂ = s/n; cos = √(1 − sin²) (21c)

Crossing technique: a ray entering a round glass surface at height y crosses the axis R/(√(n² − s²) − √(1 − s²)) past the centre, with s = y/R. With n = 1.5 and R = 10 mm, a ray very near the axis crosses 20.0 mm past the centre. A ray at 5 mm crosses at 18.2 mm, and one at 8 mm at 15.0 mm. A round surface has no exact focus: each height has its own crossing.

23phys3Small slants make a focus

The crossing technique is exact, and it is untidy. Read it near the axis, where s is small. 23h’s binomial series, with the power ½, hugs each square root.

\sqrt{n^2 - s^2} = n\sqrt{1 - \tfrac{s^2}{n^2}} = n - \tfrac{s^2}{2n} - \cdots @@ the binomial series (23h), power ½
\sqrt{1 - s^2} = 1 - \tfrac{s^2}{2} - \cdots @@ the same
\sqrt{n^2 - s^2} - \sqrt{1 - s^2} = (n - 1)\big(1 + \tfrac{s^2}{2n}\big) + \cdots @@ subtract: s²/2 − s²/(2n) = (n − 1)s²/(2n)
\htmlClass{new}{x_F = \tfrac{R}{n - 1}\big(1 - \tfrac{s^2}{2n}\big) + \cdots} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1/(1 + u) = 1 − u + ⋯ (15i), u = s²/(2n)

The dots stand for terms in s⁴ and beyond. Near the axis they are small beside the s² term, which is itself small. So every ray near the axis crosses close to one spot, R/(n − 1) past the centre, which is nR/(n − 1) behind the front. That spot is the focus. A ray at height y falls short of it by about R s²/(2n(n − 1)) = y²/(2n(n − 1)R).

Falling-short technique: rays near the axis gather at nR/(n − 1) behind a round glass front, and a ray at height y falls short of that spot by about y²/(2n(n − 1)R).

The same spot comes from the small-slant reading alone. For small slants sin θ is θ to within θ³/6, by 23b’s error bound on the matching polynomial θ (23a). Read sin as the slant itself, and the sine-bend becomes θ₂ = θ₁/n, a straight proportion. The ray then dips by θ₁ − θ₂ = θ₁(n − 1)/n and reaches the axis after a run of y over that dip, which is nR/(n − 1) for every y. The small-slant reading is what makes a lens. The dropped θ³/6 is what blurs it.

The front of the eye is a clear dome of radius about 7.8 mm, with watery fluid behind it, n ≈ 1.336. Alone it would gather light 1.336 × 7.8/0.336 ≈ 31.0 mm behind the front. The eye is about 24 mm long, so the lens inside the eye adds the rest of the bending. In daylight the pupil is about 3 mm across, so y reaches 1.5 mm, and the edge rays fall short by about 2.25/(2 × 1.336 × 0.336 × 7.8) ≈ 0.32 mm.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, focused

21a said that every value of π is a fence, a lower and an upper number, whose width you choose. A focus is the same kind of thing. The crossing technique shows that a round surface gathers light to a short stretch of the axis, never to a point. The falling-short technique says how long the stretch is: it grows as the square of the opening.

A focus is a short stretch of the axis, and you choose its length when you choose the opening.

So a lens maker does not ask whether the rays meet. They do not. The question is how wide the blur is, and whether it is narrower than what the eye, or the film, or the sensor can tell apart. Halve the opening, and the blur shrinks to a quarter. That is why a camera’s picture sharpens when its opening is stopped down.

On a bright day at Cannon Beach your pupil is about 2 mm across. At dusk it opens to about 7 mm. By how many times does the blur from the round front of the eye grow, and would you expect to see less sharply at dusk?

23phys4How far away is a star?

The earth goes round the sun at about 1.496 × 10⁸ km, a distance with the accepted name of one astronomical unit, AU. Half a year apart it stands 2 AU from where it was. A near star seems to shift against the far ones behind it, and back again. Half the full yearly swing is p, the star’s parallax: the slant at which one AU is seen from the star. The sun, the earth and the star make a right corner at the sun, so d = 1 AU / tan p.

d = \tfrac{1\ \text{AU}}{\tan p} = \tfrac{1\ \text{AU}}{p}\cdot\tfrac{p}{\tan p} @@ the right corner at the sun (21c)
\cos p = \tfrac{\sin p}{\tan p} \le \tfrac{p}{\tan p} \le 1 @@ the small-arc technique: sin p ≤ p ≤ tan p (21f)
\cos p = 1 - 2\sin^2\tfrac{p}{2} \ge 1 - \tfrac{p^2}{2} @@ the sum technique (21d), then sin(p/2) ≤ p/2 (21f)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1\ \text{AU}}{p}\big(1 - \tfrac{p^2}{2}\big) \le d \le \tfrac{1\ \text{AU}}{p}} \quad \blacksquare @@ p in radians

Parallax technique: a star whose parallax is p radians is 1 AU/p away, too far by a share less than p²/2. One second of arc is a 3600th of a degree, π/648 000 of a radian. A star with a parallax of one second is 206 265 AU away, about 3.26 light-years: the accepted name of that distance is a parsec. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the sun, has p ≈ 0.768″. So it is 1/0.768 ≈ 1.30 parsecs off, about 4.25 light-years, and the small-slant reading is off by a share under 7 × 10⁻¹². Every bit of doubt in that distance comes from measuring p.

A lens is what measures p. The round surface of 23·phys.2 looks the same from every slant through its centre, so light from a star off the axis by p gathers to the spot turned by p about C. That spot sits a run of about p R/(n − 1) to the side. Behind a lens whose near-axis rays gather 10 m past its centre, Proxima’s yearly swing moves its image by about 2 × 10 × 0.768/206 265 m, some 74 thousandths of a millimetre.

The accepted names

n is the refractive index, and the kept-sine technique is the law of refraction, Snell’s law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, measured from the normal. The crossing technique traces a ray exactly, as a ray trace does. The falling-short technique is the paraxial approximation for a single spherical refracting surface, with focal length nR/(n − 1); reading sin θ as θ is the small-angle approximation, and paraxial optics, or Gaussian optics, is optics built on it. The shortfall of the edge rays is spherical aberration, and its growth with the square of the height is the first term of the theory of aberrations (Seidel, 1856). The opening is the aperture, and stopping down is closing it. Parallax measured from the earth’s orbit is annual, or trigonometric, parallax; the parsec is its unit.

Who said it first

Ibn al-Haytham (about 1021) studied burning spheres and saw that rays far from the axis gather short of the near ones. Johannes Kepler (Dioptrice, 1611) explained the lens of the eye and the telescope with small slants. Pierre de Fermat derived the law of refraction from least time in 1662. René Descartes (1637) showed that a front shaped as a certain ellipse, not a ball, brings every parallel ray to one point: 23·phys.6.3. In 23·phys.6.5, Tycho Brahe argued about 1588 that if the earth moved, the stars would have to be so far away that their visible disks made each of them larger than the earth’s whole path round the sun; the Copernicans answered that the stars were simply far. The disks were later shown to be a blur made by the instrument and the eye, not the stars. Friedrich Bessel measured the first parallax, of 61 Cygni, in 1838. The Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, has measured the parallaxes of more than a billion stars.
