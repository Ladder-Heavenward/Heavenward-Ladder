38g · proof

The liar box

Rung zero set one statement aside: this statement is false. If it is so, it is not, and if it is not, it is. Rung one refuted it as a statement and moved on. The computing annex of rung four built it in wire, a not gate whose output runs back to its own input, and found it was neither true nor false but a timer, true and false in turn. This step builds that box from the parts of this rung and counts it. As a sameness to be settled once, the liar asks for a mark equal to one take away itself, and the only number that meets it is one half: no mark at all, but exactly the voltage at the steep middle of the gate. As a wire that answers its own answer a moment late, it flips once a moment, for ever. A ring of three gates, or any odd number, behaves the same way more slowly, and its one resting place is the middle, which the steep slope pushes away from. The time round the ring can be counted exactly: twice the number of gates, times the delay of one. That count is how chips measure their own speed.

The liar asks for x equal to one take away x, and only one half meets it: no mark. With a delay, x at each moment is one take away x a moment before, so it flips every delay. A ring of an odd number of nots has one rest, in the middle, and it does not hold; the ring runs, with a period of twice the gates times the delay.

38g1A not fed back into itself

1b took the statement that says of itself that it is not so, and refuted it: supposing it gives ⊥, and so does supposing its denial. In 38a’s arithmetic it is a mark x with x = 1 − x. Built in wire, it is one not gate with its output joined to its input. A real gate answers a change at its input only after a delay τ, a few trillionths of a second on a chip.

Delay-loop technique: a not whose output feeds its own input has no mark that rests, and with a delay τ its output is 1 and 0 in turn, each for τ, with period 2τ.

x = 1 - x \Rightarrow 2x = 1 \Rightarrow x = \tfrac12 @@ the same change to both sides (5g)
\tfrac12 \notin \{0, 1\} @@ no mark meets it: 1b’s refutation, in arithmetic
x(t) = 1 - x(t - \tau) @@ with the delay: the output now is the not of the output a delay ago
x(t) = 1 - (1 - x(t - 2\tau)) = x(t - 2\tau) @@ the same line, a delay earlier
\htmlClass{new}{x \text{ repeats every } 2\tau,\ \text{and is } 1 - x \text{ a delay on}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so it is 1, then 0, then 1: a clock

The ½ is not idle. On a wire, ½ is the middle voltage m of 38e2, where the gate’s curve crosses the line v = v. The liar’s only answer as a sameness is the gate’s one balanced point, and 38e2 proved that point does not hold when the slope there is steep. Read once, the liar has no mark. Read over time, it has a steady pattern: so and not so in turn, a moment each.

38g2An odd ring has no rest that holds

Join n not gates in a ring, each feeding the next and the last feeding the first. The ring rests when every gate’s input and output agree with its neighbours’: when v = h(v), with h = g ∘ g ∘ ⋯ ∘ g, n times (9b).

Odd-ring technique: a ring of an odd number of not gates has exactly one rest, the middle voltage m, and when |g′(m)| > 1 it does not hold.

g \text{ falls},\ \ n \text{ odd} \Rightarrow h \text{ falls} @@ a fall after a fall is a rise; an odd count of falls is a fall
h(v) - v \text{ falls strictly, from above } 0 \text{ at } v = 0 \text{ to below } 0 \text{ at the supply} @@ a falling function less a rising one; h(0) > 0 and h(supply) < supply
\text{exactly one } v \text{ with } h(v) = v @@ in-between values (12a) for one, and strict falling for no more
g(m) = m \Rightarrow h(m) = m @@ so the one rest is m
\htmlClass{new}{h'(m) = g'(m)^n,\ |h'(m)| > 1:\ \text{the rest pushes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chain rule n times (13e); the middle-gain technique’s argument (38e2)

So an odd ring cannot sit still. Any noise moves it off m, the steep slope carries it away, and it never finds another place to rest. The ring of one gate is the liar box; a ring of three or five is the same box, slower, and easier to build, because one gate feeding itself is too quick for the wire between.

38g3The period of a ring

Name the outputs round the ring x₁, x₂, …, xₙ, each the not of the one before, a delay late, and x₁ the not of xₙ.

Ring-period technique: a ring of an odd number n of not gates, each with delay τ, runs with period 2nτ; a ring of an even number repeats any pattern every nτ, and may rest.

x_{k+1}(t) = 1 - x_k(t - \tau),\ \ x_1(t) = 1 - x_n(t - \tau) @@ the ring
x_1(t) = \neg^{\,n}\, x_1(t - n\tau) @@ follow the wire round once: n nots, n delays
n \text{ odd}:\ x_1(t) = 1 - x_1(t - n\tau) @@ an even count of nots cancels in pairs (38a2), one is left
\htmlClass{new}{x_1 \text{ flips every } n\tau,\ \text{period } 2n\tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ the delay-loop technique with nτ for τ; n even gives x₁(t) = x₁(t − nτ) instead
  
n = 3,\ \tau = 10 \text{ ps}:\ \ 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 10 = 60 \text{ ps} @@ a trillionth of a second is a picosecond, ps
\tfrac{1}{60 \cdot 10^{-12}} \approx 1.67 \cdot 10^{10} \text{ Hz} @@ about 16.7 billion turns a second
n = 101,\ \tau = 15 \text{ ps}:\ \ 2 \cdot 101 \cdot 15 = 3030 \text{ ps} @@ a ring kept on chips to measure their own gates
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{3.03 \cdot 10^{-9}} \approx 330 \text{ MHz}:\ \text{read the frequency, and } \tau = \tfrac{1}{2nf}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the count turned round

The change runs round the ring twice per period: once to turn x₁ from 1 to 0, once to turn it back. A factory cannot see a delay of 15 ps directly, but it can count 330 million turns a second and divide. That is the liar box at work.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A ring of an odd number of inverters is a ring oscillator, and its period 2nτ uses the propagation delay τ of each stage. The liar statement is the liar paradox; x = 1 − x with x = ½ is its value in a three-valued or continuous logic. Rings of 101 or more stages on test chips measure process speed.

Who said it first

The liar is from Eubulides of Miletus in the fourth century BCE; the Cretan who calls all Cretans liars is older, from Epimenides. Saul Kripke’s theory of truth (1975) found a fixed point of the process of fixing truths, at which the liar is neither true nor false. Anil Gupta and Hans Herzberger in 1982 read the liar by revision, flipping at each step of a sequence, which is the ring of 38g1 in logic’s words. Ring oscillators were used in valve circuits from the 1920s and are on almost every chip made since the 1970s.

A proof that is not allowed

“The liar box oscillates, so the liar statement is true half the time and false half the time, and its truth value is ½.” Not allowed: the box is a wire, not a statement. The statement was refuted on 1b and has no mark. What the box shows is that the arithmetic sameness x = 1 − x, given a delay, becomes a rule fed its own answers, and that rule has a pattern in time. The ½ is the box’s one rest, which never holds.
