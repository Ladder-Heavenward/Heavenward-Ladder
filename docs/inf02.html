Are Every and Some Two Sides of One Arrow?

Are every and some two sides of one arrow? Take a function from one set to another. It carries each member of the first set to a member of the second, and with it each part of the second set comes back as a part of the first: the members whose outputs land in that part. That coming back is called pulling back, and rung 34 used it to say what continuous means. Pulling back is as plain as an operation gets, and it has no way back: many parts of the first set do not come from any part of the second. But it has two best guesses at a way back. One guesses from below and one from above. The guess from below sends a part of the first set to the outputs of its members: an output gets in when some member over it is in the part. The guess from above lets an output in when every member over it is in the part. So some and every, the two words of rung 2, are the two best guesses at undoing one arrow. This was seen in 1969, and it sits at the centre of the proof checkers that rung 38 described. This page proves both guesses best, reads rung 2’s four moves for every and some off the two proofs, finds the colon of 2e19 in the pulling itself, and finds necessary and possible from rung 3 as the same pair said over the ways things could go. Nothing here is open. The page is a re-reading, and the re-reading is the news.

f : X → Y pulls V ⊆ Y back to f⁻¹(V) = {x ∈ X : f(x) ∈ V}. Its two best approximations: the image f[P] = {y : some x over y is in P} on the left, f[P] ⊆ V ⇔ P ⊆ f⁻¹(V); and the every-image f∀[P] = {y : every x over y is in P} on the right, f⁻¹(V) ⊆ P ⇔ V ⊆ f∀[P]. With f a projection (x, y) ↦ y, these are ∃x and ∀x, and their four directions are 2b’s four moves.

inf2.1Parts of a Set as an Order

Fix a set X. Its parts, ordered by ⊆, are an order: parts run in a row and u ⊆ u (2d10). By 47a2 an order is a category with at most one arrow between two things: an arrow from P to Q exactly when P ⊆ Q. In this category 2f5’s P ∪ Q is the least part with P and Q as parts, and 2f8’s P ∩ Q is the greatest part of both.

For X = {1, 2, 3} the order has 8 parts. From ∅ to X there is one arrow; from {1} to {2} there is none. Between two orders, 47f3 showed that f is left of g, f ⊣ g, says only this: f(x) ≤ y exactly when x ≤ g(y). Naturality comes free, since there is at most one arrow anywhere.

One-right-side technique: a map between orders has at most one right side: if f ⊣ g and f ⊣ g′, then g = g′. The same holds for left sides, with the order turned round.

g(y) \le g(y) \ \Rightarrow\ f(g(y)) \le y @@ f ⊣ g, read right to left, with x = g(y)
f(g(y)) \le y \ \Rightarrow\ g(y) \le g'(y) @@ f ⊣ g′, read left to right
g'(y) \le g(y) @@ the same two lines with g and g′ swapped
\htmlClass{new}{g(y) = g'(y) \text{ for every } y} \quad \blacksquare @@ an order: both ways means the same

So once pulling back is fixed, its right side, if it has one, is fixed too, and so is its left side. Nobody chooses every and some. They are found.

inf2.2Pulling Back Along a Function

Let f : X → Y. 34a4 named the pull-back of a part V of Y: f⁻¹(V) is the set of x in X with f(x) in V. It exists for every f, bijection or not.

The example for the whole page: X = {1, 2, 3, 4}, Y = {a, b, c}, f(1) = f(2) = a, f(3) = f(4) = b. Nothing lands on c. Then f⁻¹({a}) = {1, 2}, f⁻¹({b, c}) = {3, 4}, f⁻¹({c}) = ∅ and f⁻¹(Y) = X. Eight parts of Y come back as only four parts of X, since c adds nothing: {1}, {1, 3} and ten others are never pulled back.

Pull-back-keeps technique: pulling back keeps ⊆, ∪, ∩ and the part left over: f⁻¹(V ∪ W) = f⁻¹(V) ∪ f⁻¹(W), f⁻¹(V ∩ W) = f⁻¹(V) ∩ f⁻¹(W), f⁻¹(Y ∖ V) = X ∖ f⁻¹(V); and along g after f it is pulling back along g, then along f.

x \in f^{-1}(V \cup W) \iff f(x) \in V \cup W @@ the pull-back, read off
\phantom{x \in f^{-1}(V \cup W)} \iff f(x) \in V \text{ or } f(x) \in W @@ merge-membership (2f5)
\phantom{x \in f^{-1}(V \cup W)} \iff x \in f^{-1}(V) \cup f^{-1}(W) @@ the pull-back twice, then 2f5 again
x \in (g \circ f)^{-1}(U) \iff g(f(x)) \in U \iff x \in f^{-1}(g^{-1}(U)) @@ g after f (9b), read through twice
\htmlClass{new}{(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∩ and the left-over part go as ∪ did

Membership is checked through f, so whatever is said of f(x) is said of x. The order of running is turned round, as 27h1’s turn-round technique turned round the readings of a space: pulling back is a map from the parts of Y to the parts of X, and it runs the other way from f.

inf2.3Some Is Left of Pulling Back

9d3 named the image: for a part P of X, f[P] is the set of f(x) for x in P. So y is in f[P] exactly when some x with f(x) = y is in P.

Image-left technique: for every part P of X and every part V of Y, f[P] ⊆ V exactly when P ⊆ f⁻¹(V). So taking images is left of pulling back.

f[P] \subseteq V \iff \forall y\,(y \in f[P] \Rightarrow y \in V) @@ part (2d)
\phantom{f[P] \subseteq V} \iff \forall y\,(\exists x\,(x \in P \wedge f(x) = y) \Rightarrow y \in V) @@ the image (9d3)
\phantom{f[P] \subseteq V} \iff \forall x\,(x \in P \Rightarrow f(x) \in V) @@ down: show an example, y = f(x) (2b7); up: use a some (2b8)
\htmlClass{new}{f[P] \subseteq V \iff P \subseteq f^{-1}(V)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pull-back (34a4)

Check it on the example. P = {1, 3} has f[P] = {a, b}. With V = {a, b}, f⁻¹(V) = X holds P; with V = {a}, f[P] is not a part of {a}, and f⁻¹({a}) = {1, 2} does not hold 3. The image is the least V whose pull-back holds P: the best guess at undoing f⁻¹ from below.

Being a left side, the image keeps sums by 47f4, and in an order of parts a sum is a union: f[P ∪ Q] = f[P] ∪ f[Q], with no work.

inf2.4Every Is Right of Pulling Back

Define the every-image of a part P of X: f∀[P] is the set of y in Y such that every x with f(x) = y is in P. Its accepted name is in the cellar.

Every-image technique: for every part P of X and every part V of Y, f⁻¹(V) ⊆ P exactly when V ⊆ f∀[P]. So the every-image is right of pulling back, and by the one-right-side technique it is the only right side.

V \subseteq f_\forall[P] \iff \forall y\,(y \in V \Rightarrow \forall x\,(f(x) = y \Rightarrow x \in P)) @@ part, then the every-image
\phantom{V \subseteq f_\forall[P]} \iff \forall x\,\forall y\,((y \in V \wedge f(x) = y) \Rightarrow x \in P) @@ x is fresh on the left; an if inside an if (3a)
\phantom{V \subseteq f_\forall[P]} \iff \forall x\,(f(x) \in V \Rightarrow x \in P) @@ down: use the every with y = f(x) (2b6); up: y = f(x) is the only y
\htmlClass{new}{V \subseteq f_\forall[P] \iff f^{-1}(V) \subseteq P} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pull-back (34a4)

On the example, P = {1, 2, 3}. Over a sit 1 and 2, both in P, so a is in. Over b sit 3 and 4, and 4 is not in P, so b is out. Over c sits nothing, and every member of nothing is in P, an empty truth, so c is in. So f∀[P] = {a, c}, while f[P] = {a, b}. Neither guess undoes f⁻¹: f⁻¹({a, c}) = {1, 2} sits inside P, and f⁻¹({a, b}) = X holds it.

Being a right side, the every-image keeps products, the right-hand twin of 47f4: f∀[P ∩ Q] = f∀[P] ∩ f∀[Q]. And the two images are mirror images through the left-over part: y is in f∀[P] exactly when no x over y is outside P, so f∀[P] = Y ∖ f[X ∖ P], which is 2b15’s “not some not” said of parts.

inf2.5Rung 2’s Four Moves, Read Off

Take X × Y and the reading π : X × Y → Y, π(x, y) = y. A condition φ(x, y) on pairs is a part P of X × Y. A condition ψ(y) that does not mention x is a part V of Y, and its pull-back π⁻¹(V) is the same condition with x added and not used. Then π[P] is {y : ∃x φ(x, y)} and π∀[P] is {y : ∀x φ(x, y)}. The quantifiers are the two sides of pulling back along a reading.

Each side gives two directions, and the four directions are rung 2’s four moves:

  • Use a some (2b8): π[P] ⊆ V from P ⊆ π⁻¹(V). From φ(b, y) reach ψ(y), which does not mention b; then ∃x φ gives ψ. The fresh-name rule is the pull-back: ψ lives on Y.
  • Show an example (2b7): P ⊆ π⁻¹(π[P]), the image-left technique with V = π[P]. φ(t, y) gives ∃x φ(x, y).
  • Make an every (2b5): V ⊆ π∀[P] from π⁻¹(V) ⊆ P. From ψ(y) and a fresh n reach φ(n, y); nothing given mentions n, because ψ lives on Y.
  • Use an every (2b6): π⁻¹(π∀[P]) ⊆ P, the every-image technique with V = π∀[P]. ∀x φ(x, y) gives φ(t, y).
\varphi(b, y) \vdash \psi(y),\ \ b \text{ not in } \psi @@ the case of a fresh name, worked
P \subseteq \pi^{-1}(V) @@ the same fact, said of parts of X × Y
\pi[P] \subseteq V @@ the image-left technique
\htmlClass{new}{\exists x\,\varphi(x, y) \vdash \psi(y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the some comes back to the margin

Rung 2 noticed a symmetry: making an every, the thing is out of your hands; using one, you choose; making a some, you choose; using one, it is out of your hands. Here the symmetry has a cause. The two choosing moves are the two ways of crossing pulling back with nothing to prove, and the two hands-off moves are the two adjunctions.

The proof checkers of 38·now keep exactly these rules, said of types in place of parts. A type may depend on a placeholder, the lists of length n for each n, and the checker’s two ways of quantifying over n, a function type and a pair type over a varying type, are right and left of the same pulling back. That is told here, not proved: it needs 47j’s types with the types allowed to vary, and is held in trust.

inf2.6The Colon Is the Pulling

2e19 reads the colon of a sift as “such that”: on its left a name and what is in hand, on its right a claim to be run down the list. When the claim is about outputs, the sift is a pull-back. For a condition ψ on Y, {x ∈ X : ψ(f(x))} = f⁻¹({y ∈ Y : ψ(y)}). Putting f(x) in for y is pulling back along f.

Take X = Y = the whole numbers, f(n) = n² and ψ(y) “y is even”. Then {n : n² is even} = f⁻¹(the evens), and that is the evens again: 3² = 9 is odd, 4² = 16 is even.

Sift-pulls-back technique: a sift by a condition read through f is the pull-back along f of the sift by the condition: {x ∈ X : ψ(f(x))} = f⁻¹({y : ψ(y)}).

x \in \{x \in X : \psi(f(x))\} \iff \psi(f(x)) @@ the sift (2e5)
\phantom{x \in \{\}} \iff f(x) \in \{y \in Y : \psi(y)\} @@ the sift again, filled with f(x)
\htmlClass{new}{\{x \in X : \psi(f(x))\} = f^{-1}(\{y : \psi(y)\})} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pull-back (34a4)

So the colon does the pulling, and the two quantifiers are its two best guesses run the other way. Each guess keeps only which members sit over which point. How the members are arranged inside X does not enter: f is the plan, and every and some are what the plan carries back.

inf2.7Necessary and Possible, the Same Pair

3b2 called □ 2b’s every said over the ways things could go, and 3b3 called ◇ 2b’s some said over the ways. Let W be the set of ways and P the part of W where a claim holds. Map W to a set with one member, {∗}. The image of P is {∗} exactly when P has a member: possible. The every-image of P is {∗} exactly when P is all of W: necessary.

Give the ways a relation, “from u, the way v can be reached”, a part R of W × W with readings s(u, v) = u and t(u, v) = v. Then ◇P = s[t⁻¹(P)] and □P = s∀[t⁻¹(P)]: pull back along where you arrive, then take some or every along where you start. Take W = {u, v, w} and R = {(u, v), (u, w), (v, v)}, P = {v}. Then t⁻¹(P) = {(u, v), (v, v)}, so ◇P holds at u and v. From u the pair (u, w) arrives outside P, so □P fails at u; it holds at v; and at w nothing can be reached, so □P holds there by an empty truth. □P = {v, w}, ◇P = {u, v}.

Reach-modal technique: with ◇P = s[t⁻¹(P)] and □P = s∀[t⁻¹(P)], □P = W ∖ ◇(W ∖ P).

s_\forall[Q] = W \setminus s[R \setminus Q] @@ the mirror line of the every-image, along s : R → W
t^{-1}(W \setminus P) = R \setminus t^{-1}(P) @@ the pull-back-keeps technique
\Box P = W \setminus s[t^{-1}(W \setminus P)] @@ put Q = t⁻¹(P)
\htmlClass{new}{\Box P = W \setminus \Diamond(W \setminus P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the definition of ◇

That is 3b4’s pair, □P and ¬◇¬P, in both directions at once. 3b4 could take only one direction, because its ways were boxes of an argument and the step from ¬¬P to P was not in hand. Here the ways are members of a set and the left-over part of a left-over part is the part again: the classical world, in which both directions hold.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The words every and some are as old as argument, but marks that bind a letter, so that “every x” could sit inside a longer statement, came late. A book of 1879 introduced them together with relations, and an American logician introduced his own independently in 1883 and named them quantifiers in 1885. For most of the next century they were treated as the two bottom rules of logic, explained by their tables and their proof rules, not by anything beneath them.

In 1969 a paper in a philosophy journal argued that the operations of logic are adjunctions, and that the quantifiers in particular are the two sides of substituting into a condition. A 1970 paper on equality built the setting in which the claim is exact. Whether such a reading explains logic or only relabels it is a fair question. The page above proves nothing new about logic. What it adds is a cause for the symmetry that rung 2 could only notice.

The middle map was later corrected in textbooks. Strictly, the quantifiers are the two sides of adding a letter that is not used, which is pulling back along a reading; substitution in general is a pulling back along any map, and keeps every connective. The correction is what this page’s footing on the four moves uses.

Type theory with types that depend on a placeholder was presented in 1971 and 1972, in a version whose first draft was found inconsistent and was revised. Its two binders over a varying type became the function and pair types of later proof checkers. A 1984 paper matched such type theories with the categories in which every pulling back has both sides. That proof had a flaw: substituting by hand and pulling back in the category agree only up to matching, and the matchings must fit together. Fixes came in 1993 and 1994, and a 2014 paper stated the match exactly, between two kinds of categories with maps between maps.

Today the checkers of 38·now are built on such type theories, some on the predicative version of 1972, others on a stronger calculus. The checkers run on rules, and a learner can use them without the arrow picture. The picture is what runs underneath, and the page above is the picture.

The accepted names

The image f[P] is the direct image, written ∃f(P) in category theory; the pull-back f⁻¹(V) is the preimage or inverse image, written f*(V); the every-image f∀[P] is the dual image or universal image, written ∀f(P). The three sit in an adjoint triple ∃f ⊣ f* ⊣ ∀f; an adjunction between two orders is a Galois connection. The pulling back along a reading is weakening. The setting of the 1970 paper is a hyperdoctrine, and the rule that pulling back and the quantifiers commute in a square is the Beck–Chevalley condition. The ways with a reach relation are a Kripke frame. The dependent function and pair types are the Π-type and the Σ-type; the categories in which every pullback functor has both adjoints are the locally cartesian closed categories.

Who said it first

Gottlob Frege introduced quantifiers in the Begriffsschrift (1879); Charles Sanders Peirce introduced them independently in 1883, with a debt to his student O. H. Mitchell, and named them quantifiers in 1885. F. William Lawvere, “Adjointness in Foundations”, Dialectica 23 (1969), and “Equality in hyperdoctrines and comprehension schema as an adjoint functor” (1970). Per Martin-Löf, an intuitionistic theory of types (preprint 1971, inconsistent; revised 1972). R. A. G. Seely, “Locally cartesian closed categories and type theory” (1984); its coherence flaw was repaired by Pierre-Louis Curien (1993) and Martin Hofmann (1994), and Pierre Clairambault and Peter Dybjer proved the biequivalence (2014).
