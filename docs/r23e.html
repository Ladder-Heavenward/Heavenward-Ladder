23e · proof

Term by term

A polynomial's slope is found one term at a time, and so is its area. An endless power sum looks like a polynomial that never stops, so it is tempting to do the same. For an endless sum that is a real question, because the slope of a total need not be the total of the slopes. This step proves that inside the reach it works. A power sum may be taken apart term by term, for slopes and for areas, and the answer has the same reach. So a power sum is smooth inside its reach, and its coefficients are its own matching polynomial. Running this backwards turns the plain geometric sum into sums for the logarithm and for the angle whose tangent is x, and gives a famous slow sum for π.

Inside its reach, a power sum can be differentiated and integrated one term at a time, and the reach does not change. So it is smooth there, and it is the matching sum of its own total. Integrating the geometric sum gives the sums for the logarithm and the arctangent.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 23d (settling everywhere at once), 23c (the reach), 16a (the area function), 16b (its slope is the height), 21m (arctan). 23f finds a smooth function its sum does not reach.

23e1Areas, term by term

Stands on 23d2 (settling everywhere at once), 16a (the area function) and 15e (areas add, scale, split).

Area-at-once technique: if continuous fₙ settle everywhere at once to f on [p, q], then ∫ₚ^q fₙ heads to ∫ₚ^q f. One N bounds the gap at every input, so it bounds the gap in areas.

f \text{ is continuous, so integrable} @@ kept continuity (23d3); 15f
n \ge N \Rightarrow |f_n(t) - f(t)| < \varepsilon \text{ for every } t \in [p, q] @@ settling everywhere at once
\Big|\int_p^q f_n - \int_p^q f\Big| \le \int_p^q |f_n - f| \le \varepsilon(q - p) @@ areas add and keep order (15e); the bound on an area (16a)
\htmlClass{new}{\int_p^q f_n \to \int_p^q f} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε(q − p) can be made as small as you like

Back to xⁿ on [0, 1]: ∫₀¹ xⁿ = 1/(n + 1) heads to 0, which is the area of the limit. Here it came out right without settling at once. But fₙ(x) = n²x(1 − x)ⁿ settles at each point to 0 on [0, 1], while its areas n²/((n + 1)(n + 2)) head to 1. At each point is not enough.

23e2The slope sum has the same reach

Stands on 23c (the reach) and 22h2 (nᵏrⁿ heads to 0).

Same-reach technique: Σ uₖ(x − w)ᵏ and its slope sum Σ k uₖ(x − w)ᵏ⁻¹ have the same reach.

|x - w| < s < r < R @@ choose s and r between |x − w| and the reach
|u_k| r^k \le B \text{ for every } k @@ Σuₖ(x − w)ᵏ settles at distance r (23c), so its pieces have a ceiling (22b)
|k u_k s^{k-1}| \le \tfrac{B}{s}\, k \big(\tfrac{s}{r}\big)^k @@ the slope sum at distance s; |uₖ| ≤ B/rᵏ
\textstyle\sum k \big(\tfrac{s}{r}\big)^k \text{ settles} @@ ratio of neighbours heads to s/r < 1 (22e2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the slope sum settles at distance } s, \text{ so its reach is at least } R} \quad \blacksquare @@ s was any distance under R; 23c1

The other direction is easier: |uₖ(x − w)ᵏ| ≤ |x − w| · |k uₖ(x − w)ᵏ⁻¹| once k ≥ 1, so where the slope sum settles in size, so does the sum.

23e3Slopes, term by term

Stands on 23e1, 23e2, 23d4 (power sums settle at once inside the reach) and 16b (the slope of an area function is the height).

Term-by-term technique: inside its reach, f(x) = Σ uₖ(x − w)ᵏ has slope f′(x) = Σ k uₖ(x − w)ᵏ⁻¹ and area ∫_wˣ f = Σ uₖ(x − w)ᵏ⁺¹/(k + 1).

g(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 1} k u_k (x-w)^{k-1} @@ the slope sum: it settles inside R (23e2)
g \text{ is continuous inside } R @@ 23d4, for the slope sum
\int_w^x g = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 1} k u_k \int_w^x (t-w)^{k-1}\,dt = \sum_{k \ge 1} u_k (x-w)^k = f(x) - u_0 @@ area at once (23e1), on [w, x], where the sum settles at once
f(x) = u_0 + \int_w^x g @@ the line above
\htmlClass{new}{f'(x) = g(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope of an area function is the height (16b)

Apply it again, and again. Smooth-sum technique: a power sum is smooth inside its reach, and uₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(w)/k!. The kth slope sum, at x = w, keeps only its first piece, k! uₖ. So a power sum is the endless matching polynomial of its own total. Two power sums about w with the same total near w have the same coefficients.

23e4Sums from the geometric sum

Stands on 23e3, 15i (the geometric sum, with its last term), 17a (ln ws an area) and 21m (arctan).

\tfrac{1}{1+t} = 1 - t + t^2 - \cdots + (-t)^{n-1} + \tfrac{(-t)^n}{1+t} @@ the finite geometric sum (15i), exact
\ln(1+x) = \int_0^x \tfrac{dt}{1+t} = x - \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \cdots \pm \tfrac{x^n}{n} + \int_0^x \tfrac{(-t)^n}{1+t}\,dt @@ 17a, term by term on a finite sum
\Big|\int_0^x \tfrac{(-t)^n}{1+t}\,dt\Big| \le \tfrac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)(1 - |x|)} \text{ for } -1 < x < 0;\ \le \tfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} \text{ for } 0 \le x \le 1 @@ 1 + t ≥ 1 − |x| or ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{\ln(1+x) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 1} \tfrac{(-1)^{k+1}x^k}{k} \text{ for } -1 < x \le 1} @@ the leftover heads to 0: the gap 23b left is closed
\tfrac{1}{1+t^2} = 1 - t^2 + t^4 - \cdots + (-t^2)^{n-1} + \tfrac{(-t^2)^n}{1+t^2} @@ the same, with −t² for t
\Big|\arctan x - \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \tfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\Big| \le \int_0^{|x|} t^{2n}\,dt = \tfrac{|x|^{2n+1}}{2n+1} @@ arctan x = ∫₀ˣ dt/(1 + t²) (21m); 1 + t² ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{\arctan x = x - \tfrac{x^3}{3} + \tfrac{x^5}{5} - \cdots,\ \ \tfrac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots} \quad \blacksquare @@ for |x| ≤ 1; at x = 1, arctan 1 = π/4 (21m)

The last sum is beautiful and nearly useless for computing: a thousand pieces give π/4 to about three places, since the leftover is about 1/(2n + 1). Put x = 1/√3 instead, where arctan is π/6, and each piece shrinks by a third. That is how π was first pushed past a hundred places.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The term-by-term technique is term-by-term differentiation and integration of power series; the smooth-sum technique says a power series is analytic and equals its Taylor series. The sum for π/4 is the Leibniz series (Madhava–Leibniz series); the sum for arctan is Gregory's series.

Who said it first

Madhava of Sangamagrama found the arctangent sum and the sum for π/4 around 1400, in Kerala. James Gregory found the arctangent sum in 1671 and Gottfried Leibniz the π/4 sum in 1673. Abraham Sharp used x = 1/√3 to reach 71 decimal places of π in 1699, and John Machin reached 100 in 1706 with a faster blend of arctangents. In 23e.4.5, the verse is Madhava's, passed down in later Kerala texts such as the Yuktibhāṣā (about 1530), which also proves it; the Scot is Gregory and the German Leibniz. George Gheverghese Joseph and Dennis Almeida (2007) argued the case for travel to Europe.
