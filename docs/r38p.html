38p · technique

Wiring a table by hand

Someone hands over a job in words: water the beds when the soil is dry and it is not raining, or whenever the gardener presses the button. The job is a table of three inputs, and 38f proved that some circuit of not-and gates does it. This step finds a small one by hand, and goes the other way too. First write the table, row by row. Then write one both for each row that gives a one, and join them with either: that always works and is always too big. Then merge rows that differ in a single input, because the input that changes between them does not matter there, and a grid laid out so that neighbours differ by one input makes the merges visible at a glance. Then turn either-of-boths into not-ands of not-ands, which costs nothing but a few nots. And finally run a finished circuit back to its table, row by row, which is how any circuit is checked, and how two circuits are shown to do the same job.

Write the table. One both per row with a one, joined by either, always works. Merge rows that differ in one input; a grid with neighbours one input apart shows the merges. Either of boths is a not-and of not-ands. Run a circuit back to its table, row by row, to check it.

38p1One both for each row

The job: d is 1 when the soil is dry, r when it rains, m when the button is pressed; water, f = 1, when m, or when d and not r. The table has 2³ = 8 rows (1e6), and f is 1 on the rows d r m = 001, 011, 100, 101, 111.

One-row-each technique: a table is the either of one both for each row where it gives 1, each both taking each input as it is when that row has a 1 there and its not when the row has a 0. Each both is 1 on its own row and 0 on every other; the either is 1 exactly on the rows listed.

001:\ \neg d\,\neg r\,m;\ \ 011:\ \neg d\,r\,m;\ \ 100:\ d\,\neg r\,\neg m @@ a both per row, product marks (38a1)
101:\ d\,\neg r\,m;\ \ 111:\ d\,r\,m @@ the rest of the rows with a 1
\htmlClass{new}{f = \neg d\neg r m \lor \neg d r m \lor d\neg r\neg m \lor d\neg r m \lor d r m} \quad \blacksquare @@ five boths of three, joined by either

It is right and it is large: five boths of three inputs each and an either of five. The next footing shrinks it.

38p2Neighbours merge

Two boths that agree in every input but one merge: x ∧ Y ∨ ¬x ∧ Y = Y, since (x ∨ ¬x) ∧ Y = 1 ∧ Y by spreading (1f). Lay the rows in a grid with d and r across, in the order 00, 01, 11, 10, so that each step across changes one input, and m down. Then rows that merge sit side by side, the two ends of a row counting as side by side too.

Neighbour-merge technique: two rows with a 1 that differ in exactly one input merge into one both without that input; a block of 2ᵏ rows that agree in all but k inputs merges into one both of the rest.

\text{grid}: \ m = 0:\ 0\ 0\ 0\ 1;\ \ m = 1:\ 1\ 1\ 1\ 1 @@ columns dr = 00, 01, 11, 10
\text{the whole row } m = 1:\ 4 \text{ rows, agreeing only in } m @@ a block of 2² rows
\to m @@ d and r drop out
\text{column } dr = 10:\ \text{rows } 100, 101 \to d\,\neg r @@ a block of 2, differing only in m
\htmlClass{new}{f = m \lor d\,\neg r} \quad \blacksquare @@ every 1 is in a block; blocks may overlap at 101

Two small boths in place of five large ones. Blocks may share a row, since a 1 joined to a 1 by either is still 1 (38a2). Every block must cover only 1s, and the biggest blocks give the smallest result.

38p3Into not-ands

38f2 found that the either of two boths is the not-and of their not-ands. A lone input in the either counts as a both of one, and the not-and of one input is its not.

Two-level-nand technique: an either of boths is the not-and of the not-ands of the boths, with a lone input entering the last gate as its not.

f = m \lor d\,\neg r @@ the merged table
= \mathrm{nand}\big(\neg m,\ \mathrm{nand}(d, \neg r)\big) @@ either of two is the not-and of their nots (38f1); not of a both is its not-and
\neg m = \mathrm{nand}(m, m),\ \ \neg r = \mathrm{nand}(r, r) @@ 38f1
\htmlClass{new}{4 \text{ not-ands in all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two nots, the inner gate, the outer gate

Four gates, sixteen switches. The split-on-one recipe of 38f2 would have spent up to sixteen gates. By hand, merging first and converting last, small tables come out small.

38p4Back to the table

A circuit with no loop gives each output as a table of its inputs. To read it, run each row through the gates from inputs to output, as 1e7 worked one row by hand.

Circuit-to-table technique: a circuit with no loop is checked by running every row; two such circuits do the same job exactly when they give the same column.

d r m = 100:\ \ \neg m = 1,\ \neg r = 1,\ \mathrm{nand}(1, 1) = 0,\ \mathrm{nand}(1, 0) = 1 @@ one row, gate by gate
d r m = 110:\ \ \neg m = 1,\ \neg r = 0,\ \mathrm{nand}(1, 0) = 1,\ \mathrm{nand}(1, 1) = 0 @@ dry, but raining: no water
\text{all } 8 \text{ rows}: 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1 @@ run by machine, rows 000 to 111
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the column of the circuit is the column of } f} \quad \blacksquare @@ same column, same statement (1e14)

A row check costs 2ⁿ runs, cheap for three inputs and impossible for sixty-four. For wide circuits, the rewrites of 1f prove sameness by lines instead of rows, and machines that search for a row where two circuits differ are how chips are checked before they are made.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

An either of one both per 1-row is the canonical sum of products (disjunctive normal form, a sum of minterms). The grid is a Karnaugh map, its blocks are implicants, and the largest blocks are prime implicants; the method that finds them in a table rather than a picture is Quine–McCluskey. The two-level not-and form is NAND–NAND logic. Checking that two circuits agree is equivalence checking, done on chips by satisfiability solvers.

Who said it first

Edward Veitch drew the grid in 1952 and Maurice Karnaugh gave it its order of neighbours in 1953. Willard Van Orman Quine (1952) and Edward McCluskey (1956) gave the method by tables. Finding the smallest either of boths for a table is, in general, as hard as the problems 39 asks about.

A proof that is not allowed

“m ∨ d¬r gives 1 on the row 100 and on the row 111, which are the rows I checked, so it is the table.” Not allowed: two rows are two rows. The table has eight, and the sameness of two columns (1e14) needs all eight, or lines of 1f’s rewrites that cover them all.
