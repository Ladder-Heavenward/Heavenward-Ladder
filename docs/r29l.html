29l · technique

The Best Path, by Hand

In the summer of sixteen ninety six a challenge went out to the mathematicians of Europe. A bead slides without friction along a wire from one point down to another, lower and off to the side. Which shape of wire brings it down fastest? Not the straight one. This step answers that question with the best-path equation of rung twenty nine, and then two more that look nothing alike: the shape of a soap film stretched between two rings, and the shape of a chain hanging between two hooks. All three have a rule that does not care where along the floor you stand, and for such rules the equation gives up a quantity that never changes along the best path. With it, each problem comes down to one step of separating the letters. The fastest slide is the curve traced by a point on a rolling wheel. The soap film and the chain share one curve, built from e.

When the rule does not mention where along the floor you stand, the best-path equation keeps one quantity fixed all along the best path. The fastest slide is traced by a point on a rolling wheel. The soap film between two rings and the hanging chain take one and the same curve, built from e.

29l1A Rule That Does Not Mention x

29g1 proved that a curve y(x) which makes ∫ L(x, y, y′) dx least, among curves with the same ends, obeys d/dx Lp = Ly, where p stands for the third input and Lp is taken at p = y′. That is an equation with second slopes in it. When x does not appear in L, one slope can be taken off at once.

No-x technique: if L depends on y and p only, then along every curve that obeys the best-path equation, L − y′Lp keeps one value.

\tfrac{d}{dx}\,L(y, y^{\prime}) = L_y\,y^{\prime} + L_p\,y^{\prime\prime} @@ the roads-add technique (28c3): two roads, through y and through p = y′
\tfrac{d}{dx}\,(y^{\prime}L_p) = y^{\prime\prime}L_p + y^{\prime}\,\tfrac{d}{dx}L_p @@ the slope of a product (13)
\tfrac{d}{dx}\,(L - y^{\prime}L_p) = y^{\prime}\big(L_y - \tfrac{d}{dx}L_p\big) @@ subtract: the terms in y″ cancel
L_y - \tfrac{d}{dx}L_p = 0 @@ the best-path technique (29g1)
\htmlClass{new}{L - y^{\prime}L_p \text{ keeps one value}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its slope is 0 on an interval (14d)
  
L = \sqrt{1 + p^2},\ \ L_p = \tfrac{p}{\sqrt{1 + p^2}} @@ length in the flat plane (29g1), no x and no y
L - pL_p = \tfrac{1 + p^2 - p^2}{\sqrt{1 + p^2}} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + p^2}} @@ the no-x technique
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + y^{\prime 2}}} = k \Rightarrow y^{\prime 2} = \tfrac{1}{k^2} - 1 @@ one value all along
\htmlClass{new}{y^{\prime} \text{ keeps one value: a straight line}} \quad \blacksquare @@ y′ is continuous, so it cannot jump between the two roots (12)

The kept quantity has a meaning in mechanics, with time for x: when the rule of motion does not mention the time, something is kept, and 29·then names it. Here it is a tool. It turns an equation in y″ into an equation in y′, and that can often be solved by separating the letters (24f).

29l2The Fastest Slide

Measure y downward from the start (0, 0) to an end (X, Y) with Y > 0. A bead released from rest on a wire without friction has dropped by y when its speed is v = √(2gy), since the work its weight does, mgy, becomes ½mv² (16·phys), and the wire pushes at right angles to the motion and does no work. The time along the wire is the total of distance over speed: T = ∫ √(1 + y′²) / √(2gy) dx.

Fastest-slide technique: a curve that makes the time of the slide least obeys y(1 + y′²) = 2r for a number r > 0. The curve x = r(θ − sin θ), y = r(1 − cos θ), traced by a point on the rim of a wheel of radius r rolling along the underside of the floor, obeys it, and the bead takes θ√(r/g) to reach the point at θ.

L = \tfrac{\sqrt{1 + p^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}},\ \ L - pL_p = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1 + p^2}} @@ no x in L; the same work as 29l1, divided by √(2gy)
y(1 + y^{\prime 2}) = 2r @@ the no-x technique: the kept value is 1/√(4gr), naming r
\tfrac{dy}{dx} = \tfrac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta},\ \ 1 + y^{\prime 2} = \tfrac{2}{1 - \cos\theta} @@ dy/dθ over dx/dθ (13), then sin² θ = 1 − cos² θ (21c)
y(1 + y^{\prime 2}) = r(1 - \cos\theta)\cdot\tfrac{2}{1 - \cos\theta} = 2r @@ the wheel’s curve obeys it
ds = r\sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta}\,d\theta,\ \ v = \sqrt{2gr(1 - \cos\theta)} @@ speed along the curve (18i), and v = √(2gy)
\htmlClass{new}{dt = \tfrac{ds}{v} = \sqrt{\tfrac{r}{g}}\,d\theta,\ \ T = \theta\sqrt{\tfrac{r}{g}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the time grows evenly with the wheel’s turn
  
r = 10\ \text{m},\ \ \theta = \pi:\ \ (X, Y) = (10\pi,\ 20) \approx (31.4,\ 20)\ \text{m} @@ the bottom of the wheel’s curve
T = \pi\sqrt{10/9.81} \approx 3.17\ \text{s} @@ the fastest-slide technique
\text{straight chute: length } D = 10\sqrt{\pi^2 + 4} \approx 37.2\ \text{m},\ \ \text{pull along it } g\cdot\tfrac{20}{D} @@ the weight’s share along a straight slope
D = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{20g}{D}\big)T^2 \Rightarrow T = D\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{20g}} \approx 3.76\ \text{s} @@ from rest under a steady pull, distance is ½ × pull × time² (9·phys)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the wheel’s curve wins by nearly 0.6 s}} \quad \blacksquare @@ it drops steeply first, gains speed early, and spends it on the long way

The fastest curve is longer than the straight chute. It trades distance for speed. And on it the time to the bottom is π√(r/g) from wherever on the curve the bead is let go, which 29l.4.1 asks you to prove.

29l3The Soap Film

Dip two wire rings of radius R, held square to one axis, into soapy water and draw them apart. The film between them pulls itself as small as it can. Spin the curve y(x) about the x-axis: by 29f2 and 29d2, the surface has area 2π ∫ y √(1 + y′²) dx, the total of the circle’s length 2πy over the distance along the curve.

Write C(w) = (e^w + e^(−w))/2 and S(w) = (e^w − e^(−w))/2 for short. Then C′ = S, S′ = C and C² − S² = 1, by slopes of exp (17) and multiplying out.

Soap-film technique: if a film spun about the x-axis has least area among those with the same rings, and y′ is not 0 all along, its curve is y = c C((x − b)/c) for numbers c > 0 and b.

L = y\sqrt{1 + p^2},\ \ L - pL_p = \tfrac{y}{\sqrt{1 + p^2}} @@ no x in L (29l1)
\tfrac{y}{\sqrt{1 + y^{\prime 2}}} = c,\ \ y^{\prime 2} = \tfrac{y^2}{c^2} - 1 @@ the no-x technique, naming the kept value c
\tfrac{dy}{\sqrt{y^2 - c^2}} = \tfrac{dx}{c} @@ separating the letters (24f), on a piece where y′ > 0
\ln\big(y + \sqrt{y^2 - c^2}\big) = \tfrac{x - b}{c} + \ln c @@ the left side has slope 1/√(y² − c²) (17, 13); b is the constant
y + \sqrt{y^2 - c^2} = c\,e^{(x - b)/c} @@ exp undoes ln (17)
y - \sqrt{y^2 - c^2} = \tfrac{c^2}{y + \sqrt{y^2 - c^2}} = c\,e^{-(x - b)/c} @@ multiply out (y + √)(y − √) = c²
\htmlClass{new}{y = c\,C\big(\tfrac{x - b}{c}\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the last two lines and halve; put-back (24f2): y′ = S, 1 + S² = C², so y/√(1 + y′²) = c
  
R = 10\ \text{cm},\ \ \text{rings at } x = \pm 5 @@ the film is the same on both sides, so b = 0
c\,C\big(\tfrac{5}{c}\big) = 10 @@ the curve passes through each ring
c \approx 8.483 \ \text{ or } \ c \approx 2.351 @@ two answers, found by halving (12)
\text{area} = \pi c\big(10 + c\,S(\tfrac{10}{c})\big) \approx 599.2 \ \text{ or } \ 684.6\ \text{cm}^2 @@ the total of 2π c C² over x, with C² = (1 + C(2w))/2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the deep curve, } c \approx 8.483,\ \text{beats two flat discs, } 2\pi R^2 \approx 628.3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shallow one obeys the equation and is not least

Pull the rings further apart and the two answers draw together. When the distance passes about 1.33 times the radius there is no answer at all, and the film snaps into two flat discs, one on each ring.

29l4The Hanging Chain

A chain of length ℓ, the same weight w per metre all along, hangs from two hooks. It settles where its weight sits lowest, that is, where the height total ∫ y ds = ∫ y √(1 + y′²) dx is least, with y measured up. But not among all curves: only among those of length ∫ √(1 + y′²) dx = ℓ.

For a best point of f on a level set of g, 28g2’s lines-up technique gives a number λ with ∇f = λ∇g. Its carry-over to paths says: a curve that makes ∫ L dx least, among curves with the same ends and the same value of ∫ M dx, obeys the best-path equation for L − λM, for some number λ. Its proof waits much higher up the ladder. Here it is held in trust.

Lowest-chain technique: a hanging chain whose height total is least among curves of its length, with y′ not 0 all along, hangs as y = λ + c C((x − b)/c) for numbers λ, c > 0 and b.

L - \lambda M = y\sqrt{1 + p^2} - \lambda\sqrt{1 + p^2} = (y - \lambda)\sqrt{1 + p^2} @@ height total less λ times length: the lines-up carry-over, held in trust
Y = y - \lambda:\ \ L - \lambda M = Y\sqrt{1 + Y^{\prime 2}} @@ Y′ = y′: sliding the curve up or down changes nothing else
\htmlClass{new}{Y = c\,C\big(\tfrac{x - b}{c}\big),\ \ y = \lambda + c\,C\big(\tfrac{x - b}{c}\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the soap-film technique (29l3), word for word
  
\text{towers } 300\ \text{m apart, same height, sag } 10\ \text{m} @@ put the lowest point at x = 0, so b = 0
c\big(C(\tfrac{150}{c}) - 1\big) = 10 \Rightarrow c \approx 1126.7\ \text{m} @@ the lowest-chain technique, solved by halving (12)
\text{length} = \int_{-150}^{150} C\big(\tfrac{x}{c}\big)\,dx = 2c\,S\big(\tfrac{150}{c}\big) \approx 300.89\ \text{m} @@ √(1 + S²) = C, and S undoes C’s slope
\htmlClass{new}{\text{under 0.3\% more wire than the span gives a 10 m sag}} \quad \blacksquare @@ shallow chains are nearly straight

The same curve comes out of a balance of pulls, with no trust at all, by 25·eng’s hanging-chain technique: the pull along the chain has a level part that never changes, and its upright part carries the weight below. Two different arguments, one curve. The soap film and the chain are the same problem because each asks for the least total of a height times a length.

The accepted names

The fastest slide is the brachistochrone problem (shortest time), and its answer is a cycloid. That the time to the bottom of a cycloid does not depend on the start makes it the tautochrone (or isochrone) curve. The no-x technique is the Beltrami identity, and L − y′Lp is a first integral of the Euler–Lagrange equation. The chain curve is the catenary, y = a cosh((x − b)/a), with C = cosh and S = sinh, the hyperbolic cosine and sine. The soap film spun from a catenary is a catenoid, a minimal surface. The lines-up carry-over is the method of Lagrange multipliers for an isoperimetric constraint, and its proof is part of the calculus of variations.

Who said it first

Johann Bernoulli set the fastest-slide problem in the Acta Eruditorum in June 1696. Solutions came from Johann himself, his brother Jakob, Gottfried Leibniz, Guillaume de l’Hôpital and Isaac Newton, whose came unsigned; Johann is said to have known him “by his claw”. Christiaan Huygens had shown in 1673 that the cycloid is the same-time curve, and built pendulum clocks on it. Galileo Galilei thought a hanging chain made a parabola; Joachim Jungius showed in 1669 that it does not, and in 1691 Leibniz, Huygens and Johann Bernoulli found the catenary. Leonhard Euler showed in 1744 that the catenoid is the least surface between two rings. Joseph Plateau studied soap films by experiment in his book of 1873. Eugenio Beltrami wrote the identity in 1868.
