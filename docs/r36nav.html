The Sky Tells Where the Land Is

The sky tells where the land is: how can a colour in the sky find an island? A canoe out of sight of land in the open Pacific has no chart and no compass, and its navigator still finds an atoll whose highest point is a few metres of sand and palms. One of the signs is overhead: a cloud whose underside is faintly green. Far to the north, sailors among the ice read a white glare on the clouds as ice ahead and a dark patch as open water. And on a day of cloud, a bee finds the hidden sun from a patch of blue sky. What makes it hard is that every one of these signs is a colour or a shaking of light that the eye barely notices, and none of them is certain. This page answers with the rung’s mixing, 34·phys’s shaken charges, 23·nav’s horizon, and 30’s odds, and says how far each sign can be trusted.

Air scatters short waves far more than long ones, so the sky is blue overhead and the sun is red when its light crosses a long path of air. Light scattered at a right angle to the sun shakes along one line, and a filter turned to find it finds the hidden sun. A cloud’s underside is lit by what lies beneath it, so a lagoon tints it green and ice tints it white. A cloud stands high, so its tint shows from much farther than the island. And each sign multiplies the odds of land by its own ratio, never to certainty.

36nav1Blue Overhead, Red at Sunset

36·bio3 laid down, as measured, that small things scatter a share of light that goes as 1/λ⁴. The rung can now say why, for the molecules of the air. Light passing a molecule shakes its charges at the light’s own pace ω, by an amount that follows the light’s strength, and here that is laid down. 34·phys’s shaken-charge technique says a charge shaken at ω sends out power in proportion to ω⁴. And ω is 2π times the frequency c/λ (6·phys), so the power sent out sideways goes as 1/λ⁴. The measured law is the shaken charge, seen in the air.

What is scattered out of the sun’s beam is lost from it. A thin layer of air of thickness ℓ takes out a share βℓ, with β larger for short waves, so along its path the beam’s strength S falls at the rate βS: 17·chem’s first-order rule, now in metres, not seconds. Straight down through the whole air, the share kept is e^(−τ), and τ is laid down as measured: about 0.215 at 450 nm, and 0.215 × (450/700)⁴ ≈ 0.037 at 700 nm.

Low-sun technique: sunlight from a height H above the horizon crosses about 1/sin H times as much air as from overhead, so it keeps the share e^(−τ/sin H) at each wavelength, and the red it keeps over the blue it keeps grows as the sun goes down. Proof: for air in flat layers, a path at the angle H to them is 1/sin H times the straight-down path through each layer (21b); the first-order technique of 17·chem gives e^(−τ/sin H); and red over blue is e^((τ_blue − τ_red)/sin H), which grows as sin H falls, since τ_blue > τ_red.

H = 90^\circ:\ \ e^{-0.215} \approx 0.81 \text{ of the blue},\ \ e^{-0.037} \approx 0.96 \text{ of the red} @@ red over blue ≈ 1.19
H = 5^\circ:\ \ 1/\sin 5^\circ \approx 11.5 @@ eleven and a half airs
e^{-0.215 \times 11.5} \approx 0.085,\ \ e^{-0.037 \times 11.5} \approx 0.66 @@ the blue is nearly gone
\htmlClass{new}{\text{red over blue} \approx 7.7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the low-sun technique

So the sky overhead is the blue scattered out of every beam, and the low sun is what the beam has left. Flat layers are a guess that fails near the horizon, where 1/sin H runs off without bound; the earth’s curve of 23·nav keeps the real path finite, about 38 airs at the horizon itself.

36nav2Skylight Is Combed

A charge shaken along a line sends out light most strongly square to the line, and none along it; that is laid down with 34·phys’s shaken-charge technique. Sunlight shakes across its own path, every way across alike (31·phys’s mixed light). So a molecule in the sky shakes in the flat sheet square to the sun’s ray.

Combed technique: skylight from a patch of sky a right angle away from the sun shakes, at best, along one line: the line square to both the sun’s ray and the line of sight; through a filter turned θ from that line, it keeps cos²θ of its strength. Proof: the molecule’s shaking lies in the sheet square to the sun’s ray; at a right angle, the line of sight lies in that sheet, and a shaking along the line of sight sends nothing to the eye; what is left is the shaking along the one line in the sheet square to the line of sight; then the two-filter technique of 21·then.

In the real sky the comb is never full. Light scattered twice, light from the ground and from haze, shakes every way, and at its best a clear sky is combed about three quarters of the way. That is enough. Turn a filter and watch a patch of blue darken and brighten, and the line of best combing points along a circle a right angle from the sun. Two patches give two circles, and the sun is where they say, behind cloud or below the horizon. Bees do this with their eyes; navigators near the pole, where a compass wanders, did it with a filter until satellites took over.

36nav3A Cloud Is Lit From Below

The underside of a cloud is lit by what lies beneath it. Sunlight S reaches the surface, each patch sends back its own share r_i(λ) (36·phys5’s r), and the cloud’s base gathers what comes up. A patch that fills a share w_i of the base’s view below adds w_i·r_i·S, and the base’s light is the sum (36·phys1). Here w_i is a share of the view, not 36f’s amount w.

Cloud-base technique: the light on a cloud’s base from below is the mix, at the strengths w_i, of the light each patch beneath sends back; so with two kinds of surface, its colour lies on the segment between theirs, nearer the one that fills more of the view. Proof: the sum of w_i·r_i·S is a mix of the lights r_i·S at strengths w_i (36c2), and col of a mix is the mix of the cols by three-numbers; with two kinds, w and 1 − w, that is 18a5’s point a share w of the way.

Deep sea sends back a few hundredths of the light, a dark blue. A lagoon a few metres deep over white coral sand sends back far more, mostly in the greens and blues, since the water above the sand takes away the reds. So a cloud over an atoll’s lagoon has a base faintly green, and the faintness is the technique: a small lagoon fills a small share of the view, and its green is mixed with a great deal of dark sea. In the far north, snow and sea ice send back most of the window’s light, so the clouds over ice are bright underneath; open water among the ice sends back little, so the clouds over a lead of water are dark. Sailors among the ice steered by both.

36nav4A Sign Seen Past the Horizon

A cloud’s base stands hundreds of metres up, and the tops of an atoll’s palms stand a few tens. 23·nav’s lighthouse technique says how far each shows over the sea: a thing at height h₁ first shows to an eye at height h₂ at about √(2Rh₁) + √(2Rh₂), with R = 6371 km.

Higher-sign technique: a sign at height h₁ shows to an eye at height h₂ from about √(2Rh₁) + √(2Rh₂) away, so a sign a hundred times higher shows from nearly ten times as far, when h₂ is small. Proof: the lighthouse technique; and √(2R · 100h₁) = 10√(2Rh₁).

h_2 = 2\ \text{m}:\ \ \sqrt{2 \cdot 6\,371\,000 \cdot 2} \approx 5.0\ \text{km} @@ the navigator’s eye
\text{palm tops},\ h_1 = 25\ \text{m}:\ \ 17.8 + 5.0 \approx 22.9\ \text{km} @@ the island itself
\text{cloud base},\ h_1 = 600\ \text{m}:\ \ 87.4 + 5.0 \approx 92.4\ \text{km} @@ the green underside
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sign shows from about four times as far as the island}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the higher-sign technique

So the colour on a cloud widens the target. An atoll seen from 23 km is a dot that a canoe can miss by a little error in heading; an atoll whose cloud shows from 92 km is a circle sixteen times the area. The navigators of the Pacific read many such signs, the swells bent by an island, birds that fish by day and fly home at dusk, a cloud that stays still over land while others drift, and the green is one of them.

36nav5How Far to Trust a Green Cloud

A green underside can come from a reef with no land, a shoal, or the light of a low sun, and a lagoon can sit under a cloud too thin to show it. So the sign is a fact by cases with its rates, and 30l’s odds-update technique says what to do with it: the odds of land given the sign are the odds before, times the ratio P(sign | land) / P(sign | no land).

Two-sign technique: if two signs are independent given land and independent given no land, the odds of land given both are the odds before times both ratios. Proof: the odds-update technique for the first sign; then for the second, whose chances given land and given no land are unchanged by the first, by 30f2’s independence given.

P(\text{land}) = 0.1:\ \ \text{odds } 1/9 @@ before any sign, on this heading, within 90 km
\text{green: } 0.7/0.05 = 14:\ \ \text{odds } 14/9,\ \text{chance } 14/23 \approx 0.61 @@ the odds-update technique
\text{a cloud that stays still: ratio } 5:\ \ \text{odds } 70/9 @@ independent given land, and given none
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chance } 70/79 \approx 0.89} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two-sign technique
Anexact, yet rigorous · a sign with its rate

No sign proves an island. Each sign is a count of cases: how often it showed over land and how often over open sea. The navigator’s confidence is the odds before times one ratio for each sign, each ratio earned by counting, and it never reaches 1. The rung said which size a statement lives at; this one lives at the size of many voyages, and a navigator who reads it rightly will still sometimes be wrong.

The accepted names

The scattering of light by molecules is Rayleigh scattering, and τ is the optical depth; 1/sin H is the air mass, and the flat-layer guess is the plane-parallel atmosphere. The combing of skylight is its polarization, and the circle of best combing a right angle from the sun is the band of maximum polarization. The green on cloud bases over a lagoon is lagoon glare or lagoon reflection on clouds. The white glare over ice is ice blink, and the dark patch over open water is water sky. The art of the Pacific navigators is wayfinding.

Who said it first

John Tyndall showed in 1869 that fine particles scatter blue light and comb it, and Lord Rayleigh explained it for molecules in 1871 and 1899. Karl von Frisch showed in the 1940s that bees read the combing of the sky. William Scoresby described ice blink and water sky for Arctic sailors in 1820. David Lewis recorded the wayfinding of Pacific navigators, the green clouds among their signs, in We, the Navigators (1972). The sunstone appears in medieval Icelandic writings, and in 2011 Guy Ropars and colleagues showed that a crystal of Iceland spar can find a hidden sun to within a few degrees.
