How Close Is a Poll?

How close is a poll? A town asks whether its schools should start later in the morning. A pollster phones a thousand people and reports 54% in favour, “plus or minus three points”. What does the three mean, and why do two polls on the same day sometimes disagree by more? This page finds the margin of error from the sample size with 30’s spreads, finds why halving it takes four times the people, averages polls together, measures one pollster’s steady lean, and finds the errors that averaging cannot remove.

A share from n people randomly chosen spreads around the true share by about √(p(1 − p)/n); twice that is the margin of error. The spread falls as 1/√n, so halving it takes four times the people. Averaging independent polls shrinks the spread the same way, but errors that all polls share, from people they all miss, do not shrink at all.

34stat1The Margin of Error From the Sample Size

Evita runs a polling firm. She asks n people, chosen at random from the town, whether they favour a later school start. If the true share in the town is p, each answer is a yes with chance p, and her poll’s share is an average of n such answers.

\text{spread} = \sqrt{\dfrac{p(1 - p)}{n}} @@ the spread of an average (30h, 30j)
p \approx 0.5,\ n = 1\,000:\ \sqrt{0.25 / 1\,000} \approx 0.016 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{margin of error} \approx 1.96 \times 0.016 \approx 3 \text{ points}} @@ 95 polls in 100 land within it (30j)

From here on, on this page, the margin is 1.96 spreads: the distance within which 95 of 100 such polls fall of the true share.

34stat2Halving It Takes Four Times the People

\text{margin} \propto \dfrac{1}{\sqrt n} @@ 
n = 4\,000:\ 1.5 \text{ points};\ \ n = 16\,000:\ 0.8 \text{ points} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each halving of the margin costs four times the calls}} @@

Margin technique: a random sample of n gives a share within about 1/√n of the truth, 95 times in 100; to halve the margin, ask four times as many. The size of the town hardly matters: a thousand people pin down a town of fifty thousand as well as a nation of millions.

34stat3Averaging Polls Together

Five firms each poll a thousand people in the same week, independently. Their average is like one poll of five thousand.

firmin favour
A54%
B51%
C55%
D52%
E53%
average53%, margin about 1.4 points

The firms differ by up to four points, which looks alarming, but is what five polls of a thousand should show: each has a margin of three.

34stat4House Lean: One Pollster’s Steady Tilt

Over a year, firm C comes out about two points above the average of the others on every question, whatever the week. That is not chance, which would go both ways. It is its house lean, from how it words questions, which hours it calls, or how it weights its answers.

\text{lean} = \text{average of (firm’s share} - \text{average of all), over many polls} @@ 
\text{spread of that average over 25 polls} \approx \dfrac{1.6}{\sqrt{25}} \approx 0.3 \text{ points} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a steady 2 point lean is far outside chance: subtract it}} @@

34stat5Errors That Move Together, So Averaging Does Not Remove Them

Suppose every firm misses the same people: those who never answer calls from unknown numbers, who lean differently on the question. Then every poll is off the same way, by an amount averaging cannot touch.

\text{total spread} = \sqrt{\dfrac{s^2}{k} + e^2} @@ k polls, own spread s, shared error e (30)
s = 1.6,\ e = 2,\ k = 10:\ \sqrt{0.26 + 4} \approx 2.1 \text{ points} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ten polls barely beat one: the shared part stays}} @@

Averaging polls shrinks the errors that are each poll’s own, but not the error they all share; the margin printed on a poll counts only the first. When the town votes, Evita compares the result with the final polls: the average missed by about the size of the shared error, as the count allows, and next time she weights her sample by how often each kind of person answers.

Accepted names

The spread is the standard error, and 1.96 of them the margin of error of a 95% confidence interval. A house lean is a house effect; averaging polls is poll aggregation; people who never answer cause nonresponse bias, part of the total survey error.

Who said it first

Jerzy Neyman showed in 1934 why random samples beat hand-picked ones and set out confidence intervals (not yet revisited for the date).
