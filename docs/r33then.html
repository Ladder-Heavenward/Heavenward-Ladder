33·then · newsflash

How does heat creep into the ground?

How does heat creep into the ground? Dig a cellar in Walla Walla and the wine in it sits at nearly the same temperature in July and in January, though the air above swings from baking to freezing. Dig a little deeper and something stranger happens: a few metres down, the ground is warmest in winter and coldest in summer. People who dug wells and mines knew this long before anyone could say why. What makes the question hard is that the surface does not hold still. It warms and cools every day and every year, and the ground below has to answer a start that never ends. This page is the news that rung 33 brings to the sciences. The heat rule, with a surface that swings, answers the cellar. Then the same rule, or one a short step from it, turns up where no one asked for heat at all: along a nerve, in the water under a farm, in a puff of smoke drifting on the wind, in the price of a promise to buy a share, in a steel rail struck with a hammer, and in the rule that matter itself obeys at its smallest.

A surface that swings sends a wave of warmth into the ground that shrinks by a factor of e and falls behind by a fixed share of a cycle every few metres, so that deep enough the seasons are reversed. The same rule, with a leak, runs along a nerve; with the water table for temperature it runs under a farm; with a wind it carries a spreading bell; after a change of knobs it prices a promise to buy; and with one more slope in time, or an i, it runs through steel and through matter.

33then1A wave of warmth into the ground

Let x be the depth below the surface, and let the surface swing about its average T₀ as T₀ + A cos ωt, once a year or once a day. The ground below obeys the heat rule, u_t = κu_xx. Look for an answer that swings at the same pace at every depth. Write the swing as the real part of Ae^(iωt) (26a), and try u = T₀ + Re(A e^(iωt) e^(−qx)).

Ground-wave technique: the swing below a surface that swings as A cos ωt is A e^(−x/d) cos(ωt − x/d), with d = √(2κ/ω). Every d metres down, the swing shrinks by a factor of e and falls behind by 1/(2π) of a cycle.

u_t = i\omega\,(u - T_0),\ \ u_{xx} = q^2\,(u - T_0) @@ the slopes of the exponentials, one knob at a time
i\omega = \kappa q^2,\ \ q = (1 + i)\sqrt{\omega/(2\kappa)} @@ the heat rule; (1 + i)² = 2i; take the root that shrinks with depth
e^{-qx} = e^{-x/d}\,e^{-ix/d},\ \ d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega} @@ split the real and imaginary parts of q
\htmlClass{new}{u = T_0 + A\,e^{-x/d}\cos\big(\omega t - x/d\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the real part; at x = 0 it is the surface’s swing

Moist soil has κ near 5 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s. For the yearly swing, ω = 2π/(3.156 × 10⁷ s), and d ≈ 2.24 m. For the daily swing, d ≈ 0.12 m: a day’s heat reaches only the top hand’s breadth. In Seattle the monthly averages swing about 7 °C either side of 11.5 °C. Two metres down, the swing is 7e^(−2/2.24) ≈ 2.9 °C, and it peaks about 52 days late, in September. At πd ≈ 7 m it is half a year late and down to e^(−π) ≈ 4% of the surface swing: the ground there is warmest in winter, by a fraction of a degree.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, under the ground

The depth d goes as √κ, and κ for soil is not one number. Dry sand, wet clay and peat differ by a factor of five, and the same field differs after rain. Take κ between 3 × 10⁻⁷ and 1 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s, and d for the yearly swing lies between 1.7 m and 3.2 m.

A depth found from a measured diffusivity is a width, and the square root halves its share.

The rule is exact, and the answer is still a width. Its shape is rigorous: the swing shrinks by a factor of e and lags by the same share of a cycle over the same depth, whatever κ is. Where the cellar should be dug is a cloud, and the square root makes it half as wide, in share, as the cloud on κ.

A ground-source heat pump near Kelowna buries its pipes at 2 m. If κ there is somewhere between 4 × 10⁻⁷ and 8 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s, how far below the surface’s swing could the swing at the pipes be? Would digging to 3 m be worth it?

33then2A leaky cable: the nerve

A long thin nerve fibre is a tube of salty water inside a thin skin that lets a little charge leak out. Let V(x, t) be how far its voltage sits from rest. Two measured laws, held in trust: charge runs along the inside down the voltage slope, against a resistance; and it leaks through the skin at a rate in proportion to V. The books on a short piece then give the heat rule with a leak:

\(V_t = \tfrac{\lambda^2}{\tau}\,V_{xx} - \tfrac{1}{\tau}V,\)

where λ, the fibre’s length scale, and τ, its time scale, are set by the skin and the salt. Leaky-cable technique: V solves the leaky rule exactly when e^(t/τ)V solves the heat rule with κ = λ²/τ; and a fibre held at V₀ at one end settles to V₀e^(−x/λ).

W = e^{t/\tau}V,\ \ W_t = e^{t/\tau}\big(V_t + \tfrac1\tau V\big) @@ the product rule
W_t = e^{t/\tau}\tfrac{\lambda^2}{\tau}V_{xx} = \tfrac{\lambda^2}{\tau}W_{xx} @@ the leaky rule cancels the leak
\text{settled: } \lambda^2V^{\prime\prime} = V,\ \ V(0) = V_0,\ V \text{ bounded} @@ V_t = 0
\htmlClass{new}{V = V_0\,e^{-x/\lambda}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tried-growth technique (24h); the growing root is ruled out

So a voltage pressed on a fibre fades to a third every λ along it. For a thin fibre λ is a millimetre or so. A nerve a metre long, from a toe to the spine, could never pass a signal by leaking alone. It does not: every so often the skin opens and tops the voltage up, and the signal is rebuilt as it goes. The rule says why that is needed.

33then3Water under a farm

Water seeps through the gravel under a field the way heat creeps through a rod. A measured law, held in trust: the flow of water through the ground, per metre of width, is −T∇h, where h is the height of the water table and T, the transmissivity, is how freely the gravel passes water. Water is kept, so the books give the heat rule again: S h_t = T Δh, with S the share of the ground that fills. Left alone, the water table settles into a still state.

Well-drawdown technique: around a well pumping Q cubic metres a second from a layer of transmissivity T, the settled water table is h(r) = h(R) − (Q/(2πT)) ln(R/r), lower toward the well.

\Delta h = \tfrac1r\big(r\,h^{\prime}\big)^{\prime} = 0 @@ settled, and the same in every direction: the round-rate technique (33g1)
r\,h^{\prime} = \text{constant} = \tfrac{Q}{2\pi T} @@ the water through each circle round the well, 2πr T h′, is the pumping Q
h = \tfrac{Q}{2\pi T}\ln r + C @@ same slopes (14e), with the log of 17
\htmlClass{new}{h(R) - h(r) = \tfrac{Q}{2\pi T}\ln\tfrac{R}{r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract

An irrigation well near Yakima pumping 0.01 m³/s from gravel with T = 0.005 m²/s lowers the water by Q/(2πT) ≈ 0.32 m for every factor of e in distance. Between 1 m and 100 m from the well, the water table differs by 0.32 × ln 100 ≈ 1.5 m. A neighbour’s well 100 m away feels the pump, and its owner may wonder why.

33then4A spreading bell on the wind

30n’s walkers spread with D = h²/(2τ). Let a steady wind carry them as well, at speed v. A crowd of them, smoke from a chimney say, has a share p(x, t) near x. Spreading gives Dp_xx, and the wind carries, as on 33e1.

Drifting-bell technique: p(x, t) = Φ(x − vt, t), the spreading bell of 33b3 with κ = D, carried at the wind’s speed, solves p_t + v p_x = D p_xx.

p(x, t) = \Phi(\xi, t),\ \ \xi = x - vt @@ ride with the wind
p_t = -v\,\Phi_\xi + \Phi_t,\ \ p_x = \Phi_\xi,\ \ p_{xx} = \Phi_{\xi\xi} @@ roads add (28c)
p_t + v\,p_x = \Phi_t = D\,\Phi_{\xi\xi} = D\,p_{xx} @@ the spreading-bell technique (33b3)
\htmlClass{new}{p = \Phi(x - vt,\ t) \text{ solves the drift rule}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a bell of spread 2Dt, centred at vt

The middle moves like vt, the width like √(2Dt). Far downwind, at distance X, the puff has taken a time X/v, and it is about √(2DX/v) wide: four times the distance, twice the width. A rule of this kind for the share of a crowd, a drift and a spread together, has an accepted name: the Fokker–Planck equation. It is what noise in motion obeys.

33then5A price that spreads like heat

A promise to buy one share of a company at a fixed price K on a fixed day T has a value now, V(S, t), that depends on today’s share price S and on the time t. In 1973 a rule was proposed for it, from the guess that the share’s price wanders like a walk whose steps are in proportion to the price, with spread σ² a year, and that money in the bank grows at the rate r:

\(V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} + rS\,V_S - rV = 0.\)

Price-to-heat technique: with S = eˣ, τ = T − t and V = e^(αx + βτ)u for the right α and β, the price rule becomes u_τ = ½σ²u_xx, the heat rule, run forward from the promise’s value on the last day.

S\,V_S = V_x,\ \ S^2V_{SS} = V_{xx} - V_x,\ \ V_t = -V_\tau @@ the chain rule with S = eˣ, τ = T − t
V_\tau = \tfrac12\sigma^2V_{xx} + \big(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)V_x - rV @@ put them in
V = e^{\alpha x + \beta\tau}u:\ \ u_\tau = \tfrac12\sigma^2u_{xx} + \big(\sigma^2\alpha + r - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)u_x + (\ldots)\,u @@ the product rule; the bracket of u is ½σ²α² + (r − ½σ²)α − r − β
\alpha = \tfrac12 - \tfrac{r}{\sigma^2},\ \ \beta = -\tfrac{(r + \sigma^2/2)^2}{2\sigma^2} @@ choose α to clear uₓ, then β to clear u
\htmlClass{new}{u_\tau = \tfrac12\sigma^2u_{xx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with start u at τ = 0 from the last day’s value, max(eˣ − K, 0) times e^(−αx)

So the price of the promise is the last day’s value spread backward by a bell of spread σ²τ, as 33b3 spread a start forward. The spreading is of the guesses about where the share will be. Whether shares wander that way is a question about markets, not about the rule. 30·econ tested the walk and found the worst days too frequent for a bell.

33then6A rail struck, and matter in a box

Strike the end of a steel rail. A measured law, held in trust: stretching steel by a share e of its length takes a pull of Ee per square metre, with E = 200 × 10⁹ Pa. The books on a short piece, as on 33c1, give ρu_tt = Eu_xx for the stretch u along the rail. So a push travels down the rail at √(E/ρ) = √(200 × 10⁹ / 7850) ≈ 5050 m/s, fifteen times as fast as through the air. A hammer blow on a rail is heard through the rail first.

At the smallest scale, matter obeys a rule with the heat rule’s shape and an i in front: iħψ_t = −(ħ²/2m)ψ_xx, with ħ = h/(2π) and h the constant of 17·phys. Its accepted name is the wave form of mechanics. Separate it as on 33a2, with ψ = 0 at the walls of a box of length L.

Box-levels technique: a particle of mass m in a box of length L has shapes sin(nπx/L), each turning as e^(−iE_nt/ħ), with E_n = n²h²/(8mL²).

\psi = \sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,e^{-iE t/\hbar} @@ the shapes of 33a2, with a turning in time instead of a fading
i\hbar\psi_t = E\,\psi,\ \ -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(\tfrac{n\pi}{L}\big)^2\psi @@ the two sides
E = \tfrac{\hbar^2n^2\pi^2}{2mL^2} @@ they match
\htmlClass{new}{E_n = \tfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ħ = h/(2π)

The numbers go as n², as the heat rule’s fading rates did, not as n, as the string’s tones did. For an electron in a box a nanometre long, E₁ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)² / (8 × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹⁸) ≈ 6.0 × 10⁻²⁰ J, about 0.38 of the energy an electron gains across one volt. Dyes and the tiny crystals in some screens get their colours from jumps between such levels.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The ground wave is the solution of the heat equation with a periodic boundary condition, and d is the damping depth (or penetration depth); the electrical engineer’s skin depth comes from the same sum. The leaky rule is the cable equation, with λ the length constant and τ the membrane time constant; the topping up is saltatory conduction along myelinated nerve fibres, or the action potential on unmyelinated ones. The water law is Darcy’s law, the books give the groundwater flow equation, and the well-drawdown technique is the Thiem equation. The drift rule is the advection–diffusion equation, a case of the Fokker–Planck equation. The price rule is the Black–Scholes equation, and the promise is a European call option. E is the stretch stiffness named after Thomas Young, and stretch in proportion to pull is Hooke’s law; the pull per area is stress and the share stretched is strain. The wave form of mechanics is the Schrödinger equation, and the box is the particle in a box.

Who said it first

Joseph Fourier worked out the yearly wave in the ground in 1822 and used it for cellars. William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) found the earth’s age from its ground in 1862, and the cable equation for the first Atlantic telegraph in 1855; Alan Hodgkin and William Rushton measured it in a nerve in 1946. Henry Darcy measured flow through sand for the fountains of Dijon in 1856, Günther Thiem gave the well formula in 1906, and Charles Theis the unsettled one in 1935. Adriaan Fokker (1914) and Max Planck (1917) wrote the drift rule. Louis Bachelier priced promises with a walk in 1900; Fischer Black, Myron Scholes and Robert Merton gave the price rule in 1973. Thomas Young defined that stiffness in 1807, and Robert Hooke published his law in 1678. Erwin Schrödinger wrote the wave form of mechanics in 1926. In the seam, the geologist was Charles Lyell’s school, the theory was Charles Darwin’s, and radioactive heat in rock was found by Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde (1903) and set against the earth’s age by Ernest Rutherford in 1904.

Wrong moves, worked

“The price rule is the heat rule, and the heat rule has one future for every start. So a promise has one fair price, and the market must pay it.” Not allowed as stated: the one future is for the rule as written, and the rule was written from a guess about how prices wander. Change the guess and the rule changes. 30·econ measured worst days that a bell makes rare; a price built on the bell will be wrong on those days.
