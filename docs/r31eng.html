Why does a tall building sway?

Why does a tall building sway? Stand at the top of a forty-storey tower in Seattle on a windy day and the floor moves under you, slowly, a few centimetres one way and back, about once every four seconds. Nothing is wrong. Every tall building sways, because steel and concrete bend a little, and a building that could not bend would crack. What makes the question hard is that a tower is not one swinging thing but thousands of floors, columns and beams, each pulling on its neighbours, and a gust pushes on all of them at once in a pattern that changes every second. 27·then found that such a motion breaks into steady swings, one along each arrow the grid only stretches. This page asks why those arrows exist at all for a building, why they are at right angles, how a push shares itself among them, and why the worst danger is a push that keeps time with one. The cure that hangs near the top of the tallest towers, a heavy weight on a spring of its own, is a grid that has been made to split one way of shaking into two.

The pulls between floors make a symmetric grid with positive energy, so a building has right-angled ways of shaking, each at its own pace. A push shares itself among the ways by its shadow on each, and a push that keeps time with one way builds it up. A tuned weight near the top splits the first way into two, one just above and one just below the old pace.

31eng1Ways of shaking

Model a building as floors of mass m joined by storeys that bend like springs. Let xᵢ be how far floor i has moved sideways. The pull on floor i is a sum of terms kᵢⱼ times the moves, so m x″ = −Kx for a grid K. Spring j pulls floor i exactly as hard as it pulls floor j the other way (14·phys), so kᵢⱼ = kⱼᵢ: K is symmetric. The energy stored in the bent storeys, ½xᵀKx, is above 0 for every move that is not 0.

Symmetric-sway technique: if m x″ = −Kx with K symmetric and ½xᵀKx above 0 for every x that is not 0, then K has a right-angled basis e₁, …, eₙ of axes, with stretches λₖ above 0; each x = eₖ cos(ωₖt) with ωₖ = √(λₖ/m) is a motion, and every motion is a sum of these, each going on its own.

The first way of shaking has every floor moving the same way, more at the top. A real tower has as many ways as it has floors in each of two directions, plus twisting ones, but its first one or two carry most of what happens. A rough rule for steel towers puts the first period near one tenth of a second for each storey, so four seconds for forty.

31eng2A push shared among the ways

Now push: m x″ = −Kx + F(t), where F lists the push on each floor. Read the push along the axes, as the motion was.

Shared-push technique: a push F drives the kth way of shaking only through its reading F · eₖ along that way, so m yₖ″ = −λₖyₖ + F · eₖ; a push at right angles to a way never starts it, and the squares (F · eₖ)² add to |F|². The first part is Qᵀ applied to the pushed equation, as in 31·eng.1. The second is the shadow-sum technique (31b2) for the right-angled basis eₖ.

F = f(1, 1) @@ the same push on each floor
e_1 = (0.526,\ 0.851),\ \ e_2 = (0.851,\ -0.526) @@ the axes, of size 1
F\cdot e_1 = 1.376f,\ \ F\cdot e_2 = 0.325f @@ the readings
\tfrac{(F\cdot e_1)^2}{|F|^2} = \tfrac{1.894}{2} \approx 95\% @@ the shadow-sum technique
\text{steady lean along } e_k: \tfrac{F\cdot e_k}{\lambda_k} = \tfrac{3.60f}{k},\ \tfrac{0.124f}{k} @@ y″ = 0 in each way
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the first way leans } 29 \text{ times as far}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a small stretch is a soft way, and the push is mostly along it

If the push swings as F cos Ωt, each way is a forced swing of 26·phys, with its own pace ωₖ. Its size grows as Ω nears ωₖ, and with light damping it becomes very large. A building is safe in a push of any one pace that keeps away from all of its own, and in danger from a push that keeps time with one. In 2000 a new footbridge over the Thames in London swayed sideways when crowds crossed it. People walking push sideways once each two steps, near one push a second, which was close to the pace of one of its sideways ways, and the walkers fell into step with the sway, making the push larger.

31eng3A weight that splits the pace

Wind past a tower sheds swirls, first off one side and then the other, at a pace near 0.2U/D for wind speed U and width D. For a tower 40 m wide with a first way at 0.2 Hz, that pace matches when U = 0.2 × 40/0.2 = 40 m/s, a strong gale. Making the tower stiffer moves its pace; making it heavier moves it back. The cure on many tall towers is to hang a weight of about a hundredth of the tower’s moving mass near the top, on a spring tuned to the tower’s own pace.

Mx'' = -kx - \kappa(x - z),\ \ \mu M z'' = -\kappa(z - x) @@ tower x, weight z of mass μM; k = Mω², κ = μMω²
u = \sqrt M\,x,\ \ v = \sqrt{\mu M}\,z @@ weigh each move by the root of its mass
\begin{pmatrix} u \\ v\end{pmatrix}'' = -\omega^2\begin{pmatrix} 1 + \mu & -\sqrt\mu \\ -\sqrt\mu & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} u \\ v\end{pmatrix} @@ symmetric again: the spectral technique applies
\lambda = \omega^2\big(1 + \tfrac\mu2 \pm \sqrt{\mu + \mu^2/4}\big) @@ middle sum 2 + μ, determinant 1
\htmlClass{new}{\mu = 0.01:\ \ 0.951\,\omega,\ \ 1.051\,\omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tower’s one way at ω becomes two, 5% each side

Weighted-grid technique: if M x″ = −Kx with M diagonal with entries above 0 and K symmetric, then the moves u = M^(1/2)x obey u″ = −M^(−1/2)KM^(−1/2)u, and that grid is symmetric; so unequal masses still give right-angled ways, at right angles in the weighted product Σmᵢxᵢyᵢ.

With the weight tuned, no way of shaking sits at ω any more, so a gust at ω no longer finds a pace to keep time with. A damper between the weight and the tower soaks up the swing that remains. In the tower called 101 in Taipei a steel ball of about 660 tonnes hangs near the top. Its splitting is the weight-and-spring arithmetic above; how much damping to give it is a matter for the forced swing, and is left open here.

The accepted names

The ways of shaking are the mode shapes, or normal modes, and their paces the natural frequencies; reading the motion along them is modal analysis. K is the stiffness matrix and M the mass matrix, and M x″ = −Kx is the generalised eigenvalue problem Kx = ω²Mx, made symmetric by mass-normalising. The swirls shed by wind are vortex shedding, with 0.2 the Strouhal number. A tuned weight with a damper is a tuned mass damper. A push that is changed by the motion it causes is self-excited, as in flutter and galloping.

Who said it first

John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh, set out the ways of shaking of elastic bodies in The Theory of Sound (1877). Vincenc Strouhal measured the singing of wires in wind in 1878, and Theodore von Kármán explained the street of swirls in 1911. Hermann Frahm patented the tuned weight in 1909 for ships, and Jacob Pieter Den Hartog worked out its best tuning in 1934 and 1940. The London footbridge opened in June 2000 and was closed two days later; it reopened in 2002 with dampers.
