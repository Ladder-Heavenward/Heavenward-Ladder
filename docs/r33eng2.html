Why Does the Wind Make You Colder?

Why does the wind make you colder? The air on a windy day may be no colder than on a still one, yet it draws heat from your skin much faster. A laptop’s fan works the same trick in reverse, and so does blowing on soup. Heat leaving a surface into moving air or water must first cross a thin layer that clings to the surface, and moving fluid makes that layer thinner. This page finds how heat crosses a layer, how thick the layer is by rung 33’s heat equation, how fast heat leaves a plate in a breeze, how big a heat sink must be, and how much a still surface loses with no wind at all.

Heat crosses a still layer of thickness δ at k·ΔT/δ per square metre. Fluid flowing past a surface clings to it; heat diffuses out into the passing fluid for the time x/U it takes to travel x, so the layer grows as √(νx/U), and the heat loss per area goes as √(U/x). Lump it into a coefficient h with q = hΔT, read h from measured formulas, and size a surface by A = Q/(hΔT). Still surfaces lose by rising air and by glow, each a few watts per square metre per degree.

33eng21Heat Across a Still Layer

Joy’s hand is at 33 °C and the air at 3 °C. Right at the skin the air does not move; heat must cross that still layer by conduction, the heat flow of 33a, at a rate set by the layer’s thickness δ and the air’s conductivity k = 0.026 W/(m·K).

q = k\,\dfrac{\Delta T}{\delta} @@ heat flows down the slope of temperature (33a)
\delta = 5 \text{ mm}:\ q = 0.026 \cdot 30 / 0.005 \approx 160 \text{ W/m}^2 @@ a calm day
\delta = 1 \text{ mm}:\ q \approx 780 \text{ W/m}^2 @@ a thinner layer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the thinner the clinging layer, the faster the loss}} @@ q goes as 1/δ

From here on, on this page, the layer is the thin sheet of fluid next to a surface through which heat must conduct. Air is a poor conductor, so the layer is what keeps you warm. Wind attacks the layer.

33eng22How Thick the Layer Is

Air flowing at speed U along a plate sticks to it and is slowed near it. A bit of air that has travelled a distance x along the plate has been next to it for a time x/U. Rung 33’s heat equation says heat, and slowing too, spread by diffusion a distance that grows as √(time).

\text{spread after time } t \sim \sqrt{\nu t} @@ diffusion spreads as √t (33b); ν the air’s spreading constant
t = x/U @@ the time spent beside the plate
\delta \approx 5\sqrt{\nu x / U} @@ the 5 from the full calculation
\htmlClass{new}{\nu = 1.5 \times 10^{-5},\ x = 0.1,\ U = 2:\ \delta \approx 4 \text{ mm}} @@ thinner as U grows, thicker down the plate

Layer technique: the clinging layer grows as the square root of the distance travelled over the speed, because diffusion spreads as the square root of time. Four times the wind makes the layer half as thick.

33eng23The Coefficient h

Engineers lump it all into one number: q = hΔT, with h the coefficient in W/(m²·K). From 33eng21 and 33eng22, h ≈ k/δ grows as √U. The full calculation for smooth flow along a plate of length L gives the average:

h = 0.664\,\dfrac{k}{L}\,\sqrt{\dfrac{UL}{\nu}}\;Pr^{1/3} @@ Pr ≈ 0.71 for air
UL/\nu = 2 \cdot 0.1 / 1.5 \times 10^{-5} \approx 13\,300 @@ no units: a pure number
h \approx 0.664 \cdot 0.26 \cdot 115 \cdot 0.89 \approx 18 \text{ W/(m}^2\text{K)} @@ k/L = 0.26, √13 300 ≈ 115, 0.71^⅓ ≈ 0.89
\htmlClass{new}{U \to 4U:\ h \to 2h} @@ wind chill: four times the wind, twice the loss

Coefficient technique: heat leaves a surface into a moving fluid at hΔT per square metre, and in smooth flow h grows as the square root of the speed. Water conducts twenty times better than air and spreads differently, so the same breeze of water takes heat far faster: cold water chills more than cold air.

33eng24Sizing a Heat Sink

A laptop’s chip makes 20 W and must stay within 40 degrees of the air. With a fan, h ≈ 20 W/(m²·K).

Q = hA\,\Delta T @@ the coefficient technique
A = \dfrac{Q}{h\,\Delta T} = \dfrac{20}{20 \cdot 40} = 0.025 \text{ m}^2 @@ 250 square centimetres
\text{a chip is } 1 \text{ cm}^2 @@ far too small alone
\htmlClass{new}{\text{fins give the area; the fan raises } h} @@ the two levers

To shed heat Q at a temperature difference ΔT, a surface needs area Q/(hΔT): fins supply area, and moving fluid supplies h. Fins packed too close trap their own layers, so there is a best spacing, set by the layer thickness.

33eng25With No Wind at All

A warm surface in still air still loses heat two ways. Warm air next to it rises by itself, making its own breeze; and the surface glows, as 25·phys found for stars.

\text{rising warm air: } h \approx 5 \text{ W/(m}^2\text{K)} @@ measured, for a person-sized surface
\text{glow: } \sigma(T_s^4 - T_a^4) \approx 4\sigma T^3\,\Delta T @@ the slope of T⁴ (14)
4 \cdot 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \cdot 300^3 \approx 6 \text{ W/(m}^2\text{K)} @@ at room temperature
\htmlClass{new}{\text{still air: about } 11 \text{ W/(m}^2\text{K) in all}} @@ half of it is glow

In still air a warm surface loses about as much by glowing as by the air it warms, a few watts per square metre per degree each. A shiny blanket that glows weakly stops the first; a windproof layer stops the breeze from thinning the second.

33eng27How Much a Fin Helps

Moving air carries heat off faster, and fins give it more area (33eng24). But a fin cools along its length, so its far end is cooler and sheds less. How much of its area really works depends on how well the fin carries heat out to its tip.

m = \sqrt{\dfrac{2h}{k\,t}} @@ how fast the fin cools along its length (33a)
\text{working share} = \dfrac{\tanh(mL)}{mL} @@ the slowest shape along the fin (33h)
\text{aluminium},\ k = 200,\ t = 1 \text{ mm},\ h = 20,\ L = 30 \text{ mm}:\ mL \approx 0.42,\ \text{share} \approx 0.94 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{plastic},\ k = 0.2:\ mL \approx 13,\ \text{share} \approx 0.07} @@ a plastic fin is almost all dead area

Fin technique: a fin’s working share is tanh(mL)/(mL) with m = √(2h/(kt)); good fins are thin and short in metal of high conductivity, so mL stays near 1 or below. And a body cools all at once, its inside keeping step with its outside, when hL/k is below about 0.1: then its temperature falls as e^(−t/τ) with τ its stored heat per degree over hA. Shown in a wind tunnel: a warm plate with thermocouples along a fin reads the temperature falling toward the tip along the cosh curve, and the heat lost matches hA times the working share.

Accepted names

Heat carried away by moving fluid is convection; driven by a fan, forced convection, and by rising warm fluid, natural or free convection. The clinging layer is the boundary layer, ν the kinematic viscosity, h the heat transfer coefficient. UL/ν is the Reynolds number (17·eng), hL/k the Nusselt number, and Pr the Prandtl number. q = hΔT is Newton’s law of cooling, and the glow is thermal radiation.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton described cooling at a rate set by the temperature difference in 1701. Ludwig Prandtl introduced the boundary layer in 1904, and Wilhelm Nusselt the numbers for heat transfer in 1915.
