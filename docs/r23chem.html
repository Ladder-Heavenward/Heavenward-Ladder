How Many Trays Does a Still Need?

How many trays does a still need? A refinery’s tall towers separate liquids by boiling them over and over. Inside, trays are stacked one above another; on each, vapour from below bubbles through liquid from above, and comes out a little richer in the part that boils more easily. This page writes the vapour’s richness as a function of the liquid’s (9), steps tray by tray as a sequence (22a), finds where the steps settle into a pinch (22c), and finds the two limits a designer works between: the fewest trays, and the least liquid sent back down.

On each tray, the vapour leaving is richer than the liquid there by a fixed curve. Condense it, and it is the liquid of the tray above. Stepping up the column repeats one function, and in odds the step multiplies by a fixed factor, so the tray count is a log. Sending less liquid back down makes the steps shrink until they settle at a pinch and never reach the top.

23chem1Vapour Is Richer Than Its Liquid

Jean runs a still separating benzene from toluene. Benzene boils more easily. When a mixture boils, the vapour has more benzene than the liquid it came from.

From here on, on this page, x is the share of benzene in a liquid and y the share in the vapour that rises from it, and α = 2.5 is how many times more easily benzene boils than toluene, for this pair.

\dfrac{y}{1 - y} = \alpha\,\dfrac{x}{1 - x} @@ the vapour’s odds are α times the liquid’s
y = \dfrac{\alpha x}{1 + (\alpha - 1)x} @@ solved for y: a function of x (9)
\htmlClass{new}{x = 0.5:\ y = \dfrac{1.25}{1.75} \approx 0.71} @@ half and half boils off 71% benzene

23chem2One Tray: Up the Curve and Across the Line

On one tray, liquid of share x meets vapour and sends up vapour of share y. That vapour rises to the next tray. If every bit of vapour reaching the top is condensed and sent back down, the liquid on the tray above has the share the vapour had.

x \to y = f(x) @@ up the curve
y \to x_{\text{next}} = y @@ across to the line y = x
\htmlClass{new}{x_{n+1} = f(x_n)} @@ one tray is one turn of the function

Drawn on the graph of the curve and the line y = x (9e), each tray is a step: up to the curve, across to the line.

Tray-stepping technique: a column is counted tray by tray by stepping up the vapour curve and across the line; with all the liquid sent back, each tray multiplies the odds by α, so the fewest trays is the log of the odds needed over the log of α. 23chem4The Steps Settle Toward a Pinch

Sending everything back makes no product. To draw off product at the top, only part of the liquid is sent back: R parts back for each part drawn off. Then the across-step goes not to y = x but to a line of slope R/(R + 1) through the top product’s share, x_D = 0.95. Stepping down from the top runs the curve backwards (9d).

y = \dfrac{R}{R+1}\,x + \dfrac{x_D}{R+1} @@ the line
x = \dfrac{y}{\alpha - (\alpha - 1)y} @@ the curve run backwards (9d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the shares fall but stay above where the line meets the curve}} @@ a fall bounded below settles (22a)

The steps shrink as they near the crossing, and settle there without ever passing it (22c). If the crossing lies above the share at which the feed comes in, no number of trays will reach it: the column is pinched.

23chem5The Fewest Trays, and the Least Liquid Sent Back

The least R that still works puts the pinch exactly at the feed. Jean’s feed is half benzene, where the curve gives y = 0.714.

\dfrac{R}{R+1} = \dfrac{0.95 - 0.714}{0.95 - 0.5} \approx 0.524 @@ the line from the top through the curve at the feed
\htmlClass{new}{R_{\min} \approx 1.1} @@ less than this, and no tray count is enough

Sending everything back gives the fewest trays and no product; sending back the least gives endless trays. Real columns run between, at about 1.2 to 1.5 times the least. More liquid sent back costs heat to boil it again every hour; more trays cost steel once. A designer weighs the two over the plant’s life.

Accepted names

α is the relative volatility and R the reflux ratio. Each ideal tray is a theoretical stage; the stepping drawing is the McCabe-Thiele method, the fewest-tray count the Fenske equation, and the least R the minimum reflux. A still with trays is a distillation column.

Who said it first

Warren McCabe and Ernest Thiele published the stepping drawing in 1925; Merrell Fenske gave the fewest-tray count in 1932.
