15·phys · physics

Where is the balance point of a shape?

Where is the balance point of a shape? A loaded bar was the easy case. Its weights hung at a few places, and the balance point was their weighted average. A flat plate is harder. Its weight is not at a few places. It is spread over every bit of it, and no list of weights is long enough to hold it. Steel shops ask the question every time a crane lifts a cut plate. Hook it anywhere but over the balance point, and it tips as it leaves the floor. The question is also far older than cranes. The first person to prove the law of the lever also found the balance points of curved plates, by cutting them into thin strips in his head. This page does the same with the strips of this rung. Each strip is nearly a bar. The turning effects of the strips add up to an area, so the balance point comes out as one area shared by another. Then it finds plates whose balance point is not on the plate at all.

Cut a plate of even thickness into thin upright strips. Each strip weighs in proportion to its height, and it sits at its own place across. Add up the turning effects of the strips, and the sums close in on an area. So the balance point across is the area under place times height, shared by the area under height. The balance point up comes the same way, with each strip's weight counted at its middle height. Pieces of a plate combine by the old weighted average, and a hole is a piece whose weight is taken away. A plate that curls round an empty space can have its balance point in the empty space.

A plate as strips

From here on, on this page, a plate is a flat sheet of even thickness, such as steel or plywood, cut to a region. The region under f on [u, v] is the set of pairs (x, y) with u ≤ x ≤ v and 0 ≤ y ≤ f(x), for f continuous and f ≥ 0 there, and f not 0 everywhere. Places are in metres: x across, y up. The plate stands up, as it hangs from a crane.

The physics premise, from weighing cut pieces: a piece of plate weighs in proportion to its area. Write ρ for the weight of one square metre of the plate. Steel is about 7850 kg a cubic metre, so steel plate 10 mm thick has ρ ≈ 78.5 kg a square metre. A plate of area A weighs ρA.

Cut [u, v] by a partition P into pieces of width Δxᵢ. Over each piece stands a thin upright strip of plate. Its weight lies between ρmᵢΔxᵢ and ρMᵢΔxᵢ, by boxes-trap-the-area (15a), with mᵢ and Mᵢ the least and greatest heights of f on the piece. And all of it lies between xᵢ₋₁ and xᵢ across. A narrow strip is nearly one of 12·phys's bars, standing on end.

Balance across

Lay a straight knife-edge under the plate, upright, at the place x = p across. Seen edge on, the plate is a loaded bar. Each strip turns it by the strip's weight times its signed distance from p: 12·phys's turning effect. The plate balances where the total T(p) is 0.

Balance-plate technique: a plate balances across at the area under place times height, shared by the area under height. Precisely: for the plate under f on [u, v], T(p) = ρ(∫ᵤᵛ x f(x) dx − p ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx), so it balances at X = ∫ᵤᵛ x f(x) dx / ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx. Start with the knife-edge at u, so that every strip is on the same side of it.

g(x) = (x - u) f(x) \ge 0 \text{ on } [u, v] @@ every strip is at or right of u
\rho\, m_i (x_{i-1} - u)\Delta x_i \le T_i \le \rho\, M_i (x_i - u)\Delta x_i @@ strip i: its weight between ρmᵢΔxᵢ and ρMᵢΔxᵢ, all of it between xᵢ₋₁ and xᵢ (12·phys's turning effect)

The ρ has gone. The balance point depends on the region alone, not on whether the plate is steel or plywood, so long as its thickness is even. Its accepted name, for a plate of even thickness, is the centroid of the region.

Balance up

Now lay the knife-edge level, at height y = q. Each strip is an upright bar. Its part below the height mᵢ is a full box, and a bar the same all along turns as if all of it sat at its middle (12·phys). Its whole lies inside the box of height Mᵢ.

\tfrac12 \rho\, m_i^2 \Delta x_i \le T_i \le \tfrac12 \rho\, M_i^2 \Delta x_i @@ about y = 0: the box of height mᵢ turns by ρmᵢΔxᵢ · mᵢ/2; the strip is inside the box of height Mᵢ, and nothing turns the other way
T(0) = \rho \textstyle\int_u^v \tfrac12 f(x)^2\,dx @@ the left and right are the lower and upper sums of ρf²/2 (f ≥ 0); continuous-is-integrable (15f), exactly-one-between (15c)
\htmlClass{new}{Y = \dfrac{\int_u^v \frac12 f(x)^2\,dx}{\int_u^v f(x)\,dx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ as across: T(q) = T(0) − qρ∫f, zero at q = Y

The pair (X, Y) is the plate's balance point. The physics premise, checked with a pin: a plate pinned at (X, Y) hangs still whichever way it is turned. Its reason waits for the geometry of rung 18.

In a steel shop in Tacoma, a plate of 10 mm steel is cut to the region under x² on [0, 1]. Where does the lifting eye go?

\textstyle\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 \text{ m}^2,\ \ \text{weight } 78.5 \cdot \tfrac13 \approx 26.2 \text{ kg} @@ area-under-a-power (15i), k = 2; the premise
\textstyle\int_0^1 x \cdot x^2\,dx = \int_0^1 x^3\,dx = \tfrac14 @@ area-under-a-power, k = 3
X = \tfrac{1/4}{1/3} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac34} \text{ m} @@ balance-plate, across
\textstyle\int_0^1 \tfrac12 x^4\,dx = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac1{10} @@ areas-scale; area-under-a-power, k = 4
Y = \tfrac{1/10}{1/3} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac3{10}} \text{ m} @@ balance-plate, up: the eye goes at (0.75, 0.3)

Two tempting guesses miss. The middle of [0, 1] is 0.5, not 0.75: the tall side weighs more. And half the average height is 1/6, not 3/10: the tall strips carry their weight high.

Pieces and holes

Pieces technique: a piece of a plate turns it as if all the piece's weight sat at the piece's own balance point. Precisely: cut a plate into two pieces, with areas A₁ and A₂ and balance points X₁ and X₂ across. Then the whole balances across at X = (A₁X₁ + A₂X₂)/(A₁ + A₂), and the same holds up.

T(p) = T_1(p) + T_2(p) @@ turning effects add (12·phys's Σ); side by side, interval-cut-in-two (15e)
= \rho A_1 (X_1 - p) + \rho A_2 (X_2 - p) @@ balance-plate for each piece: Tₖ(p) = ρ(∫xf − p∫f) = ρAₖ(Xₖ − p)
\htmlClass{new}{X = \dfrac{A_1 X_1 + A_2 X_2}{A_1 + A_2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ T(X) = 0; it is balance-formula (12·phys), with a weight ρAₖ at each Xₖ

Run backwards, it handles a hole. Let a cut-out piece have area A_c and balance point Y_c, and let the whole uncut plate have A and Y. The rest has area A − A_c, and (A − A_c)·Y_rest = A·Y − A_c·Y_c. The same holds across. A hole is a piece with its weight taken away.

A bracket is cut from a box of plate 2 m wide and 1.2 m tall, the region under the height 1.2 on [0, 2]. The arch under x(2 − x) = 2x − x² is cut out of its foot.

\text{box: } A = 2.4,\ \ Y = 0.6 @@ a bar the same all along balances at its middle (12·phys)
\text{arch: } A_c = \textstyle\int_0^2 (2x - x^2)\,dx = 4 - \tfrac83 = \tfrac43 @@ areas-add, areas-scale, area-under-a-power
\textstyle\int_0^2 \tfrac12 (2x - x^2)^2\,dx = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{32}{3} - 16 + \tfrac{32}{5}\big) = \tfrac{8}{15},\ \ Y_c = \tfrac{8/15}{4/3} = \tfrac25 @@ (2x − x²)² = 4x² − 4x³ + x⁴; balance-plate, up
A - A_c = 2.4 - \tfrac43 = \tfrac{16}{15} @@ the bracket's area
\tfrac{16}{15}\, Y_{\text{rest}} = 2.4 \cdot 0.6 - \tfrac43 \cdot \tfrac25 = \tfrac{68}{75} @@ pieces, run backwards
Y_{\text{rest}} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{17}{20}} = 0.85 \text{ m},\ \ X_{\text{rest}} = 1 @@ across: box and arch both balance at 1, so the rest does too

At x = 1 the arch reaches up to 1, and the bracket there runs only from 1 to 1.2. Its balance point (1, 0.85) is under the arch, in the hole. A hook on a chain dropped to (1, 0.85) would hang in the air. A crane lifts the bracket from two hooks on its top edge instead, one on each side of x = 1.

A high jumper who goes over the bar backwards uses the same fact. The body arches round the bar: head and shoulders go over and drop, then the hips, then the legs. At the top the body curls round the bar, and its balance point can pass under the bar while every part of the body passes over. The jumper lifts the balance point less than the height of the bar.

Who said it first

Archimedes, in On the Equilibrium of Planes, proved the law of the lever from postulates and went on to find the centres of gravity of flat regions, among them the region between the graph of x² and a straight line across it: its centre lies three fifths of the way from the tip to the line.

Archimedes, On the Equilibrium of Planes, Book II (third century BC); paraphrase

His Method, the letter of 15·phys.4.4, was written to Eratosthenes. It survived only as the faded first writing of a parchment scraped and written over in the thirteenth century as a prayer book. Johan Ludvig Heiberg read it in Constantinople in 1906. In it Archimedes weighs slices of one figure against another and says plainly that the weighing is a way to find, and not a demonstration.

The backward jump over the bar is the Fosbury flop, after Dick Fosbury, who won the high jump at the Olympic Games in Mexico City in 1968 with it. Measurements of jumpers since put the balance point of the body close to the bar at the top, and by some measurements a little under it.

Accepted names: the balance point is the centre of mass, or centre of gravity; for a plate of even thickness, the centroid of its region. ∫ x f(x) dx is the first moment of the region about the line x = 0, and the pieces technique is the method of composite areas.
