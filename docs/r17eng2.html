How Can a Model Tell You How Hard a Ship Pushes Through Water?

How can a model tell you how hard a ship pushes through water? A ship costs millions and takes years to build; its engines must be sized before its hull is tried. In the 1870s William Froude towed wooden models a few metres long down a long tank and predicted the power of full-size warships, and model tanks are still in use today. The trick is 17·eng’s matching of units: the water’s waves depend on gravity, and only one grouping of speed, length and gravity has no units. Run the model at that same grouping and its waves are a small copy of the ship’s. This page finds the grouping, scales a model up, meets the clash that forced Froude’s split, and finds the speed at which a ship starts to climb its own bow wave.

A ship’s wave push R can depend on speed v, length L, gravity g and the water’s heaviness ρ. Matching units: R / (ρv²L²) depends only on v²/(gL). Run a model at the same v/√(gL): its waves copy the ship’s, and force scales as length cubed. Friction obeys a different grouping, so it is worked out apart. A ship makes a wave as long as itself at v/√(gL) ≈ 0.4.

17eng21A Ship Makes Waves

Claire’s ship, 100 m long, pushes water aside and leaves a train of waves. Lifting that water takes work against gravity: that is the wave part of the push the engines must give.

v \ (\text{m/s}),\ L \ (\text{m}),\ g \ (\text{m/s}^2),\ \rho \ (\text{kg/m}^3) @@ speed, length, gravity, water
R \ (\text{kg m/s}^2) @@ a force
R = C\,\rho^a v^b L^c g^d @@ a product of powers (17·eng)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{five quantities, three units: two free groupings}} @@ one more than 17·eng’s drag

From here on, on this page, the wave push is the part of the water’s push on the hull spent on making waves.

17eng22Matching the Units

\text{kg}:\ a = 1 @@ only ρ carries kg
\text{s}:\ -b - 2d = -2 @@ v and g carry seconds
\text{m}:\ -3a + b + c + d = 1 @@ every quantity carries metres
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{R}{\rho v^2 L^2} = f\!\left(\dfrac{v^2}{gL}\right)} @@ d free: its grouping has no units

Froude technique: the wave push, divided by ρv²L², depends only on the one unit-free grouping v/√(gL). Two hulls of the same shape at the same v/√(gL) make waves of the same shape, scaled.

17eng23Scaling the Model Up

L_{\text{model}} = 4 \text{ m} @@ 100 m divided by 25
\text{same } \dfrac{v}{\sqrt{gL}}:\ v_{\text{model}} = \dfrac{v}{\sqrt{25}} = \dfrac{v}{5} @@ 10 m/s ship, 2 m/s model
R \propto \rho v^2 L^2 \propto L \cdot L^2 = L^3 @@ v² grows with L
\htmlClass{new}{R_{\text{ship}} = 25^3 \cdot R_{\text{model}} = 15\,625 \cdot R_{\text{model}}} @@ a few newtons become tens of kilonewtons

Run the model at the speed that keeps v/√(gL) the same, and multiply its wave push by the scale cubed.

17eng24Two Rules That Clash

The water also rubs along the hull. That friction depends on the water’s stickiness, and its unit-free grouping is speed times length over stickiness.

\text{friction: same } vL \Rightarrow v_{\text{model}} = 25\,v @@ faster, not slower
\text{waves: } v_{\text{model}} = v/5 @@ Froude’s rule
\text{no model speed obeys both} @@ in the same water
\htmlClass{new}{\text{measure total push; take away friction from a flat plank; scale the rest}} @@ Froude’s split

Froude towed flat planks to measure friction alone, took it from each model’s total, scaled the remainder as waves, and added back friction worked out for the full ship. When two effects obey different unit-free groupings, a model can copy only one, and the other is reckoned apart.

17eng25The Wave a Ship Cannot Outrun

\text{deep-water waves of length } \lambda \text{ travel at } \sqrt{g\lambda / 2\pi} @@ measured, and derived by Airy
\text{a ship’s bow wave travels with it: speed } v @@ so its length is 2πv²/g
\lambda = L:\ v = \sqrt{gL/2\pi},\ \tfrac{v}{\sqrt{gL}} \approx 0.40 @@ the ship sits in its own wave
\htmlClass{new}{L = 100 \text{ m}:\ v \approx 12.5 \text{ m/s} \approx 24 \text{ knots}} @@ push rises steeply beyond

At v/√(gL) near 0.4 a hull is cradled between its bow wave and a trough at its stern, and going faster means climbing the wave. That is why long ships are faster, and why fast boats lift out of the water and skim instead.

17eng27The Slowed Layer Along the Hull

Water touching the hull moves with it; a little way out it does not. Between lies a thin slowed layer, and its drag is the hull’s rub. Its thickness δ at distance x from the bow can depend only on x, the speed U and the water’s stickiness ν, in square metres per second.

[\nu] = \text{m}^2/\text{s},\ [x] = \text{m},\ [U] = \text{m/s} @@ 
\sqrt{\nu x / U} \text{ is a length} @@ the slowing spreads out as the water passes
\delta \approx 5\sqrt{\nu x / U} @@ the 5 measured, for a smooth layer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a model at 2 m/s, 1 m from its bow}:\ \delta \approx 5\sqrt{10^{-6} \cdot 1 / 2} \approx 3.5 \text{ mm}} @@ four times as far, twice as thick

Square-root layer technique: the slowed layer thickens as the square root of the distance from the bow, √(νx/U), so it stays thin on a fast, short hull and its rub depends on the clash of 17eng24. Shown in a towing tank with dye let out at the hull: the dyed layer widens down the model just as √x says, smooth near the bow and then churning; so tanks fit a thin wire near a model’s bow to make the layer churn from the start, as it does along the ship.

17eng28From the Tank to the Engine Room

A ship’s work goes into the waves it makes and the water it drags along its hull. The tank measures the model’s total resistance; the rub part, which does not scale like the waves (17eng24), is taken off by a standard line, the waves scaled up, and the ship’s rub added back.

C_F = \dfrac{0.075}{(\log_{10} Re - 2)^2} @@ the line the tanks agree on
\text{model, } Re = 10^7:\ C_F = \tfrac{0.075}{25} = 0.0030;\ \ \text{ship, } Re = 10^9:\ \tfrac{0.075}{49} \approx 0.0015 @@ the ship rubs half as much per area
\text{power to tow the ship} = R\,V;\ \ R = 1\,000 \text{ kN},\ V = 10 \text{ m/s}:\ 10 \text{ MW} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{engine} = \tfrac{10}{0.65} \approx 15 \text{ MW}} @@ propeller and hull pass on about two thirds

Tank-to-ship technique: split the model’s resistance into rub, from the standard line, and waves, scale the waves up, add the ship’s own rub, and divide the towing power by the share the propeller passes on. Shown at sea: a ship whose model was towed in a tank runs its trials, and its speed for each engine power falls on the tank’s prediction within a few percent.

Accepted names

v/√(gL) is the Froude number; the friction grouping vL/ν is the Reynolds number; the wave push is wave-making resistance and the friction frictional resistance. The speed at Froude number about 0.4 is the hull speed; skimming is planing; a model tank is a towing tank.

Who said it first

William Froude built a towing tank at Torquay in 1871 and checked his models against towing trials of the warship HMS Greyhound in 1871 and 1872. George Airy gave the speed of water waves in 1841.
