31h · proof

A plane with endlessly many directions

A space of functions has endlessly many directions. No list of finitely many functions builds every function, and a right-angled list can go on for ever. Does the shadow of an arrow on endlessly many directions still make sense? The readings along the directions are numbers, endlessly many of them, and their squares add to no more than the square of the size of the arrow. That is the energy bound, and it needs nothing but right angles. Whether the readings rebuild the arrow is another matter. It needs the space to have no holes, so that the endless sum of shadows has somewhere to settle, and it needs the list to leave no direction out. When both are true, the readings are the arrow, and the energy is kept exactly. And in a space with no holes, every closed flat piece has a closest point, found by the same right angle as on the plane.

In a product space with no holes, the readings of an arrow along an endless right-angled list have squares adding to at most its squared size. The sum of the shadows settles, and it is the arrow exactly when no direction is left out, with the energy then kept. Every closed flat piece has one closest point, its miss at right angles.

31h1Product spaces with no holes

From here on, a complete product space is a space of arrows with a product in which every list that crowds under the product’s size settles. L² is one, by the size-complete technique (31g4). The first footing builds another, out of lists of numbers.

Write ℓ² for the endless lists c = (c₁, c₂, …) of numbers, real or turning, with |c₁|² + |c₂|² + ⋯ finite, and put ⟨c, d⟩ = c₁d̄₁ + c₂d̄₂ + ⋯. Each running total of the product obeys the product bound for lists of n numbers, so |c₁d̄₁| + ⋯ + |cₙd̄ₙ| ≤ ‖c‖ ‖d‖ and the endless sum settles in size.

List-space technique: ℓ² is a complete product space.

c^{(1)}, c^{(2)}, \ldots \text{ crowds in } \ell^2 @@ ‖c⁽ⁿ⁾ − c⁽ᵐ⁾‖ → 0
|c^{(n)}_k - c^{(m)}_k| \le \|c^{(n)} - c^{(m)}\| \text{ for each place } k @@ one square is at most the sum of them all
c^{(n)}_k \to c_k @@ each place crowds, so settles (22c)
\textstyle\sum_{k \le K}|c^{(n)}_k - c^{(m)}_k|^2 \le \varepsilon^2 \text{ for } n, m \ge N,\ \text{every } K @@ crowding, with N fixed by ε
\textstyle\sum_{k \le K}|c^{(n)}_k - c_k|^2 \le \varepsilon^2,\ \text{so } \|c^{(n)} - c\| \le \varepsilon @@ let m grow, then K; c = c⁽ⁿ⁾ − (c⁽ⁿ⁾ − c) is in ℓ² by the product-triangle technique
\htmlClass{new}{c^{(n)} \to c \text{ in } \ell^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε was any number above 0

31h2The energy bound

Let e₁, e₂, … be an endless right-angled list in a product space. Call cₖ = ⟨f, eₖ⟩ the kth reading of f.

Energy-bound technique: for every endless right-angled list, |⟨f, e₁⟩|² + |⟨f, e₂⟩|² + ⋯ ≤ ‖f‖²: the readings of f never hold more energy than f.

P_nf = \textstyle\sum_{k \le n}\langle f, e_k\rangle e_k,\ \ f - P_nf \perp P_nf @@ the shadow-sum technique (31b2), on the first n
\|f\|^2 = \|f - P_nf\|^2 + \|P_nf\|^2 @@ the square-sum technique (31a1)
\|P_nf\|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{k \le n}|\langle f, e_k\rangle|^2 \le \|f\|^2 @@ the square-sum technique again, term by term
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_k |\langle f, e_k\rangle|^2 \le \|f\|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the running totals rise and stay under ‖f‖², so the sum settles (22d)
  
e_k = \sqrt2\,\sin(k\pi x) \text{ on } [0, 1] @@ sizes 1, at right angles: the product-to-sum technique (21k)
\langle 1, e_k\rangle = \sqrt2\,\tfrac{1 - \cos k\pi}{k\pi} = \tfrac{2\sqrt2}{k\pi} \text{ for odd } k,\ 0 \text{ for even } k @@ an area under sin (16, 21)
\tfrac{8}{\pi^2}\big(1 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{25}\big) \approx 0.933 \le 1 = \|1\|^2 @@ the first three readings that are not 0
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{8}{\pi^2}\textstyle\sum_{k \text{ odd}}\tfrac{1}{k^2} \le 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy-bound technique: Σ over odd k of 1/k² is at most π²/8

The energy bound gives a ceiling, and the readings stay under it. A step has readings that shrink only like 1/k, which is slow, and still their squares add to no more than 1. Whether they add to exactly 1 is the next footing’s question.

31h3When the readings rebuild the arrow

From here on, a right-angled list is a right-angled basis of a complete product space when the only arrow at right angles to every eₖ is 0: no direction is left out.

Energy-sum technique: in a complete product space, Σₖ⟨f, eₖ⟩eₖ settles for every f, at an arrow whose miss from f is at right angles to every eₖ; and the list is a right-angled basis exactly when f = Σₖ⟨f, eₖ⟩eₖ and ‖f‖² = Σₖ|⟨f, eₖ⟩|² for every f.

\|P_mf - P_nf\|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{n < k \le m}|\langle f, e_k\rangle|^2 @@ the square-sum technique, for m > n
\to 0 \text{ as } n, m \text{ grow} @@ the tail of a settling sum (31h2, 22d4)
P_nf \to Pf @@ the shadows crowd, and the space is complete
\langle f - Pf,\ e_j\rangle = \lim_n \langle f - P_nf,\ e_j\rangle = 0 @@ a product moves only a little when an arrow moves a little (the product bound); for n ≥ j the miss is at right angles to eⱼ
\text{basis} \Rightarrow f = Pf,\ \ \|f\|^2 = \lim\|P_nf\|^2 = \textstyle\sum|\langle f, e_k\rangle|^2 @@ the miss is at right angles to every eₖ, so it is 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{conversely, } g \perp \text{every } e_k \Rightarrow \|g\|^2 = \textstyle\sum 0 = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy sum, run on g, leaves no direction out

So in a complete product space with a right-angled basis, an arrow is its list of readings. The list is in ℓ², by the energy bound, and products are kept: ⟨f, g⟩ = Σ ⟨f, eₖ⟩ times the conjugate of ⟨g, eₖ⟩. Such a space is ℓ² under another name.

If the sines of 31h2 are a right-angled basis of L² on [0, 1], the energy sum gives (8/π²)Σ over odd k of 1/k² = 1, so 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + 1/49 + ⋯ = π²/8 ≈ 1.2337. That they are is proved on rung 32. Until then the equality is held in trust, and the bound of 31h2 is all that is proved here.

31h4Closest points in a space with no holes

31b found closest points in flat pieces built by finitely many arrows. A flat piece of a function space may need endlessly many. From here on, a space W inside a product space is closed when every list in W that settles settles at an arrow of W.

Complete-closest technique: if W is a closed space inside a complete product space, every f has exactly one closest point w in W, and f − w is at right angles to every arrow of W.

d = \inf_{v \in W}\|f - v\|,\ \ w_n \in W,\ \ \|f - w_n\| \to d @@ a greatest lower bound (8c)
\|w_n - w_m\|^2 = 2\|f - w_n\|^2 + 2\|f - w_m\|^2 - 4\big\|f - \tfrac{w_n + w_m}{2}\big\|^2 @@ the parallelogram line of 31a4, for f − wₙ and f − wₘ
\le 2\|f - w_n\|^2 + 2\|f - w_m\|^2 - 4d^2 \to 0 @@ the middle point is in W, so at least d away
w_n \to w \in W,\ \ \|f - w\| = d @@ the list crowds; the space is complete and W closed
v \in W,\ \langle f - w, v\rangle \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{the shadow of } f - w \text{ on } v \text{ is closer than } d @@ the closest-point technique (31b1): w plus that shadow is in W
\htmlClass{new}{f - w \perp W,\ \text{and } w \text{ is the only closest point}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two closest points: the same parallelogram line makes their distance 0

The proof used the parallelogram line, which only sizes from a product obey (31g1). In a space with a size and no product, closest points can fail to be one, or fail to exist. Completeness was spent once: to find the point that the nearer and nearer arrows crowd towards.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A complete product space is a Hilbert space; one with holes is a pre-Hilbert space. ℓ² is the sequence space little-ell-two. A right-angled basis is an orthonormal basis (or complete orthonormal system), and the readings are the Fourier coefficients of f with respect to it. The energy-bound technique is Bessel’s inequality, and the energy-sum technique Parseval’s identity (or Parseval’s theorem, or the Plancherel formula in its later form). That every Hilbert space with a countable orthonormal basis is ℓ² in disguise is the Riesz–Fischer theorem in its second form. The complete-closest technique is the projection theorem (or Hilbert projection theorem). The sum 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + ⋯ = π²/8 is a form of the Basel problem.

Who said it first

Friedrich Bessel found his inequality for sums of sines in 1828, and Marc-Antoine Parseval stated the equality in 1799, without proof. David Hilbert built ℓ² between 1904 and 1910 while studying integral equations, and John von Neumann named the abstract space after him in 1929. Leonhard Euler found Σ1/k² = π²/6 in 1735, by a step he could not then justify.

A proof that is not allowed

“The readings of f are all 0, so f = 0.” Not allowed until the list is known to be a basis. On rung 32 the sines and cosines will be shown to leave no direction out; before that, a list may miss a direction, and an arrow along it reads 0 everywhere.
