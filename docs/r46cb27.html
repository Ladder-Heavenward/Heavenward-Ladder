46·cb27 · newsflash for 27

Groups as Matrices

Rung 27 found that the grids that can be undone form a group, and rung 19 found groups wherever something moves. Put the two together the other way round: send each member of a group to a grid, so that doing two members is multiplying their grids. A group can then be studied by straight algebra. This page shows that every such sending of a finite group comes apart into pieces that cannot be split further, by an averaging trick that makes every grid keep lengths. It proves that between two pieces that cannot be split, the only maps that respect the group are nothing or a matching, and on one piece, a stretch. And it reads each piece by one number per member, the sum down the grid’s main diagonal, which stays the same on a conjugate family. Those numbers are perpendicular, like the turnings of rung 32, and a table of them tells everything about the pieces. The last footing is chemistry: the shakes of a water molecule, counted and sorted by its symmetries.

A representation sends each member of a group to an undoable grid, products to products. Averaging over the group gives an inner product every grid keeps, so every representation splits into pieces that cannot be split. A map between two such pieces that respects the group is nothing or undoable, and on one piece a stretch of every vector by one number. Traces, one per family, are perpendicular, and they sort the pieces.

46·cb27.1A Group Sent to Grids

A representation of a group G on a space of vectors V over ℂ, of dimension n, is a map ρ from G to the undoable n × n grids of 27d with ρ(gh) = ρ(g)ρ(h). A space W inside V is kept when ρ(g) sends W into W for every g. A representation is whole when its only kept spaces are 0 and V. Its accepted name is a representation, from the Latin repraesentare, to make present.

\text{one: } \rho(g) = (1) @@ every member to the 1 × 1 grid 1
\text{sign: } \rho(g) = (\pm1) @@ +1 for even shuffles, −1 for odd (19e); signs multiply
\text{shuffle: } \rho(g)e_i = e_{g(i)} \text{ on } \mathbb C^3 @@ each shuffle moves the three unit vectors as it moves 1, 2, 3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the shuffle representation keeps } L = \{(t, t, t)\} \text{ and } W = \{x + y + z = 0\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ shuffling the entries keeps them all equal, and keeps their sum

On W, with the turn by a third and a flip of the triangle of 19f as grids, the shuffle representation is the triangle’s own moves in the plane. It is whole: a kept line would be a line every turn by 120° sends to itself, and no such line exists.

46·cb27.2Every Representation Splits

Splitting technique: every representation of a finite group is a sum of whole representations.

\langle u, v\rangle_G = \tfrac{1}{|G|}\textstyle\sum_{g \in G}\langle \rho(g)u, \rho(g)v\rangle @@ start from any inner product of 31a, and average
\langle \rho(h)u, \rho(h)v\rangle_G = \langle u, v\rangle_G @@ as g runs over G, gh does too (19b)
W \text{ kept} \Rightarrow W^\perp = \{v : \langle v, w\rangle_G = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\} \text{ kept} @@ ⟨ρ(h)v, w⟩_G = ⟨v, ρ(h⁻¹)w⟩_G = 0, since ρ(h⁻¹)w is in W
V = W \oplus W^\perp @@ 31b’s closest point: every v is its shadow on W plus the rest
\htmlClass{new}{\text{split again until every piece is whole}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the dimension falls each time

For the shuffle representation of S₃, the ordinary inner product is already kept by every shuffle, and W is the perpendicular of L. The averaging is needed when a representation comes with grids that stretch. Over a finite group it always works; for the turns of a line by every real amount it is an integral, and for the slides of the line it fails, since a slide cannot be averaged.

46·cb27.3Maps Between Whole Pieces

A straight map T from V to V′ respects G when T ρ(g) = ρ′(g) T for every g.

One-stretch technique: if V and V′ are whole and T respects G, then T is 0 or undoable; and if V′ = V, then T is a multiple of the identity.

\text{what } T \text{ loses is kept by } \rho,\ \text{what it reaches is kept by } \rho' @@ Tv = 0 ⇒ Tρ(g)v = ρ′(g)Tv = 0; and ρ′(g)Tv = T(ρ(g)v)
\text{each is } 0 \text{ or all} @@ both whole
T \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{loses only } 0 \text{ and reaches all: undoable} @@ 27c
V' = V:\ \ T \text{ has a stretch } \lambda @@ 27f: det(T − λI) is a polynomial with a root in ℂ (25)
T - \lambda I \text{ respects } G \text{ and is not undoable} @@ det = 0
\htmlClass{new}{T - \lambda I = 0,\ \ T = \lambda I} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first part

So a whole representation of a turn-about group has dimension 1: every ρ(h) respects G, so each is a multiple of the identity, and every line is kept.

46·cb27.4Traces Sort the Pieces

The trace of a square grid is the sum of its main diagonal, and trace(AB) = trace(BA). The character of ρ is χ(g) = trace ρ(g). It is the same on a conjugate family: trace(ρ(h)ρ(g)ρ(h)⁻¹) = trace ρ(g).

Perpendicular-characters technique: for whole representations, (1/|G|) Σ χ(g) times the flip of χ′(g) is 1 when they match and 0 when they do not. The proof averages a straight map over G to make one that respects it, and applies the one-stretch technique; it is held in trust here, and the table below checks it.

\text{families: } e\ (1),\ \text{swaps}\ (3),\ \text{3-loops}\ (2) @@ 46f1: one family per loop shape
\chi_{\text{one}} = (1, 1, 1),\ \ \chi_{\text{sign}} = (1, -1, 1),\ \ \chi_W = (2, 0, -1) @@ W: the identity, a flip of the plane (stretches 1, −1), a turn by 120° (2 cos 120°)
\tfrac16(1\cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1)) = 0,\ \ \tfrac16(4 + 0 + 2) = 1 @@ one against W, and W against itself
\chi_{\mathbb C^3} = (3, 1, 0) = \chi_{\text{one}} + \chi_W @@ fixed points: 3, 1, 0
\htmlClass{new}{1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6 = |S_3|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sizes of the whole pieces, squared, add to the size of the group

Like 32a’s coefficients, the amount of a whole piece in any representation is its projection: ⟨χ_V, χ_W⟩ counts how many copies of W are in V. That is how a physicist reads off what a symmetric system can do, without solving it.

46·cb27.5How a Water Molecule Shakes

Water is bent: an oxygen with two hydrogens at 104.5°. Its symmetries, as 19·chem counted moves of molecules, are four: e; the half turn C about the line that halves the angle; the flip σ across the molecule’s plane; the flip σ′ across the plane that holds the axis and is perpendicular to the molecule. The group is turn-about, so its whole pieces have dimension 1, and there are four: A₁ (1, 1, 1, 1), A₂ (1, 1, −1, −1), B₁ (1, −1, 1, −1), B₂ (1, −1, −1, 1), in the order e, C, σ, σ′.

\text{each atom moves in 3 directions: a representation of dimension } 9 @@ three atoms
\chi = (9, -1, 3, 1) @@ only atoms left in place count: 3 · 3; 1 · (−1); 3 · 1; 1 · 1 (a half turn reverses two directions; a flip one)
\text{copies: } A_1: \tfrac{9 - 1 + 3 + 1}{4} = 3,\ A_2: 1,\ B_1: 3,\ B_2: 2 @@ the projection of the perpendicular-characters technique
\text{slides of the whole: } A_1 + B_1 + B_2;\ \ \text{turns of the whole: } A_2 + B_1 + B_2 @@ the three directions of space, and the three axes of turning, read the same way
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shakes: } 2A_1 + B_1}:\ \text{three, all seen in infrared} \quad \blacksquare @@ 9 − 3 − 3 = 3; each moves the molecule’s charge like a slide, so light can drive it

Measured, the three shakes of water vapour lie at 1595, 3657 and 3756 waves per centimetre: the bend and the stretch in A₁, and the lopsided stretch in B₁. Carbon dioxide, straight, has a stretch that keeps its centre and moves no charge, and infrared light cannot drive it; that is one reason the gases of the air differ in how they trap heat.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A whole representation is irreducible; a map that respects G is an intertwiner or G-map. The splitting technique is Maschke’s theorem, and the averaged inner product is Weyl’s unitary trick. The one-stretch technique is Schur’s lemma. The perpendicular-characters technique is the orthogonality of characters, and the table is a character table. Water’s group is the point group C₂ᵥ; A₁, A₂, B₁, B₂ are Mulliken’s labels, and the count of shakes is the method of normal-mode analysis by group theory; a shake that moves the charge is infrared active.

Who said it first

Georg Frobenius defined characters of finite groups in 1896, from a question Richard Dedekind put to him in letters. Heinrich Maschke proved the splitting technique in 1898 and Issai Schur the lemma in 1905. William Burnside and Frobenius built much of the theory around 1900. Hermann Weyl and Eugene Wigner brought it into quantum mechanics from 1927, and the chemistry of molecular shakes by characters was laid out by E. Bright Wilson in the 1930s.

A proof that is not allowed

“ρ(h) respects G, so by the one-stretch technique every ρ(h) is a multiple of the identity, in every representation.” Not allowed: ρ(h) respects G only when it commutes with every ρ(g), which is so when G is turn-about. For S₃ on W, the turn by 120° is not a multiple of the identity.
