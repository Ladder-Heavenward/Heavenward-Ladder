46e · proof

Groups Taken Apart

Rung 4 took every whole number apart into primes, in only one way. This step does the same for finite groups. A group is cut along a normal part-group into two smaller groups, the part and the quotient, and each of those is cut again, until no piece can be cut. The pieces that cannot be cut are the simple groups. Some groups come apart into pieces that are all clock groups of prime size, and those are the groups that the rest of the rung needs; they are called solvable, for a reason the next steps make plain. The step proves that being solvable passes to parts and quotients and back, that every finite group has a list of pieces, and that the list is the same however the cutting is done, as the prime list of a number is. It does not say that the pieces fix the group. Two different groups can share their pieces, as the step shows.

A group is solvable when a chain of normal steps takes it down to the identity with every step turn-about. Parts and quotients of a solvable group are solvable, and a group is solvable when a normal part and its quotient are. Every finite group has a chain whose steps are simple, and any two such chains have the same pieces.

46e1Solvable Groups

From here on, H ◁ G says that H is a normal part-group of G (43·cb19): gHg⁻¹ = H for every g in G. A group is solvable when it has a chain {e} = G₀ ◁ G₁ ◁ ⋯ ◁ Gₖ = G, each a normal part-group of the next (not necessarily of G), with every quotient Gᵢ₊₁/Gᵢ turn-about (19c). Its accepted name, solvable, comes from 46g.

G \text{ turn-about}:\ \ \{e\} \lhd G @@ every part-group of a turn-about group is normal, and G/{e} is G
S_3:\ \ \{e\} \lhd A_3 \lhd S_3,\ \ \text{quotients of sizes } 3, 2 @@ A₃ has half the members (19e), so it is normal; groups of prime size are clocks, turn-about
V = \{e, (1\ 2)(3\ 4), (1\ 3)(2\ 4), (1\ 4)(2\ 3)\} \lhd S_4 @@ conjugating a double swap gives a double swap, and these are all of them
\htmlClass{new}{S_4:\ \ \{e\} \lhd V \lhd A_4 \lhd S_4,\ \ \text{quotients of sizes } 4, 3, 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ V is turn-about (each member is its own undo, and the product of two is the third); sizes 3 and 2 are prime

So S₃ and S₄ are solvable, and so is every group of 46c’s first footings. The group of x³ − 2 comes apart as a clock of 3 under a clock of 2.

46e2Parts and Quotients Stay Solvable

Solvable-parts technique: a part-group of a solvable group is solvable; a quotient of a solvable group is solvable; and if N ◁ G with N and G/N solvable, then G is solvable.

H \subseteq G:\ \ H_i = H \cap G_i,\ \ H_i \lhd H_{i+1} @@ conjugating by h in Hᵢ₊₁ keeps H and keeps Gᵢ
H_{i+1}/H_i \to G_{i+1}/G_i,\ \ hH_i \mapsto hG_i \text{ is one-to-one} @@ hGᵢ = Gᵢ with h in H exactly when h is in Hᵢ; the second isomorphism technique (43·cb19)
\text{a part of a turn-about group is turn-about} @@ so H’s chain has turn-about steps
G/N:\ \ G_iN/N,\ \ \text{each step an image of } G_{i+1}/G_i @@ gGᵢ ↦ gGᵢN, from Gᵢ₊₁/Gᵢ onto Gᵢ₊₁N/GᵢN, keeps products; an image of a turn-about group is turn-about
N, G/N \text{ solvable}: \text{ the chain of } N, \text{ then the chain of } G/N \text{ lifted} @@ the part-groups of G/N are M/N with N ⊆ M (43·cb19), and (M′/N)/(M/N) matches M′/M
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all three}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each chain has turn-about steps

With this, being solvable is a property that can be read off pieces. The pieces are the next footing.

46e3Pieces That Cannot Be Cut

A group S with more than one member is simple when its only normal part-groups are {e} and S. A chain of pieces of a finite group G is a chain {e} = G₀ ◁ ⋯ ◁ Gₖ = G in which every quotient Gᵢ₊₁/Gᵢ is simple; those quotients are its pieces.

Pieces technique: every finite group with more than one member has a chain of pieces; and a finite group is solvable exactly when its pieces are all clocks of prime size.

G \ne \{e\}:\ \text{take } M \lhd G,\ M \ne G, \text{ as large as possible} @@ {e} is one; finitely many choices
G/M \text{ is simple} @@ a normal part of G/M is N/M with M ⊆ N ◁ G (43·cb19), so N is M or G
M \text{ has a chain of pieces; add } G @@ induction on the size (19d: |M| < |G|)
\text{a turn-about simple group has prime size} @@ each member makes a normal part-group; a member of order mp gives one of order p
\text{solvable: refine each turn-about step into prime steps} @@ a turn-about group of size n has a part-group of prime size p, and its quotient has size n/p
\htmlClass{new}{\text{solvable} \iff \text{every piece is a clock of prime size}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and the other way: clock pieces are turn-about steps

46e4The Pieces Are Fixed

Same-pieces technique: any two chains of pieces of a finite group have the same pieces, the same number of times each, in some order.

\text{chains } \cdots \lhd M \lhd G \text{ and } \cdots \lhd N \lhd G,\ \ M \ne N @@ induction on |G|; if M = N, the chains of M agree by induction
MN = G @@ MN is normal in G and holds M; G/M is simple, so MN is M or G, and it is not M, since then N ⊆ M, and G/N simple forces N = M
L = M \cap N:\ \ G/M = MN/M \cong N/L,\ \ G/N \cong M/L @@ the second isomorphism technique (43·cb19)
\text{take a chain of pieces of } L @@ the pieces technique
M \text{ has two chains: its own, and } L \text{'s with } M/L \text{ on top} @@ M/L ≅ G/N is simple
\text{by induction they have the same pieces; so do the two for } N @@ |M|, |N| < |G|
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both chains of } G \text{ have the pieces of } L,\ G/M,\ G/N} \quad \blacksquare @@ add G/M on one side and G/N on the other
  
\mathbb Z/6:\ \ \{0\} \lhd \{0, 3\} \lhd \mathbb Z/6,\ \ \text{pieces of sizes } 2, 3 @@ the quotient has 3 members
\mathbb Z/6:\ \ \{0\} \lhd \{0, 2, 4\} \lhd \mathbb Z/6,\ \ \text{pieces of sizes } 3, 2 @@ the other order
\htmlClass{new}{S_3 \text{ has pieces of sizes } 2, 3 \text{ too}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same pieces, and a different group

The pieces are fixed, like the primes of a number in 4j. Unlike the primes, they do not fix what they build: ℤ/6 and S₃ share them, and so do ℤ/4 and V. How pieces can be put together is a separate question, and a hard one.

The accepted names

A chain with normal steps is a subnormal series; with turn-about quotients it shows G is solvable. A chain of pieces is a composition series, and its pieces are the composition factors. The same-pieces technique is the Jordan–Hölder theorem. The proof’s first step uses a maximal normal subgroup. The part-group G′ of 46e.6.4 is the commutator subgroup, and G⁽ᵏ⁾ the derived series. A turn-about group is abelian.

Who said it first

Évariste Galois (1831) singled out the equations whose groups come apart in steps of prime size. Camille Jordan proved in 1869–70 that the sizes of the pieces do not depend on the chain, and Otto Hölder in 1889 that the pieces themselves do not, using quotient groups, which he introduced in the form used here. The name solvable comes from equations solvable by radicals, 46g.

A proof that is not allowed

“A part-group H of a solvable G is solvable: take G’s chain and keep the members in H. The steps are the same steps, so they are turn-about.” Not allowed as it stands: the steps of H’s chain are Hᵢ₊₁/Hᵢ, not Gᵢ₊₁/Gᵢ. They are turn-about because they sit one-to-one inside Gᵢ₊₁/Gᵢ, which needs the second isomorphism technique.
