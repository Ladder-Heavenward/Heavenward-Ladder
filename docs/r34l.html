Winding Numbers by Hand

Finn, a dog on a leash, runs circles round a lamp post while his owner walks once round the block. How many times did Finn go round the post? 34g gave the answer a name, the winding number, and proved it is a whole number that bending the path cannot change. This step computes it by hand, three ways. Draw a ray from the point and count the crossings, with signs. Total a fraction round the loop, as rung 26 did. Or send the loop through a polynomial and count how often the image goes round 0, which counts the roots inside without finding any of them. The last way comes with a rule for leashes: if the dog never gets as far from its owner as the owner is from the post, both go round the post the same number of times. With it, every polynomial has as many roots as its degree, in one more line.

The winding number of a loop round a point is the signed count of its crossings of any ray from the point, when the crossings are clean. It is also the total of one over z minus the point, round the loop, divided by two pi i. A polynomial’s roots inside a circle are counted by how many times the circle’s image winds round zero. And if a second loop stays nearer to the first than the first is to the point, the two wind the same number of times.

34l1Counting Crossings of a Ray

For a loop γ in the plane that misses the point w, its winding number round w, w(γ, w), is 34g4’s winding number of the loop (γ − w)/|γ − w| on the circle. It counts how many times γ goes round w, anticlockwise positive.

Ray-count technique: if γ crosses a ray from w finitely often, each time cleanly from one side to the other, then w(γ, w) is the number of anticlockwise crossings minus the number of clockwise ones.

34l2Winding as a Total

Winding-total technique: if γ has a continuous slope and misses w, then w(γ, w) = (1/2πi) ∮ dz/(z − w) round γ.

34l3Counting Roots Inside a Circle

Image-winding technique: if a polynomial p has no root on the circle C, then the number of its roots inside C, counted with repeats, is the winding number of the loop p(C) round 0.

\#\text{roots inside } C = \tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \tfrac{p'(z)}{p(z)}\,dz @@ the root-count technique (26h)
= \tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\textstyle\int_0^1 \tfrac{p'(\gamma(t))\,\gamma'(t)}{p(\gamma(t))}\,dt @@ γ runs once round C
= \tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\textstyle\int_0^1 \tfrac{(p\circ\gamma)'(t)}{(p\circ\gamma)(t)}\,dt @@ the chain rule
\htmlClass{new}{= w(p \circ \gamma, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the winding-total technique, for the loop p ∘ γ round 0

Leash technique: if two loops γ and δ miss w, and |δ(t) − γ(t)| < |γ(t) − w| for every t, then w(δ, w) = w(γ, w). The straight slide (1 − s)γ + sδ moves each point less than its distance from w, so it never passes through w, and 34g3’s slide-lift keeps the winding number. The dog is δ, the owner γ, the post w.

|z| = 2:\ \ |3z + 1| \le 7 < 32 = |z^5| @@ now the owner is z⁵

Standing questions

Harder questions that rely on your own making. The step is complete without them; the rung's angel wings need them.

34l.5.1buildFind how many roots p has inside |z| = 1 and inside |z| = 2, by the leash technique. Find a ring between two circles that holds exactly two of the roots.
Check with the in-between-value technique that all three roots are real.
34l.5.2edgeFind the winding number round every point the loop misses. Shade the plane by it.
Find the points where the winding number changes, and by how much it changes as you cross the loop.
34l.5.3converseIf a loop winds round no point, must it slide to a point in the plane with the points it misses taken out? Prove it when one point p is taken out.
Then take out two points p and q, and test the loop that runs round p anticlockwise, round q anticlockwise, round p clockwise and round q clockwise.
34l.5.4rerouteProve that w(γ, w) is the same for all w in one piece of the plane with the loop taken out, twice: once by the leash technique, and once by the winding-total technique and continuity.

One side holds that a root is known when a number can be computed to any accuracy. The other holds that a root is known when its existence and count are proved.

The accepted names

The winding number is the index, Ind_γ(w) or n(γ, w). The image-winding technique is the argument principle, and the leash technique is Rouché’s theorem, often taught as the dog on a leash. The check of the seam standing question is the Nyquist stability criterion.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy counted roots by integrals in the 1830s, and Eugène Rouché stated the leash in 1862. Harry Nyquist used the winding of a loop to test whether a feedback amplifier would sing, at Bell Labs in 1932. The dog on a leash appears in many textbooks of the twentieth century.

A proof that is not allowed

“z⁵ + 3z + 1 has five roots, so five lie in the unit disc.” Not allowed: the count of roots inside a circle depends on the circle. On |z| = 1 the term 3z is the owner, and only one root lies inside.
