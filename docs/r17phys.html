Is Light a Dot or a Flow?

Is light a dot or a flow? A hot body glows, and the colour of the glow is set by the temperature alone: a stove element goes dull red, then orange, and the Sun is nearly white. By 1900 physics had a rule for how much light of each wavelength a hot body gives off, built on waves and on rung 16's areas. The rule gave an endless area. In 1900 a rule with a finite area was found, by supposing that light's energy comes in lumps. This page works both rules with 16i's improper integrals and 17's exp. It finds the peak colour by 14's method, and gets the fourth power of temperature by a substitution. Then it turns the result on the Earth: how warm a planet must be to send back what the Sun sends it, and what a layer of gas does to that.

Light comes in lumps, each carrying energy in proportion to its frequency. Any amount of energy per wave averages kT; lumps of hf average hf/(ehf/kT − 1). The old law for a glowing body’s light has endless area. The lump law has a finite one, growing as the fourth power of the temperature. The brightest colour’s wavelength times the temperature is fixed. A bare planet warms until the heat it glows away matches the sunlight it keeps. One layer of air that holds some heat back raises the fourth power of its temperature by a fixed factor.

Light as a wave

Light travels as a wave at c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s. A wave has a length λ, the distance from one crest to the next, and a frequency f, the number of crests passing a point each second, so fλ = c. Violet light has λ near 400 nm (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m), red near 700 nm. Longer waves, which the eye cannot see, have the accepted name infrared; shorter ones, ultraviolet. The wave picture explains why light passed through two narrow slits makes bands of light and dark, where crests meet crests or meet troughs.

The classical count, and its endless area

Inside a hot oven, light bounces around until it is in balance with the walls. Count the waves that fit inside. Per unit volume and per unit of wavelength near λ, there are C/λ⁴ of them, for a fixed number C that comes from counting. In a body at temperature T, physics before 1900 gave every wave the same average energy, kT. Here k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K is a fixed number of nature; its accepted name is Boltzmann's constant. So the energy per unit volume per unit wavelength would be u(λ) = C kT/λ⁴. This is the classical glow formula (the Rayleigh–Jeans law).

Endless-glow technique: for every T > 0 and every λ₁ > 0, ∫₀^λ₁ C kT λ⁻⁴ dλ diverges. So the total energy ∫₀^∞ u(λ) dλ is not a number.

\textstyle\int_\delta^{\lambda_1} \lambda^{-4}\,d\lambda = \tfrac13\big(\delta^{-3} - \lambda_1^{-3}\big) @@ real-power for slopes, run backwards; change-for-any-integrable (16c)
\to \infty \text{ as } \delta \to 0^+ @@ δ⁻³ passes every bound; in-to-0 with p = 4 ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^{\lambda_1} C kT \lambda^{-4}\,d\lambda \text{ diverges}} \quad \blacksquare @@ times the positive constant CkT

The fault is at the short waves, the violet end and past it. Every oven would hold endless energy in its ultraviolet. In 1911 this was given the accepted name the ultraviolet catastrophe. Measured glows show nothing of the kind: they rise to a peak and fall away to 0 at short waves.

Energy in lumps

The lump supposition, in 1900: a wave of frequency f can hold energy only in whole lumps of hf, so 0, hf, 2hf, 3hf, and so on. Here h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is a new fixed number of nature; its accepted name is Planck's constant. In 1905 it was proposed that the light itself travels as lumps. One lump of light has the accepted name a photon, and its energy is E = hf = hc/λ.

In a body at temperature T, a state of energy E turns up with weight e−E/kT (the Boltzmann factor). The average energy is the weighted average: the sum (or area) of E times the weight, over the sum (or area) of the weights.

Lumpy-average technique: if a wave may hold any energy E ≥ 0, its average is kT. If it may hold only nhf for whole n ≥ 0, its average is hf/(ehf/kT − 1), which is less than kT and heads to kT as hf/kT → 0.

\textstyle\int_0^\infty e^{-E/kT}\,dE = kT @@ −kT e^(−E/kT) is an antiderivative; e^(−E/kT) → 0 as E → ∞ (adding-becomes-multiplying, log-unbounded)
\textstyle\int_0^\infty E\,e^{-E/kT}\,dE = (kT)^2 @@ parts for areas: E e^(−E/kT) → 0 (exponentials-pass-powers), leaving kT times the line above
\text{average} = \tfrac{(kT)^2}{kT} = kT @@ the weighted average
  
q = e^{-hf/kT},\ \ 0 \lt q \lt 1 @@ hf/kT > 0 and exp rises
\textstyle\sum_{n \ge 0} q^n = \tfrac{1}{1 - q} @@ endless-ratio-sum (15i)
(1 - q)\sum_{n \lt N} nq^n = \tfrac{q - q^N}{1 - q} - (N - 1)q^N @@ subtract the sum shifted one place; finite-ratio-sum (15i)
Nq^N \to 0,\ \ q^N \to 0 @@ ln(Nq^N) = N(ln N / N − ln(1/q)) → −∞, by log-loses-to-powers; small-powers-vanish
\textstyle\sum_{n \ge 0} nq^n = \tfrac{q}{(1 - q)^2} @@ the partial sums head there
\text{average} = hf \cdot \tfrac{q/(1 - q)^2}{1/(1 - q)} = \tfrac{hf\,q}{1 - q} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{hf}{e^{hf/kT} - 1}} @@ divide top and bottom by q
x = \tfrac{hf}{kT}:\ \ \tfrac{hf}{e^{x} - 1} = kT \cdot \tfrac{x}{e^x - 1} \lt kT @@ eˣ − 1 > x for x > 0: log-bounds at eˣ, strict away from 1
\tfrac{x}{e^x - 1} \to 1 \text{ as } x \to 0^+ \quad \blacksquare @@ (eˣ − 1)/x is a chord slope of exp at 0, heading to exp′(0) = 1 (slope-of-exp)

So low frequencies, long waves, behave classically, and high frequencies are starved. A lump of hf much larger than kT is almost never paid out. Put the lumpy average in place of kT, with f = c/λ:

u(λ) = C λ⁻⁴ · (hc/λ)/(ehc/λkT − 1) = C hc λ⁻⁵/(ehc/λkT − 1). This is the lump law of glow; its accepted name is Planck's law.

The lump law's area is finite, and grows as T⁴

Finite-glow technique: I = ∫₀^∞ x³/(eˣ − 1) dx converges.

0 \lt x \le 1:\ \ \tfrac{x^3}{e^x - 1} \le \tfrac{x^3}{x} = x^2 @@ eˣ − 1 ≥ x (log-bounds); the value at 0 is taken as 0, so it is continuous on [0, 1] (continuous-is-integrable)
e^x = \big(e^{x/5}\big)^5 \ge \big(1 + \tfrac x5\big)^5 \gt \tfrac{x^5}{5^5} @@ adding-becomes-multiplying; log-bounds at x/5
x \ge 1:\ \ e^x - 1 \ge \tfrac{e^x}{2} @@ eˣ ≥ e > 2 (e-bounds)
\tfrac{x^3}{e^x - 1} \le \tfrac{2 \cdot 5^5 x^3}{x^5} = \tfrac{6250}{x^2} @@ the last two lines
\textstyle\int_1^\infty 6250\,x^{-2}\,dx = 6250 @@ out-to-forever with p = 2
\htmlClass{new}{I \text{ converges}} \quad \blacksquare @@ comparison for areas on [1, ∞); the piece on [0, 1] is an ordinary integral

Squeezing the sums gives I ≈ 6.494. Later rungs find its exact value.

Fourth-power technique: ∫₀^∞ C hc λ⁻⁵/(ehc/λkT − 1) dλ = C (kT)⁴ I/(hc)³. So the total energy of the glow is a constant times T⁴.

x = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda kT},\ \ \lambda = \tfrac{hc}{kT}\,x^{-1},\ \ d\lambda = -\tfrac{hc}{kT}\,x^{-2}\,dx @@ substitution for areas (16d) on each [λ₁, λ₂], then the limits
\lambda^{-5} = \big(\tfrac{kT}{hc}\big)^5 x^5 @@ real-power for slopes
C hc \big(\tfrac{kT}{hc}\big)^5 \tfrac{hc}{kT} \textstyle\int_0^\infty \tfrac{x^5 \cdot x^{-2}}{e^x - 1}\,dx @@ λ: 0 → ∞ is x: ∞ → 0; the minus sign turns the ends round
= \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{C (kT)^4}{(hc)^3}\,I} \quad \blacksquare @@ finite-glow

The power given off by each square metre of a glowing surface is then σT⁴, with σ = 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. This is the fourth-power law of glow (the Stefan–Boltzmann law). It was read from measurements in 1879, and the fourth power was derived in 1884. That was sixteen years before the lump law gave σ in terms of h, c and k. Double the temperature and the glow grows sixteenfold.

The peak

Peak-moves technique: for each T, u(λ) is greatest at λ = hc/(x*kT), where x* ≈ 4.965 is the one positive solution of 5(1 − e−x) = x. So λmax T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K.

u = C hc \big(\tfrac{kT}{hc}\big)^5 g(x),\ \ g(x) = \tfrac{x^5}{e^x - 1} @@ the substitution of fourth-power; x falls as λ rises, one to one
g'(x) = \tfrac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}\,\big(5(1 - e^{-x}) - x\big) @@ quotient for slopes (13f); take x⁴eˣ out of 5x⁴(eˣ − 1) − x⁵eˣ
w(x) = 5(1 - e^{-x}) - x:\ \ w(0) = 0,\ \ w' = 5e^{-x} - 1 @@ w rises until x = ln 5, then falls for good
w(4) \approx 0.908 \gt 0,\ \ w(5) \approx -0.034 \lt 0 @@ in-between-value: a zero x* in (4, 5); one only, since w falls past ln 5
x^* \approx 4.965 @@ halving-traps (12e), halving
g' \gt 0 \text{ before } x^*,\ \lt 0 \text{ after} @@ first-test (14f): the top of g, so of u
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda_{\max} T = \tfrac{hc}{k x^*} \approx 2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}} \quad \blacksquare @@ hc/k = 0.014388 m·K

This is the peak law (Wien's displacement law). Sunlight peaks near 502 nm, in the green, so the Sun's surface is near 2.898 × 10⁻³/5.02 × 10⁻⁷ ≈ 5770 K. The Earth's surface, near 288 K, peaks at about 10 μm, deep in the infrared: the Earth glows too, invisibly.

The man who made the lump supposition later wrote that it was an act of desperation. He had to find a theoretical explanation at any price. The lumps were a purely formal assumption to which he gave little thought, beyond this: he had to get a positive result, however high the cost.

Wave or lump: the photoelectric effect

Light falling on a metal can knock electrons out of it. Experiments from 1902 onward found three facts the wave picture could not explain. Below some frequency no electrons come out, however bright the light. Above it, brighter light frees more electrons but not faster ones. And they come out at once, even in dim light, with no wait for energy to build up.

Photoelectric technique: let each photon give its whole energy hf to one electron, and let the electron need at least φ to leave the metal. Then the most energy a freed electron can have is Kmax = hf − φ. No electron is freed when f < φ/h.

K = hf - (\text{energy spent leaving}) @@ energy is kept (16·phys)
\text{energy spent leaving} \ge \varphi @@ φ has the accepted name the work function of the metal
\htmlClass{new}{K_{\max} = hf - \varphi};\ \ f \lt \tfrac{\varphi}{h} \Rightarrow \text{none} \quad \blacksquare @@ the least cost gives the most left over; one photon to one electron, so brightness counts electrons
  
1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} @@ its accepted name is the electronvolt, a unit sized for one electron
\lambda = 400\ \text{nm}:\ \ E = \tfrac{hc}{\lambda} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 3.10\ \text{eV} @@ violet
\varphi_{\text{K}} \approx 2.29\ \text{eV}:\ \ K_{\max} \approx 0.81\ \text{eV} @@ photoelectric
\lambda = 700\ \text{nm}:\ \ E = 1.77\ \text{eV} \lt \varphi_{\text{K}} @@ red frees nothing, however bright
N_A \cdot 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \approx 299\ \text{kJ per mole of photons} @@ at 300 nm, 399 kJ/mol: above the 348 kJ/mol of a C–C bond, so ultraviolet can break the molecules of life

From 1914 to 1916, Kmax was measured against f for several metals. The results were straight lines, whose slope gave h to within half a percent of the value from the glow. Light shows wave behaviour in the slits and lump behaviour on the metal. The accepted name for this double behaviour is wave–particle duality.

A planet in balance

Sunlight arrives at the Earth's distance with S = 1361 W/m² on a surface facing the Sun. Averaged over the whole globe, day and night and every latitude, each square metre gets S/4. A fraction α of it is sent back by cloud, ice and ground; its accepted name is albedo, and for the Earth α ≈ 0.30. In balance, a planet gives off as much as it takes in.

Bare-planet technique: a planet with no atmosphere, glowing by the fourth-power law, is in balance at Te = (S(1 − α)/(4σ))1/4.

\sigma T_e^4 = \tfrac{S(1 - \alpha)}{4} @@ out = in, per square metre of surface
\htmlClass{new}{T_e = \Big(\tfrac{S(1 - \alpha)}{4\sigma}\Big)^{1/4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fourth root: roots-of-every-order (8f) gives it, powers-keep-order makes it the only positive one
\tfrac{1361 \times 0.70}{4} \approx 238\ \text{W/m}^2,\ \ T_e \approx 255\ \text{K} @@ −18 °C
\text{measured mean surface} \approx 288\ \text{K} @@ +15 °C: 33 K warmer than the bare planet

The gap is the air. Water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane let most sunlight through, but they absorb infrared near 10 μm, where the ground glows. A layer that absorbs a fraction ε of the ground's infrared also gives off ε of a full glow at its own temperature, from both faces, up and down. That is a law of glow, taken here as given; its accepted name is Kirchhoff's law.

Greenhouse-layer technique: with such a layer, transparent to sunlight, in balance, the surface is at Ts = Te (2/(2 − ε))1/4.

\text{layer: } \varepsilon\sigma T_s^4 = 2\varepsilon\sigma T_a^4 @@ absorbs ε of the ground's glow, gives off εσTₐ⁴ from each face
\text{ground: } \tfrac{S(1 - \alpha)}{4} + \varepsilon\sigma T_a^4 = \sigma T_s^4 @@ sunlight plus the layer's downward glow
\sigma T_e^4 + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\sigma T_s^4 = \sigma T_s^4 @@ bare-planet; the layer line gives Tₐ⁴ = Tₛ⁴/2
\htmlClass{new}{T_s = T_e\Big(\tfrac{2}{2 - \varepsilon}\Big)^{1/4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve; the fourth root again
T_s = 288 \Rightarrow \varepsilon \approx 0.78;\ \ \varepsilon = 1 \Rightarrow T_s \approx 303\ \text{K} @@ a layer that absorbs all of it warms the ground by 2^(1/4)
\tfrac{T_s'}{T_s} = \tfrac{1}{4(2 - \varepsilon)} @@ log-slope: ln Tₛ = ln Tₑ + ¼(ln 2 − ln(2 − ε))
\Delta\varepsilon = 0.01 \Rightarrow \Delta T_s \approx \tfrac{288 \times 0.01}{4 \times 1.22} \approx 0.6\ \text{K} @@ the slope as a rate (13k)

Carbon dioxide in the air has risen from about 280 parts per million before 1750 to about 420 now. Measured another way, doubling it cuts the infrared escaping to space by about 3.7 W/m². The bare-planet slope (σT⁴)′ = 4σT³ ≈ 3.7 W m⁻² K⁻¹ at 255 K. It says the planet must warm by about 1 K to make that up. Warming adds water vapour, which absorbs more, and melts ice, which lowers α. Measurements and models put the full warming for a doubling likely between 2.5 and 4 K. The balance has three knobs, S, α and ε, and a change in any of them moves the climate.

Who said it first

Planck later wrote that what he did was an act of desperation: he had to find a theoretical explanation at any price, and the lumps were a purely formal assumption to which he gave little thought, beyond this, that he had to get a positive result however high the cost.

Max Planck, letter to Robert Williams Wood (1931); paraphrase

Einstein proposed that the energy of a beam of light is not spread out over ever larger volumes, but consists of a finite number of lumps of energy, located at points, which move without dividing and can be absorbed or given off only whole.

Albert Einstein, On a heuristic point of view concerning the production and transformation of light (1905); paraphrase

The lump supposition is Max Planck’s (1900), and the photon Albert Einstein’s (1905). The classical glow formula is Lord Rayleigh’s and James Jeans’s. Paul Ehrenfest named the ultraviolet catastrophe in 1911. Josef Stefan read the fourth power from measurements in 1879, and Ludwig Boltzmann derived it in 1884. The peak law is Wilhelm Wien’s. Philipp Lenard’s experiments of 1902 began the photoelectric facts, and Robert Millikan measured h from them, 1914 to 1916. The law that a good absorber is a good glower is Gustav Kirchhoff’s.

In 17·phys.4.4, the first physicist is Planck, the second Einstein, and the third Millikan.
