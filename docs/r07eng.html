Why Do Two Pumps Beat One?

Why do two pumps beat one? A town’s water comes up from a well through a pump that fails one day in ten. Put a second pump beside it, so either can do the job, and the water fails far less often. Put the second pump after the first, so both must run, and it fails more. Rung 7 fixed truth: each part works or fails, nothing in between. This page writes a system as the and or the or of its parts, counts how often each works with 4k’s counting, builds a fault tree from its gates, and finds the one assumption that makes the counting hold, and what breaks it.

A part either works or fails (7b). Parts in a row are joined by and: all must work. Parts side by side are joined by or: one is enough. Count equally likely days to get each chance; for parts that fail on their own, chances in a row multiply, and side by side the chances of failing multiply.

7eng1A System as the And or Or of Its Parts

Randy runs a small water works. Its pump lifts water from a well into a tank. Let P be “the pump works today”. By the either-way principle (7b), P is true or false each day, with nothing in between.

From here on, on this page, a system is a statement built from its parts’ statements with and and or (1c). Parts in a row, where water passes through each in turn, are joined by and. Parts side by side, where either one can do the job, are joined by or.

7eng2In a Row, All Must Work

Each pump fails one day in ten, and the two pumps fail on their own, neither causing nor sharing the other’s failures. Picture 10 equally likely days for pump 1 and 10 for pump 2: 100 equally likely pairs (4k). Pump 1 works on 9 of its days and pump 2 on 9 of its.

\text{pairs where both work: } 9 \times 9 = 81 @@ counting choices (4k)
\text{chance both work: } \tfrac{81}{100} @@ a ratio of counts
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{9}{10} \times \tfrac{9}{10} = 0.81} @@ in a row, the chances of working multiply

Two pumps in a row work 81 days in 100, worse than one pump’s 90. Every part added in a row is one more thing that can stop the water.

7eng3Side by Side, One Is Enough

Side by side, the water stops only when both pumps fail. Count the pairs where both fail, and take them away from all the pairs.

\text{pairs where both fail: } 1 \times 1 = 1 @@ each fails on 1 day in 10
\text{chance both fail: } \tfrac{1}{100} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chance the water flows: } 1 - \tfrac{1}{100} = 0.99} @@ one minus the chance all fail

Side by side, the water flows 99 days in 100, ten times fewer dry days than one pump. Side by side, the chances of failing multiply; in a row, the chances of working multiply. The second follows from the first by 1f’s pushing a not through: “not (P₁ or P₂)” is “not P₁ and not P₂”.

7eng4A Fault Tree

Randy’s works has more than pumps. The tank stays dry if both pumps fail, or if the power fails (both pumps run on it), or if the pipe to the tank breaks. Write the failure at the top and work down through gates.

\text{dry} \;\Leftrightarrow\; (\lnot P_1 \land \lnot P_2) \lor \lnot E \lor \lnot L @@ E: power works, L: pipe works
\text{chances of failing: pumps } \tfrac{1}{10} \text{ each, power } \tfrac{1}{50},\ \text{pipe } \tfrac{1}{200} @@ from Randy’s records
\text{both pumps fail: } \tfrac{1}{100} @@ the and gate
\text{nothing fails: } \tfrac{99}{100} \cdot \tfrac{49}{50} \cdot \tfrac{199}{200} \approx 0.965 @@ every input of the or gate works
\htmlClass{new}{\text{dry: about } 1 - 0.965 = 0.035} @@ about 1 day in 29

Fault-tree technique: write the failure as the top statement, join its causes by and gates (all needed) and or gates (any one is enough), and work the chances up from the bottom, for parts that fail on their own. The tree shows where the risk is: the two pumps now give 1 dry day in 100, while the single power line gives 1 in 50. A second pump was worth it; a second power line would be worth more.

7eng5A Shared Cause Breaks the Product

Every count above assumed the parts fail on their own: knowing one pump failed tells you nothing about the other. That is what made the 100 pairs equally likely.

Suppose the two pumps were bought together and share a fault that stops both in the same summer heat. Then the days pump 1 fails are mostly the days pump 2 fails. If they always failed together, “both fail” would happen 1 day in 10, not 1 in 100: the second pump would buy nothing.

Multiplying chances holds only for parts that fail on their own; a shared cause, such as the same power line, the same heat or the same maker’s fault, puts its own box in the tree, under an or gate above the parts it shares. That is why the power line got its own box in footing 4. Engineers make spare parts different on purpose: another maker, another power line, another room.

A reliability engineer reviewing Randy’s works, with a year of his failure records, finds the pumps failed together on 3 days and apart on 17, and puts a shared-cause box under the top gate with chance about 3 in 365.

Accepted names

Parts in a row are in series, side by side in parallel, and adding spare parts side by side is redundancy. The tree is a fault tree, its top statement the top event, its gates AND and OR gates. Parts that fail on their own are independent, and a shared cause is a common-cause failure. The chance a system works is its reliability.

Who said it first

H. A. Watson of Bell Telephone Laboratories developed fault tree analysis in 1961 and 1962, for the US Air Force’s Minuteman missile launch control system.
