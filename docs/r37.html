Deafening sound

Rung 36 found that an eye keeps three numbers of a spectrum and drops the rest. This rung turns to the ear, which keeps far more and drops other things. A sound is a change in the pressure of the air that runs through it at one speed, and rung 33’s wave equation is the rule it obeys. Three things belong to a steady sound: how often it repeats, which is its pitch; how much power it carries, which is its loudness; and its recipe of turnings, which is its quality. The rung follows a sound as it fades, bounces and passes into water, looks at a voice as a buzz shaped by a tube, and finds that a sound is a name, a set and an inductive object, as a colour was. It gives sound its own functions: filters, cancelling, compressors. It counts rhythms, speeds one up until it becomes a chord, and lets two players fall into step. And it ends with what can still be told about a sound after part of it has been lost on the way, which is more than you might think.

The gesture: a sound is a pressure that repeats, and the ear keeps its pitch, its loudness and its recipe but drops its turns and whatever a louder sound hides. What arrives is part of what was sent, and the part tells about the whole.

What it means to say it

A sound, as everyone meets it, is a thing heard. The claim of this rung is that the thing heard is a part of a pressure wave, kept by an ear that is deaf to the rest, and that the part kept still tells, exactly, part of what was sent. The deafness is the ear’s and the channel’s, not the air’s: the turns of a note, the band a phone line cuts, a quiet note beside a loud one, are all still in the air until something drops them. So the rung’s space is a human space again, built on human deafness, and it is exact anyway, because what is kept is a shadow, and shadows are exact.

Where it comes from

A plucked string was found to make whole multiples of one frequency, and pitch was found to be how often the air repeats. The speed of sound was worked out from the stiffness and weight of air, wrong at first by a sixth until it was seen that a squeeze is too quick for heat to leave. The ear was found to hear a steady note as its parts and to be nearly deaf to how they are lined up, and a voice was taken apart into a buzz and the tube it passes through. The pitch of a voice was heard on lines that carried none of its lowest turning, and that hearing is this rung’s end. The accepted names: the study is acoustics, the hearing of it psychoacoustics, and the recipe is timbre.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things, each from the ones before.

First, a sound is a pressure that obeys the wave equation, running at one speed through a medium whatever its shape; speed is frequency times wavelength.

Second, a sound that repeats has a pitch, the rate of its repeat, even when nothing at that rate is in it; the ear hears about ten octaves of pitch, and pitch goes by ratios, so its ruler is a logarithm.

Third, the power a sound carries is the average of its pressure squared, counted in decibels and spread thinner with the square of the distance; sounds at different frequencies add their powers. The ear weighs that power by frequency, hides quiet sounds behind loud neighbours, and drops the turns of a steady note.

Fourth, a sound fades by a fixed number of decibels a metre, faster high than low; at a boundary a share fixed by the two sides bounces; a voice is a buzz times a tube, with the pitch from the buzz and the vowel from the tube’s hills.

Fifth, a sound is a name, a vowel is a set of sounds, a note is an inductive object built by doubling and halving, and a chord is a set of notes; filters, cancelling and compressors are functions on sounds, and the ones that can be undone have lost nothing. A rhythm is a slow pitch, and two timekeepers lock when their speeds are close enough.

Sixth, what arrives through a channel is a shadow of what was sent, plus noise; the shadow is told to within the noise, what is at right angles to it is not told at all, and a sound from a known family can be told in full.

Anexact, yet rigorous. One box rides on this rung. 37c carries laws with a known exception rate: the ear’s weighing of loudness and its hiding of quiet sounds are drawn from listeners, and every listener differs a little, in a known direction.

What follows

A recording can drop what the ear would hide and keep what it would hear, and a room rings on after a sound stops, by the bounces of 37e. Five annexes follow the rung: 37·phys earns the stiffness of air and outruns the speed of sound, 37·eng follows a song through a room and a sound system, 37·comp holds a sound in bits, 37·bio reads the ear’s own map of pitch, and 37·crypt hides a voice, then listens to records played backwards. A tune moved to another key keeps its shape, and the notes close into a loop as the hues did (37j4). And the codes of 43·comp, which read a damaged message whole, rest on the same condition as the end of this rung: members of a family kept far enough apart.

What you can do because you know it

You can say what a sound is and how fast it goes in air and in water. You can find a sound’s pitch from its turnings, even when the lowest is missing, and place any interval on the pitch line in cents. You can turn a pressure into decibels, add sounds’ levels and follow a sound as it spreads, fades and bounces. You can say why a flute is not a clarinet, why a vowel is a vowel at any pitch, and why “s” is lost on the phone. You can prove things about notes by induction, and say why no fifth is an octave. You can say which filters, cancellers and compressors can be undone. You can count a polyrhythm and say when two players will fall into step. And you can say, with the proof in hand, how much of a sound partly lost is still told.

What it costs

Several lines are laid down, each with its rate: the stiffness and motion laws of air, the hearing window, the ear’s weighing and hiding, its bands, its deafness to the turns, the vowel regions, the share air takes. Everything else is 31’s shadows, 32’s turnings, 33’s wave equation, 17’s logarithms, 24’s rates and 4’s divisors, spent on a pressure. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that a deafened sound is still partly heard, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Marin Mersenne measured the frequencies of strings in the 1630s. Isaac Newton computed the speed of sound in 1687 and Pierre-Simon Laplace corrected it in 1816. Georg Ohm stated in 1843 that the ear hears a tone as its parts, and Hermann von Helmholtz tested it in On the Sensations of Tone (1863). August Seebeck heard the missing fundamental in 1841. Gunnar Fant set out the buzz-and-tube account of speech in 1960.

The accepted names

The study of sound is acoustics; the study of what the ear makes of it is psychoacoustics. Quality is timbre. The buzz and tube are the source–filter model. Hearing the pitch of a voice without its lowest turning is the missing fundamental.
