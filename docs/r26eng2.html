How Does a Building Ride Out an Earthquake?

How does a building ride out an earthquake? In one earthquake, short buildings fall while tall ones nearby stand; in another, it is the other way round. The ground shakes back and forth at its own pace, and a building sways at its own. This page models a building as a mass on a spring with damping (26·cb24), drives it with shaking ground written as a turning arrow (26a), finds that the sway peaks near the building’s own frequency, draws the chart engineers design from, and shows how setting a building on soft bearings moves it out of the way.

Ground shaking at ω drives a building of natural frequency ω_n. Write the shaking as Re(A e^(iωt)): the sway comes out as A over the complex number ω_n² − ω² + 2iζω_nω. Near ω = ω_n only the damping term is left, and the sway is about 1/(2ζ) times the ground’s. Moving ω_n far below the shaking makes the sway tiny.

26eng21The Building as a Mass on a Spring With Damping

Randy designs buildings in a city near a fault. A building’s floors are heavy and its columns bend like springs; its walls and joints rub and lose energy. Measure x as the sway of the top relative to the ground.

From here on, on this page, ω_n = √(k/m) is the building’s own frequency, the rate it sways at if pushed and let go, and ζ is its damping share: how much of a swing it loses, about 0.05 for a typical building.

26eng22Shaking Ground Drives It

Let the ground shake as a_g = A cos ωt, the real part of A e^(iωt) (26a). Try a sway of the same form, X e^(iωt), and each slope multiplies by iω (26·eng).

(-\omega^2 + 2i\zeta\omega_n\omega + \omega_n^2)\,X = -A @@ 
X = \dfrac{-A}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\zeta\omega_n\omega} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\omega_n^2 |X|}{A} = \dfrac{1}{\left|1 - r^2 + 2i\zeta r\right|},\ \ r = \dfrac{\omega}{\omega_n}} @@ the size of a complex number (25c)

The left side, ω_n²|X|, is the push per kilogram the columns feel. Divided by the ground’s A, it says how many times the building multiplies the shaking.

26eng23The Sway Peaks Near the Natural Frequency

r \ll 1:\ \text{ratio} \approx 1 @@ slow shaking: the building rides along
r = 1:\ \text{ratio} = \dfrac{1}{2\zeta} = 10 @@ shaking in step: ten times the ground
\htmlClass{new}{r \gg 1:\ \text{ratio} \approx \dfrac{1}{r^2}} @@ fast shaking: the building barely follows

Detuning technique: a building multiplies ground shaking by 1/|1 − r² + 2iζr|, about 1/(2ζ) when the shaking matches its own frequency and about 1/r² when the shaking is much faster; to protect a building, move its own frequency away from the shaking’s.

26eng24The Response Spectrum: Peak Sway Against Period

Real ground shaking mixes many frequencies. Engineers run a recorded earthquake through buildings of every period T = 2π/ω_n and plot the largest push each feels. The chart is the design code’s starting point.

buildingown periodpush felt in a typical strong quake
2 storeysabout 0.2 shigh: in step with the strongest shaking
10 storeysabout 1 smoderate
40 storeysabout 4 slow, but large sways

Most strong shaking near a fault is quick, with periods of a few tenths of a second, so low stiff buildings are often in step with it. Soft ground under a city can slow the shaking and put tall buildings in step instead, which is why different quakes topple different buildings.

26eng25Base Isolation Moves the Period Away From the Shaking

Set the whole building on thick bearings of rubber and steel, soft sideways. The building above moves as one block on them, with a period of about 2.5 seconds, and the bearings damp more, ζ ≈ 0.1.

\text{fixed base, period } 0.3 \text{ s}:\ r = 1,\ \text{ratio} = 10 @@ 
\text{on bearings, period } 2.5 \text{ s}:\ r \approx 8.3,\ \ |1 - 69.4 + 1.67i| \approx 68 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ratio} \approx 0.015:\ \text{about 700 times less push}} @@ the bearings take the movement

Base isolation makes the building’s own period long, far from the quick shaking, so the building above hardly feels it while the bearings take the ground’s movement. Randy watches a scale model tested on a shaking table: fixed to the table, the model rocks wildly; set on bearings, it glides as the table shakes beneath it, as the ratio says. Hospitals and emergency centres are built this way, to work the day after a quake.

Accepted names

ζ is the damping ratio; the multiplying factor is the dynamic amplification, and its peak at r = 1 is resonance. The chart is the response spectrum, and the bearings are base isolators, such as lead-rubber bearings. The swinging weight is a tuned mass damper.

Who said it first

Maurice Biot proposed the response spectrum in the early 1930s, and George Housner made it the basis of design in the following decades (not yet revisited for the dates).
