34h · proof

Something stays put

Rung 12 showed that a continuous map of a closed stretch into itself must leave some point where it was: the graph starts on or above the diagonal and ends on or below it, so it crosses. This step asks the same of the disc. Stir the top layer of a cup of coffee, gently and without tearing it, and let it settle. Is some point of the surface back where it began? The answer is yes, and the proof is a loop that cannot be undone. If some continuous map of the disc moved every point, it would let the disc be pressed onto its rim with the rim held still, and then the loop round the rim, which goes round once, would slide to a point through the disc. The circle’s loop group forbids it. The step then turns to the sphere: a continuous field of arrows lying flat along the surface of a ball must vanish somewhere. The rung proves the first half of that and holds the second in trust.

No continuous map of the disc onto its rim keeps every rim point where it is, because the loop round the rim would then slide to a point. So every continuous map of the disc into itself keeps some point fixed: if none were kept, the ray from each image through its point would press the disc onto its rim. On the sphere, an arrow field that never vanishes would slide the do-nothing map into the map that sends each point to the point straight across, and that map turns the sphere inside out, which no slide can do.

34h1The disc cannot be pressed onto its rim

Let D be the disc of points at distance at most 1 from 0 in the plane, and C its rim, the circle. A press of D onto C is a continuous map r from D to C with r(x) = x for every x on C.

On the line the same question is easy. The disc becomes [−1, 1] and its rim the two points −1 and 1. A continuous map of [−1, 1] onto {−1, 1} that keeps both would split a stretch, against the image-one-piece technique of 34c2. One dimension up, being in one piece is not enough: the rim is in one piece too. Loops do the work instead.

No-press technique: no continuous map of the disc onto its rim keeps every point of the rim where it is.

r : D \to C \text{ a press},\ \ i : C \to D \text{ the inclusion},\ \ r \circ i = \mathrm{id}_C @@ suppose a press; r keeps the rim
r_* \circ i_* = (\mathrm{id}_C)_* = \text{the do-nothing map of } \pi_1(C) @@ maps carry loops along (34g2), and a chain of maps gives a chain
\pi_1(D) \text{ has one member} @@ the straight-slide technique (34g1): D holds each segment to 0
i_*[\gamma_1] = [c],\ \ \text{so } r_*i_*[\gamma_1] = r_*[c] = [c] @@ every class in D is the class of the loop that stays put
\text{but } (\mathrm{id}_C)_*[\gamma_1] = [\gamma_1],\ \ w(\gamma_1) = 1 \ne 0 = w(c) @@ the circle-loops technique (34g4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no press exists}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a false line

34h2Every map of the disc keeps a point

Kept-point technique: every continuous map f of the disc D into itself has a point x with f(x) = x.

f(x) \ne x \text{ for every } x \in D,\ \ u(x) = x - f(x) \ne 0 @@ suppose no point is kept
r(x) = x + t(x)\,u(x),\ \ t(x) \ge 0,\ \ |r(x)| = 1 @@ the ray from f(x) through x, run on to the rim
t(x) = \frac{-x\cdot u + \sqrt{(x\cdot u)^2 + |u|^2(1 - |x|^2)}}{|u|^2} @@ |x + tu|² = 1 is a quadratic in t; its larger root (8i), never below 0 since |x| ≤ 1
r \text{ is continuous} @@ sums, products, quotients by |u|² ≠ 0, and the root, of continuous maps (11b, 11c)
|x| = 1 \Rightarrow x\cdot u = 1 - x\cdot f(x) \ge 0 \Rightarrow t(x) = 0,\ \ r(x) = x @@ dot-bound (18b): x · f(x) ≤ |x||f(x)| ≤ 1
\htmlClass{new}{r \text{ is a press of } D \text{ onto } C} \quad \blacksquare @@ against the no-press technique: some point is kept

On the line, the same technique is rung 12’s: a continuous g from [0, 1] to itself has g(x) − x ≥ 0 at 0 and ≤ 0 at 1, so by 12a it is 0 somewhere. In the disc there is no up and down to compare, and the loop takes its place.

Lay a paper map of Montana flat on the floor of a school gym in Helena, or crumple it and drop it anywhere inside the state’s outline drawn on the floor at the map’s scale. Some point of the map lies exactly over the place it shows. The technique says there is one. It does not say where, and its proof gives no way to find it.

34h3Arrows on a ball

Let S be the sphere of points at distance 1 from 0 in space. An arrow field on S gives each x on S an arrow v(x) lying flat along the surface there, so v(x) · x = 0, and it is continuous. Wind blowing along the ground is one, and so are the hairs on a ball, combed flat.

On the circle there is a field that never vanishes: v(x, y) = (−y, x), every arrow pointing anticlockwise round. On the torus there is one too, along its circles. On the sphere, every attempt leaves a bald spot or a crown, as every combed head has. That is the hairy ball claim: every arrow field on the sphere is the zero arrow somewhere. This rung proves the first half of its proof and holds the second in trust.

Field-slide technique: an arrow field on the sphere that never vanishes would slide the do-nothing map of S into the map x ↦ −x, through continuous maps of S to itself.

v(x) \ne 0,\ \ n(x) = v(x)/|v(x)| @@ the field, made of length 1
H(x, s) = \cos(\pi s)\,x + \sin(\pi s)\,n(x) @@ half a turn in the flat of x and n(x)
|H(x, s)|^2 = \cos^2(\pi s) + \sin^2(\pi s) = 1 @@ x · n(x) = 0 and both have length 1: right-triangle (18b), 21c
H(x, 0) = x,\ \ H(x, 1) = -x @@ cos 0 = 1, cos π = −1, sin 0 = sin π = 0
\htmlClass{new}{x \mapsto x \text{ slides to } x \mapsto -x \text{ on } S} \quad \blacksquare @@ H is continuous, and every H(·, s) maps S to S

Held in trust, and proved much higher up the ladder: every map of S to itself has a whole-number count of how many times it wraps S round itself, as 34g4’s winding number counts for the circle, and a slide does not change it. The do-nothing map wraps once. The map x ↦ −x is three flips, one along each axis, so it reverses the sphere’s handedness, as an odd number of flips does in 20g. It wraps −1 times. So no slide joins them, and no such field exists.

Somewhere on the earth, at every moment, the wind along the ground is calm, if the wind is continuous. The higher claim says more: a surface carries an arrow field that never vanishes exactly when its characteristic of 34f2 is 0. The sphere has 2. The torus has 0.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A press is a retraction, and the no-press technique is the no-retraction theorem. The kept-point technique is the Brouwer fixed point theorem, for the disc; a kept point is a fixed point. The hairy ball claim is the hairy ball theorem, and its higher form, that a closed surface has a nowhere-vanishing tangent field exactly when χ = 0, is the Poincaré–Hopf theorem. The wrapping count of a map of the sphere is its degree, and x ↦ −x is the antipodal map.

Who said it first

Piers Bohl proved a version of the kept-point technique in 1904. L. E. J. Brouwer proved it for every dimension in 1910–12, and Jacques Hadamard gave another proof in 1910. Brouwer also proved the hairy ball claim in every even dimension in 1912, after Henri Poincaré had found it for the ordinary sphere in 1885. Brouwer then founded intuitionism, and from the 1920s held that his own fixed point result, as stated, was not constructively true. Emanuel Sperner’s lemma of 1928 gives a way to find nearly fixed points by counting coloured triangles.

A proof that is not allowed

“The disc is in one piece and so is its rim, so there is no press: a press would split the disc.” Not allowed: a press would not split anything, since the rim is in one piece. Being in one piece settles the line’s case and not the disc’s. The proof needs the loop round the rim, which the disc can slide to a point and the rim cannot.
