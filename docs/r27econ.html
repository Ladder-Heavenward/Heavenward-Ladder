27·econ · economics

Can an economy feed itself?

Can an economy feed itself? A plane built in Everett needs aluminium, wiring, software, lawyers and lunch for the people who build it. The farms of the Yakima Valley need tractors, fuel and trucks, and the truck makers need food. Every industry buys from every other, including from itself. So to sell one more plane abroad, the whole economy must make a little more of almost everything, and each of those makers must buy a little more in turn. What makes the question hard is that there is no first industry to plan: each one’s output waits on all the others. And it is not obvious that there is an answer at all. An economy whose industries used up more than they made could never meet any demand, however hard it worked. 22·econ followed one dollar of spending round after round and added the rounds as an endless sum. This page follows the rounds through every industry at once, finds the outputs that meet a demand as one system of samenesses, and asks when the system has an answer with no negative outputs: when an economy can feed itself.

Each industry’s output must cover what the other industries buy from it and what the final buyers want. That is one system of samenesses, one grid, solved by elimination. The answer is also the sum of the rounds: the demand, what its makers buy, what those makers buy, and so on. If every industry spends less than a dollar on inputs for each dollar it makes, the rounds shrink like a geometric sum and add up. More generally, an economy can meet every demand exactly when there is some plan of outputs that leaves a surplus of every product. One new order raises every industry’s output by one column of the undoing of the grid. And the table is measured with errors, which the same undoing carries through to the outputs.

27econ1Every industry buys from every other

Split Washington’s economy, as a sketch, into three industries: farms and food, manufacturing, and services. A table says how many cents’ worth of each industry’s output go into one dollar of another’s. Write x for the outputs, in billions of dollars a year, and d for the final demand: what households, governments and the rest of the world buy.

x = Ax + d @@ each industry’s output covers what industries buy from it, (Ax)ᵢ, and what final buyers buy, dᵢ
A = \begin{pmatrix}0.10 & 0.05 & 0.01\\ 0.10 & 0.30 & 0.05\\ 0.15 & 0.15 & 0.25\end{pmatrix},\ \ d = (20, 120, 450) @@ column j: the cents of each industry’s output bought for a dollar of j’s
(I - A)x = d @@ one system of samenesses in three unknowns
\begin{pmatrix}0.9 & -0.05 & -0.01\\ 0 & 0.6944 & -0.0511\\ 0 & 0 & 0.7367\end{pmatrix}x = (20,\ 122.22,\ 481.20) @@ row moves (27j): clear x₁ from rows 2 and 3, then x₂ from row 3
\htmlClass{new}{x \approx (41.9,\ 224.1,\ 653.2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ back up from the last row

From here on, the grid A is the economy’s input–output table. Gross-output technique: the outputs that meet a final demand d solve (I − A)x = d, and when I − A can be undone they are x = (I − A)⁻¹d. Final buyers take 590 billion dollars a year here, but the industries make 919. The other 329 billion they make for each other.

27econ2Round after round

22·econ followed one dollar of spending round after round. Here each round is a whole list. First the final demand d must be made. Its makers buy Ad. Those makers buy A²d. And so on.

\textstyle\sum_i (AB)_{ij} = \sum_k\big(\sum_i a_{ik}\big)\,b_{kj} \le c\,\sum_k b_{kj} @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d); each column of A adds to at most c; no entry of B is negative
\text{every column of } A^k \text{ adds to at most } c^k,\ \text{so every entry is at most } c^k @@ induction (4c), with B = A^{k−1}
S = I + A + A^2 + \cdots \text{ settles, entry by entry} @@ compare with the geometric sum of cᵏ, for c < 1 (22e)
(I - A)(I + A + \cdots + A^k) = I - A^{k+1} \to I @@ the middle terms cancel; A^{k+1} heads to 0
\htmlClass{new}{(I - A)^{-1} = I + A + A^2 + \cdots,\ \text{with no entry negative}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (I − A)S = I, so S loses only 0 and can be undone (27d), and I − A is its undoing

Every-round technique: if no entry of A is negative and every column of A adds to at most c < 1, then I − A can be undone, its undoing is I + A + A² + ⋯, and every demand is met by outputs that are not negative; what the rounds after the kth add up to is at most cᵏ⁺¹/(1 − c) times the total demand. Each column adding to less than 1 says that every industry spends less than a dollar on inputs for each dollar it makes. That is 22·econ’s round technique, with a list in place of a number.

In the sketch the columns add to 0.35, 0.50 and 0.31, so c = 0.5. The first round of buying is (12.5, 60.5, 133.5); after three rounds the running total is (40.4, 217.2, 643.7), already within 18 billion of the answer in all. After eight rounds it is within 0.2.

27econ3When an economy can feed itself

The column test is enough, but it is not needed. Some economies have an industry that spends more than a dollar of inputs per dollar of output, and still feed themselves, because the others carry it. The right test asks for one plan that works.

p \gt 0,\ \ p - Ap \gt 0,\ \ \text{entry by entry} @@ a plan of outputs that leaves a surplus of every product
c = \max_i \tfrac{(Ap)_i}{p_i} \lt 1,\ \ \text{so } Ap \le c\,p @@ each industry uses less of its product than the plan makes
A^kp \le c^kp @@ induction: A has no negative entries, so it keeps the order; A(c^{k−1}p) = c^{k−1}Ap ≤ cᵏp
(A^k)_{ij}\,p_j \le (A^kp)_i \le c^kp_i @@ one term of a sum with no negative terms is at most the sum
(A^k)_{ij} \le c^k\tfrac{p_i}{p_j} \Rightarrow I + A + A^2 + \cdots \text{ settles} @@ compare with a geometric sum (22e)
\htmlClass{new}{(I - A)^{-1} \text{ exists, with no entry negative}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cancelling line of 27·econ.2

And the other way: if (I − A)⁻¹ has no negative entry, the plan p = (I − A)⁻¹(1, …, 1) leaves a surplus of 1 of everything. Feeds-itself technique: an economy can meet every demand that is not negative, with outputs that are not negative, exactly when some plan of outputs leaves a surplus of every product.

\Delta = \det(I - A) = (1 - a_{11})(1 - a_{22}) - a_{12}a_{21} @@ the determinant of a 2 × 2 grid (27e)
x_1 = \tfrac{(1 - a_{22})\,d_1 + a_{12}\,d_2}{\Delta},\ \ x_2 = \tfrac{a_{21}\,d_1 + (1 - a_{11})\,d_2}{\Delta} @@ the det-ratio technique (27k)
a_{11}, a_{22} \lt 1:\ \text{every top is above } 0 \text{ for every demand above } 0 @@ no entry of A is negative
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two industries feed themselves} \iff \Delta \gt 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ Δ < 0 makes both outputs negative; Δ = 0 leaves I − A without an undoing

A farm that puts 60 cents of its own grain into each dollar of grain, and 90 cents of fertiliser, beside a fertiliser plant that uses 50 cents of grain and 50 of its own fertiliser, has Δ = 0.4 × 0.5 − 0.5 × 0.9 = −0.25. To meet a demand of 10 of each, the sums ask for −40 of grain and −52 of fertiliser. No such economy can feed anyone.

27econ4One new order

Outputs are a straight map of the demand. So a change in demand moves the outputs by the same map.

\Delta x = (I - A)^{-1}\,\Delta d @@ the gross-output technique is a straight map (27c)
(I - A)^{-1} \approx \begin{pmatrix}1.124 & 0.085 & 0.021\\ 0.179 & 1.463 & 0.100\\ 0.261 & 0.310 & 1.357\end{pmatrix} @@ the sketch’s table, undone
\Delta d = (0, 1, 0):\ \ \Delta x \approx (0.085,\ 1.463,\ 0.310) @@ a billion dollars more of planes for export picks out column 2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a total of about } 1.86 \text{ billion of output}} @@ the column’s sum

Order-column technique: one more dollar of final demand for industry j raises every industry’s output by column j of (I − A)⁻¹, and the sum of that column is the total output it brings. 22·econ called such a total a multiplier. Here each industry has its own: about 1.56 for farms and food, 1.86 for manufacturing, and 1.48 for services.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a table measured with error

The table is built from surveys of firms, and it is several years old by the time it is printed. Each entry might be a cent or so off, and next year’s firms buy a little differently from last year’s. The outputs inherit every one of those errors.

Carry the table’s own error through (I − A)⁻¹, and state the outputs with it.

The same undoing carries them through. If the true table is A + E, the true outputs x′ obey (I − A)(x′ − x) = Ex′, so x′ − x is close to (I − A)⁻¹Ex. If every entry of the sketch were one cent too low, the outputs would be about 11.3, 16.0 and 17.7 billion higher: a quarter more for farms and food, but only 7% and 3% more for the others. The smallest industry is the most fragile.

A planner in Olympia wants next year’s output of farms and food to within 5%. Can a table whose entries are good to a cent give it, and what would you ask the people who build the table?

The accepted names

The table A is an input–output table, its entries technical coefficients, x the gross output and d the final demand. (I − A)⁻¹ is the Leontief inverse, and its column sums are output multipliers. The sum I + A + A² + ⋯ is a Neumann series. An economy that can meet every demand is productive, and the two-industry test, carried to n industries, is the Hawkins–Simon condition. That a table with no negative entries has a largest stretch which is real and has an arrow with no negative entries is the Perron–Frobenius theorem, proved much higher up the ladder.

Who said it first

François Quesnay drew money flowing between farmers, landlords and craftsmen in his Tableau économique of 1758. Wassily Leontief built the first tables of a whole economy, for the United States of 1919 and 1929, in 1936 and 1941, and was given the Nobel memorial prize in economics in 1973. David Hawkins and Herbert Simon gave the test for n industries in 1949. Washington State has published input–output tables of its own economy since the 1960s. In 27·econ.6.5, Ludwig von Mises (1920) and Friedrich Hayek (1945) argued that no planner could gather the knowledge prices carry; Oskar Lange (1936 and 1937) answered that a planning board could set prices by trial and error.
