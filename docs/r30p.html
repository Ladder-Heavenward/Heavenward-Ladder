30p · technique

Chance That Steps

Some things move from one condition to another in steps, and where they go next depends only on where they are now. A winter day in Seattle is wet or dry, and a wet day is more often followed by another wet one. A rental car left in Portland this week may be in Seattle or Vancouver next week. The day before yesterday does not enter. Such a process is a chain that forgets its past. All its chances for one step fit in a grid, and the chances for many steps come from multiplying by that grid again and again. Rung twenty-seven found the vectors a grid only stretches. Here one of them is the answer to a plain question: in the long run, how often is it wet? The chances settle on a list that the grid leaves where it is, whatever the start, and this step proves when and how fast.

A chain that forgets its past keeps its one-step chances in a grid whose columns add to one. Multiplying by the grid moves the chances one step on. When every entry of the grid is above zero, the chances settle on one list that the grid only stretches, by one, and they settle from every start, faster than a fixed shrinking factor. With two conditions the whole run is written down by hand.

30p1The Chance Grid

Take finitely many states, numbered 1 to k: wet and dry, or three car-rental offices. A chain moves from state to state in steps. It forgets its past: the chance that step n + 1 lands in state i, given that step n is in state j, is a number Pij that does not depend on n, nor on where the chain was before step n. Pij is a given chance (30f2). The k × k grid P is the chance grid of the chain. Its column j lists where the chain goes from state j, so every entry is at least 0 and every column adds to 1.

Write xn for the chance list after n steps: its entry i is the chance that step n is in state i. Its entries are at least 0 and add to 1.

Chance-grid technique: the chance list after n steps is xn = Pⁿx0, and every column of Pⁿ adds to 1. Two chance grids run in a row give a chance grid: P and then Q give QP, whose columns add to 1 by the last line. And I, the grid that changes nothing, is a chance grid.

(x_{n+1})_i = \textstyle\sum_j P(\text{at } i \text{ at } n + 1 \mid \text{at } j \text{ at } n)\,(x_n)_j @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3): the states at step n split the chances into separate cases
(x_{n+1})_i = \textstyle\sum_j P_{ij}\,(x_n)_j,\ \ \text{so } x_{n+1} = Px_n @@ the chain forgets its past; a grid times a vector (27d)
x_n = P^n x_0 @@ induction on n, with P⁰ = I
\textstyle\sum_i (Px)_i = \sum_j \big(\sum_i P_{ij}\big)x_j = \sum_j x_j @@ swap the order of a finite sum; each column of P adds to 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{column } j \text{ of } P^n \text{ is } P^n e_j,\ \text{and its entries add to } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ e_j has entries adding to 1, and each P keeps the total
  
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.4 \\ 0.3 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix} @@ state 1 wet, state 2 dry: after a wet day, wet with chance 0.7; after a dry day, wet with chance 0.4
x_0 = (1, 0) @@ today is wet
x_1 = (0.7,\ 0.3),\ \ x_2 = (0.61,\ 0.39) @@ the chance-grid technique: 0.7 · 0.7 + 0.4 · 0.3 = 0.61
\htmlClass{new}{x_3 = (0.583,\ 0.417),\ \ x_{10} \approx (0.5714,\ 0.4286)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the start fades; 4/7 ≈ 0.5714 is where it heads

The grid above is a model with chances near those of Seattle’s winter days. The chance of rain two days on is not 0.7 times 0.7: the chain may pass through a dry day and come back, and the whole-chance technique counts that road too.

30p2One Settled List

A settled list is a chance list π with Pπ = π: entries at least 0, adding to 1, and only stretched by P, by 1 (27f). A chain started at π stays at π for ever, by the chance-grid technique. Its accepted name is in the cellar. Measure the size of a vector d by |d| = |d₁| + ⋯ + |dk|. This π (little pi) is not the round number.

27·now met the next two techniques for the grid of the web, as the shrinking-differences and one-rank techniques. A lesson does not stand on an annex, so here are their proofs again, under the same names, for chances.

Shrinking-differences technique: if every column of P adds to 1, and m adds up the least entry of each row, then |Pd| ≤ (1 − m)|d| for every vector d whose entries add to 0.

m_i = \min_j P_{ij},\ \ m = m_1 + \cdots + m_k @@ the least entry of row i
(Pd)_i = \textstyle\sum_j (P_{ij} - m_i)\,d_j @@ subtract m_i(d₁ + ⋯ + d_k) = 0
|(Pd)_i| \le \textstyle\sum_j (P_{ij} - m_i)\,|d_j| @@ the triangle technique (8h); each P_ij − m_i is at least 0
|Pd| \le \textstyle\sum_j |d_j| \sum_i (P_{ij} - m_i) @@ add over i, and swap the order of the finite sum
\htmlClass{new}{|Pd| \le (1 - m)\,|d|} \quad \blacksquare @@ column j of P adds to 1, and the m_i add to m

One-rank technique: if every entry of P is above 0, there is exactly one settled list π. Every entry of π is above 0, and from every start x₀ the chance lists reach it: |Pⁿx₀ − π| ≤ 2(1 − m)ⁿ.

0 < m \le 1 @@ every row has an entry above 0 for its least; and m ≤ the sum of column 1, which is 1
|P^{n+r}x_0 - P^n x_0| = |P^n(P^r x_0 - x_0)| \le (1 - m)^n \cdot 2 @@ shrinking-differences n times; the difference of two chance lists adds to 0 and has size at most 2
P^n x_0 \to \pi\ \text{for some list } \pi @@ the lists crowd, entry by entry, so each entry settles (22c)
\pi_i \ge 0,\ \ \textstyle\sum_i \pi_i = 1,\ \ P\pi = \pi @@ limits keep ≥ and finite sums; x ↦ Px is continuous, so P(Pⁿx₀) heads to Pπ (11e), and it is Pⁿ⁺¹x₀
|\pi' - \pi| = |P^n(\pi' - \pi)| \le (1 - m)^n|\pi' - \pi| \to 0 @@ a second settled list π′ would have to be π
\htmlClass{new}{|P^n x_0 - \pi| = |P^n(x_0 - \pi)| \le 2(1 - m)^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ π is settled, so Pⁿπ = π; every entry of π = Pπ is a sum with weights above 0
  
C = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & 0.2 & 0.3 \\ 0.3 & 0.7 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.1 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} @@ Seattle, Portland, Vancouver; every entry above 0
(C - I)\pi = 0:\ \ -0.4\pi_1 + 0.2\pi_2 + 0.3\pi_3 = 0,\ \ 0.1\pi_1 + 0.1\pi_2 - 0.5\pi_3 = 0 @@ two of the three rows; the third is minus their sum, since the columns of C − I add to 0
\pi = \tfrac{1}{36}(13,\ 17,\ 6) @@ elimination (27j), then scale so the entries add to 1
m = 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.5 @@ the least entry of each row
\htmlClass{new}{|C^n x_0 - \pi| \le 2 \cdot 0.5^n:\ \text{within } 0.0625 \text{ after five weeks, from any start}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-rank technique

The columns of P − I always add to 0, so its rows are not free, det(P − I) = 0, and 1 is a stretch of every chance grid (27f, 27e). That much needs no entry above 0. What the entries above 0 buy is one settled list, with chances in it, reached from every start.

30p3Two States by Hand, and the Share of Time

With two states the chain has two numbers: α, the chance of leaving state 1, and β, the chance of leaving state 2. 30o3’s two-state technique ran the same chain with rates in continuous time; here time comes in steps. Then P has columns (1 − α, α) and (β, 1 − β).

Fading-start technique: if α + β > 0, the settled list is π = (β, α)/(α + β), and xn = π + (1 − α − β)ⁿ(x₀ − π) for every start x₀.

\lambda_1 = 1,\ \ \lambda_2 = (1 - \alpha) + (1 - \beta) - 1 = 1 - \alpha - \beta @@ the middle-sum technique (27l): the two stretches add to the middle sum 2 − α − β
P\,(\beta, \alpha) = (\beta - \alpha\beta + \alpha\beta,\ \alpha\beta + \alpha - \alpha\beta) = (\beta, \alpha) @@ so π = (β, α)/(α + β) is settled
P\,(1, -1) = (1 - \alpha - \beta,\ \alpha - 1 + \beta) = (1 - \alpha - \beta)(1, -1) @@ the other vector P only stretches
x_0 - \pi = c\,(1, -1) @@ both are chance lists, so x₀ − π has entries adding to 0
\htmlClass{new}{x_n = \pi + (1 - \alpha - \beta)^n(x_0 - \pi)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the leading-stretch technique (27l), worked by hand

For the wet and dry days, α = 0.3 and β = 0.4. So π = (4/7, 3/7), the second stretch is 0.3, and the chance of a wet day n days after a wet day is 4/7 + (3/7)(0.3)ⁿ. The start is forgotten by a factor 0.3 a day.

The settled list also counts time. Let Wt be 1 if day t is wet and 0 if not. The number of wet days among the first n is W₀ + ⋯ + Wn−1, and its average is the sum of the chances of a wet day.

Share-of-time technique: if Pⁿx₀ heads to π, then the average share of the first n steps spent in state i heads to πi.

E\big[\tfrac1n(W_0 + \cdots + W_{n-1})\big] = \tfrac1n\big((x_0)_i + \cdots + (x_{n-1})_i\big) @@ the average-adds technique (30g1); the average of a 0-or-1 variable is its chance of 1
|(x_t)_i - \pi_i| \le \varepsilon \text{ for every } t \ge N @@ the lists head to π
\Big|\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_{t < n}(x_t)_i - \pi_i\Big| \le \tfrac{2N}{n} + \varepsilon \text{ for } n \ge N @@ the first N terms are each within 2 of π_i; the rest within ε
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the average share heads to } \pi_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ make ε small, then n large

So the settled list answers both questions: the chance of rain on a day far ahead, and the share of rainy days in a long winter, on average. That the share itself, and not only its average, settles with chance 1 is the chain’s form of the surely-settle technique (30h4). Its proof for chains waits much higher up the ladder.

The accepted names

A chain that forgets its past is a Markov chain, and forgetting is the Markov property. The chance grid is its transition matrix; here its columns add to 1, while many books write it the other way up, with rows that add to 1, and multiply the chance list from the left. A grid with entries at least 0 and columns adding to 1 is a stochastic matrix. The settled list is the stationary distribution (or steady state, or invariant distribution). A chain whose grid has some power with every entry above 0 is regular, or irreducible and aperiodic. The one-rank technique is a case of the Perron–Frobenius theorem, and the shrinking factor 1 − m is Dobrushin’s ergodic coefficient. The share-of-time technique, with chance 1 in place of on average, is the ergodic theorem for Markov chains. The balance of the reroute standing question is detailed balance.

Who said it first

Andrey Markov introduced chains in 1906, to show that the weak law of large numbers does not need independence. In 1913 he counted the vowels and consonants in the first 20 000 letters of Pushkin’s Eugene Onegin and treated them as a two-state chain. Oskar Perron (1907) and Georg Frobenius (1912) proved that a positive matrix has one positive leading eigenvector. Wolfgang Doeblin and Roland Dobrushin gave the contraction bounds in the 1930s and 1950s. Andrey Kolmogorov set chains on the axioms of chance in the 1930s.
