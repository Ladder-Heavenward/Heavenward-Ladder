19c · proof

Socks and shoes

You put on socks, then shoes. To undo it you take off the shoes first, then the socks. This step proves that every group works the same way: to undo two moves, undo the last one first. It is the first thing about groups that needs order, and it explains why solving an equation runs its steps backwards.

The undoer of a combination is the undoers in the opposite order, because regrouping lets each one cancel its partner from the inside out. The undoers in the same order work for every pair exactly when the group is turn-about.

19c1Undo the last move first

Stands on 19b2 (one-inverse) and 19a3 (regroup).

Do b, then a: the move ab. To undo it, first undo a, the move done last, with a⁻¹. Then undo b with b⁻¹. That is b⁻¹ after a⁻¹, the move b⁻¹a⁻¹.

Socks-and-shoes technique: to undo two moves, undo the last one first: (ab)⁻¹ = b⁻¹a⁻¹.

(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1}) = a(bb^{-1})a^{-1} @@ regroup, twice
= aea^{-1} = aa^{-1} = e @@ b⁻¹ undoes b; e does nothing; a⁻¹ undoes a
\htmlClass{new}{(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-inverse: an undo from one side is the inverse
  
f(x) = 2x,\ \ g(x) = x + 3,\ \ (g \circ f)(x) = 2x + 3 @@ double, then add 3
(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}:\ \ y \mapsto \tfrac{y - 3}{2} @@ take away 3, then halve
2x + 3 = 11 \Rightarrow x = \tfrac{11 - 3}{2} = 4 @@ solving an equation is socks-and-shoes

Rung 5 solved 2x + 3 = 11 by taking away 3 first and halving second. That order was this technique, used before it was named.

19c2The wrong order

Stands on 19c1 and 19a6 (S₃ is not turn-about).

Undo in the wrong order: undo b first, with b⁻¹, and then a, with a⁻¹. That is the move a⁻¹b⁻¹, and in S₃ it can land somewhere else. Take a = s₁₂ and b = s₂₃. Then ab = [2 3 1] = r, and (ab)⁻¹ = r⁻¹ = r². But a⁻¹b⁻¹ = s₁₂s₂₃ = r. Undoing in the wrong order is a different move, unless the two turn about.

s_{12}s_{23} = r @@ 19a2
(s_{12}s_{23})^{-1} = s_{23}^{-1}s_{12}^{-1} = s_{23}s_{12} = r^2 @@ socks-and-shoes; each swap is its own inverse (19b4)
s_{12}^{-1}s_{23}^{-1} = s_{12}s_{23} = r \ne r^2 @@ the wrong order does not undo

19c3Any number of moves

Stands on 19c1 and 4e (induction).

Undress-in-reverse technique: for any n moves, (a₁a₂⋯aₙ)⁻¹ = aₙ⁻¹⋯a₂⁻¹a₁⁻¹. Coat, shoes, socks: whatever went on last comes off first.

n = 1:\ \ a_1^{-1} = a_1^{-1} @@ nothing to reverse
(a_1 \cdots a_n a_{n+1})^{-1} = \big((a_1 \cdots a_n)\,a_{n+1}\big)^{-1} @@ regroup
= a_{n+1}^{-1}\,(a_1 \cdots a_n)^{-1} @@ socks-and-shoes, for two moves
= \htmlClass{new}{a_{n+1}^{-1}a_n^{-1} \cdots a_1^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the induction hypothesis (4e)

19c4When the order does not matter

Stands on 19c1, 19b4 (undo-the-undo) and 19a6 (turn-about).

Turn-about-undo technique: (ab)⁻¹ = a⁻¹b⁻¹ for all a and b exactly when the group is turn-about.

\text{turn-about} \Rightarrow (ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1} = a^{-1}b^{-1} @@ socks-and-shoes; turn b⁻¹ and a⁻¹ about
(xy)^{-1} = x^{-1}y^{-1} \text{ for all } x, y;\ \ x = a^{-1},\ y = b^{-1} @@ suppose; any members may be put in
(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1} = ab @@ the supposition, with undo-the-undo
(a^{-1}b^{-1})^{-1} = ba @@ socks-and-shoes, with undo-the-undo
\htmlClass{new}{ab = ba} \quad \blacksquare @@ chain for =

So in ℤ under +, −(a + b) = (−a) + (−b), and in the non-zero reals 1/(ab) = (1/a)(1/b). The rule was always socks-and-shoes; the order did not show.

The accepted names

The move of 19c.4.1, aba⁻¹b⁻¹, is the commutator of a and b. The reverse-order rule is often called the shoes-and-socks rule, or socks-and-shoes, in textbooks too.

Who said it first

In 19c.4.5, the physicist is Josef Loschmidt (1876), and the friend is Ludwig Boltzmann, whose H-theorem (1872) said that a gas always moves toward disorder. Boltzmann's answer was that the backward motions are possible but overwhelmingly rare: a matter of counting, which rung 30 takes up.
