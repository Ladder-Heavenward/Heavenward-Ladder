Why Is Hot Steel Plunged Into Water?

Why is hot steel plunged into water? A smith heats a blade to red, plunges it into a trough, and it comes out hard enough to cut the bar it was made from, and brittle enough to break if dropped. This page follows iron from the rock to the blade and finds what the water did.

Steel is iron with a little carbon, and what it becomes depends on whether the carbon had time to walk. Cooled slowly the carbon gathers into plates; cooled fast it is trapped, and trapped carbon stops the lines that let iron bend.

33eng31Iron From Rock

Terry watches a furnace tapped at a steelworks: a white stream runs out where red rock and black coke went in. The rock is iron joined to oxygen. In 6·chem’s count the iron is at +3. Carbon takes the oxygen and leaves the iron at 0: two Fe₂O₃ and three C give four Fe and three CO₂. The iron comes out with carbon dissolved in it. With more than about 2 parts of carbon in a hundred by weight it is cast iron, hard and brittle. Steel is iron with between 0.005 and 2, and most steel has less than 1.

The ore is not all iron oxide. Sand and clay come with it, and they do not melt at the furnace’s heat. The furnace is charged with limestone as well. Limestone joins with the sand to make a glass that does melt, floats on the iron, and is run off. A substance added to carry off what is unwanted as a liquid is a flux, and the liquid is slag.

A plumber’s flux is the same subject at a smaller size: copper pipe has a skin of oxide that solder will not wet, and the flux dissolves the skin and floats it away as the solder runs in. Slag left in the metal is a weak place: brittle pockets of it were found in rivets from the hull of a liner that sank on her first crossing.

Flux technique: what will not melt or will not leave can be joined to something that makes it a liquid, and a liquid can be run off.

33eng32Mixing Metals

Bronze is copper with tin, brass is copper with zinc, steel is iron with carbon, and none is a compound with a fixed formula. An atom of a second kind enters a crystal in one of two ways. It takes the place of an atom of the first kind, or, if it is small enough, it sits in a space between them. Carbon in iron is of the second sort.

Two metals mix in every proportion, like copper and nickel on 12·chem’s map, only when three things hold: they stack the same way, their atoms are close in size, and they differ little in grasp (6chem2). Fail one and the map shows a limit to how much of one the other will take, and beyond the limit a second kind of grain appears. Either way the stray atoms stop lines (27phys25), which is why the alloy is harder than either metal, and why pure gold is too soft for a ring.

Mixing technique: two metals mix without limit only if they stack alike, are close in size and close in grasp; otherwise each takes a limited share of the other.

33eng33Two Irons

Iron atoms stack in two ways. Below 911.5 °C each atom sits at the corner of a cube with one more at the cube’s centre. From there to 1396 °C there is an atom at each corner and one in the middle of each face. Carbon atoms are small and sit in the spaces between iron atoms. The spaces of the hot arrangement take about a hundred times as much carbon as those of the cold one. So a steel that is one clear solution when red hot has carbon to get rid of when it cools.

33eng34The Map for Iron and Carbon

12·chem drew a map of temperature against mixture for two metals and read amounts off it with a lever. The same map for iron and carbon has a point 12·chem did not meet: one solid turning into two solids. At 0.77 parts of carbon in a hundred, cooling through that point, the hot iron becomes the cold iron, holding about 0.02, and a compound of three irons to one carbon, holding 6.7. They form in thin plates laid side by side.

The lever (12chem4) gives the share of the compound: 0.77 less 0.02, over 6.7 less 0.02, about 0.11.

33eng35Carbon Has to Walk

To make those plates, carbon atoms must travel from where the cold iron is forming to where the compound is forming. An atom in a solid moves by random hops, and rung 33’s spreading holds: the distance covered grows as the square root of D times the time. D itself falls steeply with temperature, as e to the minus Q over RT (27phys26). So coarse plates need slow cooling, and fast cooling leaves fine ones.

Walk technique: an atom in a solid travels a distance of about the square root of Dt, and D falls with cooling as e to the minus Q over RT; a change that needs atoms to travel can be outrun by cooling.

33eng36Quench

Now the smith plunges the red blade into water. The carbon has no time to walk at all. The iron still changes its stacking, by a shear of whole planes, and the carbon is trapped in spaces too small for it, stretching each cube along one side. Every trapped atom is a stray atom in 27phys25’s sense, and there are a great many. Lines cannot move. The blade is as hard as steel gets, and brittle as glass.

Quench technique: cool faster than carbon can walk and the carbon is trapped; trapped carbon stops the lines that let iron bend.

A smith judges the heat with a magnet on a rod. Cold steel pulls it. Past a certain red heat the steel stops pulling it, and that is near the heat from which a quench works. Two things have happened close together: iron above 770 °C no longer keeps its lone electrons lined up (38phys17), and the hot stacking of 33eng33 is not a magnet at any heat.

Afterwards the quenched blade holds magnetism where soft iron loses it. The walls between magnetic regions stick on the same trapped carbon and fine particles that stop the lines, so one treatment hardens the steel in both senses: hard to bend, and hard to demagnetise (42·phys reads the loop).

33eng37Temper, and a Map With Time on It

The smith then warms the blade gently. Carbon walks a short way and gathers into very small particles of the compound. The steel gives up some hardness and stops being brittle. The engineer’s chart for this has time across, on 17’s scale of powers of ten, and temperature up, with a curve where each change starts and one where it finishes. A cooling path that misses the nose of the curves is a quench.

The same idea hardens aluminium for aircraft: 4 parts of copper in a hundred are dissolved hot, trapped by a quench, then let gather into small particles by warming for hours. Warm too long and the particles grow few and coarse, and the metal softens.

Path technique: a steel’s properties are set by its path on the chart of temperature against time, as much as by what is in it.

Accepted names

The furnace is a blast furnace. The cold stacking is body-centred cubic and the iron ferrite; the hot stacking is face-centred cubic and the iron austenite. The compound Fe₃C is cementite, the plates pearlite, the point the eutectoid. Quenched steel is martensite. The chart is a time-temperature-transformation diagram. Hardening aluminium this way is precipitation hardening, or ageing, and warming too long is overageing. D is the diffusion coefficient. What is added to carry off the unwanted as a liquid is a flux and the liquid slag. A mixture of metals is an alloy: by taking places it is substitutional, between places interstitial. The heat past which iron stops being a magnet is the Curie temperature. The liner of 33eng31 was the Titanic.
