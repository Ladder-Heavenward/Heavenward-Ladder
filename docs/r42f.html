Flows and Cuts

Water from a reservoir runs through a city’s mains to a treatment plant. Each main carries at most so much an hour, and at every junction what flows in flows out. How much can reach the plant? An answer from above is easy to find: draw a line across the map that separates the reservoir from the plant, and add up what the mains crossing it can carry. Nothing can get past that line faster. The surprise is that the best such line is always exactly right: the most that can flow equals the least that any separating line lets through. The step proves it by a recipe. Push water along any route that still has room, counting a main already in use as room to send water back. When no route has room, the dots still reachable from the reservoir mark out a separating line that every route to the plant crosses full, and the flow equals that line’s total. On the way it finds that whole-number mains give a whole-number best flow, and that the matchings of the last step are flows in disguise.

A flow obeys each link’s capacity and keeps what goes in equal to what comes out at every inside dot. Every flow is at most every cut. When no path from source to sink has room left, the dots still reachable give a cut the flow fills exactly, so the largest flow equals the smallest cut. Whole capacities give a whole largest flow.

42f1Flows and Cuts

A network is a finite set of dots with two named dots, the source s and the sink t, and one-way links: pairs (u, w) in order (2h), each with a capacity c(u, w) ≥ 0. A flow gives each one-way link a number f(u, w) with 0 ≤ f(u, w) ≤ c(u, w), so that at every dot other than s and t what flows in equals what flows out. Its value |f| is what flows out of s less what flows in.

A cut is a set S of dots holding s and not t. Its capacity c(S) is the total capacity of the one-way links from S to the rest.

Flow-under-cut technique: the value of any flow is at most the capacity of any cut.

|f| = \textstyle\sum_{v \in S} (\text{out of } v - \text{into } v) @@ only s gives a nonzero term; inside dots balance
= (\text{flow from } S \text{ to the rest}) - (\text{flow from the rest into } S) @@ links inside S appear once out and once in, and cancel
\le \text{flow from } S \text{ to the rest} @@ flows are at least 0
\htmlClass{new}{|f| \le c(S)} \quad \blacksquare @@ each flow is at most its capacity

The second line says more: the net flow across every cut is the same number, the value. The ledger kept at each dot is kept across every line drawn between s and t.

42f2Room Left

Given a flow f, a link (u, w) has room forward c(u, w) − f(u, w), and room back f(u, w): sending less along it is the same as sending some back from w to u. A route with room is a path from s to t, each step along some link with room forward or against some link with room back.

Push-along technique: if a route with room has least room r, pushing r along it gives a flow of value |f| + r. Forward steps gain r, backward steps lose r, and both stay between 0 and the capacity, by the choice of r. At each inside dot of the route one step comes in and one goes out; in each of the four cases, two forward, two back, or one of each, the balance is kept.

c(s,p) = 8,\ c(s,q) = 7,\ c(p,q) = 4,\ c(p,t) = 5,\ c(q,t) = 9 @@ a network
s \to p \to t:\ \text{push } 5;\ \ s \to q \to t:\ \text{push } 7 @@ least rooms 5 and 7
s \to p \to q \to t:\ \text{rooms } 3, 4, 2;\ \text{push } 2 @@ value 14
\htmlClass{new}{\text{from } s \text{: room only to } p \text{, then to } q \text{; nothing reaches } t} \quad \blacksquare @@ s→q full, p→t full, q→t full

The cut {s, p, q} has capacity c(p, t) + c(q, t) = 14. So 14 is the largest flow, by the flow-under-cut technique: the cut proves it, and the flow proves the cut is smallest.

42f3The Largest Flow Is the Smallest Cut

Bottleneck technique: if no route has room, the dots reachable from s by steps with room form a cut whose capacity is the value of the flow; so the largest flow equals the smallest cut.

S = \text{dots reachable from } s \text{ by steps with room} @@ t ∉ S: no route has room
(u, w),\ u \in S,\ w \notin S \Rightarrow f(u, w) = c(u, w) @@ room forward would put w in S
(w, u),\ w \notin S,\ u \in S \Rightarrow f(w, u) = 0 @@ room back would put w in S
|f| = \text{flow out of } S - \text{flow into } S = c(S) - 0 @@ the second line of the flow-under-cut proof
\htmlClass{new}{|f| = c(S) \ge \text{every flow},\ \ c(S) \le \text{every cut}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the flow-under-cut technique, both ways

Whole-flow technique: if every capacity is a whole number, pushing from the zero flow stops, and the largest flow can be taken with every f(u, w) whole. Each push adds a whole number at least 1 to the value, and the value never passes c({s}). The distance to that ceiling is a falling measure (4l). Every room stays whole, so every push is whole.

With capacities that are not fractions, pushing along badly chosen routes can go on for ever, and the values settle below the largest flow. Choosing a route with fewest links each time stops in at most |V| · |E| pushes, whatever the capacities; that was shown in 1972, and is stated here.

42f4Matchings as Flows

Take a two-sided graph with sides X and Y. Add a source joined to each dot of X, and a sink joined from each dot of Y. Turn each link into a one-way link from X to Y. Give every link capacity 1.

\text{a matching of } k \text{ links} \to \text{a flow of value } k @@ send 1 along s → x → y → t for each matched pair
\text{a whole flow of value } k \to \text{a matching of } k \text{ links} @@ each x receives at most 1, so sends 1 to at most one y; each y likewise
\text{largest matching} = \text{largest flow} @@ the whole-flow technique gives a whole largest flow
\htmlClass{new}{= \text{smallest cut}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bottleneck technique

A smallest cut here can be read as a cover. Give the X to Y links capacity |X| + 1 in place of 1, which changes no whole flow’s value; then a smallest cut S never lets one of them cross, and the dots of X outside S, with the dots of Y inside S, are a cover with c(S) dots. So 42e’s cover-equals-matching technique is the bottleneck technique on a special network.

The accepted names

A network is a flow network; room is residual capacity, and the network of rooms is the residual graph. A route with room is an augmenting path. The bottleneck technique is the max-flow min-cut theorem, and the push-along recipe is the Ford–Fulkerson method; choosing fewest links each time is the Edmonds–Karp algorithm (1972; Yefim Dinitz had a faster form in 1970). The whole-flow technique is the integrality theorem. The statement of 42f.6.3 is Menger's theorem (1927).

Who said it first

Theodore Harris and Frank Ross studied, in a report of 1955 for the United States Air Force, the smallest cut of the Soviet rail network into Eastern Europe. Lester Ford and Delbert Fulkerson proved the max-flow min-cut theorem in 1956, as did Peter Elias, Amiel Feinstein and Claude Shannon the same year. Karl Menger had the version for paths in 1927. Ford and Fulkerson's example of a recipe that never stops is from their book of 1962.

A proof that is not allowed

“The smallest cut is a bottleneck, so no flow can be larger. Push flow until it can go no further; then it is as large as the bottleneck.” Not allowed: “it can go no further” must mean no route with room, counting room back. With only forward steps, the recipe can stop below the largest flow: route the first push through a middle link that a better flow does not use.
