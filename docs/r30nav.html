30·nav · navigation

How sure am I of where I am?

How sure am I of where I am? A ferry leaves Seattle for Bremerton on a winter morning in fog. It must thread Rich Passage, the narrow water between Bainbridge Island and the Kitsap shore, past the rocks at its eastern mouth. The captain has more than one way of knowing where the boat is. The satellite receiver gives one answer. The radar, timing echoes off the shore, gives another. Speed and heading, carried forward from the last good fix, give a third. They never quite agree. Every navigator since the first ships has faced this: several answers, each a little wrong, and one boat. Should you trust the best one and ignore the rest? Take a plain average? What makes it hard is that the answers are not equally good, and how good each one is changes as the trip goes on. This page uses rung thirty’s spreads to settle it. Two fixes are best combined with weights, each fix weighted by one over its spread, and the result is better than either. A plain average can be worse than the best fix alone. A position carried forward grows less certain as it ages, and a new fix pulls it back. And errors east and north, joined, give the chance of being more than any distance off, which is what the captain needs to decide whether to go through.

Each fix is the true place plus an error that averages nothing, with a known spread. A weighted average of two fixes is again the true place plus an error, and its spread is the square of each weight times that fix’s spread, added. Choose the weight to make that spread least, and the weight on each fix comes out in proportion to one over its spread. The spread of the result is then one over the sum of one over each spread: the trusts add. A poor fix gets a small weight, and still helps a little. A plain average gives it half the say, and is worse than the good fix alone whenever the poor fix’s width is more than about one and three quarter times the good one’s. Between fixes, a position carried forward from speed and heading is a random walk, and its spread grows in step with the time. Each fix pulls it back, and the spread settles into a saw tooth. Finally, errors east and north are independent and at right angles, so their spreads add like the squares of the sides of a right triangle, and the chance of being more than a distance off falls off as an exponential of the square of that distance.

30nav1Two fixes, one line

Work across the ferry’s track first: one number, x, how far the boat is to the left of its line. Fix A reads A = x + e_A and fix B reads B = x + e_B, with independent errors that average 0 and have widths a and b. Combine them as X = wA + (1 − w)B.

E\,X = w x + (1 - w) x = x @@ the average-adds technique (30g): any w keeps the average right
\operatorname{Var} X = w^2 a^2 + (1 - w)^2 b^2 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g)
\tfrac{d}{dw}\operatorname{Var} X = 2wa^2 - 2(1 - w)b^2 = 0 \iff w = \tfrac{b^2}{a^2 + b^2} @@ slope 0 (13); the slope rises, so this is the bottom (14g)
\tfrac{w}{1 - w} = \tfrac{b^2}{a^2} = \tfrac{1/a^2}{1/b^2} @@ each weight in proportion to one over its spread
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Var} X = \tfrac{a^2b^2}{a^2 + b^2} = \tfrac{1}{1/a^2 + 1/b^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ put w back in, and simplify

Weigh-by-trust technique: two independent fixes are best combined with weights in proportion to one over their spreads, and then one over the spread of the result is the sum of one over each spread, so the result is better than either fix. A phone’s satellite fix under open sky has a width of about 2.0 m in each direction (30·nav.4 finds this from the published figure). A radar range off the shore might have a width of 10 m. Then w = 100/104 = 0.96 on the satellite fix, and the width of the result is 1.96 m: the radar helps, a little.

30nav2Trusts add, and the plain average

With n fixes of widths σ₁, …, σ_n, combine the first two by the weigh-by-trust technique, then combine the result with the third, and so on. At each stage one over the spread grows by one over the new fix’s spread.

\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2} = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{\sigma_n^2},\ \ \text{weights } \tfrac{1/\sigma_i^2}{\sum_j 1/\sigma_j^2} @@ the weigh-by-trust technique, n − 1 times
n \text{ equal fixes of width } s:\ \ \sigma = s/\sqrt n @@ the sample-average technique (30q) again
\text{plain average of two: } \operatorname{Var} = \tfrac{a^2 + b^2}{4} @@ the independent-spreads-add technique, with w = ½
\tfrac{a^2 + b^2}{4} < a^2 \iff b^2 < 3a^2 @@ multiply by 4, take a² away
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the plain average beats the better fix alone exactly when } b < \sqrt3\,a} \quad \blacksquare @@ √3 ≈ 1.73

Trusts-add technique: with weights in proportion to one over each spread, one over the spread of the result is the sum of one over each spread, while a plain average of two fixes beats the better fix only when the worse is less than √3 times as wide. The satellite fix of 2 m and the radar of 10 m, averaged plainly, give a width of √104/2 = 5.1 m: two and a half times worse than the satellite fix alone. Weighted, they give 1.96 m. The radar’s answer is not thrown away; it is given the small say it has earned.

30nav3A fix that ages

Between fixes, the boat’s place is carried forward from speed and heading: dead reckoning. Small errors in the log’s speed and in the current add up minute by minute, as the steps of a random walk (30n). So the spread of the carried position grows by a fixed amount q for each minute. Let P be its spread just after a fix, and let fixes of spread R come every t minutes.

P^- = P + qt @@ predict: the root-n technique (30n), t more steps of the walk
P^+ = \tfrac{1}{1/P^- + 1/R} = \tfrac{P^- R}{P^- + R} @@ correct: the weigh-by-trust technique, carried position against fix
P^+ = P:\ \ P(P + qt + R) = (P + qt)R @@ a steady saw tooth: the same spread after every fix
P^2 + qt\,P - qtR = 0 @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{P = \tfrac{-qt + \sqrt{(qt)^2 + 4qtR}}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the quadratic formula (8i), the positive root

Predict-and-correct technique: between fixes a carried position’s spread grows by q each minute, and each fix brings it back by the weigh-by-trust technique, so the spread settles into a saw tooth whose low point solves P² + qtP − qtR = 0. Take q = 225 m² a minute, a width of 15 m after one minute of carrying, and radar fixes of width 10 m every 5 minutes. Then qt = 1125 and P = 92.4: a width of 9.6 m just after each fix, growing to √1217 = 34.9 m just before the next. If the satellite fix is lost, 10 minutes of carrying from 2 m reach √(4 + 2250) = 47.5 m.

30nav4East and north: the chance of being far off

Now the plane. Let the error east be E and the error north N, independent, each a bell of width σ, by 30m’s stretched-density technique (1/σ)φ(x/σ). Their joint density is the product, (1/(2πσ²)) e^{−(x² + y²)/(2σ²)}. The distance off is R, with R² = E² + N².

P(R > r) = \iint_{x^2 + y^2 > r^2} \tfrac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\,e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx\,dy @@ independent, so the densities multiply; the either-order-sized technique (30e)
= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_r^\infty \tfrac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\,e^{-\rho^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,\rho\,d\rho\,d\theta @@ distance and turn in place of east and north (28i)
= \int_r^\infty \tfrac{\rho}{\sigma^2}\,e^{-\rho^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,d\rho = \Big[-e^{-\rho^2/(2\sigma^2)}\Big]_r^\infty @@ the turn gives 2π; the inside has the slope shown (17)
\htmlClass{new}{P(R > r) = e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and E R² = 2σ², spreads adding at right angles

Circle-chance technique: if the errors east and north are independent bells of width σ, the chance of being more than r off is e^{−r²/(2σ²)}. Turned round: the circle that holds the boat with chance 95% has radius σ√(2 ln 20) = 2.45σ. The published figure for a phone under open sky is a 4.9 m radius at 95%, so σ ≈ 2.0 m.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting and the triangle

The two errors are independent and at right angles. Their spreads add like the squares of the sides of a right triangle: the average of R² is σ² + σ². Nothing about the boat is known exactly, yet the chance of each distance off is known exactly, given the widths. That is rigour of the second kind, and a decision can stand on it.

Give a position with its width, and decide with the chance of being wrong stated: errors at right angles add like the squares of a triangle’s sides.

Orchard Rocks lie at the eastern mouth of Rich Passage. Suppose the captain will pass within 30 m of a hazard only if the chance of being more than 30 m off the fix is below 1 in 1000. That needs 30 ≥ σ√(2 ln 1000) = 3.72σ, so σ ≤ 8.1 m. On the satellite fix, σ = 2 m: go. On radar alone, σ = 10 m, the chance of being more than 30 m off is e^{−4.5} = 1.1%, eleven times the line: slow down, or stand further off.

The satellite fix and the radar disagree by 40 m, when their widths are 2 m and 10 m. Is that one of the chances the circle-chance technique allows, or a sign that one of them is broken?

The accepted names

Weighting each fix by one over its spread is inverse-variance weighting; one over a spread is a precision, and the trusts-add technique says precisions add. The predict-and-correct technique, in one dimension, is the Kalman filter; P is the error variance, q the process noise and R the measurement noise. A position carried from speed and heading is dead reckoning. The distance off, when errors east and north are independent bells of one width, has the Rayleigh distribution; the 95% radius is R95, and the 50% radius the circular error probable.

Who said it first

Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805, and Carl Friedrich Gauss, who said he had used it since 1795, justified it from the bell in 1809. Pierre-Simon Laplace showed how to weight observations of unequal precision. Lord Rayleigh found the distribution of the distance off in 1880, for the sum of many sound waves of random phase. Rudolf Kálmán published the predict-and-correct filter in 1960, and Stanley Schmidt at the Ames Research Center fitted it to the navigation of the Apollo spacecraft. In 30·nav.6.5, jamming and faking of satellite signals at sea has been reported in many places since about 2017, and navies and some merchant fleets went back to teaching navigation by the stars.
