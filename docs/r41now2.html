41·now2 · research front

How regularly are the primes spread?

How regularly are the primes spread? Below a million there are 78 498 primes. A smooth count, made by adding up the chance one in the log of each number, gives 78 627, off by 129. Below a billion the smooth count is off by 1700, out of fifty million. The errors grow, but far more slowly than the counts, roughly like the square root. In 1859 a short paper, the only one its author wrote on numbers, showed that the errors are set exactly by where the function ζ of 41g is zero, once ζ is carried into the complex plane, and guessed that all those zeros lie on one line. If they do, the error is never much more than a square root. It is the most famous open question in mathematics. Machines have found more than ten trillion zeros, every one on the line, and nobody can prove the next one is. This page builds the smooth count, measures how big an error a coin-tossing model of the primes would make, shows how a zero sets the size of a wave in the error, and counts twin primes against a model that knows about clocks, the other open question of the rung.

Adding the chance 1/ln t gives a smooth count li(x) that beats x/ln x by about x/(ln x)². A model that makes each number prime by a coin toss with that chance predicts errors about the square root of the count, and the true errors are of that size so far. Each zero of ζ gives a wave in the error of size x to the power of its real part, so all zeros on the line of real part ½ is the same as errors of square-root size. Twins follow a count with a clock correction, and are still not proved endless.

41now21A smoother count

41g held in trust that the share of primes near t is about 1/ln t. Adding that share up from 2 to x gives the smooth count li(x), the area under 1/ln t. It is a better count than x / ln x, and parts says by how much.

Parts-count technique: li(x) = x/ln x − 2/ln 2 + the area under 1/(ln t)² from 2 to x, so li(x) exceeds x/ln x by about x/(ln x)².

\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t} @@ the shares added up
u = \frac{1}{\ln t},\ \ dv = dt:\ \ du = -\frac{dt}{t(\ln t)^2},\ \ v = t @@ parts (16e)
\int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t} = \Big[\frac{t}{\ln t}\Big]_2^x + \int_2^x \frac{dt}{(\ln t)^2} @@ uv minus the area under v du
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{li}(x) = \frac{x}{\ln x} - \frac{2}{\ln 2} + \int_2^x \frac{dt}{(\ln t)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last area is near x/(ln x)², since ln t is near ln x over most of the range

At x = 10⁶: x/ln x = 72 382, li(x) = 78 627, and π(x) = 78 498. The error of li is 129; that of x / ln x is 6116. At x = 10⁹: π(x) = 50 847 534 and li(x) = 50 849 234, an error of 1700, while x/ln x is short by 2.6 million.

41now22How big should the error be?

Make a pretend set of primes: for each n from 3 on, toss a coin that comes up yes with chance 1/ln n, independently. Its count up to x has average about li(x). How far from the average does such a count usually stray?

Spread-of-count technique: a count of independent yes or no tosses with chances qₙ has spread squared Σ qₙ(1 − qₙ), at most its average Σ qₙ; so the pretend count strays from li(x) by about √li(x), near √(x / ln x).

X_n \in \{0, 1\},\ \ P(X_n = 1) = q_n @@ one toss
\operatorname{E}X_n = q_n,\ \ \operatorname{E}X_n^2 = q_n @@ X² = X for 0 and 1
\operatorname{Var}X_n = q_n - q_n^2 @@ spread squared is E X² minus (E X)²
\operatorname{Var}\textstyle\sum X_n = \sum q_n(1 - q_n) \le \sum q_n @@ spreads squared of independent counts add (30h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread} \le \sqrt{\textstyle\sum q_n} \approx \sqrt{\operatorname{li}(x)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ with qₙ = 1/ln n
Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

A count of primes is not a draw from anything: π(10⁶) is 78 498, fixed for ever. Yet the coin model gives it a cloud. At x = 10⁶ the pretend counts stray from li by about √(78 627 − 6247), near 270, and the true error, 129, sits well inside the cloud. At 10⁹ the cloud is about 6900 wide and the error 1700 sits inside it again.

A count from the primes is called surprising only when it stands outside the cloud a coin model gives it, and the model is trusted only where the clocks do not interfere.

The model is a tool, not a fact about primes. It knows nothing of clocks: it makes even numbers prime as often as odd ones, and it predicts too few twins, as the fourth footing shows. Its predictions are trusted where clocks do not bear, and a count that stood far outside the cloud would be news.

Below 10⁹ the coin model’s cloud is about 6900 wide. If the true error had been 40 000, would that disprove anything, and what?

41now23Each zero gives a wave

ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + ⋯ settles only for s > 1. Like Γ in 26l, it can be carried beyond its formula, to every complex s except 1, in one way only; that is held in trust. The carried ζ is 0 at −2, −4, −6, …, and at endlessly many other points, all with real part strictly between 0 and 1. The first is ½ + 14.1347 i. Also held in trust, from the paper of 1859: the error π(x) − li(x), suitably smoothed, is a sum of one term x^ρ/ρ for each such zero ρ, plus small parts.

Zero-size technique: for ρ = β + iγ and x > 1, the term x^ρ has size exactly x^β; so a zero with real part ½ gives a wave of size √x / |ρ|, turning as γ ln x.

x^\rho = e^{\rho\ln x} = e^{\beta\ln x}\,e^{i\gamma\ln x} @@ the exponential turns adding into multiplying (26b)
|e^{i\gamma\ln x}| = 1 @@ a turning has size 1 (26a)
|x^\rho| = e^{\beta\ln x} = x^\beta @@ the other factor is real and positive
\htmlClass{new}{\beta = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow |x^\rho/\rho| = \sqrt{x}/|\rho|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the wave’s angle γ ln x turns slowly as x grows

The first zero, γ = 14.1347, gives a wave that comes round each time ln x grows by 2π/14.1347 = 0.4445, that is, each time x grows by a factor 1.56. The hypothesis of 1859 is that every such zero has β = ½. If one had β = 0.6, its wave would grow like x^0.6, and the error would pass √x times any power of ln x. So the hypothesis is the same as: |π(x) − li(x)| stays below a fixed multiple of √x ln x. In the form proved equal to it in 1976, below √x ln x / (8π) for x ≥ 2657: at 10⁹ that bound is 26 075, and the error is 1700.

41now24Twins, with the clocks put back

The coin model would make n and n + 2 both prime with chance 1/(ln n)², and predict 6247 twin pairs below a million. There are 8169. The model forgot the clocks. On the clock of a prime p, a prime above p is never at hour 0. For one number the coin model allows for that through the share 1/ln n. For two numbers at once it allows for it twice, as if the hours of n and n + 2 were independent, and they are not.

Twin-correction technique: on the clock of an odd prime p, n and n + 2 both miss hour 0 for p − 2 of the p hours of n, against (p − 1)²/p if they missed it independently; on the clock of 2 the shares are ½ against ¼. So the clocks raise the twin count by 2 times the product of 1 − 1/(p − 1)² over the odd primes.

n \not\equiv 0,\ n + 2 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p \iff n \notin \{0, -2\} @@ two hours ruled out, for p odd
\text{share } \tfrac{p - 2}{p},\ \ \text{against } \big(\tfrac{p - 1}{p}\big)^2 @@ each hour of n equally often (41a)
\tfrac{p(p - 2)}{(p - 1)^2} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{(p - 1)^2} @@ the ratio for p odd
p = 2:\ \tfrac12 \big/ \tfrac14 = 2 @@ n odd makes n + 2 odd
\htmlClass{new}{\text{twins} \approx 2\textstyle\prod_{p > 2}\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{(p - 1)^2}\big)\int_2^x \tfrac{dt}{(\ln t)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clocks are independent by 41c, so the ratios multiply

The product is 0.6602, so the factor is 1.3203, and the corrected count below a million is 1.3203 × 6247 = 8248, against the true 8169. Below a billion the true count is 3 424 506. The count is predicted to grow for ever. Nobody has proved that it does not stop: 41·now’s bound of 246 is the nearest anyone has come.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

li(x) is the logarithmic integral, and the parts-count technique its first asymptotic terms; the count with li is the prime number theorem in its sharper form. The coin model is Cramér’s random model. The carried ζ is the analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function; −2, −4, … are its trivial zeros, the others the nontrivial zeros in the critical strip, and the line of real part ½ the critical line. The hypothesis of 1859 is the Riemann hypothesis, and the sum over zeros is the explicit formula. The twin-correction technique gives the Hardy–Littlewood twin prime constant, C₂ = 0.6601…, and the count 2C₂ ∫ dt/(ln t)². The first x with π(x) > li(x) is Skewes’s number.

Who said it first

Bernhard Riemann gave the continuation, the explicit formula and the hypothesis in his paper of 1859 to the Berlin Academy. Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin proved in 1896 that no zero has real part 1, which gives the prime count. Helge von Koch showed in 1901 that the hypothesis is the same as an error of size √x ln x, and Lowell Schoenfeld gave the explicit form √x ln x/(8π) in 1976. John Littlewood proved in 1914 that π(x) − li(x) changes sign endlessly often; Stanley Skewes bounded the first change in 1933, and later work brought the bound below 10³¹⁷. Harald Cramér gave the coin model in 1936. Xavier Gourdon checked the first ten trillion zeros in 2004. The hypothesis is one of the seven problems for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offered a prize in 2000.

A proof that is not allowed

“The coin model gives errors of size √x, the primes are like coins, so the errors are of size √x.” Not allowed: the primes are not coins, and the model is wrong about twins and about even numbers. Its success at one size is evidence, read inside the cloud, not a proof; the proof would be the hypothesis itself.
