30·then · newsflash

Why does pollen jiggle in water?

Why does pollen jiggle in water? Put a drop of water under a microscope with a few grains from inside the pollen of pinkfairies, a wildflower of western Montana, and the grains never sit still. They shiver and wander, a little this way and a little that, and they keep it up for hours, for days, for as long as anyone has watched. At first people thought the grains were alive. Then the same jiggle turned up in grains of dust, of glass and of soot, which are not alive at all. What makes the question hard is that nothing seems to push them. The water looks perfectly still. For most of a century the jiggle sat in the microscope as a curiosity, while chemists argued over whether atoms were real things or only a way of keeping the books on how substances combine. This page is the news that rung thirty brings to the sciences. With chance as size, averages as integrals and the bell curve in hand, four old questions get answers that can be checked. Were the friar’s pea counts as rough as honest counts should be? What does a gas do to its walls, and how fast do its molecules move? Why does a grain jiggle, and how far does it get? And how can a lump of matter that obeys no clock decay on a timetable?

Count peas, and the counts miss the ratio three to one by a little. Scale each miss by its spread and square it, and the total of those squares has a known average and a known spread, so a total can be too large, and it can also be too small. Two squared bells together have a chance of staying under any line that comes straight from the bell’s own total, and pairs of them can be added up exactly, so the chance of a fit as close as the friar’s can be fenced in. Molecules striking a wall make the gas law, and the gas law then says what their average energy is. If the molecule’s three speeds along the three directions come apart from one another and no direction is special, each one spreads like a bell. A grain in water is struck from every side, held back by the water’s drag, and the two together make its spread grow in step with time, so that its typical distance grows like the root of time. And a nucleus that does not age has a chance of lasting that halves on a fixed timetable, while the count of its decays has a spread that sets how well any age can be read from it.

30then1The pea counts tested

5·bio set out the friar’s seven experiments. In each, a share of the second generation showed the first form. His model says each seed shows it with chance ¾, apart from every other seed (30f’s independent). Then the count X of first forms among n seeds is a sum of n independent parts, each 1 with chance ¾ and 0 otherwise. Each part has average ¾ and spread ¾ · ¼ = 3/16. So X has average 3n/4 and spread 3n/16 (30g). By the central limit technique (30j), the scaled miss Z = (X − 3n/4)/√(3n/16) is close to a bell.

\operatorname{E} Z = 0,\ \ \operatorname{E} Z^2 = 1 @@ a miss scaled by its spread width has average 0 and spread 1 (30g)
\textstyle\int x^4\varphi(x)\,dx = \big[-x^3\varphi\big] + 3\int x^2\varphi(x)\,dx = 3 @@ φ the bell density, with φ′ = −xφ; parts (16e); the bell's spread is 1 (30j)
\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) = \operatorname{E}Z^4 - (\operatorname{E}Z^2)^2 = 3 - 1 = 2 @@ the spread of Z², for a bell
S = Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2,\ \ \operatorname{E}S = k @@ the average-adds technique (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}S = k,\ \ \operatorname{Var}S = 2k,\ \text{spread width } \sqrt{2k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the Zᵢ come from separate experiments: the independent-spreads-add technique (30g)

Fit-total technique: if the law holds and the counts are large, the total of the squared scaled misses of k separate counts has average k and spread width √(2k). So a total can be too large, which says the law fits badly. It can also be too small, which says the counts fit better than chance allows.

30then2How rare is a fit this close?

Take two independent bells, Z₁ and Z₂. 30j found the bell’s total by turning its square into a double integral in polar coordinates. The same turn gives the chance that Z₁² + Z₂² stays under a line s.

P(Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \le s) = \iint_{x^2 + y^2 \le s} \tfrac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy @@ independent: the densities multiply (30f)
= \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\sqrt s} \tfrac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 1 - e^{-s/2} @@ polar coordinates and their extra r (28i)
T_1 = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \text{ has density } \tfrac12 e^{-t/2} @@ the slope of 1 − e^(−t/2)
f_m(t) = \tfrac{(1/2)^m\,t^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}e^{-t/2} \Rightarrow f_{m+1}(t) = \textstyle\int_0^t f_m(u)\,\tfrac12 e^{-(t-u)/2}\,du = \tfrac{(1/2)^{m+1}t^m}{m!}e^{-t/2} @@ the total of m independent pairs, plus one more pair; either order (30e)
\htmlClass{new}{P(Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_{2m}^2 \le s) = 1 - e^{-s/2}\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\tfrac{(s/2)^j}{j!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope of the right side is f_m(s), and both sides are 0 at s = 0 (16)

30s held in trust the chance law of such totals; its accepted name is the χ² law. Even-total technique: the chance that 2m independent squared bells total s or less is 1 − e^(−s/2) times the first m terms of the endless sum for e^(s/2) taken away from 1. It proves that law for an even number of experiments, with its chances in closed form. Seven is odd, but a total of squares only grows as squares are added. So S₆ ≤ S₇ ≤ S₈, where S₈ adds one more independent squared bell to S₇, and S₆ leaves the last one out. By the chance-rules technique (30f), a smaller event has a smaller chance.

P(S_8 \le 2.14) = 1 - e^{-1.07}\big(1 + 1.07 + \tfrac{1.07^2}{2} + \tfrac{1.07^3}{6}\big) \approx 0.024 @@ the even-total technique, m = 4
P(S_6 \le 2.14) = 1 - e^{-1.07}\big(1 + 1.07 + \tfrac{1.07^2}{2}\big) \approx 0.094 @@ m = 3
\htmlClass{new}{0.024 \le P(S_7 \le 2.14) \le 0.094} @@ the chance-rules technique: {S₈ ≤ s} ⊆ {S₇ ≤ s} ⊆ {S₆ ≤ s}

Worked to more places, the χ² law for seven gives 0.048: about one time in twenty-one, a set of seven honest experiments would fit this well. That alone accuses no one. One time in twenty-one happens. In 1936 a statistician totalled the same squares over every count in the friar’s paper, dozens of them, and the chance of a fit that close came out at about seven in a hundred thousand.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

A test is a rule laid down before the counts are seen. Here is one: call the counts too good when S falls among the lowest 5% of totals that honest counts would give. Run on honest counts, this rule cries foul wrongly once in twenty times. That rate is not a weakness hidden in the method. It is stated, and it is the method.

A verdict drawn from counts states the chance that it is wrong, and that chance is fixed before the counts are seen.

The friar’s seven fall at 4.8%, just inside the line. The verdict “too good” is anexact: it may be wrong. It is rigorous because how often it is wrong is known in advance. The whole paper, at seven in a hundred thousand, falls far inside the line, and the chance that the verdict is wrong is now very small. Even then the test names no cause. A helper who counted with the ratio in mind, a friar who stopped counting when the ratio looked right, or plants sorted by eye at the borderline would all leave the same mark.

A class in Corvallis counts 400 seeds from a cross and gets exactly 300 of the first form and 100 of the second. Would you call that too good? What chance of being wrong would you attach to the verdict?

30then3Molecules on the wall

A gas is N molecules, each of mass m, flying about a box with a wall of size A at each end, a length L apart. The gas law of 15·chem, written for molecules as 22·comp wrote it, is PV = NkT. What does it say about the molecules?

\text{one bounce: push } 2mv_x,\ \text{ once every } \tfrac{2L}{v_x} @@ straight back at the same speed (14·phys); back across and home again
\text{average force of one molecule} = 2mv_x\cdot\tfrac{v_x}{2L} = \tfrac{mv_x^2}{L} @@ push per bounce, times bounces per second
P = \tfrac{N\,m\,\operatorname{E}[v_x^2]}{AL} = \tfrac{N\,m\,\operatorname{E}[v_x^2]}{V} @@ the average-adds technique (30g) over N molecules; pressure is force over A
N\,m\,\operatorname{E}[v_x^2] = NkT @@ the gas law (15·chem)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}\big[\tfrac12 mv_x^2\big] = \tfrac12 kT,\ \ \operatorname{E}\big[\tfrac12 mv^2\big] = \tfrac32 kT} \quad \blacksquare @@ no direction is special; average-adds over v² = v_x² + v_y² + v_z²

Wall-push technique: if the gas law holds, each molecule’s energy of motion along any one direction averages ½kT, and its whole energy of motion averages (3/2)kT. Temperature is the average energy of motion, in other units. Now ask how the speeds spread. Suppose the three parts v_x, v_y, v_z are independent (30f), and the chance density depends only on the speed, as no direction is special.

f(v_x)f(v_y)f(v_z) = g(v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2) @@ independent parts; only the speed counts
\phi(a) + \phi(b) + \phi(c) = \psi(a + b + c),\ \ \phi(a) = \ln f(\sqrt a) @@ take ln (17c)
\phi'(a) = \psi'(a + b + c) \text{ for every } b, c \Rightarrow \phi' = -\alpha \text{, a constant} @@ partial slopes (28a): the left does not hold b or c
f(v_x) = \sqrt{\tfrac{\alpha}{\pi}}\,e^{-\alpha v_x^2},\ \ \tfrac{1}{2\alpha} = \operatorname{E}[v_x^2] = \tfrac{kT}{m} @@ a bell with spread kT/m (30j); the wall-push technique fixes α
\htmlClass{new}{F(v) = 4\pi v^2\big(\tfrac{m}{2\pi kT}\big)^{3/2}e^{-mv^2/(2kT)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add up a thin shell of radius v, of area 4πv² (28m)

Speed-spread technique: if the three parts of a molecule’s speed are independent and no direction is special, each part spreads as a bell with spread kT/m, and the speed has density 4πv²(m/2πkT)^(3/2) e^(−mv²/2kT). Its peak is at √(2kT/m), its average at √(8kT/πm), and the root of its average square at √(3kT/m). For the nitrogen in a classroom in Seattle at 20 °C, m = 28.0 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, and those are 417, 471 and 511 m/s. A molecule crosses the room in a second, if nothing is in its way. Everything is in its way: it strikes another molecule several billion times a second.

30then4The jitter of pollen explained

A grain of radius a sits in water with stickiness η. Pulled through the water at speed v, it is held back by a drag fv, with f = 6πηa: a measured law, held in trust. The water’s molecules also strike it from every side, a kick K(t) that does not depend on where the grain is. The grain is a molecule too, only a very large one: by the wall-push technique, carried over as a premise, its energy of motion along x averages ½kT.

Mx'' = -fx' + K(t) @@ drag plus kicks; M the grain’s mass
(xx')' = x'^2 + xx'' @@ the slope of a product (13)
M(xx')' = Mx'^2 - f\,xx' + xK @@ multiply the first line by x; put in the second
M\tfrac{d}{dt}\operatorname{E}[xx'] = kT - f\operatorname{E}[xx'] @@ average-adds (30g); E[xK] = 0 since the kick does not care where the grain is; E[Mx′²] = kT
\operatorname{E}[xx'] \to \tfrac{kT}{f} \text{, closing 63\% of the gap each } \tfrac Mf @@ the target technique (24·then); M/f is about a tenth of a microsecond
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}[x^2] = 2Dt,\ \ D = \tfrac{kT}{f} = \tfrac{kT}{6\pi\eta a}} \quad \blacksquare @@ E[x²]′ = 2E[xx′] = 2kT/f, from a start at x = 0

Jiggle technique: a grain in a liquid, struck at random and held back by drag, spreads so that its average squared distance along a line grows as 2Dt, with D = kT/(6πηa). 30n’s spread-in-time technique proved 14·bio’s x² = 2Dt from independent steps, and left D to the laboratory. The jiggle technique finds D itself, from the temperature, the liquid’s stickiness and the grain’s size.

A grain 1 μm across, in water at 20 °C with η = 1.00 × 10⁻³ Pa s, has D = 4.29 × 10⁻¹³ m²/s. In 30 s it wanders about 5.1 μm along each line: five of its own widths. Turn the technique round. Watch many grains, measure the average of x² over 30 s, and solve for k. Then R/k is the number of molecules in a mole, read off a microscope. Counts of this kind, made from 1908 on, found it near 6 × 10²³, matching the number got from gases, from electric charge and from the glow of hot bodies. After them, the last prominent doubters of molecules gave way.

30then5Decay as chance

17·chem watched a sample of radioactive atoms shrink by a fixed share each half-life. One nucleus, though, either decays or does not. A measured law says what it does: a nucleus that has lasted to time t is no more and no less likely to last a further time s than a new one. It does not age.

G(t) = P(\text{lasts to } t),\ \ P(\text{lasts to } t + s \mid \text{lasts to } t) = G(s) @@ the measured law, in 30f’s given chance
G(t + s) = G(t)\,G(s) @@ the given-chance technique (30f): P(A and B) = P(A | B) P(B)
\htmlClass{new}{G(t) = e^{-\lambda t},\ \text{ halving every } \tfrac{\ln 2}{\lambda}} \quad \blacksquare @@ equal steps give equal factors (17j); the doubling-time technique

No-ageing technique: a nucleus that does not age lasts to time t with chance e^(−λt), so its chance of lasting halves every ln 2/λ, whatever its age. 30m’s forgets-the-wait technique ran the other way, from e^(−λt) to no ageing; the two together say that not ageing and lasting by e^(−λt) are the same thing. A sample shrinks on 17·chem’s timetable because each nucleus in it gambles alone. Count the decays in a short window. Each of N nuclei decays there with a small chance, apart from the rest, so the count is 22·stat’s rare count. Its spread equals its average (the mean-and-spread technique), and its spread width is the root of the average.

t = \tfrac1\lambda\ln\tfrac{A_0}{A} @@ the age from a rate A against a fresh rate A₀ (17·chem)
\tfrac{dt}{dA} = -\tfrac{1}{\lambda A} @@ the slope of ln (17b)
n \text{ counts: spread width } \sqrt n,\ \text{ a share } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n} \text{ of } n @@ the mean-and-spread technique (22·stat)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{width of } t \approx \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a share δ of A moves t by about δ/λ

Age-width technique: an age read from n counted decays is uncertain by about 1/λ times 1/√n. For carbon 14, with a half-life of 5730 years, 1/λ is 8267 years. Forty thousand counts pin an age to about 41 years either way. Along the Copalis River on the Washington coast stand the grey trunks of a ghost forest: red cedars killed when the coast dropped and the tide came in. Carbon counted in their outermost rings placed the drop between about 1680 and 1720. Tree rings matched later narrowed it to the winter of 1699 to 1700, and tsunami records written in Japan narrowed it to one January night.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The total S of squared scaled misses is Pearson’s chi-squared statistic, and the totals of k squared independent bells have the chi-squared distribution with k degrees of freedom (the even-total technique gives its chances for even k). A test that a fit is too good uses the lower tail. The wall-push technique is the heart of the kinetic theory of gases, and the speed-spread technique gives the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of speeds. The jitter is Brownian motion; f = 6πηa is Stokes’s law, D = kT/f the Stokes–Einstein relation, and Mx″ = −fx′ + K the Langevin equation. The number of molecules in a mole is Avogadro’s number. The no-ageing law is the memoryless property, and dating by carbon 14 is radiocarbon dating.

Who said it first

Robert Brown watched particles from the pollen of Clarkia pulchella in 1827, from seed that David Douglas had brought back from the Pacific Northwest; the plant was named for William Clark, who had collected it in 1806. Brown tried dust, glass and soot and found the same motion. Karl Pearson gave the chi-squared test in 1900. Ronald Fisher’s paper of 1936, “Has Mendel’s work been rediscovered?”, found the friar’s whole set of counts too close to expectation, giving a chance near 0.00007 of so close a fit. Daniel Bernoulli derived the gas law from molecules striking walls in 1738; John Herapath, John James Waterston, August Krönig and Rudolf Clausius returned to it from the 1820s to the 1850s. James Clerk Maxwell found the spread of speeds in 1860 by the argument of 30·then.3, and Ludwig Boltzmann rebuilt it from collisions. Albert Einstein (1905) and Marian Smoluchowski (1906) explained Brownian motion; Paul Langevin wrote the drag-and-kick equation in 1908. Jean Perrin measured the jiggle and the settling of grains from 1908, found Avogadro’s number near 6 × 10²³, and won the Nobel Prize in 1926; Wilhelm Ostwald, long a doubter of atoms, gave way in 1909. Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy described decay as a fixed share per unit time in 1902, and Egon von Schweidler read it as chance in 1905. Willard Libby built radiocarbon dating in 1949. Brian Atwater found the ghost forests of the Washington coast in the 1980s; Kenji Satake and colleagues matched the Japanese tsunami of 1700 in 1996, and David Yamaguchi and colleagues matched the tree rings in 1997.

In 30·then.7.5, Fisher (1936) is the accuser, though he blamed an assistant more than the friar; Allan Franklin, Anthony Edwards, Daniel Fairbanks, Daniel Hartl and Teddy Seidenfeld argue the other side in Ending the Mendel–Fisher Controversy (2008).
