31b · proof

The closest point

Once arrows have lengths, one question comes up everywhere: which point of a flat piece of the space is closest to a given arrow? A line through the points of a scatter, a straight line hugging a curve, a short recipe standing in for a long one: each is a closest point. With a product, the answer is a shadow. Drop the arrow straight down onto the flat piece, and the miss stands at right angles to everything in it. When the flat piece has measuring directions at right angles to each other, the shadow is a sum of one reading along each, and nothing has to be solved. When it does not, the right angles still give a short list of samenesses that fix the answer. Rung thirteen’s best line through a scatter turns out to be exactly this shadow, taken in a space with one direction for every point of the data, and the facts it found about its misses were right angles all along.

The closest point of a flat piece of the space is the shadow, the one point whose miss is at right angles to the whole piece. With measuring directions at right angles, the shadow is a sum of readings. Without them, the right angles give samenesses to solve. The best line through a scatter is such a shadow.

31b1The closest point on a line

Fix a product, and an arrow e of size 1. The arrows te, for every number t, make a line through 0. Which of them is closest to f? 31a3’s proof already took away the part of f along g. Here that part gets its job.

Closest-point technique: if ‖e‖ = 1, the point of the line through e closest to f is ⟨f, e⟩e, and the miss f − ⟨f, e⟩e is at right angles to e. The arrow ⟨f, e⟩e is the shadow of f on the line, as 27i2 named it for lists.

p = \langle f, e\rangle e,\ \ \langle f - p,\ e\rangle = \langle f, e\rangle - \langle f, e\rangle\langle e, e\rangle = 0 @@ straight in the first slot; ⟨e, e⟩ = 1
f - te = (f - p) + (p - te),\ \ p - te = (\langle f, e\rangle - t)\,e @@ the miss, split
\|f - te\|^2 = \|f - p\|^2 + |\langle f, e\rangle - t|^2 @@ the square-sum technique (31a1): f − p ⊥ e
\htmlClass{new}{\|f - te\| \ge \|f - p\|} \quad \blacksquare @@ equal only at t = ⟨f, e⟩, where the second square is 0

For an arrow g ≠ 0 that is not of size 1, use e = g/‖g‖: the shadow is (⟨f, g⟩/‖g‖²)g. On [0, 1], with the area product, the multiple of x closest to x² is (1/4)/(1/3) = 3/4 of it. The miss x² − (3/4)x has squared size 1/5 − 3/16 = 1/80.

31b2The closest point in a flat piece

From here on, a right-angled list is a list e₁, …, eₙ of arrows of size 1, each at right angles to the others. Let W be the space they build. 27i2’s read-off technique read lists off along such directions. With any product the same reading works, and it does more: it finds the closest point of W to an arrow that is not in W.

Shadow-sum technique: if e₁, …, eₙ is a right-angled list building W, the point of W closest to f is Pf = ⟨f, e₁⟩e₁ + ⋯ + ⟨f, eₙ⟩eₙ; the miss f − Pf is at right angles to every arrow of W, and no other point of W is as close.

\langle f - Pf,\ e_k\rangle = \langle f, e_k\rangle - \textstyle\sum_j \langle f, e_j\rangle\langle e_j, e_k\rangle = \langle f, e_k\rangle - \langle f, e_k\rangle = 0 @@ ⟨eⱼ, eₖ⟩ is 1 when j = k and 0 otherwise
f - Pf \perp w \text{ for every } w = \textstyle\sum c_ke_k \text{ in } W @@ straight in the second slot, up to conjugates (31a2)
\|f - w\|^2 = \|f - Pf\|^2 + \|Pf - w\|^2 @@ the square-sum technique: Pf − w is in W
\htmlClass{new}{\|f - w\| > \|f - Pf\| \text{ for every } w \ne Pf \text{ in } W} \quad \blacksquare @@ ‖Pf − w‖ > 0 when w ≠ Pf (31a1)

So f splits into a part in W and a part in W⊥ (27i2): f = Pf + (f − Pf). The map P is the shadow map onto W. Doing it twice is doing it once, since Pf is already in W.

e_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \ e_2 = \sqrt{\tfrac32}\,x @@ sizes 1 under ∫ from −1 to 1 (31a1), at right angles
\langle e^x, 1\rangle = e - e^{-1} \approx 2.3504,\ \ \langle e^x, x\rangle = 2e^{-1} \approx 0.7358 @@ areas (17); the second by parts (16)
Pe^x = \tfrac{2.3504}{2} + \tfrac{0.7358}{2/3}\,x @@ the shadow-sum technique, written with 1 and x
\htmlClass{new}{Pe^x \approx 1.1752 + 1.1036\,x,\ \ \|e^x - Pe^x\| \approx 0.230} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tangent line 1 + x misses by about 0.348

The tangent line of rung 13 is best at one point. The shadow is best over the whole interval, in the product’s sense: it gives up the exact touch at 0 to miss less everywhere else.

31b3When the directions are not at right angles

Let v₁, …, vₖ be free arrows building W, at whatever angles. The closest point is still w = c₁v₁ + ⋯ + cₖvₖ with the miss at right angles to W, because the proof of 31b2 used only that. Asking for the right angles one direction at a time gives k samenesses in the k unknowns cⱼ.

Miss-at-right-angles technique: let v₁, …, vₖ be free and G the grid with Gᵢⱼ = ⟨vⱼ, vᵢ⟩. Then w = Σ cⱼvⱼ is the point of W closest to f exactly when Gc = b, where bᵢ = ⟨f, vᵢ⟩, and G is undoable, so there is exactly one such c.

f - w \perp W \iff \langle f - w,\ v_i\rangle = 0 \text{ for each } i @@ W is built by the vᵢ; straight in the second slot
\iff \textstyle\sum_j c_j\langle v_j, v_i\rangle = \langle f, v_i\rangle,\ \text{so } Gc = b @@ straight in the first slot
Gc = 0 \Rightarrow \big\|\textstyle\sum c_jv_j\big\|^2 = \sum_i \bar c_i (Gc)_i = 0 @@ expand the size; the vⱼ are free, so every cⱼ = 0
G \text{ loses only } 0,\ \text{so it is undoable} @@ the undoable-group technique (27d3)
\htmlClass{new}{c = G^{-1}b \text{ gives the closest point, and only it}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the closest point has its miss at right angles, as in 31b2

For lists of n numbers with the dot, put the vⱼ as the columns of a grid X with n rows and k columns. Then G = XᵀX and b = Xᵀf, and the samenesses read XᵀXc = Xᵀf.

31b4The best line is a shadow

13·stat fitted a line y = k + bx to points (xᵢ, yᵢ), i = 1 to n, by making Σ eᵢ² least. Read the data as two arrows of lists of n numbers: y = (y₁, …, yₙ) and x = (x₁, …, xₙ), with 1 = (1, …, 1). The fitted values k1 + bx run over the flat piece built by 1 and x, and Σ eᵢ² is the squared size of the miss under the dot. So the best line is the shadow of y on that piece.

X = [\,1\ \ x\,],\ \ X^{\mathsf T}X = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum x_i \\ \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2\end{pmatrix},\ \ X^{\mathsf T}y = \begin{pmatrix}\sum y_i \\ \sum x_iy_i\end{pmatrix} @@ the miss-at-right-angles technique, with v₁ = 1 and v₂ = x
e \perp 1:\ \ \textstyle\sum_i e_i = 0;\ \ e \perp x:\ \ \sum_i x_ie_i = 0 @@ the miss at right angles to the piece: 13·stat’s residuals technique, read as right angles
S_{yy} = \textstyle\sum(\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \sum e_i^2 @@ the square-sum technique: ŷ − ȳ1 lies in the piece, e at right angles to it
x: 1, 2, 3, 4;\ \ y: 2, 1, 4, 3 @@ 8·stat’s lists
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 10 \\ 10 & 30\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} k \\ b\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 28\end{pmatrix} @@ Σxᵢ = 10, Σxᵢ² = 30, Σyᵢ = 10, Σxᵢyᵢ = 28
\htmlClass{new}{k = 1,\ \ b = 0.6} \quad \blacksquare @@ solved by elimination (27j); 13·stat’s slope Sₓᵧ/Sₓₓ = 3/5 agrees

13·stat found the line by completing a square, one unknown at a time. Here it is found with no slopes and no squares completed, and the same words fit a curve through the data as well as a line: put x² beside 1 and x as a third column, and the best parabola is the shadow on a piece with three directions. What the shadow cannot do is choose the columns. Which columns to put in is a guess about the world, and 31·stat asks how it can go wrong.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The shadow of f on W is its orthogonal projection, and P is the orthogonal projector; the fact that the projection is the closest point is the best approximation property, and the miss at right angles is the orthogonality principle. A right-angled list is an orthonormal set. The samenesses Gc = b are the normal equations, and G is the Gram matrix. Fitting by the shadow of y on the columns of X is least squares; the columns make up the design matrix, and the shadow is often written X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, with the hat matrix in the middle.

Who said it first

Adrien-Marie Legendre published least squares in 1805, and Carl Friedrich Gauss claimed it from 1795 and published his account in 1809; neither drew the shadow. Jørgen Pedersen Gram studied the samenesses and the grid that bears his name in 1883, fitting series by least squares. The reading of least squares as a projection in a space of n directions became standard in the 20th century, with Ronald Fisher’s geometric pictures of the 1910s and 1920s.

A proof that is not allowed

“The miss f − Pf is at right angles to e₁ and to e₂, so it is at right angles to everything.” Not allowed: it is at right angles to everything in the space e₁ and e₂ build, and to nothing in particular outside it.
