Why Does More Choice Take Longer?

Why does more choice take longer? A driver facing two exits picks faster than one facing eight. A cook finds the salt at once in a rack of three jars and hunts in a rack of thirty. Yet the time does not grow in step with the choices: eight takes far less than four times as long as two. This page finds that time grows by a fixed amount for each doubling of the choices (17), sees the doubling as yes-or-no questions, finds that the smallest change we notice grows with what is already there, turns that into feeling as a log of strength with 17i, and ends with the powers that fit some senses better.

Picking among n equally likely choices takes a + b log₂(n + 1): each doubling adds the time of one more yes-or-no question. The smallest change we notice is a fixed share of the stimulus, so adding up noticeable steps gives feeling as a log of strength. For many senses a power, feeling ∝ strength^a, fits better, a straight line on log-log paper.

26bio2Halving the Choices as Yes-or-No Questions

To find one of 8 equal choices, ask: is it in the left half? Then: in the left half of that? Three questions settle it, because each halves what is left.

2^q = n + 1 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{q = \log_2(n + 1)} @@ the log undoes the power (17)

Bits-per-choice technique: choosing among N equally likely things takes a fixed time plus a fixed time for each yes-or-no question needed, log₂ N; the mind seems to narrow the choices by halves. A designer laying out a menu or a control panel uses this: one menu of 16 items is quicker than four menus of 4 opened in turn, though both settle 4 bits, because each menu opened costs its own fixed start.

26bio3The Least Change You Can Notice Grows With the Stimulus

Hold a 100 g weight, then one slightly heavier: most people notice a difference at about 102 g. Holding 1,000 g, they need about 1,020 g. The smallest noticeable change is not a fixed amount but a fixed share.

\Delta I = k\,I @@ k about 0.02 for lifted weights
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{\Delta I}{I} = k} @@ the same share at every strength

26bio4Feeling as the Log of Strength

Count feeling in noticeable steps: each step is the same to the person, and each step is the same share of the strength. Adding up shares is adding up dI/I, whose total is a log (17i).

dS = c\,\dfrac{dI}{I} @@ one noticeable step, the same size each time
\htmlClass{new}{S = c\,\ln\dfrac{I}{I_0}} @@ the slope of ln is 1/x (17i); I₀ the faintest felt

If each noticeable step is a fixed share of the strength, felt strength grows as the log of the real strength. That is why loudness is measured in decibels: ten times the strength of a sound sounds like one more fixed step, not ten times louder.

26bio5Powers Instead of Logs

When people are asked instead to give numbers to how strong things feel, their answers fit a power of the strength better than a log, with a different power for each sense.

S = k\,I^a @@ 
\log S = \log k + a\log I @@ a straight line on log-log paper (17)
\htmlClass{new}{a \approx 0.67 \text{ loudness},\ \ 1 \text{ length},\ \ 3.5 \text{ electric shock}} @@ below 1 squeezes, above 1 stretches

Power or log, felt strength squeezes a huge range of real strength into a small range of feeling, which is how eyes and ears work from a whisper to a jet engine. A sound engineer setting a volume knob makes it turn in equal felt steps, not equal strength.

Accepted names

The choice time rule is the Hick-Hyman law; the least noticeable change is the just-noticeable difference, its fixed share is Weber’s law, the log is Fechner’s law, and the power Stevens’ power law. The study of felt strength is psychophysics.

Who said it first

William Hick published the choice time rule in 1952 and Ray Hyman extended it in 1953; Gustav Fechner set out the log in 1860; Stanley Smith Stevens the powers in 1957 (not yet revisited for the dates).
