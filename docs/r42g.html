42g · proof

The Best Corner

A small glass works has three shops. One frames doors, one frames windows, and the third cuts and fits the glass for both. Each shop has so many hours a week. A batch of doors earns so much, a batch of windows so much more, and each uses hours in two of the shops. How many of each should it make? Rung 18 answered a question of this shape with two unknowns on a sheet of paper: the best plan sits at a corner of the region the limits cut out. A real plan has hundreds of unknowns, and the sheet becomes a space of that many directions; the corners are still where to look, and this step proves it. Then it finds that every such question has a twin. Ask what each shop’s hour is worth, the least prices that would make the shops’ hours at least as valuable as anything they can make. Every set of such prices bounds every plan from above. And the best plan and the best prices meet at exactly the same number. The proof of that runs through a recipe for taking one unknown out of a list of limits, and it ends with a single fact: a list of limits has no answer exactly when a combination of them says that zero is less than a negative number.

Maximise a sum of the unknowns, each times a price, under limits that are sums at most a bound, with every unknown at least 0. If the region has a corner and the sum is bounded, a corner is best. Prices for the limits that cover every unknown’s worth bound every plan. A list of limits has no answer exactly when a combination with weights at least 0 reads 0 at most a negative number. So the best plan and the best prices are equal.

42g1Plans and Corners

A plan is a list x = (x₁, …, xₙ) of numbers. A limit reads a₁x₁ + ⋯ + aₙxₙ ≤ b. A program asks for the largest value of c · x (27i) over the plans with Ax ≤ b and x ≥ 0, where A is an m × n grid (27d), b a list of m bounds, and ≤ between lists means each entry. The plans that obey every limit are the allowed plans. The accepted name of the whole question is a linear program.

\text{doors } x,\ \text{windows } y;\ \text{earn } 3x + 5y @@ in thousands of dollars per batch
x \le 4,\ \ 2y \le 12,\ \ 3x + 2y \le 18,\ \ x, y \ge 0 @@ hours of the door shop, the window shop, the glass shop
\text{corners } (0,0), (4,0), (4,3), (2,6), (0,6) \text{ give } 0, 12, 27, 36, 30 @@ 18g’s best-corner technique, in the plane
\htmlClass{new}{(2, 6):\ 36} \quad \blacksquare @@ the best corner

In n unknowns, a corner is an allowed plan at which some n of the m + n limits, counting each xⱼ ≥ 0, hold with equality and have rows that make a free list (27b). Those n equations have exactly one answer, so there are at most as many corners as ways to choose n of the m + n limits.

Tight-corner technique: if the allowed plans have a corner and c · x is bounded above on them, some corner gives the largest value.

x \text{ allowed, not a corner; its tight rows build less than all } n \text{ directions} @@ the rows of the limits holding with equality there
d \ne 0 \text{ at right angles to every tight row, with } c \cdot d \ge 0 @@ the kernel is not 0 (27c); take −d if needed
x + t\,d \text{ stays allowed for small } t \ge 0 \text{; follow it until a new limit is tight} @@ tight limits stay tight; loose ones stay loose a while
\text{if none ever tightens: } c \cdot d > 0 \text{ is unbounded, and } c \cdot d = 0 \text{ go along } -d @@ the whole line cannot fit: x ≥ 0 stops one side unless d = 0
x' \text{ allowed, } c \cdot x' \ge c \cdot x \text{, with one more tight row, free from the old ones} @@ d is at right angles to the old rows and not to the new one
\htmlClass{new}{\text{after at most } n \text{ moves a corner at least as good; the best of the finitely many corners is best}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each move adds a free row

18g’s best-corner technique is the case n = 2, on a bounded region. The difference here is that the region need not be bounded, only the value, and the number of directions is anything.

42g2Prices

Put a price yᵢ ≥ 0 on each limit: what an hour of shop i is worth. A plan’s product uses up hours, and column j of A says how many of each shop’s hours unit j uses. The prices cover unit j when the hours it uses are worth at least what it earns. With Aᵀ for the grid read with rows as columns, the twin program asks for the least value of b · y over the y with Aᵀy ≥ c and y ≥ 0.

Price-bound technique: if x is an allowed plan and y covering prices, then c · x ≤ b · y.

c \cdot x \le (A^{\mathsf T} y) \cdot x @@ c ≤ Aᵀy entry by entry, and x ≥ 0
= y \cdot (A x) @@ the same sum of aᵢⱼ yᵢ xⱼ, taken by rows instead of columns
\htmlClass{new}{\le y \cdot b} \quad \blacksquare @@ Ax ≤ b entry by entry, and y ≥ 0
  
p + 3r \ge 3,\ \ 2q + 2r \ge 5,\ \ p, q, r \ge 0 @@ doors use 1 hour of the door shop and 3 of glass; windows 2 of the window shop and 2 of glass
y = (0, \tfrac32, 1):\ \ 4 \cdot 0 + 12 \cdot \tfrac32 + 18 \cdot 1 = 36 @@ covering: 0 + 3 = 3, 3 + 2 = 5
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every plan earns at most } 36\text{, and } (2, 6) \text{ earns } 36} \quad \blacksquare @@ the price-bound technique: both are best

The door shop’s price is 0: at the best plan its 4 hours are not all used, and an hour more of it would earn nothing. Shops with slack have price 0, and units with a positive amount are covered exactly. That pattern holds at every pair of best answers; 42g.6.3 asks for its proof.

42g3Taking One Unknown Out

A list of limits Σⱼ aᵢⱼxⱼ ≤ bᵢ, i = 1 to m, may have no answer at all. To settle it, sort the limits by the sign of xₙ’s coefficient. A positive one bounds xₙ above, a negative one below, and a zero one leaves it free.

One-out technique: the limits have an answer exactly when the list made of the zero ones, together with one limit for each pair of an upper and a lower one, has an answer in x₁, …, xₙ₋₁; each new limit is a sum of two old ones with weights at least 0, made so that xₙ drops out. Given an answer of the new list, every lower bound on xₙ is then at most every upper bound, so an xₙ between the largest lower and the smallest upper obeys them all.

No-answer technique: Ax ≤ b has no answer exactly when some y ≥ 0 has yᵀA = 0 and y · b < 0.

y \ge 0,\ y^{\mathsf T}A = 0,\ y \cdot b < 0,\ \text{and } Ax \le b @@ suppose both
0 = y^{\mathsf T}A x \le y \cdot b < 0 @@ weights at least 0 keep ≤
\bot @@ so no answer
\text{other way: take out } x_n, x_{n-1}, \dots, x_1 \text{ by the one-out technique} @@ induction on n (4c)
\text{each limit at the end reads } 0 \le b' \text{ and is } y^{\mathsf T}(Ax \le b) \text{ for some } y \ge 0 @@ weights at least 0, multiplied and added
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no answer} \Rightarrow \text{some final limit has } b' < 0:\ \text{its } y \text{ is the certificate}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-out technique, run back up

So a “no” always comes with a short reason anyone can check, as the marriage technique’s did. Taking unknowns out by the one-out technique is honest and slow: the number of limits can square at every step.

42g4Equal Prices

Equal-prices technique: if the program has an allowed plan and its twin has covering prices, both have best answers, and the best plan earns exactly the least price total.

\text{seek } x, y:\ Ax \le b,\ -x \le 0,\ -A^{\mathsf T}y \le -c,\ -y \le 0,\ -c \cdot x + b \cdot y \le 0 @@ an answer is a plan and prices with c · x ≥ b · y
\text{an answer gives } c \cdot x = b \cdot y \text{, both best} @@ the price-bound technique gives ≤ too
\text{no answer} \Rightarrow u, p, w, q, \lambda \ge 0:\ A^{\mathsf T}u \ge \lambda c,\ A w \le \lambda b,\ b \cdot u < c \cdot w @@ the no-answer technique, the weights read off by unknown
\lambda > 0:\ u/\lambda \text{ covers},\ w/\lambda \text{ allowed},\ c \cdot w \le b \cdot u @@ the price-bound technique; against the last line
\lambda = 0:\ b \cdot u \ge (A x_0) \cdot u \ge 0 \ge (A^{\mathsf T} y_0) \cdot w \ge c \cdot w @@ x₀ allowed and y₀ covering, given; against b · u < c · w
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an answer exists: } \max c \cdot x = \min b \cdot y} \quad \blacksquare @@ both cases end in ⊥

Two questions, one about making and one about pricing, have one answer. Rung 28’s lines-up technique priced a single smooth limit by the stretch λ of its gradient; here every limit gets a price, and the limits may have corners. 42n finds both answers at once by hand.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A program is a linear program, its allowed plans the feasible region (a convex polyhedron), and a corner a vertex or basic feasible solution. The twin is the dual program, and covering prices are dual feasible solutions, or shadow prices. The price-bound technique is weak duality; the equal-prices technique is strong duality. The one-out technique is Fourier–Motzkin elimination, and the no-answer technique is a form of Farkas's lemma. The pattern of 42g.6.3 is complementary slackness. The glass works is the Wyndor Glass example from an operations research textbook of 1967.

Who said it first

Joseph Fourier described elimination for inequalities in 1826; Theodore Motzkin rediscovered it in 1936. Gyula Farkas proved his lemma in 1902. Leonid Kantorovich wrote on planning production by programs in 1939, in Leningrad. George Dantzig set out the general question and his recipe in 1947, and John von Neumann, told of it, guessed the twin program at once, from his work on games. Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans shared the economics prize of 1975.

A proof that is not allowed

“The value rises as we move from (0, 0) toward (2, 6), and falls beyond it, so (2, 6) is best.” Not allowed: rising along one route and falling beyond is a fact about that route. The proof is either the list of every corner, by the tight-corner technique, or a set of covering prices with total 36, by the price-bound technique. The second is shorter and is the one that scales.
