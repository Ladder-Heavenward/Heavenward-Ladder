How Do Polarized Sunglasses Cut Glare?

How do polarized sunglasses cut glare? On a bright afternoon on Lake Tahoe the water throws the sun back in a white sheet, and an angler cannot see the fish a metre down. Put on polarized sunglasses and the sheet fades, and the stones and the fish come up through it. Tilt your head sideways and the glare comes back. Ordinary dark glasses dim everything alike; these dim the glare far more than the rest. 21·then found that a filter keeps cos² of the turn between the light’s line of shaking and its own, and that a third filter between two crossed ones lets light through. What makes the question hard is why light off water should shake along any one line at all, when sunlight shakes every way at once. This page makes the filter a shadow map, finds what it does to light that shakes every way, follows light through stacks of filters as products of shadow maps, and then finds the angle at which water sends back light that shakes only one way.

A filter is the shadow map onto its line: it keeps the part of the shaking along its line, cos² of the strength, and sends sunlight’s mixed shaking through at one half. Filters in a row multiply as shadow maps, and the last one sets the line. Light off water at the angle whose tangent is the water’s n shakes only level, so a filter with an upright line blocks it.

31phys1A Filter Is a Shadow Map

Light travelling straight at you shakes in the plane across its path. At one place and one moment its shaking is a vector E in that plane, and its brightness is |E|², averaged over the shaking. A filter has a line, along a vector d of size 1. It keeps the part of E along d and soaks up the rest.

Filter-shadow technique: a filter with line d acts on the shaking by the shadow map P = ddᵀ, which is symmetric and does the same thing twice as once; the kept brightness is |PE|² = |E|² cos²θ, and kept plus lost is all of it. Here θ (little theta) means the angle between the light's shaking and the filter's line.

PE = (E\cdot d)\,d @@ the closest-point technique, on the line through d (31b1)
P^{\mathsf T} = (dd^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = dd^{\mathsf T},\ \ P^2 = d(d^{\mathsf T}d)d^{\mathsf T} = P @@ dᵀd = 1
|PE|^2 = (E\cdot d)^2 = |E|^2\cos^2\theta @@ θ the angle between E and d
\htmlClass{new}{|PE|^2 + |(I - P)E|^2 = |E|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shadow-sum technique (31b2): the part kept and the part across

So 21·then’s cos² is the size of a shadow, squared. Sunlight is not one vector. Its line of shaking wanders through every angle, many millions of times faster than any eye can follow, with no angle favoured.

Mixed-light technique: light whose line of shaking takes every angle alike passes any filter at one half of its brightness, and leaves it shaking along the filter’s line.

\text{kept} = |E|^2 \cdot \tfrac1{2\pi}\textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta @@ the average over every angle alike (16)
\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1,\ \ \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 @@ a quarter turn moves one onto the other
\htmlClass{new}{\text{kept} = \tfrac12|E|^2,\ \text{along } d} \quad \blacksquare @@ each average is a half

31phys2Filters in a Row

Two filters with lines d₁ and then d₂ act by P₂P₁. Light leaving the first shakes along d₁, so the second keeps cos² of the angle between the lines.

Stack technique: filters with lines d₁, …, dₖ, in that order, send E to (E · d₁)(d₁ · d₂)⋯(dₖ₋₁ · dₖ)dₖ; the light leaves along the last line, and two filters’ maps give the same result in either order only when their lines are the same or at right angles.

P_2P_1E = (E\cdot d_1)\,P_2d_1 = (E\cdot d_1)(d_1\cdot d_2)\,d_2 @@ the filter-shadow technique, twice
P_k\cdots P_1E = (E\cdot d_1)(d_1\cdot d_2)\cdots(d_{k-1}\cdot d_k)\,d_k @@ the same, k times
P_1P_2 = (d_1\cdot d_2)\,d_1d_2^{\mathsf T},\ \ P_2P_1 = (d_1\cdot d_2)\,d_2d_1^{\mathsf T} @@ the two orders
\htmlClass{new}{P_1P_2 = P_2P_1 \iff d_1\cdot d_2 = 0 \text{ or } d_1 = \pm d_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ otherwise one sends light along d₁, the other along d₂
  
0^\circ:\ \tfrac12 @@ the mixed-light technique
0^\circ \to 45^\circ:\ \tfrac12\cos^2 45^\circ = \tfrac14 @@ the stack technique
0^\circ \to 45^\circ \to 90^\circ:\ \tfrac14\cos^2 45^\circ = \tfrac18 @@ and the light now shakes at 90°
\htmlClass{new}{0^\circ \to 90^\circ:\ \cos 90^\circ = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ take the middle one out, and nothing passes

A shadow map is never undone. P has determinant 0: the part across the line is gone, and no later filter can bring it back. The middle filter does not add light; it lets through a part that the crossed pair would have thrown away whole.

31phys3Glare off Water

Light reflected off water comes from the water itself. The light that goes in sets the charges at the surface shaking along its own shaking, and those charges send out the reflected light. This page holds in trust one fact about such sending: a shaking charge sends no light along its own line of shaking, and sends most at right angles to it.

Split the shaking of the light that goes in into two parts at right angles: the level part, along the water and across the plane that holds the rays, and the part in that plane. The level part stays level in the water, and the reflected ray is across it, so it is sent back. The part in the plane shakes, in the water, at right angles to the ray going down.

Level-glare technique: if the sun’s rays meet water of index n at the angle θ from straight down with tan θ = n, the reflected light shakes only level.

\sin\theta = n\sin\varphi @@ the kept-sine technique (23·phys); φ the angle of the ray in the water
\text{reflected ray at right angles to the ray in the water} \iff \theta + \varphi = 90^\circ @@ the reflected ray leaves at θ on the other side
\Rightarrow \sin\theta = n\cos\theta,\ \ \tan\theta = n @@ sin φ = cos θ
\text{in-plane shaking in the water lies along the reflected ray} @@ it is at right angles to the ray going down, and in the plane
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no in-plane part is sent back: the glare is level}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fact held in trust

For water, n ≈ 1.33 and the angle is about 53° from straight down, the sun 37° above the water. At other angles a little of the in-plane part is sent back too, and the glare is mostly level. Sunglasses with an upright line keep cos² of 90° of the level part, which is nothing, and half of the mixed light from the fish and the stones. Tilt your head and the line tilts with it, and the level glare comes back as cos² of the new angle from level.

The accepted names

The line of shaking is the polarisation, and a filter is a linear polariser, whose action is an orthogonal projection; the cos² law is Malus’s law. Light whose shaking takes every angle alike is unpolarised light. The angle with tan θ = n is Brewster’s angle, and the level shaking is s-polarisation (from the German senkrecht, at right angles to the plane of the rays), the in-plane one p-polarisation. How much of each is reflected at every angle is given by the Fresnel equations. That a shaking charge sends nothing along its line is the dipole radiation pattern, with brightness going as sin² of the angle from the line.

Who said it first

Étienne-Louis Malus found in 1808 that reflected light is polarised, looking at the sunset on the windows of the Luxembourg Palace through a crystal, and published the cos² law in 1809. David Brewster found the angle with tan θ = n in 1815. Augustin-Jean Fresnel gave the reflected shares in 1823. Edwin Land made the first sheet polariser in 1929, and polarized sunglasses in 1936.
