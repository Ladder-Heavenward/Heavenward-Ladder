8d · support

Adding and multiplying cuts

To say that a real squares to 2, or that counting passes a real by more than 1, you need arithmetic on the line. This step builds it from the fractions, one operation at a time. It builds the sum of two cuts, the negative of a cut, the product of two positive cuts and then of any two, and the reciprocal. Each is a set of fractions, and each must be proved a cut. Then the twelve techniques of 5c are proved for the reals. Some take a line, because they are rules about fractions in disguise. Some take care, because the product had to be defined by signs. Last, order and arithmetic are shown to agree. The result: the real numbers obey the twelve techniques and have no holes.

Add cuts by adding their fractions. Negate and invert through the fractions a cut misses, taking care never to keep a greatest member. Multiply positive cuts by multiplying positive fractions; let the signs ride outside.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 4g (m < S(m)), 4h (times-does-not-lessen), 5a (member-with-a-0), 5c and 5d (the twelve techniques, one-negative, negative-of-a-negative, negative-of-a-sum, negative-out on ℚ, cancel for times on ℚ), 5e (keep-order for plus on ℚ, keep-order for times on ℚ, compare-by-cross-multiplying, another-between), 7·cb4 (least-member-for-any-set), 7d (definitions by cases), 8a (cuts, members-below-non-members, members-are-the-fractions-below, fractions-keep-order, chain for less on ℚ), 8b (three-way-order on ℝ, max) and 8c (no-holes). 8e and 8f compute with reals; 8g moves terms across < on the line.

8d1What has to be built

Stands on 5c (the twelve techniques on ℚ) and 8a (cuts).

Rung 5 built the fractions' operations on representatives and checked that the choice of representative did not matter. Cuts have no representatives: a cut is one set. So each operation is a sift of ℚ, and the work is to check the four conditions. The plan: sums (easy), then negatives (one subtle clause). Then two stepping techniques about walking through a cut. Then products of positives, products by signs, reciprocals, and then the rules. Two techniques about fractions come first.

And the reciprocal of a positive fraction is positive, and reciprocals of positives turn order about. Positive-reciprocal technique on ℚ: for a positive s, s⁻¹ is positive; and 0 < x < y ⇒ y⁻¹ < x⁻¹. Stands on 8d1, 8a4 (the four conditions) and 2g (the short form).

Three rules come almost free. Turn-about technique for plus on ℝ: α + β = β + α, since r + s = s + r by turn-about for plus on ℚ, and then the two sets are the same by same-members. 8d3The negative of a cut, carefully

Stands on 8d2 and 5d (negative of a negative).

So the negatives are −r and everything below: a set with a greatest member, −r, and so not a cut. The fix is to drop that one possible greatest member, by asking for a little room:

From here on, for a real α, −α names {r ∈ ℚ : ∃s ∈ ℚ (r < s ∧ −s ∉ α)}: the fractions r with some s above r whose negative α misses. Read it “negative α”, or “minus α”. And α − β names α + (−β).

8d4Order and adding

Stands on 8d3 and 8b2 (three-way order).

From here on, a real α is positive when 0* < α, and negative when α < 0*. By members-are-the-fractions-below, α is positive exactly when 0 ∈ α.

Order-and-arithmetic-agree technique, first part: α ≤ β ⇒ α + σ ≤ β + σ; α < β ⇒ α + σ < β + σ; and α < β ⇔ β − α is positive. If α ⊆ β, every r + t with r ∈ α is a member of β + σ: the first. Suppose also α + σ = β + σ. Add −σ to both sides and use regroup for plus on ℝ, negative on ℝ, plus-zero on ℝ: α = β, a clash. That is the second. For the third, add −α to both sides of α < β: 0* < β − α. And back, add α. ∎ So the line's order, made from ⊆, is 5c's order made from “positive”. Three-way technique on ℝ: exactly one of α = 0*, α positive, −α positive. It follows by three-way-order on ℝ with 0*, and order-and-arithmetic-agree's third part, which turns α < 0* into 0* < −α. Positive-sum technique on ℝ: a sum of positives is positive. 0* < β gives α < α + β by order-and-arithmetic-agree. And 0* < α, so 0* < α + β by at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ. ∎

8d6Products and reciprocals, by signs

Stands on 8d5, 8b2 (three-way order) and 7d8 (definition by cases).

From here on, for reals α and β, α · β, also written αβ, names one of five things. It is the positive product of positive-product-is-a-cut when α and β are positive. It is 0* when α = 0* or β = 0*. It is −((−α)β) when α is negative and β positive. It is −(α(−β)) when α is positive and β negative. It is (−α)(−β) when both are negative. Read it “α times β”. For a real α other than 0*, α⁻¹ names α′ of positive-reciprocal when α is positive, and −((−α)′) when α is negative. Read it “the reciprocal of α”. And α/β names αβ⁻¹, for β ≠ 0*. Like +, the marks · and ⁻¹ are operators: · has two gaps and ⁻¹ one, and each result is a real.

8d8The fractions keep their arithmetic

Stands on 8d7 and 8a8 (fractions-keep-order).

Fraction-products-agree technique: (rs)* = r*s* for rationals r and s. Take positive r and s first. A member xy of r*s* with 0 < x < r, 0 < y < s is below rs (keep-order for times on ℚ twice). And for 0 < p < rs, pick x with p/s < x < r (another-between); then y = p/x < s, and p = xy. Other signs follow by signs-come-out and fraction-negatives-agree, and 0 by anything-times-zero. 8d9A number system with no holes

Stands on 8d7, 8d8 and 8c3 (no-holes).

From here on, a regular number system is a set with an adding, a multiplying, a 0, a 1, negatives, reciprocals of everything but 0, and a meaning for positive, in which the twelve techniques of arithmetic hold. Together they make induction over the series entirely regular there: whatever 5d proved from the twelve alone holds in every regular number system without being proved again. It has no holes when every part of it with a member and an upper bound has a least upper bound. Upper bounds are read with “w < b when b − w is positive”.

No-holes-system technique: ℝ, with the + and · of this step, 0 and 1, −α, α⁻¹ and positive, is a regular number system with no holes. The twelve techniques are the table above. Having no holes is 8c's no-holes technique, since by order-and-arithmetic-agree the order read from “positive” is the order ⊆ that no-holes used. ∎ The fractions are a regular number system with holes (8c's no-least-fraction-bound). There is a further technique: any two regular number systems with no holes are copies of each other, matched member for member with sums, products and order carried. The ladder does not need it, and leaves it there.

8d11Running it backwards

Stands on 8d1 to 8d9.

Handed a set of fractions offered as an operation on cuts, check (iv) first. A greatest member is the usual flaw, and the cure is to ask for room. Wanting a rule for reals, ask what kind of rule it is. Is it a rule about members in disguise (sums, positive products)? Then reduce to ℚ by same-members. Is it about signs? The accepted names

A regular number system is an ordered field. One with no holes is a complete ordered field, and the no-holes-system technique says that ℝ is one.

Who said it first

The inventor of cuts is Richard Dedekind.

Dedekind claimed that once numbers are cuts, real proofs become possible of facts such as √2 · √3 = √6, which, so far as he knew, no one had ever proved before.

Dedekind, Continuity and Irrational Numbers (1872), §6, after Beman’s translation (1901); paraphrase

Accepted names: the turn-about techniques are the commutative laws; the regroup techniques are the associative laws; the spread technique is the distributive law; plus-zero and times-one name the additive and multiplicative identities; the negative and the reciprocal are the additive and multiplicative inverses.
