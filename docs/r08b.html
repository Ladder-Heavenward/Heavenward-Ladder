8b · proof

Any Two Reals Compare

Order on the line is being a part of, and being a part of is only a partial order. Of two sets of cats, neither need be a part of the other. Cuts are special. Two cuts are lower heaps of splittings of the same fractions, and of two lower heaps one is always inside the other. The proof is five lines, and its first line is rung 7's either-way principle: either the first cut is a part of the second, or it is not. This step proves it. Then it shows that the either-way principle cannot be avoided: if any two reals compare, every statement is true or false.

If one cut is not a part of a second, some fraction of the first lies outside the second. Everything in the second sits below that fraction, so everything in the second is in the first.

8b1The Claim

Any-two-compare technique on ℝ: of any two reals, one is at most the other. In marks: . With ≤ unpacked it says: of any two cuts, one is a part of the other.

\text{let } \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb R @@ speak of anything
\alpha \subseteq \beta \lor \neg(\alpha \subseteq \beta) @@ either-way (7d): split on the first being a part of the second
\textbf{case } \alpha \subseteq \beta:\ \ \alpha \le \beta @@ ︿ ≤
\textbf{case } \neg(\alpha \subseteq \beta): @@ the other case
\quad \exists r\,(r \in \alpha \land r \notin \beta) @@ ﹀ ⊆; not-every, then negated-if forward (7e)
\quad \text{call it } r;\ \text{let } s \in \beta @@ use the some; chase a member of β
\quad s \lt r @@ members-below-non-members (8a) for the cut β: s is in, r is out
\quad \htmlClass{new}{s \in \alpha} @@ cut (iii) for α: below a member
\quad \beta \subseteq \alpha;\ \ \beta \le \alpha @@ s was anything; ︿ ⊆; ︿ ≤
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha \le \beta \lor \beta \le \alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ add an option in each case; reason by cases; the call closes

Look at what the second case did. It found one fraction r inside α and outside β, and that single fraction was enough. Members-below-non-members put all of β below it, and α's closing downward swept all of β inside. Two lower heaps of splittings of one set of fractions cannot cross.

8b2Exactly One of Three

Three-way-order technique on ℝ: for reals α and β, exactly one of α < β, α = β, β < α is true. At least one: split on α = β by either-way. If not, any-two-compare on ℝ gives α ≤ β or β ≤ α. Join each with α ≠ β (the turn-about technique for not-same for the second), and that packs α < β or β < α. At most one: α < β and β < α give α ⊆ β and β ⊆ α. So α = β by double-inclusion, which clashes with α ≠ β. α < β with α = β clashes at once; likewise β < α with α = β. ∎ This is 4h's any-two-compare and 5d's any-two-compare on ℚ again, on the line. With it, ¬(α < β) ⇔ β ≤ α, by 7e's techniques.

From here on, as 6e's word allows, ℝ is a chain under ≤: any two of its members compare. A split “α < β, α = β or β < α” is licensed for any reals, by three-way-order on ℝ.

8b3A Witness for Less

Witness-for-less technique: α < β ⇔ ∃r ∈ ℚ (r ∈ β ∧ r ∉ α). Rightward: α ⊆ β and α ≠ β. If every member of β were in α, then β ⊆ α, and double-inclusion gives α = β, a clash. So ¬∀r (r ∈ β ⇒ r ∈ α), and not-every with negated-if forward gives the r. Leftward: take r ∈ β and r ∉ α. Every member of α is below r (members-below-non-members), so in β (cut (iii) for β). So α ⊆ β. And r shows α ≠ β (if α = β then r ∈ α by the swap axiom). ∎ So “less” on the line always comes with a fraction caught between the two cuts: in the larger, missed by the smaller. 8e uses this to put a fraction strictly between any two reals.

8b4The Greater and the Lesser

From here on, for reals α and β, max(α, β) names β if α ≤ β, and α if not; min(α, β) names α if α ≤ β, and β if not. Read them “the greater of α and β” and “the lesser of α and β”. The same line defines max and min of two fractions, with 5d's ≤. Each mark is an operator: a statement with two gaps, whose result is a real.

This is 7e's definition by cases, on the statement α ≤ β. Greater-is-the-merge technique: max(α, β) = α ∪ β and min(α, β) = α ∩ β. Case α ≤ β: α ⊆ β, so α ∪ β = β and α ∩ β = α, by 2f's techniques (u ⊆ v ⇔ u ∪ v = v, and u ∩ v = u likewise). Case ¬(α ≤ β): by three-way-order on ℝ β < α, so β ⊆ α. The same techniques give α ∪ β = α and α ∩ β = β. ∎ So the union and the intersection of two cuts are cuts: always one of the two. For more than two cuts, the union is 8c's business. The intersection of endlessly many need not be a cut at all.

8b5Why It Can Fail Without the Either-Way Principle

The proof of any-two-compare on ℝ cited either-way on its second line. Could a cleverer column avoid it? No: the comparison is exactly as strong as the either-way principle. Take any statement P, and sift:

The low mark P is 4f's low mark, carrying the statement. If P is true, α_P = 1*; if ¬P, α_P = 0*. With P unknown, α_P cannot be listed, like 7d's U and V. It is a real all the same, and proving so needs no either-way. (i) −1 is in. (ii) 1 is missed, since 1 < 0 fails and 1 < 1 fails. (iii) Take a member r. If r < 0, below it is below 0. If r < 1 ∧ P, below it is below 1 with the same P. (iv) For r < 0 take r/2, and for r < 1 ∧ P take (r + 1)/2 with the same P. Every or in that check was in hand, not supplied by either-way.

Comparing-gives-either-way technique: if any two reals compare, then P ∨ ¬P, for any statement P.

\alpha_P \le \tfrac12^{*} \;\lor\; \tfrac12^{*} \le \alpha_P @@ the hypothesis, used with α_P and ½* (fraction-cut)
\textbf{case } \alpha_P \le \tfrac12^{*}:\ \text{assume } P @@ aiming to refute
\quad \tfrac12 \lt 1 \land P,\ \text{so } \tfrac12 \in \alpha_P @@ join; add an option; ︿ the sift
\quad \tfrac12 \in \tfrac12^{*},\ \tfrac12 \lt \tfrac12,\ \bot @@ carry the ⊆ through; ﹀; three-way on ℚ at 0
\quad \htmlClass{new}{\neg P} @@ refute
\textbf{case } \tfrac12^{*} \le \alpha_P:\ \ 0 \in \tfrac12^{*} @@ 0 < ½ (½ positive, 5f)
\quad 0 \in \alpha_P:\ \ 0 \lt 0 \lor (0 \lt 1 \land P) @@ carry the ⊆ through; ﹀ the sift
\quad \htmlClass{new}{P} @@ 0 < 0 is refuted: the remaining-option technique; keep right
P \lor \neg P \quad \blacksquare @@ add an option in each case; reason by cases

No line cites either-way. So any-two-compare on ℝ and either-way prove each other, given the rest of the ladder. Either-way gave any-two-compare on ℝ above, and any-two-compare on ℝ gives either-way here. 7·cb4's bottom-gives-either-way did the same for the bottom form of foundation. Suppose a column proved any-two-compare on ℝ from rung 1's techs and the set tools alone, never citing either-way or Choice. It would prove P ∨ ¬P for every P, and 7b's world of stages shows rung 1's techs cannot. This is the precise sense in which the comparison “can fail without it”. The intuitionists of 7b build their reals from approximations instead of cuts, and on their line two reals need not compare. What they can prove is weaker, such as: if α < β, then any third real is above α or below β.

Now take P to be the even-sum guess G: every even number above 2 is a sum of two primes. Then α_G is a perfectly good real. By either-way it is 0* or 1*, and any-two-compare on ℝ compares it with anything you like. Nobody can tell you which fraction it is. It is 7c's mark ⟦G⟧ in a new costume: α_G is the cut of the number ⟦G⟧, since ⟦G⟧ = 1 exactly when G is true. The line, built classically, holds numbers like this. That is the price of every comparison being allowed.

8b7Running It Backwards

Wanting α ≤ β or β ≤ α, cite any-two-compare on ℝ; wanting one of three, three-way-order on ℝ. Wanting α < β, find a fraction in β and missing from α (witness-for-less). Handed α ≤ β false, conclude β < α (three-way-order on ℝ). Handed a comparison of reals built from a statement nobody has decided, ask what the comparison would tell you about the statement. If it would settle it, the comparison came from either-way, and has no address. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

One of the first to write the intuitionists’ rules down was Arend Heyting.

Heyting, who first wrote the intuitionists' rules down, explained that on their line one may not assert of every real that it is less than, equal to or greater than another, and showed why with numbers built from questions nobody has decided, in the spirit of α_P.

Heyting, Intuitionism: An Introduction (1956)

Accepted names: the either-way principle is the law of excluded middle; the even-sum guess is Goldbach’s conjecture; three-way-order is trichotomy.
