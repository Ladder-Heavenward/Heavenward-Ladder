26b · proof

The Exponential Still Turns Adding Into Multiplying

On the line, the growth function turned adding into multiplying: e to the x plus y is e to the x times e to the y. That was proved for real numbers, by a route that used order. Does it survive in the plane? It does, and the proof is now shorter: multiply the two endless sums term by term, and the counting of rung 4 does the rest. From that one law come all the others. The growth function is never zero, its size is the growth of its floor part, and it repeats itself every time its input climbs by a full turn.

e to the z plus w is e to the z times e to the w, for all complex z and w. So e to a plus b i is e to the a, turned by b. The exponential repeats with period two pi i.

26b1Adding Becomes Multiplying, in the Plane

Plane-adding technique: for all z and w in ℂ, e^(z + w) = e^z e^w. Multiply the two sums, and each group of terms is a binomial.

e^z e^w = \textstyle\sum_n c_n,\ \ c_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{z^k}{k!}\frac{w^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} @@ the product technique (23h4); its proof uses only sizes and the triangle, which hold in ℂ (25c)
c_n = \frac{1}{n!}\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} z^k w^{n-k} @@ n!/(k!(n − k)!) is n choose k
= \frac{(z+w)^n}{n!} @@ the two-term-power technique (4k); its proof uses only the field techniques, so it holds in ℂ
\htmlClass{new}{e^z e^w = \textstyle\sum \frac{(z+w)^n}{n!} = e^{z+w}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ e^(z+w) (26a2)

Rung 17 proved the same law for reals by a longer road. Here the sums carry it, and the sums never needed order.

26b2What the Law Gives

Growth-and-turn technique: for z = a + bi, e^z = e^a(cos b + i sin b); so |e^z| = e^a, e^z is never 0, and 1/e^z = e^(−z). The floor part grows; the upright part turns.

e^{a+bi} = e^a e^{bi} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b1)
= e^a(\cos b + i\sin b) @@ the turn-growth technique (26a3)
|e^z| = e^a\,|\cos b + i\sin b| = e^a @@ sizes multiply (25c1); cos² + sin² = 1
e^z e^{-z} = e^0 = 1 @@ 26b1 with w = −z
\htmlClass{new}{e^{a+bi} = e^a(\cos b + i\sin b),\ \ e^z \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a product equal to 1 has no factor 0

Read as a move, multiplying by e^z stretches by e^a and turns by b. 21·cb20 found that multiplying multiplies lengths and adds turns. Now both halves are one function: growth for the length, turning for the angle.

26b3The Exponential Repeats

Whole-turn technique: e^z = 1 exactly when z = 2πik for some whole number k; so e^z = e^w exactly when z − w is a whole number of 2πi.

e^{a+bi} = 1 \Rightarrow e^a = |e^{a+bi}| = 1 \Rightarrow a = 0 @@ 26b2; exp is one-to-one on the line (17e)
\cos b + i\sin b = 1 \Rightarrow \cos b = 1,\ \sin b = 0 @@ equal parts
b = 2\pi k @@ cos b = 1 only at whole turns (21)
e^{2\pi i k} = \cos 2\pi k + i\sin 2\pi k = 1 @@ the other way
\htmlClass{new}{e^z = e^w \iff z - w = 2\pi i k} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^z = e^w ⇔ e^(z − w) = 1 (26b2)

On the line, exp was one-to-one: each output came from one input. In the plane each output other than 0 comes from endlessly many inputs, a whole turn apart. That will make the logarithm of 26c many-valued.

The accepted names

The plane-adding technique is the functional equation of the exponential. The growth-and-turn technique gives e^z = e^x(cos y + i sin y) for z = x + iy. The whole-turn technique says exp is periodic with period 2πi.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler used e^(x + iy) = e^x(cos y + i sin y) freely in the 1740s. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the product of two sums that settle in size in 1821, which made the term-by-term multiplication rigorous.
