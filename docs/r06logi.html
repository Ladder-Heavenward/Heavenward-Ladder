6·logi · logistics

Can Every Job Get a Driver?

Can every job get a driver? Every morning in the harvest, a dispatcher in Salinas has a list of loads to move and a list of drivers. Lettuce must come in from the fields near Gonzales and Soledad before it wilts. A tanker of fertilizer must go out to Chualar. Not every driver can take every load: a tractor-trailer needs one licence, a tanker of ammonia another. So each load has its own set of drivers who could take it, and the dispatcher must pick one from each set. That is 6b's choosing function, met in a yard full of trucks. What makes the question hard is that the picks are not separate. A driver picked for one load is gone for the others. This page counts the ways to pick, finds the one shape that makes picking impossible, and ends in a cold store, where the picks do not matter and a rule must still make them.

An assignment gives each job a driver from that job's set. Count them by multiplying the sizes of the sets. A fair one gives no driver two jobs. If some group of jobs has fewer drivers between them than there are jobs in the group, no fair assignment exists. Where every pick is as good as any other, a rule that picks still has to be written down.

6logi1An Assignment Is a Choosing Function

Here is one morning's board, with five drivers: Ana, Ben, Cruz, Dev and Eli. Only Ana, Ben and Cruz hold the licence for a tractor-trailer. Only Cruz and Dev may drive the ammonia tanker. Anyone can take the box van.

jobthe loadwho can take ithow many
glettuce from Gonzales, tractor-trailerAna, Ben, Cruz3
slettuce from Soledad, tractor-trailerAna, Ben, Cruz3
tammonia to Chualar, tankerCruz, Dev2
vboxes to Monterey, vanall five5

From here on, on this page, for a job j, D(j) is the set of drivers who can take it. An assignment is a function f on the jobs with f(j) ∈ D(j) for every job j.

That is 6b's choosing function, with one change. 6b picks from each member of a collection, and a collection is a set, so two equal members are one member. Here jobs g and s have the same set of drivers. The collection {D(g), D(s), D(t), D(v)} has only three members, and a choosing function for it gives g and s the same driver. So the picks are made by job, not by set: the assignment is a choosing function for the list of sets, one entry per job.

How many assignments are there? Pick for g, then for s, then t, then v. Each stage has as many choices as its set has members, whatever was picked before. So 4k's stages-multiply counts them, as 6c's counting-pickings did. The number of assignments is the product of the sizes of the sets.

3 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = \htmlClass{new}{90} @@ one factor for each of g, s, t, v

No axiom is needed. There are four jobs, and 6c's finite-choice picks from finitely many sets in turn. The question of this page is a different one.

6logi2Fair Assignments: No Driver Twice

Most of those 90 send one driver out on two loads at once, which no one can do. An assignment is fair when it gives no driver two jobs: f(j) = f(j′) only when j = j′. A fair assignment is a matching of the jobs with the drivers it uses, in 4k's sense. Count them by cases on the tanker, which has the fewest drivers.

f(t) = \text{Cruz}:\ \ g, s \text{ from Ana, Ben}:\ 2 \cdot 1 @@ two drivers left for the two trailers, in order
\quad v \text{ from Dev, Eli}:\ 2,\ \ \text{so } 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4 @@ the van takes one of the two left
f(t) = \text{Dev}:\ \ g, s \text{ from Ana, Ben, Cruz}:\ 3 \cdot 2 @@ stages-multiply
\quad v \text{ from the two left}:\ 2,\ \ \text{so } 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 12 @@ one of Ana, Ben, Cruz is left, and Eli
4 + 12 = \htmlClass{new}{16} @@ separate-piles: the two cases share nothing

Fair assignments do not simply multiply. The choices for s depend on the choice for g. So the cases have to be split by hand, and the count needs care.

6logi3When No Fair Assignment Exists

Now Cruz calls in sick, and a third load of lettuce comes in, from Greenfield. It too needs a tractor-trailer. The jobs g, s and the Greenfield load f each have drivers {Ana, Ben}. Three loads, two drivers who can take any of them. Whatever the dispatcher does, one load waits.

First, a count to stand on. No-room technique: a set with k members cannot be matched with a part of a set with fewer than k members. Let X have k members, and let Y have m members. Let f be a matching of X with a part of Y. The proof of k ≤ m is by induction on m.

m = 0:\ \ Y = \varnothing,\ \text{so } X = \varnothing,\ k = 0 @@ each member of X has a partner in Y; one-count
Y \text{ has } S(m) \text{ members};\ \text{take } y \in Y @@ the step; Y has a member
y = f(x) \text{ for some } x \in X,\ \text{or not} @@ settled: X is finite, finite-is-settled, along X by induction
\text{not}:\ f \text{ matches } X \text{ into } Y \setminus \{y\},\ k \le m @@ lead-one-out gives Y ∖ {y} m members; the induction
y = f(x):\ f \text{ matches } X \setminus \{x\} \text{ into } Y \setminus \{y\} @@ partners of the others are not y; lead-one-out on both
\htmlClass{new}{k \le S(m)} \quad \blacksquare @@ X ∖ {x} has at most m members, so X at most S(m)

Now the obstacle. Blocking-group technique: if some k of the jobs have, between them, fewer than k drivers who can take them, no fair assignment exists. Suppose a fair assignment f existed. On those k jobs it is a matching of them with drivers who can take them. That is a matching of a set with k members with a part of their drivers' merge, which has fewer than k members. No-room forbids it.

D(g) = D(s) = D(f) = \{\text{Ana}, \text{Ben}\} @@ three tractor-trailer loads
D(g) \cup D(s) \cup D(f) = \{\text{Ana}, \text{Ben}\} @@ 2 drivers between them
3 \text{ jobs},\ 2 \text{ drivers}:\ \htmlClass{new}{\text{no fair assignment}} @@ blocking-group, with k = 3

A job that nobody can take, with D(j) = ∅, is a blocking group of one. It blocks everything, as 6b's empty-member technique says. The blocking group is the same idea, grown up. Is a blocking group the only way to fail? That question is left for the star.

6logi4Picking in Turn Can Get Stuck

A busy dispatcher works down the board and gives each job the first free driver on its list. That is finite-choice's picking in turn, made into a rule. It can fail when a fair assignment exists. Say job g can go to Ana or Ben, and job t only to Ana. Down the board, g takes Ana, and t is left with nobody. Yet g to Ben and t to Ana is fair. Picking in turn can strand a job that a better order would have served. The fix is a swap: move g from Ana to Ben, and give Ana to t. Dispatchers do this all day.

6logi5The Cold Store That Has to Pick

Last, a pick that does not matter. A cooler in Salinas holds 40 cases of the same strawberries, and an order comes in for 3. Any 3 will do. There are 40 choose 3 = 9880 ways to pick them, by 4k, and the customer cannot tell them apart. In 6b's words these cases are socks. No condition the customer cares about picks one.

The order still has to be filled, so the store writes a rule that picks. Number the cases by the day they came in, and take the 3 oldest: least first, by 4h's least-member. That is 6b's least-chooses, applied to strawberries. It has a reason, too: older fruit spoils first. When every pick is as good as any other, a rule that picks has to be written down anyway, and the best rules pick by a number.

Who said it first

The converse in 6·logi.7.3 was proved by Philip Hall in 1935, for choosing distinct members from a list of sets; Dénes Kőnig had a form of it for graphs a few years before. The swap that rescues a stranded job is the step of the method of Harold Kuhn (1955), who named it after Kőnig and Egerváry. The two mathematicians of 6·logi.7.4 stand for the letters of 1905 in which Émile Borel, René Baire and Henri Lebesgue doubted Ernst Zermelo's axiom, and Jacques Hadamard defended it.

Accepted names

A fair assignment is a system of distinct representatives, or a matching that covers every job. Hall's result is Hall's marriage theorem, and the blocking group's absence is Hall's condition. Finding the best fair assignment when each pairing has a cost is the assignment problem. Picking in turn is a greedy algorithm. Taking the oldest stock first is FIFO, first in, first out; for food it is often FEFO, first expired, first out.
