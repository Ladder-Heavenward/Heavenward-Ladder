32g · proof

Samples Suffice

A song on a phone is not a wave. It is a list of numbers, forty-four thousand one hundred of them for every second, each the height of the wave at one instant. Between the instants the phone knows nothing. And yet what comes out of the speaker is the whole wave, smooth, with nothing missing that the ear can find. How can a list of heights at separate instants hold everything that happens between them? It cannot, for most waves: a wave can wiggle between two samples and be back by the next, and the samples never see it. The step before gave the way out. A wave that holds no frequency above some top one cannot wiggle fast. This step proves that for such a wave the samples, taken at twice the top frequency, are enough. They are the coefficients of its transform, read in a different light, and from them the whole wave can be rebuilt by one sum. Take them any slower, and frequencies above half the rate disguise themselves as lower ones.

If a signal has no frequency above B, its transform lives on one interval of length 2B, and can be treated as repeating. The coefficients of that repeating transform are the signal’s samples at the spacing 1 over 2B. So the samples fix the transform, and the transform fixes the signal, which is rebuilt as a sum of shifted copies of one wave, each scaled by a sample. Sampled more slowly, two frequencies a whole rate apart give the same samples.

32g1Samples Are Coefficients

A signal f has no frequency above B when f(x) = ∫ from −B to B of F(ξ)e^(2πixξ) dξ for some F of finite energy on [−B, B]: by the undo-transform technique (32e3), F is its transform, and the transform is 0 outside [−B, B]. Its accepted name is a band-limited signal, with band B.

Treat F as a function on [−B, B] that repeats every 2B. The turnings that fit it are e^(2πinξ/(2B)), and its coefficients, with the weight 1/(2B), are its shadows on them (32a, the edge standing question 32a.5.2).

Samples-are-coefficients technique: if a signal has no frequency above B, its samples at the spacing 1/(2B) are the coefficients of its transform on [−B, B], up to the factor 2B; so the samples fix the signal.

c_n(F) = \tfrac{1}{2B}\int_{-B}^{B} F(\xi)\,e^{-2\pi i n\xi/(2B)}\,d\xi @@ a coefficient for a repeat of length 2B
f\big(\tfrac{-n}{2B}\big) = \int_{-B}^{B} F(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i(-n/(2B))\xi}\,d\xi @@ the signal at x = −n/(2B)
c_n(F) = \tfrac{1}{2B}\,f\big(\tfrac{-n}{2B}\big) @@ the same total
\text{the samples } f(k/(2B)) \text{ fix every } c_n(F), \text{ so they fix } F @@ the energy-kept technique (32b3): a function is fixed by its coefficients
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the samples fix } f} \quad \blacksquare @@ f is a total of F (32e3)

This is 32f’s squeeze from the other side. A signal with no frequency above B cannot change much in a time much less than 1/(2B), so a sample every 1/(2B) misses nothing.

32g2Rebuilding Between the Samples

Write sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu), with sinc(0) = 1. It is the box’s transform from 32e1: 1 at u = 0, and 0 at every other whole number.

Sample-rebuild technique: a signal with no frequency above B is the sum, over every whole k, of its sample f(k/(2B)) times sinc(2Bx − k).

F = \textstyle\sum_n c_n(F)\,e^{2\pi i n\xi/(2B)} \text{ in size on } [-B, B] @@ the energy-kept technique (32b3), for a repeat of length 2B
f(x) = \int_{-B}^{B} F(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i x\xi}\,d\xi = \textstyle\sum_n c_n(F)\int_{-B}^{B} e^{2\pi i\xi(x + n/(2B))}\,d\xi @@ a total against a bounded function follows nearness in size (the product-bound technique, 31a3)
\int_{-B}^{B} e^{2\pi i\xi u}\,d\xi = 2B\,\text{sinc}(2Bu) @@ the box’s transform (32e1), stretched
f(x) = \textstyle\sum_n \tfrac{1}{2B}f\big(\tfrac{-n}{2B}\big)\cdot 2B\,\text{sinc}(2Bx + n) @@ the samples-are-coefficients technique (32g1)
\htmlClass{new}{f(x) = \textstyle\sum_k f\big(\tfrac{k}{2B}\big)\,\text{sinc}(2Bx - k)} \quad \blacksquare @@ k = −n

At a sample instant x = k/(2B), every term but the kth is 0, and the kth is the sample: the sum passes through every sample. Between them, every sample has its say, each one a fading wave centred on its own instant.

32g3Too Few Samples, and Frequencies in Disguise

Sample at the rate r, every 1/r. Same-samples technique: sampled at the rate r, the turnings of frequencies ξ and ξ + r give the same samples; so a frequency above r/2 is read as one between −r/2 and r/2.

e^{2\pi i(\xi + r)k/r} = e^{2\pi i\xi k/r}\,e^{2\pi i k} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b), at the instants k/r
e^{2\pi i k} = 1 @@ the whole-turn technique (26b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{frequencies } \xi \text{ and } \xi + r \text{ give the same samples}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every whole k

A compact disc samples at r = 44 100 per second, so it can hold every frequency up to 22 050 Hz, past the top of human hearing near 20 000 Hz. A tone at 30 000 Hz, if it reached the sampler, would be read as 30 000 − 44 100 = −14 100: a turning of 14 100 Hz the other way, which a real wave of sines shows as an audible 14 100 Hz tone. So the sound is filtered to remove everything above about 20 000 Hz before it is sampled. 32k says why the rate is 44 100 and not 40 000.

The accepted names

The sample-rebuild technique is the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem (also credited to Whittaker and Kotelnikov), and the sum Σ f(k/2B) sinc(2Bx − k) the Whittaker–Shannon interpolation formula. Twice the top frequency, 2B, is the Nyquist rate, and half the sampling rate the Nyquist frequency. A frequency in disguise is an alias, and the same-samples technique describes aliasing; the filter before sampling is an anti-aliasing filter. The spokes that seem to turn backwards are the wagon-wheel effect.

Who said it first

Edmund Whittaker gave the rebuilding sum in 1915. Harry Nyquist showed in 1928 that a telegraph line of band B carries 2B independent pulses a second. Vladimir Kotelnikov proved the sampling theorem in 1933, and Claude Shannon gave the proof on this page in 1949. In 32g.5.5, Joseph Fourier made the claim in his memoir of 1807, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange, one of its examiners, objected; the memoir was not printed until 1822. Dirichlet proved part of Fourier’s claim in 1829; the rest of the line is still being drawn (32c).

A proof that is not allowed

“A wave is fixed by its values at the instants k/(2B), since a smooth wave cannot change much between them.” Not allowed: smoothness is not the point. A smooth wave of frequency 3B has the same samples as one of frequency B. What the proof needs is that the transform lives on [−B, B].
