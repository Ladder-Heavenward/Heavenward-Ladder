38d · proof

What Is True Everywhere Is Provable

The last step showed that a proof reaches only what is true in every world of its givens. This step goes the other way, and it is the harder road. Suppose a statement is true in every world where the givens are true. Is there a proof? Rung seven answered for statements built from letters with the joining words: a statement whose column is all true can be proved. With every and some there is no column to check, since a world can have endlessly many members. The answer is still yes, and the proof builds a world out of nothing but names. Start with givens that do not clash. Grow them, one statement at a time, until every statement or its denial is in, and every some has a named example. Then let the names themselves be the members. That world makes exactly the grown statements true. So every collection that does not clash has a world, and from that the way back follows. A gift comes with it. If every finite part of a collection has a world, the whole collection has one, because a proof only ever uses finitely many givens.

A clash-free collection of statements grows into a full one, and its names make a world for it. So whatever is true in every world of the givens is reached from them, and a collection whose finite parts all have worlds has a world.

38d1Two Questions, One Answer

7e’s always-true-lines technique proved the way back for statements built from letters: a column of all T can be proved. The new question is the same one with every and some. It turns into a question about worlds.

Two-questions technique: if every clash-free collection of statements has a world, then whatever is true in every world of G is reached from G.

G \nvdash \varphi @@ suppose, aiming at G ⊭ φ
G \cup \{\neg\varphi\} \vdash \bot \Rightarrow G \vdash \neg\neg\varphi \Rightarrow G \vdash \varphi @@ refute drops ¬φ; not-not out (7e)
G \cup \{\neg\varphi\} \text{ is clash-free} @@ the line above, turned and denied, against the first line
M \models G \cup \{\neg\varphi\} \text{ for some world } M @@ the hypothesis: every clash-free collection has a world
M \text{ is a world of } G \text{ in which } \varphi \text{ is false} @@ so G ⊭ φ
\htmlClass{new}{G \models \varphi \Rightarrow G \vdash \varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turn-and-deny turned back technique (7e)

So the whole weight falls on one claim: a collection that does not clash has a world. The next two footings build one, from the collection itself.

38d2Growing a Collection

Let G be clash-free in a language whose marks can be listed. Add new names c₀, c₁, c₂, …, used nowhere in G. List every statement of the larger language: φ₀, φ₁, φ₂, …, as the proof-listing technique lists strings (38b3).

Grow G in rounds. G₀ = G. At round n, put φₙ in if that keeps the collection clash-free, and ¬φₙ in if not. If the statement put in is ∃x ψ(x), put in ψ(c) as well, for the first new name c not yet used. G* is everything put in at some round.

Full-growth technique: a clash-free collection in a listed language grows into a clash-free collection that holds each statement or its denial, and holds a named example of each some it holds.

G_n \cup \{\varphi\} \vdash \bot \text{ and } G_n \cup \{\neg\varphi\} \vdash \bot \Rightarrow G_n \vdash \neg\varphi,\ G_n \vdash \neg\neg\varphi \Rightarrow G_n \vdash \bot @@ refute twice, then clash
\text{so one of } \varphi_n,\ \neg\varphi_n \text{ keeps the collection clash-free} @@ if Gₙ is clash-free
G_n \cup \{\exists x\,\psi(x),\ \psi(c)\} \vdash \bot \Rightarrow G_n \cup \{\exists x\,\psi(x)\} \vdash \bot @@ use a some: c is used nowhere in Gₙ or ⊥, so it is fresh (38b2)
\text{every } G_n \text{ is clash-free} @@ induction on n (4c)
\text{a proof from } G^* \text{ uses finitely many givens, all in some } G_n @@ a proof is finite (38b3)
\htmlClass{new}{G^* \text{ is clash-free, full, and named}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each φₙ or ¬φₙ was put in at round n

Nothing was chosen without a technique. The listing fixed the order, and at each round exactly one of the two was put in by a settled test of the technique: φₙ when that keeps the collection clash-free, ¬φₙ otherwise. The test itself is no recipe, but it is a condition, and 4d’s recursion builds the rounds. No axiom of choice is spent. A language too large to list would need its statements lined up some other way, and 38e meets that.

38d3A World Made of Names

A full clash-free collection holds everything it reaches: if G* ⊢ χ and ¬χ were in G*, G* would reach ⊥. Call two terms with no placeholders kin when the statement t = t′ is in G*. Self-sameness, turn-about and chain are reached, so kin is a sameness relation, and it splits the terms into classes (2h, 5·cb2).

The world of names M* has the classes as its members. A name means its own class. f([t₁], …, [tₙ]) = [f(t₁, …, tₙ)], and R holds of [t₁], …, [tₙ] when R(t₁, …, tₙ) is in G*. Swap makes both well defined: kin terms put in give kin results.

Term-world technique: a statement is true in the world of names exactly when it is in G*.

\text{atoms: } M^* \models t = t' \iff [t] = [t'] \iff (t = t') \in G^* @@ the definition of the world
\neg\varphi:\ M^* \models \neg\varphi \iff \varphi \notin G^* \iff \neg\varphi \in G^* @@ induction on the statement; G* is full and clash-free
\varphi \land \psi,\ \varphi \lor \psi,\ \varphi \Rightarrow \psi @@ the same: G* holds what it reaches, and is full
\exists x\,\psi:\ \exists x\,\psi \in G^* \Rightarrow \psi(c) \in G^* \Rightarrow M^* \models \psi(c) @@ G* is named; induction
\exists x\,\psi:\ M^* \models \psi[\,[t]\,] \Rightarrow \psi(t) \in G^* \Rightarrow \exists x\,\psi \in G^* @@ every member is a class [t]; show an example
\forall x\,\psi \text{ is } \neg\exists x\,\neg\psi \text{ in force} @@ each gives the other in the calculus, with not-not out (7e)
\htmlClass{new}{M^* \models \varphi \iff \varphi \in G^*} \quad \blacksquare @@ every way of building is covered (38a1)

So G, inside G*, has a world: forget the meanings of the new names, and M* is a world for G’s own language. Every clash-free collection in a listed language has a world. With the two-questions technique, that is the way back.

Reached-if-true-everywhere technique: in a listed language, whatever is true in every world of G is reached from G. In marks: G ⊨ φ gives G ⊢ φ. With 38c, the two marks ⊢ and ⊨ say the same thing.

38d4Finitely Many Suffice

Finite-parts technique: if every finite part of a collection of statements has a world, the whole collection has a world.

G \text{ has no world} @@ suppose, aiming to refute
G \models \bot @@ nothing to check: there is no world of G
G \vdash \bot @@ the reached-if-true-everywhere technique
F \vdash \bot \text{ for the finite part } F \text{ of givens the proof uses} @@ a proof is finite (38b3)
F \text{ has no world} @@ the world-means-clash-free technique (38c2)
\htmlClass{new}{\bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ clash, with the hypothesis on F; so G has a world

34d called a space compact when finitely many of any open cover suffice. The accepted name of this technique is compactness, for the same reason: a demand that every finite part can meet, the whole can meet.

\lambda_n:\ \exists x_1 \cdots \exists x_n\,(x_1 \ne x_2 \land x_1 \ne x_3 \land \cdots \land x_{n-1} \ne x_n) @@ “at least n members”, one statement for each n
G \text{ has a world with at least } n \text{ members, for every } n @@ suppose
\text{each finite part of } G \cup \{\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots\} \text{ has a world} @@ a world of G with enough members makes its λₙ true
G \cup \{\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots\} \text{ has a world, and it is not finite} @@ the finite-parts technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no collection of statements has exactly the finite worlds as its worlds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a collection with worlds of every finite size has an endless one

The accepted names

The reached-if-true-everywhere technique is the completeness theorem for first-order logic: if Γ ⊨ φ then Γ ⊢ φ. The full-growth technique combines Lindenbaum’s lemma (every consistent set extends to a maximal consistent one) with Henkin’s witnesses; the new names are Henkin constants and the world of names is the term model, or Henkin model. The term-world technique is the truth lemma. The finite-parts technique is the compactness theorem. The two-questions technique says that completeness is the same as the model existence theorem: every consistent set has a model.

Who said it first

Kurt Gödel proved completeness in his doctoral thesis (Vienna, 1929), published in 1930, by another road; compactness for countable languages was a corollary there. Anatolii Maltsev proved compactness for languages of any size in 1936 and used it in algebra in 1941. Leon Henkin found the proof on this page, with witnesses and a world of names, in his thesis of 1947, published in 1949. The question had been posed by David Hilbert and Wilhelm Ackermann in their book of 1928.

A proof that is not allowed

“G does not reach φ. So by the reached-if-true-everywhere technique, φ is false in every world of G.” Not allowed: the technique, turned round, gives only that φ is false in some world of G. In the others φ may well be true.
