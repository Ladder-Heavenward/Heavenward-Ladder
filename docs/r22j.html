22j · technique

From counting to rare events

Some things happen rarely, but have a great many chances to happen: a misprint among thousands of letters, a call to a help line from among thousands of people, a meteor in a patch of sky. Counting them exactly would need to know how many chances there were, and you seldom do. This step shows you do not need to. When the chances are many and each is small, only their average count matters, and the chances of seeing none, one, two or more come from a single endless sum. Along the way that sum turns out to be the growth function of the seventeenth rung, written out piece by piece.

Many tries, each with a small chance, behave like one rule that needs only the average count. The chance of exactly k is the average to the kth power over k factorial, times e to the minus the average. Those chances add to one because e to a power is an endless sum.

22j1A tiny chance, many times

Many-small-chances technique: for any real x, (1 + x/N)ᴺ heads to eˣ as N grows. In particular, (1 − λ/N)ᴺ heads to e^(−λ). 17e did x = 1.

\ln\big(1 + \tfrac{x}{N}\big)^N = N\ln\big(1 + \tfrac{x}{N}\big) = x \cdot \tfrac{\ln(1 + h)}{h},\ h = \tfrac{x}{N} @@ for x ≠ 0 and N past |x|
\tfrac{\ln(1 + h)}{h} = \tfrac{\ln(1 + h) - \ln 1}{h} \to 1 @@ the slope of ln at 1 is 1/1 (17b), as h heads to 0
N\ln\big(1 + \tfrac{x}{N}\big) \to x @@ the product technique (10c)
\htmlClass{new}{\big(1 + \tfrac{x}{N}\big)^N = \exp\big(N\ln(1 + \tfrac xN)\big) \to e^x} \quad \blacksquare @@ exp is continuous (17e; 22h1)

Read with x = −λ: if each of N tries has chance λ/N, the chance that none of them comes off is (1 − λ/N)ᴺ, which is about e^(−λ) once N is large. It does not matter whether N is a thousand or a million. Only λ, the average count, is left.

22j2The chance of exactly k

Rare-events technique: if each of N tries has chance λ/N, the chance of exactly k successes heads to e^(−λ) λᵏ / k! as N grows. Only λ is left in the answer.

P_N(k) = \tbinom{N}{k}\big(\tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^k\big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{N-k} @@ k of N, each with chance λ/N (14·stat)
\tbinom{N}{k}\tfrac{1}{N^k} = \tfrac{1}{k!} \cdot \tfrac{N}{N}\cdot\tfrac{N-1}{N}\cdots\tfrac{N-k+1}{N} \to \tfrac{1}{k!} @@ 4k's count; k fixed, each of the k fractions heads to 1
\big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{N-k} = \big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N \big(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^{-k} \to e^{-\lambda} \cdot 1 @@ 22j1; k is fixed
\htmlClass{new}{P_N(k) \to e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the product technique (10c)

Take something that has, on average, a quarter of a chance to happen each day, out of a great many small chances. On about 78 days in 100, it does not happen. It happens once on about 1 day in 5, twice on about 1 day in 40, and three times on about 1 day in 500.

22j3The chances add to one

Exp-as-a-sum technique: for every λ ≥ 0, e^λ = 1 + λ + λ²/2! + λ³/3! + ⋯. So the chances e^(−λ) λᵏ/k! add to one. The rung 23 power sums take this to every real λ.

\textstyle S = \sum \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} \text{ settles} @@ the ratio of neighbours is λ/(k + 1), which heads to 0 (22e2)
\big(1 + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N = \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{N} \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}\, c_{N,k},\ \ c_{N,k} = \tfrac{N}{N}\cdot\tfrac{N-1}{N}\cdots\tfrac{N-k+1}{N} @@ the two-term-power technique (4k), as in 22j2
0 \le c_{N,k} \le 1 \Rightarrow \big(1 + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N \le S @@ every piece is at most λᵏ/k!, and λ ≥ 0
M \le N \Rightarrow \big(1 + \tfrac{\lambda}{N}\big)^N \ge \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{M} \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}\, c_{N,k} @@ drop the pieces past M; they are not negative
e^\lambda \ge \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{M} \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} \text{ for every } M,\ \ e^\lambda \le S @@ let N grow (22j1); each c_{N,k} heads to 1
\htmlClass{new}{e^\lambda = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0} \tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!},\ \ \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{-\lambda}\tfrac{\lambda^k}{k!} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the running totals climb to S and never pass e^λ (22a2)

With λ = 1 this is the sum 22i fenced in eight pieces. Its total is e. The number that 17e found as a limit of powers is also an endless sum of fractions, and the sum is the fast way to compute it.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The chances e^(−λ) λᵏ/k! are the Poisson distribution with mean λ; the rare-events technique is the Poisson limit theorem, also called the law of rare events or the law of small numbers. The exp-as-a-sum technique is the power series (Taylor series) of the exponential, which rung 23 builds in general.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre found the limit in 1711. Siméon Denis Poisson published it in 1837, in a book on the verdicts of juries. Ladislaus Bortkiewicz made it famous in 1898 with a count of Prussian cavalrymen killed by the kicks of their horses: about 0.7 a corps a year, and the record fitted the rule.
