0b · proof

The Same as Itself

This step gives the ground floor's claim, a thing is the same as itself, a mark, a name, and a companion: the axiom that a statement carried across a sameness may still be written. From those two it argues, in plain English, that sameness turns about and chains, and it works through the swaps that look like carrying a fact and are not. The arguments are told in words here, shown in lines on the rung's conclusion, and proved on 4a, one move to a line, each with its reason.

If this is the same thing as that, then whatever you have said of this, you may say of that, by putting that's name where this's name stood. A fact travels in no other way.

0b1Operators

An operator is a member that tells you the technique: fill its gaps, carry it out, and it gives you something new. What it gives is called its result. “Operator” is the accepted name, used from here on.

Some of 0a's statements with gaps do more than make a claim. “Mix the blue paint with the yellow” has two gaps, “mix … with …”. Carry it out and it gives green paint, a thing you can name and talk about. So mix is an operator, its technique is the move from two paints to a third, and green paint is its result. Nobody proved the technique. It was built, by saying what the words mean, the way 1a builds unpack.

The Ladder's first operator comes in this step. Its two gaps take names, and its result is a sameness: a statement you can have, use, and prove things from. For a while, that is the only result the ladder needs.

0b2The Operator for Sameness

A cat is asleep on the mat, and her name is Serena. “The cat asleep on the mat” and “Serena” are different names, and there is one cat. That is sameness on this ladder: one thing under more than one name. Here is the mark, brought in as 0a brought in its marks, by a line that says what it means:

The mark's name is borrowed from English. Things are equal when they are the same in every way that matters for the matter at hand: “equal shares of pizza”, “an equal number of turns on the swing”. So the mark is the equal sign, and x = b is read “x equals b”, or “x is equal to b”.

x = b is a statement in 0a's sense, with two gaps like 0a's M(x, b): “… = …”. It says that the two names name one thing. Its kind does not set it apart; its use does: the two axioms of this step are about this statement alone. Let s name Serena, m name the cat asleep on the mat, and g name Gomez the dog:

The letters on either side are names, and the mark says they name the same thing. That is all the mark will ever mean, on any rung, however elaborate the names on either side of it become.

Syntax, here, means logical order: the order the marks come in, and what that order shows. As a statement with two gaps, = could be written the way 0a writes its other claims, mark in front and names in a bracket: =(s, m). Call that statement syntax. Or the mark can stand between the names: s = m. Call that operator syntax. Both say the same thing.

The ladder almost always chooses operator syntax, and the reason is English, which puts the word that ties two things between them: “Serena is the same as the cat on the mat”, “Zoe lives next door to Randy”. So s = m reads aloud the way you would say it. =(s, m) is just as correct, and you may meet it, but it reads like a label on a list.

With two gaps, x = b can also be taken as a claim about one name, the other kept fixed. “… equals b” is a claim with one gap; call it B, and B(x) is x = b. “x equals …” is another; call it A, and A(b) is x = b as well. One statement, taken three ways: a sameness of two names, a claim about x, a claim about b.

A(b) still says “x equals b”, not “b equals x”. Choosing which name fills the gap does not turn the sameness about; that takes a technique, which this step builds. Two things done just now are techniques built on 1a: reading the mark out in words is an unpack, and taking the statement as a claim about one name is a transform.

0b3Equal, as Something

English says the mark's statement three ways. “This is equal to that” is the plain reading of this = that: one thing, two names. “This has equality to that” makes the sameness a noun. “This equals that” makes it a verb. Three wordings, one mark, one statement: the marks do not care which you say aloud.

Equal is always equal as something, and the matter at hand says what. Say what you are considering, and the names name that. Then everything the argument says is said of that, and the axioms of this step may carry any of it across.

As objects, the shell in your hand is the shell you picked up on the beach: one shell, two descriptions. As a substance, the ice cube = the puddle it melted into: one water, two names. There the names name the water, not the cube.

So be careful what a fact is about. “The ice cube is square” is about the cube's shape, not the water, and does not carry to the puddle. Say it, and you have changed what the names name, and the sameness no longer stands.

0b4A Thing Is the Same as Itself

The ground floor's claim, as a statement you may write at any time, with nothing before it. Its name is the self-sameness axiom. It is earned by what breaks without it: nothing would say that the Serena of one line is the Serena of the next, no name could be used twice, and no argument could get past its first line.

In words, as the rung page said (rung 0): whatever thing you name, by whatever letter, it is the same thing as the thing you named. With s naming Serena: s = s, Serena is Serena. Notice the freedom in “for any name whatever”. The axiom is about no one name. It is a pattern you may fill with any name you have, and the filled statement is yours for the writing. The second road to the turn-about will use that freedom.

On the rung where you learn an axiom, you cite it each time you use it, to show you have mastered it. After that it stands behind every argument, as a given does.

0b5Swap Equals

Start with a cat, and the principle of the rung page (rung 0): Serena is the cat asleep on the mat, and grey, so the cat on the mat is grey. Everything about sameness is in that “of course”, and the ladder gives it an axiom so that an argument may cite it.

The companion axiom says what sameness is for. If x and b are the same thing, a statement about x is just as true with b's name where x's stood. Nothing beneath it proves it. It too is earned by what breaks without it: knowing that the cat on the mat is Serena would tell you nothing about the cat on the mat, and sameness would be a word that does no work. The axiom is true whether or not anyone ever writes a swap down; writing one is how you use it. It runs both ways: a statement about b is just as true with x's name where b's stood. In 0a's bracket writing, with P any statement with a gap:

The swap writes b at whichever of x's places you choose: all of them, or some. Each place where x's name stands is one you may swap at or leave alone. You may not swap where some other name stands. The swap back works the same way.

Here it is on the cat, in marks. Serena is the cat asleep on the mat: s = m. Serena is grey: call “… is grey” P, so this is P(s). By the swap axiom, you may write P(m): the cat on the mat is grey. Writing it is a move, cited to the swap axiom. “Of course” is not a reason; the swap axiom is.

0b6Sameness Turns About

“x = b” says that the names name one thing. It does not, by itself, let you write “b = x”. That feels like nothing: of course, if x is b then b is x. But on this ladder you may write only what an axiom lets you write, or what a technique builds from it. The mark's “from here on” line is a meaning, not a technique. The self-sameness axiom lets you write x = x. The swap axiom lets you carry a statement across a sameness, either way. Neither is “turn a sameness about”. So the turning has to be built out of the axioms, and so does every habit with the equal sign you have ever taken for granted.

Now the earliest technique on the ladder. A technique is a licensed way of getting a statement. It is proved from the axioms, as this one is, or built by saying what words mean, as an operator is. An axiom is neither: it is laid down. The claim in words: if x is the same thing as b, then b is the same thing as x. In marks:

First on the cat. From s = m, swap m in at s's place: m = m. Then swap s in at the right-hand place: m = s. Here is the same argument in letters, said slowly, because the trick in it recurs all the way up the ladder.

We have x = b and want b = x. A swap needs a statement to work inside, and x = b is the one we have. Read it as a claim about x, with its gap at the left: “… = b”. That is the plan.

Swap b in at x's place: b = b. Now read b = b as a claim about b with its gap at the right: “b = …”. The swap axiom runs both ways, so with x = b in hand, x may stand where b stood: b = x. The left-hand b is left alone, and the axiom covers that: you swap at the places you choose. Choosing the places is the whole art. Swap at both places of b = b and you get x = x, true and useless here.

A second road takes one swap, and starts from the self-sameness axiom. Start from x = x, read it as “… = x”, and swap b in at the left: b = x. Either road earns the turn, and either costs sentences to write. That cost is what a technique saves: once it is proved, “the turn-about technique” beside a sentence stands in for the swaps, and you hop past them.

That was an argument told in words. 1a gives the ladder's form for an argument, and 4a proves the turn-about technique, one move to a line, each with its reason; the rung's conclusion shows it in that shape ahead of time. From then on it may be cited by name, as an axiom may.

Why build the turn as a technique, and not lay it down as an axiom? The turn-about does something, rather than being something. The swap axiom is how names are handed out into statements. A technique is how we work statements by their qualities, to reach the same claim with slightly different pieces.

Take three lines. “Mike is the boy in the red cap, so the boy in the red cap is Mike”: this may be written. “Mike rides Spirit the horse, so Spirit rides Mike”: this may not. “Mike rides Spirit, so Spirit is ridden by Mike”: this may. Sameness and riding are both statements with two gaps, so the shape alone turns neither. Sameness turns because of what it is: the swap axiom carries any fact from one name to the other, and that is enough to turn it. Riding has no such axiom. It can only be read from its other end, in new words that make the same claim.

Put the moves together. From “Mike is the boy in the red cap” and “Mike rides Spirit”, “Spirit is ridden by the boy in the red cap” follows. No axiom licenses the change from “rides” to “is ridden by”; that is a technique, 1a's transform. So the swap axiom is not the act of swapping. It is the fact that what a swap writes may be written, whether or not any swap is ever chosen.

0b7Sameness Chains

The claim in words: if x is the same thing as b, and b is the same thing as c, then x is the same thing as c. A technique again, not an axiom. In marks:

First on the cat. Serena is the cat on the mat, s = m. The cat on the mat is Zoe's cat; call Zoe's cat a, so m = a. Read s = m as “s = …”, a claim about m, and swap a in: s = a. Serena is Zoe's cat.

In general it is the same trick, from the other side. Read x = b as a claim about b: “x = …”. With b = c in hand, the swap axiom puts c in b's place: x = c. The self-sameness axiom was not needed, because the statements in hand already gave something to swap inside. To find a route, ask of each statement you have, “what is this a claim about?”. It is usually a claim about several things at once, and each is a way to use it.

Longer chains need no new technique; chain, and then chain again on the result. From x = b, b = c and c = d: chain the first two to get x = c, then chain that with c = d to get x = d. That is the other thing a technique is for: once proved, it is carried out again and again, and its argument is not told again. Samenesses chained this way are said to run in a row: x = c and c = d run in a row to x = d.

0b8Swapping at the Places You Choose

The swap axiom (0b5) is more supple than the cat shows: a name can stand in a statement at several places, and each place is chosen separately. Let x name Zoe, and suppose Zoe is the baker; call the baker b, so x = b. “x lives next door to x's sister” has x at two places. By the swap axiom, you may swap at either or both:

  • at the left place: b lives next door to x's sister (the baker lives next door to Zoe's sister);
  • at the right place: x lives next door to b's sister (Zoe lives next door to the baker's sister);
  • at both places: b lives next door to b's sister (the baker lives next door to the baker's sister).

All of these may be written. Each says something of the one woman under some arrangement of her names. Which one you want depends on what you mean to do next, and that is why the axiom leaves the choice of places to you.

0b9A Sameness, or a Claim

Most statements make a claim about a thing: “Evita runs”, “Serena is grey”, “Gomez is a poodle”. Some say “is” and some do not, and the claim is the same. “Evita is a runner” and “Evita runs” are one claim, R(e). The “is” only joins the description to its thing.

A sameness is another kind of statement: two gaps with the mark between them, saying that two names name one thing. “Serena is the cat asleep on the mat” is a sameness, s = m. English says both kinds with “is”, so a claim can be mistaken for a sameness. The test is the swap: x = b is a fair statement only if everything the argument will say of one can be said of the other. “Serena” and “the cat asleep on the mat” pass: whatever you say of the cat, you have said of Serena. “Evita” and “a runner” fail, because “a runner” names no one thing for anything to be said of. “Evita is a runner” is a claim with one gap, R(e), with no sameness in it.

Two sentences can be the same the way two names can: one claim under two wordings. “Terry went for a jog” and “Terry went for a run” are different sentences that say the same of Terry. So a sameness may stand between names of claims, when the claims are one. The sentences stay different, as names do: “has the word ‘jog’ in it” may be said of only one of them. Much of mathematics is this move made carefully: writing a sentence again as a different sentence that makes the same claim.

A word for a kind, like “poodle”, names no one thing, so “Gomez is a poodle” is a claim, not a sameness. Watch what happens if you write it as one anyway. Gomez and Finn are both poodles:

0b10Swaps That Are Not Moves

Carrying a fact is licensed by one thing only: the things are the same thing. Here are swaps that look like moves cited to the swap axiom and are not. In each, one of two things has gone wrong. What stands in the place of x = b is not a sameness. Or what stands in the place of P(x) is not a statement about the thing.

  • “s looks just like n; s is Zoe's cat; so n is Zoe's cat?” Not licensed: looking alike is not sameness. Serena and her look-alike Murphy are different cats, and only Serena is Zoe's.
  • “s is a cat; n is a cat; s is grey; so n is grey?” Not licensed: being of the same kind is not sameness. Cats differ, and nothing here says s = n.
  • “x = b; the name ‘x’ is in red ink; so the name ‘b’ is in red ink?” Not licensed: the statement is about the name, not the thing, and swapping inside a name is not licensed.
  • “The new kid is the kid who won the spelling bee; Randy knows that the new kid sits by the window; so Randy knows that the spelling-bee winner sits by the window?” Not licensed: a “knows that” sentence is about Randy's knowledge, in which the name itself does work; it is not a plain statement about the kid.

The spelling-bee case fools people who have understood the rest. The new kid and the spelling-bee winner are the same person, so the sameness may be written. “The new kid sits by the window” is a statement about that person, so by the swap axiom the spelling-bee winner sits by the window. But “Randy knows that the new kid sits by the window” is a statement about Randy, and about what he would agree to under one name. He may never have heard that the names name the same person. Swapping inside his “knows that” changes what is said about him.

The ladder keeps clear of such sentences. Its statements are about things, never about what anyone knows, believes, says or has heard. So a swap cited to the swap axiom is always safe where the ladder makes one.

The ink case is the same failure in a plainer coat. 0a gave quote marks their job: a name between them is mentioned, and the statement is about the name. The swap axiom is about facts of things, so no swap is ever made between quote marks. When x = b, the things are the same, and the names are still different names. “‘x’ is in red ink” says nothing about whether “‘b’ is in red ink”.

0b11Finding a Route

To find a route, start from what you want and ask what would give it. You want d = x, and you have x = b, b = c and c = d. Nothing you have has d on the left, so the last step must be a turn-about, and the thing to turn is x = d. And x = d is the chain: x = b with b = c gives x = c, and that with c = d gives x = d. The route: chain, chain again, turn about.

Try the other order: turn each statement about (d = c, c = b, b = x) and chain those. Both routes work, and this one is longer. There is never only the one route, and finding a shorter one is a pleasure the ladder means you to have.

Here is everything you may write with the equal sign, so far. A sameness of any name with itself (the self-sameness axiom). A statement about b, from a statement about x and x = b, at the places you choose, and the same the other way (the swap axiom). A sameness turned about (the turn-about technique). Two samenesses chained into one (the chain technique). Nothing is carried across a likeness, a kind, a mention, or a “knows that”, or across a claim written as if it were a sameness. That is the whole of the sign's rules on the ground floor.

Notice a loop, like the language loop of 0a. The self-sameness axiom justifies the equal sign. The swap axiom justifies breaking speech into names and statements. Yet the axioms could only be written once 0a had defined names and statements, and this step the equal sign. For such a loop the ladder works in foreclosure (introduction, 6): the thing is held in trust, flagged where it is first used, and finished later. 0a opened three flags for this step: the equal sign it listed, the fact that a statement about a name is not a statement about its thing, and its language loop. This step closes the first two, the second in 0b10. The loop stays stepped around until 4d. This step opens one flag of its own: the turn-about and chain techniques are argued here in words, and 4a proves them; the rung's conclusion shows them in lines ahead of time. Foreclosure is the only foreshadowing the ladder has, and every one is flagged where it opens and closed where it is finished. Some things take a while to assemble, so the ladder steers the boat while building it. Apologies if a bit of water leaks.

Who said it first

Wittgenstein argued that this whole business is empty. To say of two things that they are the same is nonsense, he wrote, and to say of one thing that it is the same as itself is to say nothing at all.

Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921), 5.5303; paraphrase

The chain technique is Euclid's first common notion, “things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another”, said of sameness rather than of size. The “knows that” case is Frege's, from “On Sense and Reference” (1892), told with the evening star and the morning star, which are both the planet Venus; the lesson tells it with the new kid.

Two more uses of “equal”

“The soup is equally salty and spicy.” This is a claim about measures. Rate the salt and rate the spice, each on its own scale, and the two ratings are the same: how salty = how spicy. The mark stands between the ratings, not between saltiness and spiciness themselves.

“Equal parts flour and water.” The part is the unit. One part of flour is one part of water, whatever a part holds, a cup or a bucket. What a part holds drops out, and only the count of parts is left to be the same. So both are the mark at work: between two measures, and between two parts taken as units.

Accepted names: the self-sameness axiom is reflexivity of equality; the swap axiom is substitution of equals (or Leibniz's law); the turn-about technique is symmetry; the chain technique is transitivity.
