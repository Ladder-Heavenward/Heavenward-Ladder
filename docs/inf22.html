Why Do Coverings and Equations Obey One Fact?

Why do coverings and equations obey one fact? Rung 34 matched the connected coverings of a space with the part-groups of its loop group. Rung 46 matched the fields between ℚ and a splitting field with the part-groups of the group of an equation. Both are the same fact. In each case there is a category whose things are sums of connected pieces, and a forgetful map that counts points: the points over a chosen point, or the ways to place a field inside a larger one. The group acts on those points, the category is matched with the sets on which the group acts, and the connected pieces are matched with part-groups. The group can even be read off the forgetful map, as ∞γ read a group off its representations. Read that way, ℚ has loops of its own, and a question posed in 1983 asks whether the points of a curve can be told by them.

A covering of the circle gives its fibre with a shift (lift the loop once round). A field E inside a splitting field K gives its placements E → K, shuffled by G. One-orbit: a set with an action of G and one orbit is matched with G/H, H fixing a point. Coverings of the circle by 3 sheets ↔ 3ℤ; ℚ(∛2) ↔ {e, (2 3)}. The symmetries of the forgetful map on finite G-sets are G; on finite coverings of the circle they are more than ℤ. Complex conjugation is a loop of ℚ.

inf22.1Coverings as Sets With a Shift

Take the circle as the numbers of size 1 in the plane, with base point 1, and the covering z ↦ z³ of 34g5. Over 1 lie three points, e^{2πik/3} for k = 0, 1, 2. The loop that goes once round, t ↦ e^{2πit}, lifts from each of them by the covering-lift technique, and the lift ends at a point over 1 again.

Shift technique: lifting the loop once round moves each point over the base point to another; so a covering gives a set with a shift, and the covering is connected exactly when the shift reaches every point from any one.

\tilde\gamma(t) = e^{2\pi i (k + t)/3} @@ a path upstairs with (γ̃(t))³ = e^{2πit}, starting at e^{2πik/3}
\tilde\gamma(1) = e^{2\pi i (k + 1)/3} @@ where the lift ends
k \mapsto k + 1 \bmod 3 @@ the shift on the three points over 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one orbit; the loops that bring 0 back: } 3\mathbb{Z}} \quad \blacksquare @@ by <a href="r34g.html#5161ee">34g4</a>, a loop going round w times shifts by w

The line over the circle gives the whole numbers with n ↦ n + 1; two circles side by side, each over the circle by z ↦ z, give two points that the shift leaves alone. The part-group of loops that bring a point back is the one 34g5 matched with the covering.

inf22.2Fields as Sets With a Shuffle

Let K be the splitting field of x³ − 2 over ℚ, with roots r₁ = ∛2, r₂ = ω∛2, r₃ = ω²∛2, and G its group, all six shuffles of the roots by 46c4. A field E between ℚ and K can be placed inside K in several ways: a placement is a map from E to K keeping sums and products and fixing ℚ. Follow a placement by a member of G and it is a placement again.

Placement technique: the placements of E in K form a set on which G acts with one orbit, of size [E : ℚ], and the members of G that fix the plain placement are Aut(K/E).

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}):\ \sqrt[3]{2} \mapsto r_1, r_2, r_3 @@ a placement is fixed by where ∛2 goes, and it must go to a root
\text{fixing the placement } \sqrt[3]{2} \mapsto r_1\text{: } \{e, (2\ 3)\} @@ the shuffles that leave r₁ where it is

inf22.3The Shared Shape

Both footings have the same shape. A connected thing gives a set with an action of a group and one orbit, and the thing is recorded by which members fix a chosen point. Things that are not connected are sums of connected ones, and their sets are sums of orbits.

One-orbit technique: a set X on which G acts with one orbit is matched with the classes gH, where H is the part-group fixing a point x, by g · x ↦ gH; and this matching respects the action.

\text{every member of } X \text{ is } g \cdot x \text{ for some } g @@ one orbit
g \cdot x = g' \cdot x \iff g^{-1} g' \cdot x = x \iff g^{-1} g' \in H @@ undo g on both sides
g \cdot x = g' \cdot x \iff gH = g'H @@ the classes of H
\htmlClass{new}{X \leftrightarrow G/H,\ \ h \cdot (g \cdot x) \mapsto hgH} \quad \blacksquare @@ well defined, one-to-one, onto, and respecting the action

So coverings with three sheets that are connected are the classes of 3ℤ in ℤ, and the placements of ℚ(∛2) are the classes of {e, (2 3)} in the six shuffles. 34g5’s matching and 46d3’s matching are this technique applied to two forgetful maps: points over the base point, and placements in K.

inf22.4The Group Read Off the Forgetful Map

As in ∞γ.3, a symmetry of the forgetful map is a family that shuffles each set, one shuffle per thing, commuting with every map that respects the action. For finite sets on which a finite G acts, every g gives one.

Read-off technique: the symmetries of the forgetful map on sets with a G-action are exactly the members of G.

G \to X,\ \ g \mapsto g \cdot x \text{ respects the action} @@ G acting on itself
u \text{ is fixed by } a = u_G(e) @@ <a href="r47g.html#7c833a">47g2</a>’s arrows-into technique
u_X(x) = u_X(e \cdot x) = u_G(e) \cdot x = a \cdot x @@ u commutes with the map g ↦ g · x
\htmlClass{new}{u = \text{“act by } a\text{”}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and each a gives such a u

Now do the same with the coverings of the circle that have finitely many sheets. A symmetry shifts the n-sheet covering by some aₙ mod n, and commuting with the map from the n-sheet covering onto the m-sheet one, for m dividing n, asks that aₙ and aₘ agree mod m. Each whole number gives such a family, but at each step from 2ᵏ to 2ᵏ⁺¹ sheets there are two choices, so by 35c2 there are more families than whole numbers. Read off finitely many sheets, the loop group of the circle comes back larger than ℤ.

inf22.5What a Loop Means for ℚ

Take every field of finite degree over ℚ inside ℂ, with their placements, and read the group off that forgetful map. It is the group of symmetries of all the numbers that solve equations over ℚ, and it plays for ℚ the part the loop group plays for a space: its part-groups match the fields, as loops match coverings. One member is easy to name.

Mirror-loop technique: the flip of ℂ, a + bi ↦ a − bi, sends every splitting field inside ℂ to itself and is a member of its group; for x³ − 2 it is the swap (2 3).

\overline{z + w} = \bar z + \bar w,\ \ \overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w,\ \ \bar q = q \ (q \in \mathbb{Q}) @@ the flip keeps sums and products and fixes ℚ
f(\bar r) = \overline{f(r)} = 0 @@ the flip of a root is a root, since f has rational coefficients
\bar r_1 = r_1,\ \ \bar\omega = \omega^2 \Rightarrow \bar r_2 = r_3 @@ ∛2 is real; ω is not
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the flip is } (2\ 3) \in G} \quad \blacksquare @@ applied twice it does nothing: a loop of order 2

For a curve given by equations over ℚ, each point with rational coordinates gives a way to split the curve’s loop group over ℚ’s. The question posed in 1983 asks whether every such splitting comes from a point, so that the points could be told by loops alone. Special cases have been proved, among them one over fields like the numbers of a prime’s clock taken endlessly far (2005); the question itself stands.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞八.oq.1Are the points of a curve told by loops? The question was posed in a letter of 1983 for curves of genus at least 2 over fields of finite degree over ℚ. Special cases and variants have been proved since, among them a version for the field of a curve over the endless clocks of a prime (2005) and partial results over small number fields (2012); the question itself is still a conjecture.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1983 a mathematician wrote a letter to a colleague setting out a geometry in which a curve is recovered from its loop group over the loops of its field; the colleague never answered, and the letter was published in 1997.

In 2005 a proof by logic settled a version of the question, for the fields of curves over the endless clocks of a prime, and showed that it fails for some other fields. In 2012 further partial results were reported over small number fields.

The accepted names

A category of sums of connected pieces with a point-counting forgetful map is a Galois category; the forgetful map is the fibre functor, and the group read off it is the fundamental group. The shift is the monodromy action. The one-orbit technique is the orbit–stabiliser theorem; a set with one orbit is a transitive G-set, matched with G/H. Read off finite coverings, the loop group of the circle is the profinite completion ℤ̂. The group of all algebraic numbers is the absolute Galois group Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ), the étale fundamental group of a point over ℚ; the flip is complex conjugation. The 1983 question is Grothendieck’s section conjecture in anabelian geometry; the endless clocks of a prime are the p-adic numbers.

Who said it first

Alexander Grothendieck, SGA 1 (Galois categories); “Brief an G. Faltings” (27 June 1983; published in Geometric Galois Actions 1, 1997). Jochen Koenigsmann, “On the ‘section conjecture’ in anabelian geometry” (J. reine angew. Math. 588, 2005). Jakob Stix, report on progress on the section conjecture (Oberwolfach, 2012).

Earlier Proofs, in Arrows

34g5’s covering-lift technique, 34g4’s circle-loops technique and 46d3’s middle-field technique are three readings of ∞八’s shared shape. Lifting each path in exactly one way makes the points over the base point a set on which loops act, the shift of ∞八.1. The circle’s loops act on the points of the line over one point, the whole numbers, in one orbit, with every loop but the do-nothing moving every point, which is why the loops are the whole numbers. And matching part-groups with middle fields is ∞八.3’s one-orbit technique read for placements.
