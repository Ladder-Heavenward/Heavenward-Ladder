Wave Recipes by Hand

Three waves come up again and again wherever anything is switched, swept or bounced. The square wave flips between two levels: a clock in a chip, a light blinking on a buoy. The sawtooth climbs steadily and drops: the sweep of an old television, the tide of charge in a flash. The triangle climbs and falls at the same steady pace: a ball bounced between two walls. Each one is a sum of turnings, and the recipe for each, the list of how much of each speed goes in, can be worked out by hand in a few lines. This step does that, and then finds the rule behind the three recipes. How fast the amounts fall off with speed is set by how rough the wave is. A wave that jumps needs a lot of every speed. A wave with only corners needs much less. Each smoother kind needs less again. And a few old endless sums drop out along the way, read off the recipes at one point.

The square wave is four over π times the odd sines, each over its speed. The sawtooth is twice the sines with alternating signs, each over its speed, and any wave can be slid, stretched or lifted without working its recipe again. The triangle is the square’s recipe divided by the speed once more, as cosines. A jump makes the amounts fall like one over the speed, a corner like one over its square.

32h1The Square Wave

The square wave q is 1 on (0, π), −1 on (−π, 0), repeated. It is odd, so it has sines alone (32a3).

Square-recipe technique: the square wave is (4/π)(sin x + sin 3x / 3 + sin 5x / 5 + ⋯), the odd speeds alone, each over its speed.

32h2The Sawtooth, Slid and Stretched

32b4 found the sawtooth that is x on (−π, π): cₙ = i(−1)ⁿ/n, which in sines reads x = 2(sin x − sin 2x / 2 + sin 3x / 3 − ⋯). Real sawtooths start at other places, climb to other heights and repeat over other times. None of that needs a new total.

Slide-and-stretch technique: sliding a wave by s multiplies its nth coefficient by e^(−ins); running it over a repeat of length T instead of 2π keeps every coefficient, with the turning e^(2πint/T) in place of e^(inx); and adding a constant changes c₀ alone.

c_n(f(\cdot - s)) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int f(x - s)\,e^{-inx}\,dx = e^{-ins}\,c_n(f) @@ slide the variable; f repeats, so the window does not matter
g(t) = f(2\pi t/T):\ \ \tfrac1T\int_0^T g(t)\,e^{-2\pi int/T}\,dt = c_n(f) @@ slide the variable, x = 2πt/T
\htmlClass{new}{c_0(f + k) = c_0(f) + k,\ \ c_n(f + k) = c_n(f) \text{ for } n \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the constant is the 0th turning (32a1)
  
\tfrac tT = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{x}{2\pi},\ \ x = \tfrac{2\pi t}{T} - \pi @@ on [0, T), x runs over [−π, π)
\tfrac{x}{2\pi} = \tfrac1\pi\textstyle\sum_n (-1)^{n+1}\tfrac{\sin nx}{n} @@ 32b4’s recipe, scaled by 1/(2π)
\sin\big(n(\tfrac{2\pi t}{T} - \pi)\big) = (-1)^n\sin\tfrac{2\pi nt}{T} @@ a slide by π flips odd speeds

32h3The Triangle, and How Fast Recipes Fall

The triangle w is |x| on [−π, π], repeated. Its slope is the square wave, w′ = q, off the corners. A slope is a multiplication for coefficients too.

Corner-and-jump technique: if f repeats every 2π, is continuous, and its slope f′ is sized and bounded, then cₙ(f′) = in cₙ(f); so a wave with a jump has coefficients falling like 1/n, one with corners and no jump like 1/n², and each further continuous slope buys one more power of n.

c_n(f') = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\Big[f(x)\,e^{-inx}\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} + \tfrac{in}{2\pi}\int f(x)\,e^{-inx}\,dx @@ parts (16), piece by piece between the corners
\Big[f(x)\,e^{-inx}\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = 0 @@ f is continuous and repeats, and so does e^(−inx)
c_n(f') = in\,c_n(f) @@ what is left
c_n(w) = \tfrac{c_n(q)}{in},\ \ c_n(q) = \tfrac{2}{\pi in} \text{ for odd } n @@ w′ = q; the square recipe in turnings (32h1)
\htmlClass{new}{w(x) = \tfrac\pi2 - \tfrac4\pi\big(\cos x + \tfrac{\cos 3x}{9} + \tfrac{\cos 5x}{25} + \cdots\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ cₙ(w) = −2/(πn²) for odd n; c₀ = π/2, the average of |x|

At x = 0 the triangle is 0, and the recipe gives π/2 − (4/π)(1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + ⋯), which is 0 again by 32h1: two waves, one sum. The square jumps, and its amounts fall like 1/n; the triangle has corners and no jumps, and its fall like 1/n². The sound of each tells the same story: a square wave from a synthesiser is buzzy, a triangle is mellow, and a pure turning is a flute-like hum.

The accepted names

The three waves are the square, sawtooth and triangle waves of electronics and synthesis. The slide-and-stretch technique gathers the shift and scaling properties of Fourier coefficients, and the corner-and-jump technique the differentiation property with its consequence for decay: the smoother the function, the faster its coefficients decay. 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − ⋯ = π/4 is the Leibniz series (the Madhava–Leibniz series); 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + ⋯ = π²/8 is a companion of the Basel sum. The recipe of 32h.5.2 is a Weierstrass-type function, continuous with no slope anywhere when the speeds grow fast enough against the amounts.

Who said it first

Madhava of Sangamagrama found the series for π/4 around 1400, as recorded by later scholars of the Kerala school. James Gregory found the arc series in 1671, and Gottfried Leibniz the series for π/4 in 1673. Fourier worked the square wave’s recipe in his memoir of 1807. Robert Moog’s synthesisers of the 1960s made square, sawtooth and triangle waves the raw material of electronic sound, shaped afterwards by filters (32i).

A proof that is not allowed

“The triangle’s slope is the square wave, so take the square’s recipe and find the slope of every term.” Not allowed: the slope of each term of the square’s recipe gives (4/π) Σ cos nx, which settles nowhere. Going from a wave to its slope multiplies by in and can break a sum; going from a slope to its wave divides, and the corner-and-jump technique proves that direction from parts.
