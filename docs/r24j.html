24j · technique

Back From s to t

24i turned a rate statement into an equation for F. To finish, F must be read back as a function of t. When F is a ratio of polynomials, 17k’s partial fractions break it into pieces the table already holds. This step reads back a second-order statement with its two starts, a statement with a switched-on push, proves that two functions with the same transform cannot differ in the way that matters here, and reads the far future straight off F without reading back at all.

Split F into partial fractions and read each piece from the table. Y = (s + 3)/((s + 1)(s + 2)) gives y = 2e^(−t) − e^(−2t). Continuous functions with the same transform are equal. If y settles, its resting value is the limit of sY(s) as s goes to 0.

24j1Reading Back by Pieces

(s^2Y - s) + 3(sY - 1) + 2Y = 0 @@ transform, with both starts (24i3)
Y(s^2 + 3s + 2) = s + 3 @@ algebra
Y = \dfrac{s+3}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \dfrac{2}{s+1} - \dfrac{1}{s+2} @@ partial fractions (17k)
\htmlClass{new}{y = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}} @@ check: y(0) = 1, y′(0) = −2 + 2 = 0

Read-back technique: when Y is a ratio of polynomials with the top of lower degree, split it into partial fractions and read each piece from the table. The roots of the bottom, −1 and −2, are the growth rates 24h3 tried; the transform finds them and fits the starts in one pass.

24j2A Push Switched On

A tank empties through a pipe at a rate in proportion to its level, y′ = −y, and at t = 0 a tap is opened that adds 1 litre a minute. It starts empty.

sY + Y = \dfrac1s @@ 𝓛1 = 1/s
Y = \dfrac{1}{s(s+1)} = \dfrac1s - \dfrac{1}{s+1} @@ partial fractions
y = 1 - e^{-t} @@ read back
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the level rises to } 1 \text{ litre and rests}} @@ in, 1 a minute; out, the level a minute

A push that starts at t = 0 costs nothing extra: its transform is one more term on the right. That is why engineers reach for the transform whenever a switch is thrown.

24j3One Transform, One Function

Same-transform technique: if f and g are continuous on t ≥ 0, grow no faster than some e^(ct), and have the same transform for all s > c, then f = g. Its full proof is held in trust for a later rung. What this rung uses is the case it can check: every function it reads back is a sum of terms Ae^(at), and those are fixed by their transforms.

\sum A_i e^{a_i t} \text{ has transform } \sum \dfrac{A_i}{s - a_i} @@ 24i2, the aᵢ distinct
\text{if two such sums have the same transform, their difference has transform } 0 @@ subtract
\sum \dfrac{B_i}{s - a_i} = 0 \text{ for all large } s \Rightarrow \text{every } B_i = 0 @@ multiply by s − aⱼ and let s → aⱼ: Bⱼ = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the two sums are equal}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so reading back gives the one answer

24j4The Far Future, Read Off F

\mathcal{L}y^{\prime} = sY - y(0) @@ 24i3
s \to 0:\ \int_0^\infty y^{\prime}\,dt = \lim_{t\to\infty} y - y(0) @@ the fade becomes 1
\lim_{t\to\infty} y(t) = \lim_{s \to 0} sY(s) @@ when y settles
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tank: } sY = \tfrac{1}{s+1} \to 1} @@ the resting litre, with no reading back

Resting-value technique: if y settles to a limit (22’s settling) and y′ has a transform near s = 0, the resting value is the limit of sY(s) as s goes to 0. The condition matters: Y = 1/(s − 1) gives sY → 0, yet eᵗ never rests. Check the roots of the bottom first: all of them below zero, and y settles.

Accepted names

Reading back is the inverse Laplace transform; the same-transform technique is Lerch’s theorem; the resting-value technique is the final value theorem, and s → ∞ gives the initial value theorem. The switched-on push is the Heaviside step function, and the roots of Y’s bottom are its poles.

Who said it first

Mathias Lerch proved that a transform fixes its function in 1903. The final value theorem became a tool of control engineers from the 1930s.
