Fixing truth

On 1b the ladder did something deliberate and a little strange. It gave you a tech that turns “this statement leads to something totally false” into “this statement is not so”, and a tech that turns a statement into “it is not so that it is not so”. Then it refused the step back: from the double not to the plain statement there was no tech. Every page since has kept that gap open and marked the places where it mattered. This rung closes it. The principle is that every statement is true or false, whether or not anyone can show which. The surprise is that on this ladder you do not have to lay it down. Rung 6's choosing axiom, together with rung 2's sifting, already proves it.

And you have always believed this. A light is on or it is off. A door is shut or it is not. You never needed a proof to expect one or the other. This rung asks what it costs the ladder to believe it too, and writes the price down.

Every statement is so or not so, with nothing between. Where every case can be checked, checking proves it. Where the cases never end, picking without a rule proves it anyway, and tells you nothing about which.

What it means to say every statement is true or false

The principle is an or: P is true, or ¬P is true. The or is 1c's, so it would allow both, but both is impossible, since P and ¬P together clash. So the content is “at least one”. Read it slowly, because it is easy to hear more than it says. It does not say that you can find out which. It does not say that someone already knows. It says that one of the two is so. And it says this for every statement the ladder can write, including statements about endlessly many things that nobody will ever finish checking.

Compare it with what rung 1 lets you do. To make an or, 1c's tech is to add an option: from P you may write P ∨ Q. So on rung 1 every or you have came from somewhere. Either you had one side in hand, or a technique handed you the whole or. 4h's settled-order, for example, handed you “one number is less than another, or not” by a column that compared them. The principle of this rung is an or that arrives with no side in hand and no column behind it. That is what is new, and it is exactly the step from ¬¬P to P in another form. 7a proves that the two are the same principle.

Why the ladder waited

Rung 1 could have laid the principle down on its first page. It waited so that you could see, at every later step, what each result costs. Look at what the gap marked along the way. 1f's rulebook had a licence column, with five techniques allowed in one direction only. 2e's techniques for sets had one inclusion where the usual statement is a sameness. 4h proved that a set of numbers whose membership can be decided, number by number, has a least member. For any other set it got only “it is not so that it has none”. 4k counted the parts of a finite set that can be decided, and not the rest. 5·cb2 proved that two classes that share a member are the same. It did not say that two classes are always the same or share nothing. 6b found that “not empty” and “has a member” come apart. 6e's proof that the maximal-member principle gives choice stopped one line short. Each of these is a place where the principle of this rung is the missing line. 7d, 7·cb4 and 7·cb6 go back and write it.

Waiting also showed that the gap is real. 7a builds a world, in the style of 3d and 6d, in which every tech of rung 1 is honoured and the principle fails. It is a world of stages, where a statement is true at a stage once it has been established, and might be established later. In such a world “not not P” can be true while P is not. So no amount of cleverness with rung 1's techs alone would ever have closed the gap.

Two ways of doing mathematics

Early in the century before this one, a Dutch mathematician argued that the principle should be refused. A statement about endlessly many things, he said, is true only when someone has a proof of it. Then “P or not P” would mean that every question has been answered or will be, which nobody has a right to assume. His followers kept rung 1's gap in all their mathematics. A leader of the majority answered that to take the principle away from mathematicians would be to forbid the astronomer his telescope. Both sides were right about something. A proof that uses the principle can show that a thing exists without telling you where it is. A proof that does not use it hands you the thing, and is worth more. The ladder kept that difference in view for six rungs so that you would feel it. From here on it lets you use the principle freely, while asking you to notice when you do.

Where the principle comes from on this ladder

When you can check, you do not need to be told. Take a set with a number of members, and a condition that can be checked at each member. Then its every and its some can be checked too: look at every member, one at a time. 7b proves this by induction on the number of members, and so the principle adds nothing about finitely many checkable cases. Its whole weight falls on statements whose cases never end. “Some even number above 2 is not a sum of two primes” is the standing example. Every even number anyone has tried is a sum of two primes, and every single case can be checked. But there are endlessly many cases, and nobody has proved that none fails.

Then the twist. In 1975 it was noticed that the choosing axiom proves the principle outright. The argument uses only the set {0, 1}, two sifts of it by a condition that mentions any statement P you like, and a choosing function for the two sifts. The sifts are built so that whether they are the same set depends on P. A choosing function must pick the same member from the same set. So its picks carry news about P into the open, where an ordinary check of 0 against 1 can read it. 7c works it line by line. The same argument was found again, independently, a few years later. So on this ladder the principle is a technique of rungs 0 to 6, and the gap closes by a proof, not by a new axiom. The road does not run back: the principle does not give choice, as 6d's worlds already showed.

What you can do because you know it

With the principle in hand, ¬¬P gives P. Every technique of 1f's rulebook may be used in both directions, for any statements, in any place. You may reason by cases on any statement at all, “if P, then this; if not, then that”, even when nobody knows which case you are in. Every part of a set is settled, so a set with n members has 2ⁿ parts, every one of them. Every mark of 4·comp is 0 or 1. Two classes are the same or share nothing. 7d proves each of these in a few lines.

The most famous gain is a proof shape. To prove P, suppose ¬P and reach something totally false. Rung 1 already gave you ¬¬P from that. Now you take the last step to P. This is proof by contradiction, the shape that opens more proofs than any other. 7e is the page where your hands get fast at it: how to write the supposition, and how to recognise the collision. It also shows how to tell when a proof by contradiction is really a direct proof in costume. That is worth knowing, because the direct proof says more.

The two newsflashes for rungs 4 and 6 finish what those rungs left open. 7·cb4 proves that every set of numbers with a member has a least member, so ≤ is at last a well-order of ℕ. It also brings back foundation's form with a bottom, which 3a·f stated in words and set aside: every set with a member has a member that shares nothing with it. 7·cb6 removes the double negative from 6e's column, so the maximal-member principle gives choice. And it proves the other direction too, that choice gives the maximal-member principle, by an argument that needs no run past the counting numbers.

The rung's science page is about electricity. A switch is open or closed, and a circuit of switches computes with true and false, as 4·comp showed. 7·phys does the electrical level properly. It covers charge and current, voltage as energy carried by each unit of charge, and resistance. It covers the current-voltage proportion, the rules for one path and for side-by-side paths, the junction and loop laws as equations to solve, and power. It ends by naming the three levels at which people use electrons and where the ladder takes each.

At the end of the rung you will know what the principle says and does not say, and why rung 1 could not prove it. You will know why rung 6 can, and what it buys. You will also know the price, and the habit worth keeping from the side that refused it: when a proof tells you that one of two things is true, ask whether it tells you which.

That is the start of it. Logicians with a lifetime behind them still argue about this principle, and still call themselves beginners at it.

Who said it first

The Dutch mathematician is L. E. J. Brouwer; the answer about the telescope is David Hilbert’s (1927). The proof that choice gives the principle is Radu Diaconescu’s (1975); Nicolas Goodman and John Myhill found the same argument in 1978.

Brouwer believed that the laws of logic were drawn from reasoning about finite collections, where everything can be inspected, and that to trust the excluded middle for endless collections is a step mathematics has no warrant for.

Brouwer, “De onbetrouwbaarheid der logische principes” (The unreliability of the logical principles), Tijdschrift voor Wijsbegeerte 2 (1908)

Accepted names

The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle. Those who refuse it are intuitionists, and mathematics that keeps rung 1’s gap is constructive mathematics; mathematics that uses the principle freely is classical. The even-sum guess is Goldbach’s conjecture. The current-voltage proportion is Ohm’s law, and the junction and loop laws are Kirchhoff’s laws.
