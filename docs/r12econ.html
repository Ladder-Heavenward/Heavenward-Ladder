Must There Be a Price Where Buyers and Sellers Match?

Must there be a price where buyers and sellers match? Economists have drawn it that way since the eighteen hundreds: demand falls as the price rises, supply climbs, and the two cross. But 11·econ found a market where they never cross. When picking had a cost, supply jumped clean over demand, and fruit stayed on the trees. So the question is a real one. It is hard for two reasons. Supply and demand are guesses, read from what buyers and sellers did at the few prices they met, not at every price. And the price where they match is rarely one that algebra can solve for. This page proves that a matching price exists whenever supply and demand have no jumps, buyers want more than sellers offer at a low price, and sellers offer more than buyers want at a high one. The proof is 12a's in-between values. Then the page asks what the proof leaves out: where the price is, how many there are, whether a market ever finds one, and what happens when prices come in whole cents.

Take the gap: what sellers offer at a price, less what buyers want there. At a low price the gap is negative, and at a high price it is positive. If the gap has no jumps, it passes through nothing on the way, and at that price buyers and sellers match. Halving traps the price as tightly as you like. If supply only rises and demand only falls, it is the only matching price. Over many seasons a fishery's supply can turn back down, and then there can be two. Prices charged in whole cents have holes, and the match can fall between two of them.

12econ1The Words

Take the first week of the Dungeness crab season at Pillar Point Harbor, near Half Moon Bay. Boats sell crab off the dock to fish markets and restaurants, by the pound. At each price some pounds are landed and offered, and some are wanted. The numbers below are made up to be the right size, not measured; their shape is the usual one.

From here on, on this page, p is a price in dollars per pound. D(p) is the pounds a day buyers would take at p, and S(p) the pounds a day the boats would land and offer at p, as in 11·econ. The gap is E(p) = S(p) − D(p): the pounds offered but not bought at p. When E(p) is negative, buyers want more than is offered.

The guess for the dock is D(p) = 6000 − 400p and S(p) = 50p³. Demand is a line, as in 11·econ. Supply is not: at a low price only the boats near the harbor go out, and each dollar more sends boats further and pulls more pots. So E(p) = 50p³ + 400p − 6000. Setting it to 0 asks for the answer of a sameness of degree 3, and the ladder has no formula for that.

Still, two readings settle a lot. At $4 a pound, S(4) = 3200 and D(4) = 4400, so E(4) = −1200: buyers are short. At $5, S(5) = 6250 and D(5) = 4000, so E(5) = 2250: crab is left over. Somewhere between $4 and $5 the gap changes sign, and it has no jumps on the way.

12econ2A Matching Price Exists

Matching-price technique. Let S and D be continuous on [v, w]. Suppose S(v) < D(v): at the low price, buyers want more. Suppose S(w) > D(w): at the high price, sellers offer more. Then some price p with v < p < w has S(p) = D(p). If supply and demand have no jumps, and the shortage at a low price turns into a surplus at a high one, then some price between makes them match.

E(v) = S(v) - D(v) \lt 0 \lt S(w) - D(w) = E(w) @@ the two suppositions, each moved across (adding-keeps-answers)
\text{some } c \text{ with } v \lt c \lt w \text{ and } E(c) = 0 @@ in-between-value (12a)

For the dock, E is a polynomial, so it is continuous everywhere (11d), and v = 4, w = 5 do the rest. A matching price lies strictly between $4 and $5. The proof does not solve anything. It spends the line with no holes, 12a's least upper bound, at exactly one place.

12econ4What the Proof Does Not Say: How Many

One-match technique. Let S rise on [v, w]: p < q gives S(p) < S(q). Let D fall on [v, w]: p < q gives D(p) > D(q). Then at most one price in [v, w] has S(p) = D(p). When supply only rises and demand only falls, there is at most one matching price.

p \lt q \Rightarrow E(p) \lt E(q) @@ same-direction-add (8g)
E(p) = 0 = E(q) \text{ with } p \ne q @@ suppose two matches; name the smaller p
E(p) \lt E(q),\ \text{so } 0 \lt 0 @@ the line above

Both suppositions matter. Over many seasons a high price draws more boats, and together they take more crab than the stock replaces. Then the catch falls, and so does what the fleet can land. Fishery economists found this in 1970: where anyone may fish, the long-run supply first rises with the price and then turns back down.

Matching-price runs on [0, 7] and finds the first. Run on [7, 12], with the shortage and surplus the other way round, it finds the second: the technique only needs the gap to change sign. At $2.64 the fleet lands about 4940 pounds a day. At $11.36 it lands about 1450. Both are matches. The proof says nothing about which one a market would settle at.

12econ5What the Proof Does Not Say: Whether a Market Gets There

11·econ guessed that a price drifts toward the match: where buyers are short they bid it up, and where crab is left over the boats cut it. That guess is a law, read from markets, with a range and exceptions. It is not a technique. The matching-price technique says a match exists. It says nothing about the road to it, or how long the road takes, or whether the market is still there when it arrives. A proof that a price exists is not a proof that anyone finds it.

Halving is one road, and it looks like haggling. Try a price, see whether buyers or sellers are left over, and move toward the other side. A market does not halve exactly, and it may overshoot. Whether it still closes in is a question for rung 13 and later.

12econ6What the Proof Does Not Say: In Whole Cents

The dock does not charge $4.3942 a pound. It charges whole cents, and the cents are like 12d's fractions: a line with holes. At $4.39, E is about −13.8: buyers are short by about 14 pounds a day. At $4.40, E is about 19.2: about 19 pounds are left over. No price in whole cents matches.

So the market clears near the match, not at it. A few pounds go unsold, or a few buyers go without. On a line with holes the in-between-value technique fails at its least upper bound line, as 12d showed for x² − 2. Here the fault is small, because a cent is small beside the trade. The matching price lives on the line, not on the price list.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change, proved

12a proved the mathematical side of a principle met in 6·chem, 10·bio and 11·chem: a continuous change from below 0 to above 0 passes through 0. The dock's gap runs from a shortage at $4 to a surplus at $5. It cannot get from one to the other without a middle where nobody is short and nothing is left over.

A market whose gap has no jumps has a middle, even when no one trades there.

The middle at about $4.3942 is a price nobody charges, between two cents. Yet the trades at $4.39 and $4.40 are measured against it: one leaves 14 pounds short, the other 19 pounds over. The middle is what makes those two numbers small.

12econ8Running It Backwards

Handed a matching price and one of the two guesses, solve for what the other must be there: with S = 50p³ and a match at $4, demand must be 3200 pounds at $4. Handed a table of the gap at a few prices, count its sign changes: each one between neighbours holds a match, so the table proves at least that many. Handed a halving record of kept halves, read the trap back from the bits, as 12e did.

Who said it first

Léon Walras, in the Elements of Pure Economics (1874), set a whole economy of matching prices down as equations and pictured an auctioneer who calls prices and gropes toward the match; the groping has the accepted name tâtonnement. Whether the equations had an answer was argued for sixty years. Abraham Wald gave the first proofs for special cases in the 1930s. Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu proved in 1954 that a whole economy has matching prices, with a technique about continuous functions that has the accepted name a fixed point theorem; for one good and one price it is the in-between-value technique of this page. The fishery whose long-run supply turns back is Parzival Copes's, in 1970.

Hayek argued that the knowledge a planner would need never exists in one mind. It is scattered among many people, each knowing a little about their own time and place, and a price passes on just enough of it.

Friedrich Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945); paraphrase

In 12·econ.9.4, the side that doubted planners is Ludwig von Mises (1920) and Hayek; the side that trusted them is Oskar Lange (1936–37), who answered that a planning board could copy the market’s trial and error. The argument has the accepted name the socialist calculation debate.

Accepted names

A matching price is an equilibrium price, or the market-clearing price. S − D is the excess supply, and D − S the excess demand; a negative excess supply is a shortage, a positive one a surplus. A supply that falls at high prices is a backward-bending supply curve.
