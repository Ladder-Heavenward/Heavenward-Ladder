41·comp · computing

Must Computing Cost Energy?

Must computing cost energy? 22·comp found one cost no engineering can remove: wiping a bit clean sends at least a fixed small amount of heat into the room, and a gate that sends two inputs to one output has wiped something. That left the other half of the question open. Must a computer wipe? Looking at a bit and writing down what you saw feels like it should cost something too, and for decades physicists thought it did: the small being at the door between two halves of a box of gas was supposed to pay for looking at each molecule. Rung thirty eight’s research front measured how far today’s switches are from the floor, and left the floor itself for here. This page separates the steps of a computation that must pay from the ones that need not. Copying a bit onto a clean cell can be done by a gate that forgets nothing. The small being pays only when it wipes its notebook, and then exactly as much as it gained. Any computation at all can be run without forgetting, by running it forward, copying the answer, and running it backward. The price is paid only for what is thrown away.

Copying onto a clean cell forgets nothing and need cost nothing; wiping must pay kT ln 2 a bit. The small being gains kT ln 2 from each look and pays it back when it wipes. Any computation can be done forgetting nothing, by computing, copying the answer, and uncomputing. Today’s switches spend about seventeen thousand times the floor.

41comp1Looking Can Be Free

22comp5’s gate-loss technique said a gate pays heat for each bit it forgets, and a gate whose rows can be run backwards forgets nothing. Measuring a bit A means making a second cell hold the same value. Do it with the gate (A, B) ↦ (A, A ⊕ B), ⊕ being 1e’s either-but-not-both, on a cell B that is known to hold 0.

Free-copy technique: copying a bit onto a cell known to hold 0 can be done by a gate that forgets nothing, so the gate-loss technique asks no heat for it.

(0, 0) \mapsto (0, 0),\ \allowbreak \ (0, 1) \mapsto (0, 1),\ \allowbreak \ (1, 0) \mapsto (1, 1),\ \allowbreak \ (1, 1) \mapsto (1, 0) @@ the four rows of (A, B) ↦ (A, A ⊕ B)
\text{four }\allowbreak \text{rows }\allowbreak \text{to }\allowbreak \text{four }\allowbreak \text{different }\allowbreak \text{outputs} @@ it can be run backwards: it is its own undoing
\text{with } B = 0\text{: } (A, 0) \mapsto (A, A) @@ the second cell now holds A
\htmlClass{new}{\text{bits }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{forgotten: } 0\text{, }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{heat }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{asked: }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{none}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gate-loss technique

The catch is the clean cell. A cell known to hold 0 was made so, and if it was made by wiping, that was paid then. A computer that copies into fresh cells for ever needs an endless supply of clean ones, or must wipe its old copies. The cost has moved from the looking to the cleaning.

41comp2The Small Being’s Books

22·comp.7.5 set a small being at a door in a box of gas, sorting molecules with no work done. Take the smallest case: one molecule in a box at temperature T, as in 22comp4. The being slides a wall into the middle, looks which side the molecule is on, and writes it in a one-bit notebook. Then it puts a piston against the wall on the empty side and lets the molecule push it back to the end, slowly, at a fixed temperature.

Being’s-books technique: each cycle of look, push and wipe gains at most kT ln 2 of work and pays at least kT ln 2 of heat, so the being gains nothing.

\text{look: }\allowbreak \text{copy }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{side }\allowbreak \text{onto }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{clean }\allowbreak \text{notebook }\allowbreak \text{bit} @@ the free-copy technique: no heat asked
\text{push: }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{molecule }\allowbreak \text{doubles }\allowbreak \text{its }\allowbreak \text{room }\allowbreak \text{at }\allowbreak \text{temperature } T @@ the halved-room technique (22comp3), run backwards, with N = 1
\text{work }\allowbreak \text{gained} = kT\ln 2\text{, }\allowbreak \text{drawn }\allowbreak \text{as }\allowbreak \text{heat }\allowbreak \text{from }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{room} @@ the gas keeps its energy at fixed temperature
\text{to }\allowbreak \text{look }\allowbreak \text{again }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{notebook }\allowbreak \text{must }\allowbreak \text{be }\allowbreak \text{clean: }\allowbreak \text{wipe }\allowbreak \text{it} @@ its old bit is not wanted
\text{heat }\allowbreak \text{into }\allowbreak \text{the }\allowbreak \text{room} \ge kT\ln 2 @@ the erasure technique (22comp4)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{net }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{work }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{per }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{cycle} \le kT\ln 2 - kT\ln 2 = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the books balance at best

At 300 K that is about 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J gained and at least as much paid, each cycle. The second law of heat is not broken by looking. It is saved by wiping. A being with an endless notebook would never wipe, and would turn heat into work for as long as its notebook lasted; that notebook, filled with random bits, is itself a store of the entropy the gas lost, k ln 2 a bit, as 22comp1 counted it.

41comp3Computing Without Forgetting

22comp5 met a gate with three inputs whose 8 rows go to 8 different outputs, and which gives A · B on its third wire when that wire starts at 0. With it and the copy gate, every table of 1e can be built forgetting nothing, at the price of extra wires: the inputs ride along, and the work in between is left as a heap of bits nobody wants. Call that heap the scrap.

Uncompute technique: any computation of f(x) can be done with gates that forget nothing, ending with x, f(x) and clean cells, in about twice the steps.

(x, 0, 0, 0) \mapsto (x, \text{scrap}, f(x), 0) @@ run the gates forward; nothing is forgotten, so the scrap remains
(x, \text{scrap}, f(x), 0) \mapsto (x, \text{scrap}, f(x), f(x)) @@ the free-copy technique onto the clean last cells
(x, \text{scrap}, f(x), f(x)) \mapsto (x, 0, 0, f(x)) @@ run the first gates backwards, in reverse order: each undoes itself
\htmlClass{new}{(x, 0, 0, 0) \mapsto (x, 0, 0, f(x))} \quad \blacksquare @@ the scrap and the first copy are cleaned by undoing, not by wiping

The same holds for tape machines: a machine can be built so that each configuration has at most one configuration before it, and such machines compute everything a tape machine computes, retaining a record of their moves and undoing it at the end. What is paid for is only what is thrown away at the end. Keep x and f(x), and nothing need be paid; throw x away, and the bits forgotten are what x held beyond f(x). The cost is room and time: the scrap must fit somewhere while it waits to be undone, and every step is taken twice.

41comp4How Far From the Floor

39·eng gave a switch’s heat as C V² for each charge and discharge of its gate. Take the numbers of a chip of the 2020s: a switch with C ≈ 0.1 fF, that is 10⁻¹⁶ F, at V = 0.7 V, ten billion switches, a clock of 3 GHz, and a tenth of the switches changing on each tick.

C V^2 = 10^{-16} \times 0.49 \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J} @@ one switching
kT\ln 2 = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 \times 0.693 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J} @@ the floor at 300 K (22comp4)
\frac{4.9 \times 10^{-17}}{2.87 \times 10^{-21}} \approx 1.7 \times 10^4 @@ each switching costs about seventeen thousand floors
10^{10} \times 0.1 \times 3 \times 10^9 = 3 \times 10^{18} \text{ switchings }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{second} @@ count of switches, share changing, ticks a second
3 \times 10^{18} \times 4.9 \times 10^{-17} \approx 147\ \text{W} @@ the chip’s power from switching
\htmlClass{new}{3 \times 10^{18} \times 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \approx 8.6 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{W}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the floor, if each switching wiped one bit

So the floor is not what heats a chip today. A chip pays about seventeen thousand times the floor, and almost all of it is not for forgetting: it is the charge dumped from each gate every time it switches, which a slow, reversible circuit could in principle recover, as 39·now described. The floor was measured directly in 2012: a glass bead held in a beam of laser light, its two resting places used as a bit, was wiped again and again, and the heat it gave off fell toward kT ln 2 as each wipe was made slower. A fast wipe paid more. That is the other half of the price: the floor is reached only by going slowly, and computing slowly is its own cost.

The accepted names

The copy gate is the controlled-NOT, or Feynman gate. The free-copy technique is the thermodynamically reversible measurement of Bennett, and the being’s-books technique is the exorcism of Maxwell’s demon through Landauer’s principle; the one-molecule cycle is the Szilard engine. The uncompute technique is Bennett’s compute-copy-uncompute method, and a machine with at most one configuration before each is a reversible Turing machine. The extra heat of a fast wipe is the finite-time cost of erasure.

Who said it first

Leo Szilard (1929) set up the one-molecule engine and placed the cost in measurement; Léon Brillouin (1951) agreed. Rolf Landauer (1961) argued that only erasure must cost. Charles Bennett (1973) built reversible Turing machines and the uncompute method, and in 1982 placed the demon’s cost in erasing its memory. Edward Fredkin and Tommaso Toffoli (1982) designed reversible circuits. John Earman and John Norton (1998, 1999) argued the exorcism is circular. Antoine Bérut and colleagues at the École normale supérieure de Lyon measured the erasure floor with a bead in a laser trap in 2012.

A proof that is not allowed

“A computation of f(x) that keeps x and f(x) forgets nothing, so a computer computing f in a loop on new inputs never pays.” Not allowed: each new input must be written into cells that held something, and those cells were wiped, or the old inputs kept for ever. The uncompute technique moves the cost to the inputs and outputs; it does not abolish it.
