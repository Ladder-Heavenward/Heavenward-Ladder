18·logi · logistics

How many of each loaf should a bakery bake?

How many of each loaf should a bakery bake? Each morning a baker in Eugene decides how many round country loaves and how many long thin ones to bake. Each kind earns something. Each uses up some flour, some room in the oven and some of the bakers’ time, and there is only so much of each. Bake more of one kind, and there is less left for the other. What makes it hard is that the limits pull in different directions. The loaf that earns most may be the one that eats the oven, and the one that is quick to shape may eat the flour. There are tens of thousands of possible plans, and trying them all by hand is hopeless. This page draws each limit as a line, with 18g’s lines in coordinates. The allowed plans fill a region with straight sides. It shows that the best plan sits at a corner of that region, never in its middle, and finds what one more bag of flour is worth.

Each limit says that a sum of so much per loaf stays under a fixed amount. That is one side of a line. All the limits together leave a region with straight sides and a few corners. The earnings are a sum of so much per loaf too, so plans with the same earnings lie on one line, and all those lines run the same way. Push that line outward until it is about to leave the region. The last place it touches is a corner. From any plan inside, a small step in the right direction earns more and breaks no limit. A limit with room to spare at the best corner is worth nothing more.

A bakery in Eugene, as a model

Here is a model of one bakery’s day: a bundle of facts to work on, not a survey of bakeries. It bakes x round loaves and y long ones. A round loaf takes 0.5 kg of flour, and a long one 0.25 kg. The bakery has 100 kg of flour a day. The oven holds 30 loaves of either kind a load, and there is time for 10 loads. Shaping a round loaf takes 1 minute, and a long one 3 minutes. Two bakers shape for 6 hours each, 720 minutes in all. A round loaf earns $3 over its costs, and a long one $2.

\text{flour}:\ \ 0.5x + 0.25y \le 100 \iff 2x + y \le 400 @@ times 4, which keeps the order (8d)
\text{oven}:\ \ x + y \le 300 @@ 30 loaves a load, 10 loads
\text{hands}:\ \ x + 3y \le 720 @@ minutes of shaping
x \ge 0,\ \ y \ge 0 @@ no loaf is baked less than 0 times
\text{earnings}:\ \ g(x, y) = 3x + 2y @@ dollars over costs

By sides-of-a-line (18g), each limit keeps one side of a line: a half-plane. The plans allowed by all five limits are the points in all five half-planes at once. That set holds the whole segment between any two of its points, so it is convex (18g). It is bounded, so it is a polygon, and its corners are where two of its edge lines meet (two-lines-meet).

y = 0,\ 2x + y = 400:\ \ (200, 0) @@ flour runs out first along the bottom
2x + y = 400,\ x + y = 300:\ \ (100, 200) @@ subtract: x = 100
x + y = 300,\ x + 3y = 720:\ \ (90, 210) @@ subtract: 2y = 420
x + 3y = 720,\ x = 0:\ \ (0, 240) @@ hands run out first up the side
(0, 0) @@ and each corner checked against the other limits: (100, 200) uses 700 of the 720 minutes

The best plan is at a corner

Best-corner (18g) says that g takes its largest value on the region at a corner. So only five plans need checking, out of tens of thousands.

corner(0, 0)(200, 0)(100, 200)(90, 210)(0, 240)
earnings, $0600700690480

The best plan is 100 round loaves and 200 long ones, for $700. It uses all the flour and every place in the oven. It leaves 20 minutes of shaping unused.

Why never the middle? Plans that earn the same lie on one line, 3x + 2y = constant. All such lines have slope −3/2 (9e), so they never cross. Raise the constant and the line slides, keeping its slope, in the direction (3, 2). Slide it as far as the region allows. The last points it touches are on the region’s edge, and a straight edge it cannot run along unless it is parallel to it. So it leaves at a corner.

No-middle technique: at a plan with room to spare in every limit, a small enough step in the direction of the earnings breaks no limit and earns more. So the best plan is never a point with room to spare everywhere.

\text{at } Q \text{ every limit } \alpha x + \beta y \le c \text{ has } \alpha x_Q + \beta y_Q \lt c @@ room to spare in each
\text{step to } Q + s(3, 2):\ \ \text{each limit changes by } s(3\alpha + 2\beta) @@ adding-keeps-answers: the limits are of the first degree
\text{take } s \gt 0 \text{ smaller than each room} \div |3\alpha + 2\beta| @@ finitely many limits, so a least one exists
\htmlClass{new}{g(Q + s(3, 2)) = g(Q) + 13s \gt g(Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ sides-of-a-line’s last line: 3² + 2² = 13

Which corner, and for how long

Prices change. Suppose a round loaf earns $a instead of $3, and a long one still $2. The best corner stays at (100, 200) for a while, then jumps. It stays while the earnings line is no steeper than the flour line and no shallower than the oven line.

Corner-holds technique: a corner is best when stepping from it along either of its two edges earns no more. Its two edge lines cut out a wedge, the whole region lies in that wedge, and every point of the wedge is the corner plus some of each edge’s direction.

\text{along the oven edge, toward } (90, 210):\ \ \text{direction } (-1, 1) @@ x + y stays 300
\text{change in earnings} = -a + 2 \le 0 \iff a \ge 2 @@ a step of s changes g by s(−a + 2)
\text{along the flour edge, toward } (200, 0):\ \ \text{direction } (1, -2) @@ 2x + y stays 400
\text{change in earnings} = a - 4 \le 0 \iff a \le 4 @@ the same, with this direction
X = (100, 200) + s(-1, 1) + t(1, -2),\ \ s, t \ge 0,\ \text{for every } X \text{ in the region} @@ two-lines-meet: s and t are found from X; s, t ≥ 0 by the flour and oven limits
\htmlClass{new}{2 \le a \le 4 \Rightarrow g(X) \le g(100, 200)} \quad \blacksquare @@ g(X) = g(100, 200) + s(2 − a) + t(a − 4)

So the round loaf may earn anything from $2 to $4 and the plan does not change. Above $4 the best plan is 200 round loaves and no long ones. At exactly $4 the two corners tie, and so does every plan on the flour edge between them.

What one more bag is worth

The baker can buy more flour, rent more oven time, or hire more hands. Which is worth it? Loosen one limit a little and find the new best corner. The flour line and the oven line still meet at the best corner, a little further along.

\text{flour } 101 \text{ kg}:\ \ 2x + y \le 404,\ x + y \le 300 \Rightarrow (104, 196) @@ the corner slides along the oven edge
g(104, 196) = 312 + 392 = 704 @@ $4 more: each kilogram of flour is worth $4 at the margin
\text{oven } 301 \text{ places}:\ \ 2x + y \le 400,\ x + y \le 301 \Rightarrow (99, 202),\ g = 701 @@ each place in the oven is worth $1
\text{hands } 721 \text{ minutes}:\ \ \text{the best corner does not move},\ g = 700 @@ 20 minutes were already spare
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a limit with room to spare is worth } \$0 \text{ at the margin}} @@ the corner did not touch its line

Worth-at-the-margin technique: loosen one limit by one unit, find the new best corner, and the gain in earnings is what that unit is worth. The worth holds only while the same two lines meet at the best corner. Past that, a different limit takes over. Buy 25 kg more flour, and the corner runs along the oven edge to (200, 100). At 150 kg it reaches (300, 0), where the oven edge ends. Past that, more flour is worth $0.

A corner need not fall on whole loaves. With other numbers it might be (87.5, 212.5). Rounding down always keeps the limits, but the best plan in whole loaves need not be the corner rounded. Finding it is a harder question, held in trust much higher up the ladder.

Illegal moves

\text{bake only the loaf that earns most}\,? @@ round loaves alone stop at 200, for $600; the best mix earns $700
\text{the best plan is in the middle of the region}\,? @@ no-middle: from a plan with room in every limit, a step earns more
\text{every limit is used up at the best plan}\,? @@ at (100, 200) the hands have 20 minutes spare
\text{spare hands are worth their wage}\,? @@ at the margin they are worth $0, while the corner stays put
\text{the best corner rounded down is the best in whole loaves}\,? @@ not in general: check the whole plans near it

Who said it first

Kantorovich wrote that the problems of organising production, such as distributing work among machines and making the best use of raw materials, could be solved by a single method, and that the method also yields multipliers that measure how much each limited resource is worth.

Leonid Kantorovich, Mathematical Methods of Organizing and Planning Production (1939); paraphrase

In 18·logi.4.5, the mathematician of Leningrad is Leonid Kantorovich, and the trust was the Leningrad Plywood Trust. George Dantzig found the method that walks from corner to better corner, the simplex method, in 1947, for planning in the United States Air Force. Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans shared the economics prize of 1975 for the theory of the best use of resources.

Accepted names: finding the best corner is linear programming; the limits are constraints, the allowed region is the feasible region, and g is the objective function. A worth at the margin is a shadow price, or dual value. A limit with room to spare is slack; one used up is binding. The best plan in whole numbers is integer programming.
