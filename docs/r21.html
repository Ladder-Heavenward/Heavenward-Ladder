Rung 21 · theory

Rounding an arc

Rung 18 measured an angle by halving the quarter turn again and again. Rung 20 turned the plane but could only name a turn by a pair of numbers. This rung measures an angle a second way: how far you walk round a circle of radius one. That walk defines the most famous number in mathematics, and the two coordinates of where you stop become the cosine and the sine. From two plain facts, that the point stays one unit from the centre and that turning twice adds the walks, comes every formula of trigonometry, the slopes of the waves, and the area of the disc. The walk and the halving turn out to measure one size, in different units.

You know the circle’s famous number as a button and a string of digits. Walking round a circle to define it is the climb. The digits were only the door.

An angle is a distance walked round a circle of radius one. The point where you stop has the cosine across and the sine up. Turning twice adds the distances, and that one fact grows into the whole subject. In the plane’s numbers, turning by an angle is raising the quarter turn to a power.

The class · read it through, then go to 21a

What it means to say it

An angle used to be a pair of rays, and its size a power of the quarter turn, found by halving. This rung measures it by a walk: stand on the circle of radius one, at its rightmost point, and walk round until you face along the second ray. The length of that walk is the angle. Nothing about it was chosen by a committee; the circle decides it.

Notice what this buys. Because the size is a length, everything rung 18 knew about lengths of paths applies to it, and turning one way and then another simply adds the two walks. The rest of the rung is that addition, followed carefully.

Where it comes from

Astronomers in Greece and India measured the sky with chords and half-chords of circles, and tables of them go back more than two thousand years. The sine began as the Sanskrit word for a bowstring. Roger Cotes measured angles by the radius in 1714, and Leonhard Euler, in 1748, made sine and cosine into functions rather than lengths in a picture. The name “radian” is from 1873. The proof that π can never be written exactly, as a fraction or by ruler and compass, came in 1882.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things, each from the ones before.

First, an angle is the length of a walk round the circle of radius one, and π is the length of half of that circle.

Second, the place where the walk stops has two coordinates, the cosine across and the sine up, and every real number names such a place.

Third, that place stays one unit from the centre, so the squares of the cosine and sine add to one.

Fourth, turning by one angle and then another is turning by their sum. Every formula for the sum of two angles comes from this.

Fifth, walking backwards, turning half a circle, and turning to the other angle in a corner each change the coordinates in a simple way.

Sixth, for a short walk, the height reached is almost exactly the length walked.

Seventh, the slope of the sine is the cosine, and the slope of the cosine is minus the sine.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 21a carries a green box: the cloud. Every value that uses π carries a width you choose and state. And 21h carries the triangle: a slanted motion split into parts along directions you choose.

What follows

With those seven, the techniques fill in the school course and pass it. Two sides and the angle between them settle a triangle. Every wave of the sine’s shape is the sine moved. The halving and the walk are proved to measure one size. The identities grow from the sum of turns. The waves have slopes, and they can be run backwards. The disc has area π times its radius squared, and the ball is two thirds of the can that holds it. Two lengths tied by a triangle have speeds tied by it too. An angle at the rim of a circle is half the angle at its centre. Curves can be drawn by turning and reaching.

Then two call backs. A place on the globe is two turns, round and up, and the distance between two cities is a walk on a great circle. And the plane’s numbers multiply by multiplying their lengths and adding their turns, so turning by any angle is raising the quarter turn to a power.

What you can do because you know it

You can find any side or angle of a triangle from the rest, and the area too. You can fit a wave to four readings of a tide. You can prove an identity without fooling yourself, and find every angle that answers an equation. You can find slopes, highs, lows and areas for functions built from waves, the volumes of round solids, and the speed of one changing length from another. You can find the distance between two cities and every root of one.

That is the trigonometry the world runs on, from surveyors to sound engineers. The people who built it would tell you it was a beginning, and so it is for you.

And 21·bio asks why sunflower seeds spiral: a fixed turn per seed makes spokes when it is a simple fraction of the whole, and the turn farthest from every simple fraction makes arms in neighbouring rabbit counts. And 21·eng asks how a crank turns a wheel into a push: where the piston sits for each angle of the crank, how fast it moves, and why it pauses at each end. It carries a box on the triangle: a motion split into parts.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends 16’s areas and substitution, 17’s logarithm, 18’s lengths of paths, 20’s moves, and 20·cb8’s numbers that turn. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that an angle is a power of i, and what you can do because you know it.
