Why Does a Signal Bounce Back Down a Cable?

Why does a signal bounce back down a cable? Plug the wrong aerial into a radio transmitter and part of its power comes straight back down the cable, heating the set instead of the sky. Send a pulse down a long cable with a break in it, and an echo returns, from which a technician can tell exactly where to dig. This page treats a cable as carrying a wave, as 33e’s wave equation; finds the echo at the far end as a complex number with 26’s arithmetic; finds when there is no echo at all; reads the standing wave a meter shows; and treats an aerial as an end that lets the wave go, ending with radar’s fourth-power fall (17).

A cable carries a wave at a fixed speed and has a fixed ratio of voltage to current, Z₀. At the far end, whatever is plugged in sets its own ratio Z. The mismatch sends back a share (Z − Z₀)/(Z + Z₀) of the wave, a complex number when Z is. Match Z to Z₀ and nothing comes back. An aerial is an end matched to open space: the wave goes on outward and never returns.

34eng21A Line Carries a Wave

Zoe maintains radio links. A cable is two conductors side by side; a signal travels along it as a wave of voltage and current, obeying the wave equation (33e). In a typical cable it travels at about two thirds of the speed of light, 2 × 10⁸ m/s.

From here on, on this page, Z₀ is the cable’s own ratio of voltage to current for a wave travelling along it, 50 ohms for Zoe’s cable, and Z is the ratio set by whatever is plugged in at the far end.

34eng22The Reflection at the End

At the end, the arriving wave alone has ratio Z₀, but the load demands Z. Both can hold only if a wave also comes back. Adding the arriving and returning waves and asking for ratio Z fixes the returning share, ρ. For a load with coils or capacitors, Z is complex (26·eng), and so is ρ.

\htmlClass{new}{\rho = \dfrac{Z - Z_0}{Z + Z_0}} @@ the reflection technique
Z = 75:\ \rho = \dfrac{25}{125} = 0.2,\ \text{power back } \rho^2 = 4\% @@ 
Z = 50 + 50i:\ \rho = \dfrac{50i}{100 + 50i} = 0.2 + 0.4i,\ \ |\rho|^2 = 20\% @@ complex arithmetic (25)
\text{open end } (Z \to \infty):\ \rho = 1;\ \ \text{shorted end } (Z = 0):\ \rho = -1 @@ all sent back

Reflection technique: a wave on a line of ratio Z₀ meeting a load of ratio Z sends back the share (Z − Z₀)/(Z + Z₀) of its voltage; its square’s size is the share of power returned.

34eng23Matching: No Echo When the Load Equals the Line

Z = Z_0:\ \rho = 0 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all the power is taken by the load}} @@

Zoe uses the echo to find faults. A pulse sent down a buried cable returns from a break after 1 microsecond. The break is at 2 × 10⁸ × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 2 = 100 m, out and back. An open break echoes the pulse the same way up; a crushed, shorted one echoes it upside down. The crew digs at 100 m and finds it.

34eng24Standing Waves and the Ratio a Meter Reads

With a steady signal, the arriving and returning waves add. Where they are in step the voltage is largest, 1 + |ρ| times the arriving wave; where they are opposite, smallest, 1 − |ρ|. The pattern stands still along the cable.

\text{ratio} = \dfrac{1 + |\rho|}{1 - |\rho|} @@ 
|\rho| = 0.2:\ 1.5;\ \ |\rho| = 0.45:\ 2.6 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a matched end reads exactly } 1} @@

A transmitter’s meter reads the ratio of the largest to smallest voltage along the cable; 1 means a perfect match, and anything above 2 means a fifth or more of the power is coming back. Zoe adjusts a matching box between cable and aerial until the meter reads close to 1.

34eng25An Antenna as a Matched End That Lets the Wave Go

An aerial matched to its cable takes the wave and passes it on into open space, where it spreads outward and never returns: the end of the cable becomes the start of the sky. A radar uses one aerial both ways. Its wave spreads over a sphere going out, weakening as 1/R², and the echo spreads again coming back.

\text{echo power} \propto \dfrac{1}{R^2} \times \dfrac{1}{R^2} = \dfrac{1}{R^4} @@ out and back
\text{twice the range: } 16 \text{ times weaker} = 10\log_{10} 16 \approx 12 \text{ decibels} @@ logs (17)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{to see twice as far, a radar needs 16 times the power}} @@

An aerial is a matched end that lets the wave go; a radar’s echo falls as the fourth power of the range, so reach is bought very dearly in power.

Accepted names

A cable treated this way is a transmission line; Z₀ its characteristic impedance, ρ the reflection coefficient (often Γ), and the meter’s ratio the voltage standing wave ratio, VSWR. Finding faults by echoes is time-domain reflectometry, and the fourth-power fall is in the radar range equation.

Who said it first

Oliver Heaviside worked out the equations of a line carrying a wave in the 1880s (not yet revisited for the date).
