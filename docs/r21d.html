21d · proof

The sum formulas from turns

Turn the circle a little, and every point on it moves along by the same distance. So walking one distance and then another is the same as walking their total. Written in coordinates, that plain fact becomes a pair of formulas for the cosine and sine of a sum of two angles. They are the most used formulas in all of trigonometry, and here they come from nothing more than turning.

Turning by one angle and then by another is turning by their sum. In coordinates, that gives the cosine and sine of a sum from the cosines and sines of the parts.

21d1Moves keep the lengths of paths

Path-length-kept technique: a slide, a turn or a flip carries every path to a path of the same length. Length is the area under speed. A slide does not change the velocity at all. A turn or flip through the origin sends the velocity (x′(t), y′(t)) to its own turn or flip, which has the same length.

Q(t) = R(P(t)) = (a\,x(t) - b\,y(t),\ b\,x(t) + a\,y(t)) @@ the turn by (a, b)
Q'(t) = (a\,x'(t) - b\,y'(t),\ b\,x'(t) + a\,y'(t)) = R(P'(t)) @@ slopes of sums and multiples
|Q'(t)| = |P'(t)| @@ the turn-keeps technique (20d2)
\htmlClass{new}{\int |Q'| = \int |P'|} \quad \blacksquare @@ equal speeds, equal areas: the same length (18i)

21a used this for flips without proving it. Here it is proved for all three.

21d2Turning adds arcs

Turning-adds-arcs technique: the turn by the pair (cos B, sin B) sends the point at arc A to the point at arc A + B. That turn sends (1, 0) to the point at B, and the circle to itself. It carries the arc from (1, 0) to the point at A onto an arc of the same length that starts at the point at B. A turn keeps which way round, so the image arc also runs anticlockwise.

R_B = R_{(\cos B,\ \sin B)},\ \ R_B(1, 0) = (\cos B, \sin B) @@ 20j1: a turn is where it sends (1, 0)
\text{arc from } (1, 0) \text{ to } P_A \text{ has length } A @@ P_A is the point at arc A (21b)
R_B(\text{that arc}) \text{ runs from } P_B,\ \text{anticlockwise, length } A @@ 21d1, and D is kept (20g2)
\htmlClass{new}{R_B(P_A) = P_{A + B}} \quad \blacksquare @@ walk B, then A more

So the turn by B is the turn by the pair (cos B, sin B), and the pair of a sum is the product of the pairs: 20j’s rule is adding angles.

21d3The sum formulas

Sum technique: cos(A + B) = cos A cos B − sin A sin B, and sin(A + B) = sin A cos B + cos A sin B. Apply the turn formula R(x, y) = (ax − by, bx + ay), with (a, b) = (cos B, sin B), to (x, y) = (cos A, sin A).

R_B(\cos A, \sin A) = (\cos B\cos A - \sin B\sin A,\ \ \sin B\cos A + \cos B\sin A) @@ 20j1
R_B(P_A) = (\cos(A + B),\ \sin(A + B)) @@ 21d2
\htmlClass{new}{\cos(A + B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B} @@ the first coordinates agree
\htmlClass{new}{\sin(A + B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B} \quad \blacksquare @@ and the second

21d4Doubles and the tangent of a sum

Double technique: cos 2A = cos² A − sin² A and sin 2A = 2 sin A cos A. Put B = A in the sum technique. With the circle technique, cos 2A = 2cos² A − 1 = 1 − 2sin² A.

\cos 2A = \cos^2 A - \sin^2 A = 2\cos^2 A - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 A @@ B = A; then sin² = 1 − cos², or cos² = 1 − sin²
\sin 2A = 2\sin A\cos A @@ B = A
\tan(A + B) = \frac{\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B}{\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B} @@ where the bottom is not 0
\htmlClass{new}{\tan(A + B) = \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide top and bottom by cos A cos B ≠ 0

The accepted names

The sum technique gives the angle addition formulas (compound angle formulas); the double technique the double-angle formulas. The method of 21d.4.5 is prosthaphaeresis, Greek for “adding and taking away”.

Who said it first

The sum formulas for chords are in Ptolemy's Almagest (about 150 AD). In 21d.4.5, prosthaphaeresis was worked out by Paul Wittich and used at Tycho Brahe's observatory around 1580; Nicolaus Reimers Ursus published it in 1588, and Tycho accused him of theft. John Napier's logarithms (1614) replaced it.
