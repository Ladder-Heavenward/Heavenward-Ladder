Can a Cell Untie Its Own DNA?

Can a cell untie its own DNA? A bacterium keeps its genes on one closed ring of DNA, about four and a half million base pairs round. The ring is two strands, wound round each other once every ten and a half base pairs, and both strands are closed loops. To copy the ring the cell must pull the two strands apart, and two closed loops wound round each other four hundred thousand times cannot be pulled apart by any amount of stretching. What makes the question hard is that it is not a question of chemistry or of force. It is a count, and 34m’s counts do not change under any moving at all. So the cell must cut. This page counts how many times two closed strands are wound together, shows that no stretching changes the count, and finds what one cut can do to it: one, or exactly two. Then it weighs the rings in a tube, which come out in a bell of whole-number counts, and finds what a cell must do to separate two daughter rings after copying.

Two closed strands drawn with crossings have a linking count: half the sum of the signs of the crossings between them. No twist, poke or slide changes it, and it is a whole number. The count of a ring of DNA is its number of turns, and it splits as twist plus coil. An enzyme that cuts one strand, passes the other through and rejoins changes the count by exactly 1; one that cuts both strands and passes another piece of the double strand through changes it by exactly 2. In a tube of rings at rest the counts make a bell about the relaxed count, with spread N over 2K. After copying, the two daughter rings are linked, and every pass of one through the other changes their count by 1.

34bio1Two Strands, One Count

Two closed loops A and B lie in space, drawn with crossings. 34m gave every crossing a sign, +1 or −1. Some crossings are of A with itself, some of B with itself, and some of A with B. Only the last kind can tell whether the two are tangled together.

Strand-link technique: the linking count lk(A, B), half the sum of the signs of the crossings of A with B, is unchanged by the twist, the poke and the slide, and it is a whole number: the sum of the signs of the crossings where A passes over B.

\text{twist: one crossing of a strand with itself} @@ not a crossing of A with B: lk does not move
\text{poke: two crossings of the same two strands, of opposite sign} @@ if they are A with B they add +1 − 1 = 0
\text{slide: each of the three crossings keeps its strands and its sign} @@ the strand moved passes over both or under both
\text{switch the crossings where A is over B: } \text{lk} \to \text{lk} - \textstyle\sum_{A\text{ over }B}\text{sign} @@ a switched crossing changes sign, and lk by −sign
\text{now A lies under B everywhere: lower it away, lk} = 0 @@ moving A straight down never passes it through B
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lk}(A,B) = \textstyle\sum_{A\text{ over }B}\text{sign},\ \text{a whole number}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines before

So a count of crossings, which depends on the drawing, gives a number that depends only on the two loops, as 34m’s colourings did for one. Two rings lying apart have lk = 0. Two rings linked once, drawn with two crossings of the same sign, have lk = ±1. A ring threaded twice round another has lk = ±2. The sign depends on which way each loop is walked: reverse one and lk changes sign.

There are tangles that lk cannot see. Three rings can hang together so that no two are linked: cut any one and the other two fall apart. Each pair has lk = 0.

34bio2Turns of a Ring, Twist and Coil

DNA is two strands of sugar and phosphate held together by base pairs, wound round a common axis in a right-handed helix. In water, at rest, it makes one full turn every 10.5 base pairs. Close a stretch of N base pairs into a ring, each strand joined to itself, and the two strands are two closed loops.

Lay the ring flat, its axis a circle on the table. Each full turn of the helix carries one strand once over the other and once under it: two crossings of A with B, of the same sign. So lk is the number of turns. Write Lk for it, the accepted name. A relaxed ring of N base pairs has Lk₀ = N/10.5 turns, if that is a whole number, and as near to it as a whole number can be if not.

A ring with fewer turns than Lk₀ is strained. It can unwind its helix a little, holding fewer than one turn per 10.5 base pairs, or it can keep its helix and coil its axis in space, like a telephone cord twisted and let go. The first is twist, Tw: the turns of the strands about the axis. The second is coil, written Wr: the signed crossings of the axis with itself, averaged over every direction it can be seen from. Held in trust, and proved much higher up the ladder: Lk = Tw + Wr, for every shape the ring takes. Neither Tw nor Wr is a whole number, and either can change as the ring jostles. Only their sum is fixed, by the strand-link technique.

The plasmid pBR322, a small ring used in every laboratory that clones genes, has N = 4361, so Lk₀ = 415.33. Taken from a bacterium it has about Lk = 390, 25 turns short: σ = (Lk − Lk₀)/Lk₀ ≈ −0.06. Its axis coils round itself in a tight braid, and the ring runs fast through a gel because it is compact.

34bio3Cut, Pass, Rejoin

No moving of the ring changes Lk. A cell changes it all the time, by enzymes that cut. One kind cuts one strand, lets the other strand pass through the gap, and seals the cut. Another kind holds one stretch of the double strand, cuts both of its strands, passes a second stretch of the double strand through the gap, and seals both cuts.

Cut-and-pass technique: passing one strand through a cut in the other changes Lk by exactly ±1; passing a stretch of double strand through a cut in both strands of another changes Lk by exactly ±2.

\text{one strand through the other: one crossing of A with B switched} @@ the drawing, before and after, differs at the one place of the pass
\text{a switched crossing of sign } s \text{ changes } \tfrac12\textstyle\sum \text{ by } -s @@ from s to −s: half of −2s
\text{so the first kind changes Lk by } \pm1 @@ the strand-link technique on each side of the cut
\text{a stretch of double strand over another: 4 crossings} @@ A over A, A over B, B over A, B over B
\text{A over B and B over A have one sign } s @@ the two strands run opposite ways, so reversing both of a pair keeps its sign
\text{the pass switches all four; only A over B and B over A count} @@ crossings of a strand with itself do not enter lk
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the second kind changes Lk by } -\tfrac12(2s + 2s) = \pm2} \quad \blacksquare @@ never by 1, never by 3

The first kind relaxes a strained ring one turn at a time. The second, whether it adds turns or removes them, keeps Lk even or keeps it odd: from Lk = 390 it can reach 414 or 416 and never 415. In bacteria one enzyme of the second kind, called gyrase, uses the energy of ATP to push Lk down by 2 each time, against the strain, and keeps the cell’s DNA short of turns. That shortfall makes the strands easier to pull apart where a gene is read.

34bio4A Bell of Rings

Relax a tube of pBR322 with an enzyme of the first kind, and let it stand. Every ring has a whole-number Lk, and the enzyme keeps changing them by 1. Lk₀ = 415.33 is not whole, so no ring can be quite at rest: each is strained a little, by Lk − Lk₀ turns. The energy of that strain, measured for rings longer than about 2000 base pairs, is (K kT/N)(Lk − Lk₀)², with K ≈ 1100.

Topoisomer-bell technique: in a tube of relaxed rings of N base pairs, the share with linking count Lk is in proportion to e^(−K(Lk − Lk₀)²/N), a bell about Lk₀ with spread N/(2K).

E(Lk) = \tfrac{KkT}{N}(Lk - Lk_0)^2 @@ the measured strain energy
\text{share}(Lk) \propto e^{-E/kT} = e^{-K(Lk - Lk_0)^2/N} @@ the energy-share technique (30·phys.1), over the whole-number states Lk
e^{-x^2/(2v)} \text{ with } v = N/(2K) @@ the same exponent, matched
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a bell about } Lk_0 \text{ with spread } \tfrac{N}{2K}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 30g’s spread of the bell, read off the exponent; held only near the bell, where the sum over whole numbers is close to the area

For pBR322 the spread is 4361/2200 ≈ 1.98, so the typical miss is 1.4 turns. The two commonest rings are Lk = 415, with 28% of the tube, and Lk = 416, with 25%; then 414 with 18% and 417 with 14%. On a gel they run as separate bands, a ladder of whole numbers, and their heights fit the bell: that is how K was measured. The 25 turns of a ring taken from a cell have a share of e^(−157.6) ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶⁹ in the bell. They are held there by gyrase spending energy.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

The tube’s average linking count is near 415.33. That is a statement about the crowd, and it is true of the tube.

A crowd can average a count that none of its members can have.

It is false of every ring in it. Each ring’s count is a whole number, exactly, and stays so until an enzyme cuts it. No ring has 415.33 turns.

So the bell is a crowd claim built from claims about each one that are exact. The gel shows both sizes at once: each band is sharp, because each count is whole, and the heights of the bands are the crowd’s bell.

A gel of a relaxed ring of 2686 base pairs shows bands with shares 0.28, 0.36 and 0.20 at three neighbouring counts. Which is Lk = 256? Is anything in the tube at Lk = 255.81?

34bio5Copying a Ring

To copy a ring the cell opens the two strands at one place and copies each, moving round the ring in both directions. Each strand of the parent ends in one daughter ring, paired with a new strand. A daughter ring holds one strand of A and one new one; the other holds B and a new one. So the strands A and B, which began with Lk(A, B) ≈ N/10.5, must end apart, with Lk(A, B) = 0.

Ring-pass technique: separating the two strands of a ring of N base pairs takes cuts whose changes to Lk add to −N/10.5; any turns not removed before the copying ends leave the two daughter rings linked, and each pass of one daughter through the other changes their linking count by exactly ±1.

\text{Lk}(A,B) \text{ at the start} = Lk_0 \approx N/10.5 @@ 34·bio.2
\text{Lk}(A,B) \text{ at the end} = 0 @@ two rings that lie apart
\text{only cuts change Lk} @@ the strand-link technique
\text{daughters: two closed loops, each a double strand} @@ count crossings of daughter with daughter, along their axes
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a pass of one daughter through the other switches one crossing: } \pm1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cut-and-pass technique, with whole daughters for strands

The ring of the gut bacterium has about 4.64 million base pairs, so its two strands are linked about 442 000 times. Each copying fork moves about 1000 base pairs a second, and there are two, so the cell must remove about 190 turns every second for the forty minutes of copying. Enzymes ahead of the forks do most of it. What is left when the forks meet is a pair of daughter rings linked a few times, and an enzyme of the second kind passes one through the other until their count is 0, and then until they lie apart.

Linking count 0 is not enough. Two rings can have lk = 0 and still hang together: one ring threaded through the other, doubling back with a twist, drawn with five crossings, as the converse standing question below asks you to show. The enzyme must keep passing until they truly fall apart, and lk alone cannot tell it when to stop.

34bio6Knots in a Ring

An enzyme of the second kind can pass a ring’s double strand through itself. That changes a crossing of the ring with itself, and so can tie the ring in a knot, or untie one. In a cell this is a hazard: a knotted ring cannot be read or copied cleanly. In a tube it is a tool, because knotted rings of the same length are more compact, and run through a gel faster, the more crossings their knot needs.

Knot-pass technique: a ring tied in the overhand knot cannot be untied by any moving; one pass of the double strand through itself can untie it, and no ring is untied with fewer passes than this.

c(\text{overhand}) = 9,\ \ c(\text{plain ring}) = 3 @@ the three-colour technique (34m2)
\text{so no moving unties the overhand knot} @@ a number of the knot that differs
\text{a pass switches one crossing of the drawing} @@ as in the cut-and-pass technique, with the ring for both strands
\text{switch one crossing of the three-crossing drawing} @@ the strand that was over at that crossing now lies under
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the drawing left is a plain ring, undone by a twist and a poke}} \quad \blacksquare @@ draw it and check: one crossing is a kink, and the other two lie together

Enzymes that untie knots in cells mostly pass the strand where it is bent sharply, which makes them pick passes that simplify. Sorting knotted rings on a gel by their crossings has been used to watch enzymes that cut and rejoin at fixed places: each kind of enzyme ties its own family of knots, and the family tells how the enzyme holds the strand.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The linking count is the linking number, and the strand-link technique is its invariance under Reidemeister moves; the three rings that hang together with no two linked are the Borromean rings, and the pair with lk = 0 that cannot be pulled apart is the Whitehead link. Lk = Tw + Wr is the Călugăreanu–White–Fuller theorem, and Wr is the writhe. The enzymes that cut and rejoin are topoisomerases: type I cuts one strand, type II cuts both, and gyrase is a type II topoisomerase; in the gut bacterium Escherichia coli, topoisomerase IV unlinks the daughter rings. Rings of one sequence that differ only in Lk are topoisomers, σ is the superhelical density, the strained ring is negatively supercoiled, and two linked rings are a catenane. The fewest passes that untie a knot is its unknotting number.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss counted linking by an integral in 1833. Gheorghe Călugăreanu proved Lk = Tw + Wr in 1959–61, and James White and Brock Fuller extended it in 1969–71. James Wang found the first topoisomerase in 1971, and Martin Gellert and colleagues found gyrase in 1976. Wang and Robert Depew, and David Horowitz and Wang, measured K from the bands of topoisomers in 1975 and 1984. Nicholas Cozzarelli, De Witt Sumners and others read the knots tied by recombinases from gels in the 1980s.

Wrong moves, worked

“A ring of 4361 base pairs relaxed by an enzyme of the second kind ends at the bell’s top, Lk = 415.” Not allowed: an enzyme of the second kind changes Lk by 2 and keeps it odd or even. From a ring at 390, it reaches 414 or 416, and its own bell is a bell over the even counts only. Only an enzyme that cuts one strand reaches every count.
