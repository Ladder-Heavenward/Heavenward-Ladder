How Does a Charger Turn a High Voltage Into a Low One?

How does a charger turn a high voltage into a low one? A laptop charger’s insides may hold 20 volts, and a phone wants 5. Wasting the difference as heat would make the charger hot and slow. Instead a switch flicks on and off hundreds of thousands of times a second, and a coil smooths the pulses. This page finds the output as the average of the switched voltage over one cycle, a 15 area over a width; finds the coil’s current as a running area of voltage; finds why faster switching smooths it; and turns the circuit around to step a voltage up.

Switch the input on for a share D of each cycle and off for the rest. The average voltage is D times the input: area over width. A coil’s current changes by the area of the voltage across it, over its size L; in steady running the current climbs and falls by equal amounts each cycle, a triangle.

15eng31A Switch Flicked On and Off Very Fast

Mike is designing a charger that takes 20 V and must give a phone 5 V. A transistor switch connects the input for a short time, then disconnects it, over and over, 500,000 times a second. Each cycle lasts T = 2 microseconds (µs).

From here on, on this page, the duty D is the share of each cycle the switch is on. The voltage after the switch is 20 V while on and 0 V while off: a train of rectangles.

15eng32The Duty Cycle: the Output Is the Average

A coil and a large store of charge after the switch smooth the rectangles into a steady voltage. The steady voltage is the average over one cycle: the area under the rectangle train over one cycle, divided by the cycle’s width.

\text{area over one cycle} = 20 \cdot D\,T + 0 \cdot (1 - D)\,T @@ areas add (15e)
\text{average} = \dfrac{20\,D\,T}{T} = 20\,D @@ area over width
\htmlClass{new}{V_{\text{out}} = D\,V_{\text{in}}:\ \ 5 = 20\,D \Rightarrow D = 0.25} @@ on a quarter of the time

Duty-average technique: a voltage switched on for the share D of each cycle averages to D times the input; smoothing passes the average and blocks the switching. Nothing is burned off: the switch is either fully on (no voltage across it) or fully off (no current through it), so in principle it wastes nothing. Real chargers reach over 90% efficiency this way.

15eng33The Coil’s Current Rises and Falls in a Triangle

A coil resists changes in its current: the voltage across it sets how fast its current changes, rate = voltage ÷ L, where L is the coil’s size in henries. So the change in current over a time is the area under the voltage across the coil, divided by L.

\text{on: across the coil } 20 - 5 = 15 \text{ V, for } 0.5\,\mu\text{s} @@ D·T = 0.25 · 2 µs
\text{rise} = \dfrac{15 \cdot 0.5 \times 10^{-6}}{10 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.75 \text{ A} @@ area of voltage, over L
\text{off: across the coil } 0 - 5 = -5 \text{ V, for } 1.5\,\mu\text{s} @@ 
\text{fall} = \dfrac{5 \cdot 1.5 \times 10^{-6}}{10 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.75 \text{ A} @@ the same
\htmlClass{new}{\text{rise} = \text{fall}:\ \text{the current repeats each cycle}} @@ a triangle wave

The equal rise and fall is no coincidence: in steady running the coil’s current must end each cycle where it began, so the area of coil voltage over one cycle is 0. That is another way to get V_out = D·V_in: (V_in − V_out)·D·T = V_out·(1 − D)·T.

15eng34Ripple Shrinks as Switching Gets Faster

The triangle’s height, 0.75 A here, is the ripple: the wobble on top of the steady current. It is the area of 15 V over the on-time, over L, and the on-time is D·T.

\text{ripple} = \dfrac{(V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}})\,D\,T}{L} @@ 
500 \text{ kHz}:\ T = 2\,\mu\text{s},\ \text{ripple } 0.75 \text{ A} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{1 \text{ MHz}:\ T = 1\,\mu\text{s},\ \text{ripple } 0.375 \text{ A}} @@ twice as fast, half the ripple

Ripple shrinks in proportion to the cycle time, so faster switching allows a smaller coil for the same smoothness. That is why small modern chargers switch at hundreds of thousands or millions of times a second. The price is a little loss each time the switch changes over, so designers balance size against heat.

15eng35Stepping Up Instead

Rearrange the same parts: the coil first, then the switch to ground, then a one-way valve for current to the output. While the switch is on, the input charges the coil; while it is off, the coil’s current is pushed into the output on top of the input.

\text{on: across the coil } V_{\text{in}} \text{ for } D\,T @@ the coil charges
\text{off: across the coil } V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}} \text{ for } (1 - D)\,T @@ negative: the coil discharges
V_{\text{in}}\,D\,T + (V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}})(1 - D)\,T = 0 @@ total area 0 each cycle
\htmlClass{new}{V_{\text{out}} = \dfrac{V_{\text{in}}}{1 - D}} @@ always more than the input

The step-up gives the input divided by 1 − D, so a duty near 1 gives a large step. A 5 V battery driving a 12 V light needs D = 1 − 5/12 ≈ 0.58. A power engineer measuring a real step-up at that duty, with good parts, reads close to 12 V; the small shortfall is the loss in the switch and valve.

Accepted names

The step-down is a buck converter and the step-up a boost converter; both are switched-mode power supplies. D is the duty cycle, the coil an inductor with inductance L, the store of charge a capacitor, and the one-way valve a diode. Zero average coil voltage is volt-second balance.

Who said it first

Switched-mode supplies grew from mid-twentieth-century work on transistor switching and spread with fast power transistors in the 1970s; the buck and boost formulas are standard in first courses on power electronics.
