29·then · newsflash

Does nature take the laziest path?

Does nature take the laziest path? A ball tossed across a schoolyard in Olympia, light bending into the water of Puget Sound, and the earth swinging round the sun each seem to choose one path out of all the paths they could take. People have asked for nearly four hundred years whether that choice is a kind of thrift: whether nature spends as little of something as it can. Rung fourteen asked it of light, which takes the quickest path, and heard the reply that light cannot know in advance which path is quickest. Rung twenty-nine has built the tool the question needs. Its best-path equation finds the path that makes a total least, and it is a rule about each moment, not about the whole trip. This page writes mechanics as a best path, finds where the path is truly least and where it is only level, sends light along the straightest paths of a ruler made from the slowness of glass and air, and finds that every symmetry of the total keeps something. Then it draws the motion in a plane of places and pushes, where a patch of starts keeps its area however it is sheared, and finds the changes of knobs that keep the rules of motion the same.

Take the energy of motion minus the stored energy, and total it along a path. Of all the paths between two places at two times, the one a body takes makes that total level: no small change of the path changes it, to first order. The best-path equation then gives mass times acceleration equals force. For a short trip the total is least; past half a swing of a spring it is only level. Light takes the straightest path of a ruler whose length is the slowness, so in air whose warmth changes with height it bends, and a hot road shows the sky. When the total does not change as the path slides in space, momentum is kept; when it does not change as the clock is shifted, energy is kept; when it does not change as the path turns, angular momentum is kept. In the plane of places and pushes, the motion flows with no spread, so every patch of starts keeps its area. A change of knobs whose slope grid has determinant one keeps the rules of motion, and turns the swing of a spring into a steady turn.

29then1Mechanics as a best path

Stands on 29g (the best-path equation), 14·phys (momentum and kinetic energy) and 16·phys (stored energy).

A body of mass m moves along a line, at place x(t). Its kinetic energy is ½mx′². Let V(x) be its stored energy at x, so the force on it is −V′(x). Fix where it starts at the time t₀ and where it ends at the time t₁. Of all the paths between those two, which one does it take?

From here on, the total along a path of the kinetic energy minus the stored energy is its action: A = ∫ from t₀ to t₁ of (½mx′² − V(x)) dt. The action is a number for each path, as 29g’s totals were. 29g’s best-path equation says what a path must obey if it makes such a total least or greatest. Its proof used only that the total has slope 0 at t = 0 for every nudge. So the equation holds for a path that makes the total level in that sense too: no nudge changes it to first order.

L(x, x') = \tfrac12 m x'^2 - V(x) @@ the thing totalled; t plays the part of 29g’s x
\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} = m x',\ \ \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x} = -V'(x) @@ the one-knob technique (28a): the other knob held still
\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(m x'\big) = -V'(x) @@ the best-path equation (29g)
\htmlClass{new}{m x'' = -V'(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ mass times acceleration is the force

Action-to-force technique: a path that makes the action level obeys mass times acceleration equals force; so the laws of motion of rung fourteen are the best-path equation of one total. With several knobs, x is an arrow, and the same lines run knob by knob. The action is not a new law of nature. It is a second way of writing the old one, and the two say the same.

V = mgy,\ \ g = 9.81 \text{ m/s}^2,\ \ y(0) = y(2) = 0 @@ thrown straight up from a hand and caught two seconds later
y'' = -g \Rightarrow y = 9.81t - 4.905t^2 @@ the action-to-force technique; the ends fix the two constants
A_0 = \textstyle\int_0^2 \big(\tfrac12 m (9.81 - 9.81t)^2 - mg(9.81t - 4.905t^2)\big)\,dt = -\tfrac13 m g^2 @@ for a baseball, m = 0.145 kg: A₀ ≈ −4.651 J s
y_\varepsilon = y + \varepsilon\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{2} \Rightarrow A_\varepsilon - A_0 = \textstyle\int_0^2 \big(m y'\eta' - mg\eta\big)\,dt + \tfrac12 m\int_0^2 \eta'^2\,dt @@ η = ε sin(πt/2) is 0 at both ends; the square of y′ + η′ opened out
\textstyle\int_0^2 \big(m y'\eta' - mg\eta\big)\,dt = \big[m y'\eta\big]_0^2 - \int_0^2 \big(m y'' + mg\big)\eta\,dt = 0 @@ parts (16e); η is 0 at the ends and my″ = −mg
\htmlClass{new}{A_\varepsilon - A_0 = \tfrac12 m\varepsilon^2\cdot\tfrac{\pi^2}{4} = \tfrac{m\pi^2}{8}\varepsilon^2 \gt 0} @@ ∫ of (π/2)² cos²(πt/2) over [0, 2] is π²/4; for ε = 1 m, about 0.179 J s more

So the true toss has less action than every nudged copy of it of this kind, by an amount that grows as the square of the nudge. The first-order part is zero: that is what the best-path equation says. The second-order part is the total of ½mη′², and it can only be positive.

29then2Least, or only level?

Stands on 29·then.1, 24·phys (the even-time technique) and 21g (the slopes of sin and cos).

29·then.1’s ball had the least action of its nudged copies. Is the action of the true path always least? Try a mass on a spring, with stored energy ½kx², starting at rest at x = 0 and ending at x = 0 a time T later. The path x = 0, staying put, obeys mx″ = −kx. Nudge it by η = ε sin(πt/T), which is 0 at both ends (29g’s nudge).

A_\varepsilon - A_0 = \tfrac12\textstyle\int_0^T \big(m\eta'^2 - k\eta^2\big)\,dt @@ the first-order part is 0 by the best-path equation, as in 29·then.1
\textstyle\int_0^T \eta'^2\,dt = \varepsilon^2\tfrac{\pi^2}{T^2}\cdot\tfrac T2,\ \ \int_0^T \eta^2\,dt = \varepsilon^2\cdot\tfrac T2 @@ the averages of cos² and sin² over a half turn are ½ (21n)
A_\varepsilon - A_0 = \tfrac{\varepsilon^2T}{4}\Big(\tfrac{m\pi^2}{T^2} - k\Big) @@ put them in
\htmlClass{new}{A_\varepsilon \lt A_0 \iff T \gt \pi\sqrt{m/k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ π√(m/k) is half of 24·phys’s period 2π√(m/k)

Half-swing technique: for a trip on a spring longer than half a swing, the true path is not the least: a nudged path has less action, and the true path’s action is only level. For a trip shorter than half a swing, every nudge has more action; that is stated here, not proved, since it needs every nudge and not only these. A cart of 0.5 kg on a spring of 20 N/m swings once in about 0.99 s, so past about 0.50 s its path is no longer least.

So nature does not take the laziest path. It takes a path on which the action is level, where no small nudge changes it to first order. Sometimes that level place is a bottom, as for the ball. Sometimes it is a saddle (28l), lower than some of its neighbours and higher than others. The rule is the best-path equation, and it speaks of each instant: the force now fixes the acceleration now. Light does not need to know in advance which path is quickest, any more than a ball does. Rung fourteen’s objection is answered by the local rule; the word “least” was a summary, and an inexact one.

Rung twelve asked what a physicist should do with a least value no technique promises. The best-path equation is a test that a least path must pass. It does not promise that a least path exists. Here the second-order total decides, path by path. Where no technique decides, a level path found by the equation is a candidate, not yet a least one.

29then3Light takes the straightest path of a ruler

Stands on 29d (the ruler and the ruler-length technique), 29g (straightest paths), 14h (light takes the quickest path) and 23·phys (the kept-sine technique).

Light moves at c/n in a stuff of refractive index n. The time it takes along a path is the total of n ds over c. So the quickest path is the shortest path for a new ruler: 29d’s ruler with E = G = n² and F = 0, that is ds̃² = n²(dx² + dy²). Light takes the straightest paths (29g) of the ruler made from the slowness.

\text{time} = \tfrac1c\textstyle\int n(x)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx @@ a path y(x) in air whose index n changes with x only; the ruler-length technique (29d)
L = n(x)\sqrt{1 + y'^2},\ \ \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial y} = 0 @@ L does not hold y
\tfrac{d}{dx}\Big(\tfrac{n(x)\,y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}\Big) = 0 @@ the best-path equation (29g)
\tfrac{y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = \sin\theta @@ θ is the angle between the ray and the x direction, across the layers
\htmlClass{new}{n\sin\theta \text{ is the same all along the ray}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a slope of 0 is flat

Straightest-light technique: light’s paths are the straightest paths of the ruler n²(dx² + dy²); where n changes across layers only, n sin θ is kept all along a ray. At a sharp boundary this is 23·phys’s kept-sine technique. In air that warms toward the ground, it bends rays smoothly.

n - 1 \approx 2.93\times10^{-4}\cdot\tfrac{288}{T} @@ for air at sea level, T in kelvin; a measured law
\text{air at } 50\,^\circ\text{C on the road, } 30\,^\circ\text{C at eye height: } \Delta n \approx 1.72\times10^{-5} @@ near Stockton on a summer afternoon
n_{\text{eye}}\cos\alpha = n_{\text{road}} @@ the straightest-light technique: a ray that just grazes the road; θ = 90° − α from the upright
\alpha \approx \sqrt{2\,\Delta n} \approx 0.0059 \text{ rad} \approx 0.34^\circ @@ cos α ≈ 1 − α²/2 (23h), and n ≈ 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{eye at } 1.5 \text{ m: the road shows the sky past about } 1.5/0.0059 \approx 255 \text{ m}} @@ rays looking down by less than α curve back up before the road

The bright wet patch that never gets closer is the sky, seen along a ray that dipped and turned. A lens whose index grew toward its rim as 2/(1 − x² − y²) would bend light along the lines of 29c’s disc plane: its ruler is the disc plane’s ruler.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a measurement picks the principle

Two accounts of light both gave rung twenty’s bend at a boundary. One said light takes the quickest path. Then it must move slower in water, by the factor n ≈ 1.33. The other said light is a stream of tiny bodies pulled into the water as they cross. Then it must move faster in water. On paper both fit every angle anyone had measured.

When two principles fit every angle, look for the quantity on which they disagree, and measure it with its error.

In 1850 light was timed across a few metres of water and of air with a turning mirror. It was slower in water, by about the factor 4/3. Neither account was a proof. The measurement chose between them, within its error, and the quickest-path account stood.

Two accounts of a mirage on a hot road fit the picture you see. One bends the light in warm air. The other says the road is wet with a film too thin to see. What could you measure from the roadside to tell them apart?

29then4Every symmetry keeps something

Stands on 29·then.1, 29g (the best-path equation), 28c (the roads-add technique), 27·phys (the kept-spin technique), 24·phys (the swing-size technique) and 14·phys (momentum).

If the thing totalled does not change when the whole path is slid, shifted in time or turned, the action has a symmetry. Each symmetry hands a quantity that never changes along a true path.

L \text{ does not hold } x:\ \ \tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} = \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0 @@ sliding the path changes nothing; the best-path equation
\Rightarrow\ p = \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} = mx' \text{ is kept} @@ for L = ½mx′² − V with V the same everywhere: the momentum of 14·phys
L \text{ does not hold } t:\ \ E = x'\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} - L @@ a new quantity to test
\tfrac{dE}{dt} = x''\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} + x'\tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} - \Big(\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x}x' + \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'}x''\Big) = x'\Big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x'} - \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x}\Big) = 0 @@ the product rule, and the roads-add technique (28c) for L along the path; then the best-path equation
\htmlClass{new}{E = mx'^2 - \big(\tfrac12mx'^2 - V\big) = \tfrac12mx'^2 + V \text{ is kept}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy: 24·phys’s swing-size technique, out of a symmetry

Symmetry-keeps technique: if the thing totalled does not hold a place knob, the slope of L along that knob’s rate is kept; if it does not hold the time, x′ ∂L/∂x′ − L, the energy, is kept. Rung fourteen asked whether two quantities can both be kept. They can: momentum by the slide, energy by the shift in time. They come from different symmetries, so neither crowds out the other. When two carts stick together, the slide is still a symmetry and the momentum is kept; the energy of motion is not, because some of it goes into heat and dents, which this L leaves out.

L = \tfrac12 m\big(r'^2 + r^2\varphi'^2\big) - V(r) @@ a planet in its plane, at distance r and turn φ from the sun; the speed in round measures
\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial \varphi} = 0 \Rightarrow mr^2\varphi' \text{ is kept} @@ turning the path changes nothing; the best-path equation in the φ knob
\tfrac{d}{dt}(\text{area swept}) = \tfrac12 r^2\varphi' @@ a thin wedge of radius r and angle dφ has area ½r² dφ (21n)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{equal areas in equal times}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 27·phys’s kept-spin technique, out of a turn

The earth passes closest to the sun, 147.10 million km away, at 30.29 km/s, and furthest, 152.10 million km away, at 29.29 km/s. At both points it moves square to the line from the sun, so r²φ′ = r times the speed: 4.4557 × 10⁹ and 4.4550 × 10⁹ km²/s. They agree to 1 part in 7000.

29then5Places and pushes: the kept area

Stands on 29·then.4, 28j (the spread technique), 28d (the swap-knobs technique) and 27e (the determinant).

Draw the motion in a plane whose two knobs are the place x and the momentum p = ∂L/∂x′. Here p is 14·phys’s momentum, not 29g’s slot for the slope; the momentum is the slope of L in that slot. Each point of that plane is a start: where the body is and how hard it is moving. Write the energy of 29·then.4 in these knobs, and call it H(x, p). For L = ½mx′² − V, H = p²/(2m) + V(x).

H = \tfrac{p^2}{2m} + V(x),\ \ p = mx' @@ the energy in the knobs x and p
\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial p} = \tfrac pm = x' @@ the one-knob technique
\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial x} = V'(x) = -mx'' = -p' @@ the action-to-force technique
\htmlClass{new}{x' = \tfrac{\partial H}{\partial p},\ \ p' = -\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial x}} @@ two first-order rules in place of one second-order one

The rules say that each start moves with the arrow (∂H/∂p, −∂H/∂x). A patch of starts is carried along, as a patch of water is carried by its current. 28j’s spread technique says how fast a patch’s area grows: by the total of the spread of the current inside it.

\text{spread} = \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big(\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Big) + \tfrac{\partial}{\partial p}\Big(-\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial x}\Big) @@ the spread of the current (28j)
= \tfrac{\partial^2 H}{\partial x\,\partial p} - \tfrac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p\,\partial x} = 0 @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d), when H’s second slopes are continuous
\tfrac{d}{dt}(\text{area of the patch}) = \text{flux of the current out of its edge} = \textstyle\iint \text{spread} = 0 @@ the edge moves out at the current’s part across it; the spread technique (28j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every patch of starts keeps its area}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every H, not only for a spring

Kept-area technique: under the two rules of motion, a patch of starts in the plane of places and pushes keeps its area as it is carried, however it is stretched and sheared. A falling stone shows it plainly. Starts with x and p in a square go to x + pt/m − gt²/2 and p − mgt. The slope grid has rows (1, t/m) and (0, 1), with determinant 1. The square is sheared into a slanting slab, thinner and longer, of the same area. The picture carries a ring of starts of a pendulum.

So a cloud of starts cannot be squeezed into a smaller cloud by any force that has a stored energy. It can only be drawn out into thinner and longer threads. That is why a beam of particles in an accelerator cannot be focused smaller in both place and push at once, by magnets alone.

29then6Changes that keep the rules

Stands on 29·then.5, 28c (the chain-of-flat-maps technique), 27e (the determinant) and 21c (sin² + cos² = 1).

Swap the knobs x and p for new knobs X and P, each a function of the old. When are the two rules of motion still true in the new knobs, with the same energy H written in them?

X' = X_x x' + X_p p' = X_xH_p - X_pH_x @@ the roads-add technique (28c), then the two rules
H_p = H_XX_p + H_PP_p,\ \ H_x = H_XX_x + H_PP_x @@ H written in X and P; the roads-add technique again
X' = H_P\big(X_xP_p - X_pP_x\big) @@ the H_X parts cancel
P' = -H_X\big(X_xP_p - X_pP_x\big) @@ the same lines for P
\htmlClass{new}{X_xP_p - X_pP_x = 1 \Rightarrow X' = \tfrac{\partial H}{\partial P},\ \ P' = -\tfrac{\partial H}{\partial X}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bracket is the determinant of the change’s slope grid (27e)

Area-one-change technique: a change of knobs whose slope grid has determinant 1 keeps the two rules of motion, with the same energy written in the new knobs. These are exactly the changes that keep every area of the plane of places and pushes (28i’s area-scale technique). Choose the new knobs well and the rules become easy.

x = \sqrt{\tfrac{2P}{m\omega}}\sin X,\ \ p = \sqrt{2m\omega P}\cos X @@ for the spring, H = p²/(2m) + ½mω²x²
x_Xp_P - x_Pp_X = \cos^2X + \sin^2X = 1 @@ check the determinant of the change the other way; the undo of an area-one change is area-one
H = \omega P\cos^2X + \omega P\sin^2X = \omega P @@ put the change in
\htmlClass{new}{X' = \omega,\ \ P' = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the area-one-change technique: P is kept, and X turns at the steady rate ω

One more change of this kind is the deepest. Look for a single function S(x, t) whose slope along x is the momentum p, and whose slope along t is −H. Then ∂S/∂t + H(x, ∂S/∂x) = 0. The paths of the bodies cross the level curves of S square-on, as 29·then.3’s rays cross the fronts of a wave. This is the wave form of mechanics; the basement gives its accepted name. Much higher up the ladder, it is where the mechanics of atoms begins. For a free body from x = 0 at t = 0, S = mx²/(2t): its slope along x is mx/t, the momentum of a body that went the distance x in the time t.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The thing totalled, kinetic minus stored energy, is the Lagrangian, and the total is the action; that a true path makes the action level is the principle of stationary action, often called the principle of least action or Hamilton’s principle. The best-path equation of an action is the Euler–Lagrange equation. The half-swing technique is about conjugate points: past the first one, the action stops being least. The straightest-light technique is Fermat’s principle with a varying index, and the light ruler is the optical metric; the lens of 29·then.3 is a cousin of Maxwell’s fish-eye lens. The symmetry-keeps technique is the simplest case of Noether’s theorem (1918); the kept p is a conjugate momentum, and a knob L does not hold is a cyclic coordinate. The plane of places and pushes is phase space, H is the Hamiltonian, and the two rules of motion are Hamilton’s equations. The kept-area technique is Liouville’s theorem. A change of knobs that keeps the two rules is a canonical transformation, and the knobs of the steady turn are action-angle variables. S is Hamilton’s principal function, and ∂S/∂t + H(x, ∂S/∂x) = 0 is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat derived the bend of light from least time in 1662, against Claude Clerselier’s charge of final causes. Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis announced least action in 1744 and 1746. Leonhard Euler gave the principle for a body in a field in 1744, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange wrote mechanics from it in the Mécanique analytique (1788). William Rowan Hamilton gave his two rules and the principal function in 1834 and 1835, from his earlier work on rays of light, and Carl Jacobi completed the method in the 1830s and 1840s. Joseph Liouville proved the kept area in 1838. Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of an action keeps a quantity in 1918. Léon Foucault timed light in water with a turning mirror in 1850; Hippolyte Fizeau and Louis Breguet reached the same verdict that year. The quarrel over vis viva (14·phys) is settled on 29·then.4: Leibniz’s mv² and Descartes’s followers’ mv are both kept, by different symmetries. Dirichlet’s principle (12c) was rescued by David Hilbert in 1900, after Karl Weierstrass’s example of 1870; the second-order test of 29·then.2 goes back to Adrien-Marie Legendre (1786) and Jacobi (1837).

In 29·then.8.5, the president is Maupertuis, of the Berlin Academy. The critic who pointed to the curved mirror is usually named as Patrick d’Arcy (1749). The rival letter, attributed to Gottfried Leibniz, was produced by Samuel König in 1751; the Academy, led by Euler, declared it not genuine. The writer is Voltaire, whose Diatribe of Doctor Akakia (1752) was burned in Berlin by order of Frederick the Great.
