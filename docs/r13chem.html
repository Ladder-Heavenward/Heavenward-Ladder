How Fast Is a Chemical Change Going Right Now?

How fast is a chemical change going right now? A chemical change is a motion too, only what moves is the amount of each substance. 10·bio measured an enzyme's rate and 11·chem heated a pan at a steady rate; both said “rate” before rung 13 could say what a rate at an instant is. Here it can. The rate at which a substance is used up is the slope of its concentration, with a minus sign. The balanced change ties the rates of all its substances into one rate of reaction. And a table of readings, taken at instants, gives chords that close in on that slope. From the slopes the data reveal how the rate depends on what is left, without any function this ladder has not built. Last, 10·bio's enzyme and 11·chem's pan, read again as slopes.

The rate of a reaction is how fast a concentration changes: its slope against time, with a minus sign for anything used up. That slope is divided by its coefficient in the balanced change, so that every substance reports the same rate.

The reaction and its table

Dinitrogen pentoxide, N₂O₅, is a white solid that turns straight to a gas when warmed. As a gas it falls apart on its own into nitrogen dioxide and oxygen. Counting atoms as 5·chem taught, the balanced change is

(4 nitrogen and 10 oxygen on the left; 4 nitrogen and 8 + 2 = 10 oxygen on the right). It became a classic of the study of reaction rates after two chemists measured it carefully in 1921. Near 45 °C it goes at a steady, well-behaved pace that is easy to follow over tens of minutes.

From here on, on the chemistry pages, a chemical formula in square brackets, as in [N₂O₅], names the concentration of that substance as a function of the time t, in seconds. Concentration is 10·bio's word: how much of it per litre, here in mol/L. Read [N₂O₅] as “the concentration of N₂O₅”. A reuse, said out loud: square brackets have named 2g's class [x], 5b's [m, n], and 8g's and 12a's closed intervals [c, d]. Here a chemical formula stands inside, never a letter for a number or a pair of numbers, and that says which. mol/L is also written M; 10·bio explained why this ladder does not (8·phys's M is mega).

They were computed from the behaviour this reaction is known to follow, with one constant of 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ per second, about the size measured for N₂O₅ near 45 °C. They start from 0.0200 mol/L, and are rounded to three figures as a laboratory table would be. So they behave as real readings would, without the scatter. Average rate: a chord

The average rate of use over a stretch of time is the amount used divided by the time taken. With 11·chem's Δ, Δ[N₂O₅] is the concentration at the end less the concentration at the start: a displacement, negative for a substance being used up. So the average rate of use is the chord slope with its sign turned about, because chemists want a rate of use to be a positive number.

\text{average rate of use} = -\dfrac{\Delta[\mathrm{N_2O_5}]}{\Delta t} @@ the chord slope, its sign turned about
= -\dfrac{0.0148 - 0.0200}{600 - 0} @@ two rows of the table: 0 s and 600 s
= -\dfrac{\htmlClass{new}{-0.0052}}{600} @@ the displacement is negative: N₂O₅ is being used up
\approx \htmlClass{keep}{8.7 \times 10^{-6}}\ \text{mol/L per s} @@ units by 5j's per: mol/L divided by s

Over the next 600 s, from 600 to 1200, the same reckoning gives (0.0148 − 0.0110)/600 ≈ 6.3 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L per s. The reaction slowed. An average rate belongs to a stretch of time, not to an instant, and it changes with the stretch.

The rate at an instant: a slope

From here on, the rate of change of [A] at the instant t is its slope, written d[A]/dt. That is 13e's d-mark, read as one mark, with [A] naming the outputs and t the input. For a substance being used up it is negative, and its rate of use is −d[A]/dt. Its unit is mol/L per s.

From a table you cannot take a true limit, but you can take narrower chords. Two habits make it work. First, centre the chord on the instant, a reading before and one after. A chord from 600 forward to 1200 leans toward the flatter part and comes out too small. A centred chord's two halves err in opposite directions and nearly cancel. Second, do not go too narrow with rounded readings. Here are finer readings around 600 s from the same run, to four figures.

The chords settle near 7.4 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L per s, and the behaviour the numbers came from gives about 7.41 × 10⁻⁶. The narrowest chord wobbles because its top is the difference of two nearly equal rounded readings. That is the laboratory's version of the limit: narrow enough to see the slope, not so narrow that the rounding takes over. On paper, chemists do the same by drawing the tangent line to a plotted curve with a ruler and reading its slope from two points far apart on the line.

One reaction, one rate

While N₂O₅ disappears, NO₂ and O₂ appear. Atoms are neither made nor lost (5·chem's ledger). So for every 2 mol of N₂O₅ used up, 4 mol of NO₂ and 1 mol of O₂ are made, over every stretch, however short. Divide each change by its coefficient and the three agree; divide by the time and take the limit, and the three slopes agree too. That common number is the rate of reaction.

From here on, the rate of the reaction 2 N₂O₅ → 4 NO₂ + O₂ at the instant t is −½ d[N₂O₅]/dt, which is also ¼ d[NO₂]/dt and d[O₂]/dt. It is each substance's slope divided by its coefficient, with a minus sign for those used up. 10·bio's “rate” was this, for the enzyme's change of substrate into product.

-\dfrac{d[\mathrm{N_2O_5}]}{dt} \approx 7.41 \times 10^{-6} @@ N₂O₅ used, per litre per second
\text{rate} = \tfrac12 \cdot 7.41 \times 10^{-6} \approx \htmlClass{new}{3.70 \times 10^{-6}} \ \text{mol/L per s} @@ divide by its coefficient, 2
\dfrac{d[\mathrm{NO_2}]}{dt} = 4 \cdot 3.70 \times 10^{-6} \approx 1.48 \times 10^{-5} @@ NO₂ appears four times as fast as the reaction runs
\dfrac{d[\mathrm{O_2}]}{dt} \approx 3.70 \times 10^{-6} @@ O₂, coefficient 1: at the reaction's own rate

Without the coefficients, “how fast is this reaction?” has three answers, one per substance. With them it has one. (In the gas some NO₂ pairs up into N₂O₄; a careful study allows for that. The bookkeeping above is the part that never changes.)

Reading a concentration curve

Draw the readings in 9e's plane, time across and concentration up, and the curve tells its story through its slopes. Its slope is negative everywhere: by 13k's slope-sign-near the concentration is lower just after each instant than at it. The slope is less steep later: the rate of use shrinks as the reactant runs low. And the linearization (13k) predicts, near the point where it is drawn and nowhere else.

[\mathrm{N_2O_5}](700) \text{ is about } 0.01482 - 7.41 \times 10^{-6} \cdot 100 = 0.01408 @@ near: the value plus the slope times the step; the table says 0.01409
[\mathrm{N_2O_5}](2000) \text{ “is about” } 0.0200 - 1.00 \times 10^{-5} \cdot 2000 = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ far: the line at 0 s, 2000 s away. The behaviour behind the table gives about 0.0074

The rate at 0 s is 1.00 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L per s. If it stayed so, 0.0200 mol/L would be gone in 2000 s; the table still shows 0.00813 at 1800 s. The rate falls because it depends on how much is left.

What the slopes reveal: first-order behaviour, from the data

Divide each rate of use by the concentration at that instant, using centred chords from the first table. At 300 s, (0.0200 − 0.0148)/600 ÷ 0.0172 ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ per second. At 600 s, 7.33 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.0148 ≈ 5.0 × 10⁻⁴. At 1500 s, (0.0110 − 0.00813)/600 ÷ 0.00945 ≈ 5.1 × 10⁻⁴. The ratio stays put while the rate and the concentration both fall by more than half. So the data say that the rate of use is directly proportional to the amount left, 5j's words: −d[N₂O₅]/dt = k · [N₂O₅], with k about 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ per second. A reaction whose rate is directly proportional to one concentration is called first order, and k its rate constant. A line giving a rate from concentrations, like this one, is called a rate law. Units check (5j): mol/L per s divided by mol/L is per second.

Read chemically, it says the molecules behave as if each had the same chance per second of falling apart, regardless of the others: twice as many molecules, twice as many falling apart each second. A chemist wrote down the same shape of rule in 1850, for sugar breaking down in acid. It was the first time a reaction's rate was measured and tied by a rule to the amount left.

One more thing the readings show. The concentration halves, from 0.0200 to 0.0100, between 1200 and 1500 s: read by the nearby chord, at about 1390 s. From 600 s, it halves from 0.0148 to 0.0074 between 1800 and 2400 s: about 1410 s later. The same time, about 23 minutes, at each stage, within what the readings can show. A function whose slope is always proportional to its value is not a polynomial, since a polynomial's slope has lower degree (13d's polynomial-slope). That function waits for rung 17, which writes this curve's formula and proves the halving time is the same at every stage.

An enzyme's rate, as slopes

10·bio's enzyme turns substrate into product at the rate v(s) = V_max · s/(K_m + s) when the substrate's concentration is s. That rate is a slope: v is −d[S]/dt at the instant the substrate stands at s, the rate of use of the substrate. And v, as a function of s, has a slope of its own.

v(s) = \dfrac{V_{\max}\, s}{K_m + s} @@ 10·bio's line; V_max and K_m fixed for the enzyme
v'(s) = \dfrac{V_{\max}(K_m + s) - V_{\max}\, s}{(K_m + s)^2} = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{V_{\max} K_m}{(K_m + s)^2}} @@ quotient for slopes (13f): bottom times top's slope, less top times bottom's slope, over the bottom squared
v'(s) \gt 0 @@ quotient-slope-sign (13i): the top V_max K_m is positive. More substrate, faster, at every s
v'(0) = \dfrac{V_{\max}}{K_m} @@ 10·bio's low-end: lim v(s)/s, which is the chord slope (v(h) − v(0))/h since v(0) = 0. low-end was a slope all along
v'(K_m) = \dfrac{V_{\max}}{4K_m} @@ at the half-way strength, a quarter of the slope at 0

For 10·bio's invertase, K_m = 16.7 mmol/L and V_max ≈ 0.76 mmol/L per minute, so v′(0) ≈ 0.046 per minute and v′(K_m) ≈ 0.011 per minute. Far out the slope heads to 0, the ceiling of 10·bio's ceiling seen as a slope. Read the substrate's concentration over time with this: −d[S]/dt = V_max [S]/(K_m + [S]). The slide writes “far above” as ≫ and “far below” as ≪. They are a loose comparison with no exact line, and nothing is proved from them, so they need no earning. When [S] is far above K_m the rate of use is nearly V_max, hardly depending on what is left, which is called zero order. When [S] is far below K_m the rate is nearly (V_max/K_m) · [S], directly proportional to what is left: first order, as N₂O₅, with rate constant V_max/K_m. At [S] = 1.67 mmol/L, a tenth of K_m, v ≈ 0.069 mmol/L per minute, and v/[S] ≈ 0.041 per minute, near V_max/K_m ≈ 0.046.

A pan of water, as slopes

11·chem drew the temperature T of 100 g of ice, heated to steam, against the heat put in, Q, as five climbing pieces with rates of about 4.78, 0, 2.39, 0 and 4.98 °C per kJ. Those rates are slopes, by 13a's slope-of-a-line on each piece. And at the four joints there is no slope, by 13f's joint-slopes, since the pieces arrive with different slopes. Now heat the pan with 11·chem's 500 W heater, so that Q = 0.5t kJ with t in seconds. The temperature against time is a chain, T(Q(t)), and 13e's chain for slopes multiplies the rates. On the water piece, dT/dt = dT/dQ · dQ/dt ≈ 2.39 · 0.5 ≈ 1.20 °C per second. On the ice it is about 2.39 °C per second; on the steam, about 2.49; while it melts or boils, 0. And take 11·chem's heat held, H, against T. On the water piece H = 37.58 + 0.418T kJ, whose slope dH/dT is 0.418 kJ per °C, which is m · c = 100 g · 4.18 J/(g · °C). The specific heat capacity is the slope of the heat held per gram. At 0 °C and 100 °C, H jumps, so there it has no slope (13b's slope-means-no-gap turned about).

Who said it first

The 1921 measurements of N₂O₅ were made by Farrington Daniels and Eldridge Johnston. The 1850 study of sugar in acid was Ludwig Wilhelmy’s. The ledger of atoms is Antoine Lavoisier’s. The enzyme’s rate is Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten’s (1913); the d-mark is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s. The law of mass action is Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage’s (1864). The spending money picture below is from Silvanus P. Thompson’s Calculus Made Easy.

Accepted names

The enzyme’s rate v(s) = V_max · s/(K_m + s) is the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Thompson notes that if you are spending money, the rate of spending is the slope of what you have with a minus sign in front, because what you have is going down, not up.
