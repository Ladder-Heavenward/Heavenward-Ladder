41·cb19 · newsflash for 19

Counting up to Symmetry

Rung 19 built groups of moves and proved that the size of a part-group divides the size of the whole. That fact has a counting side the rung did not use. How many different necklaces of six beads can be made from black and white beads, when two necklaces that turn into each other are the same necklace? There are 64 strings of six colours, but most necklaces are counted several times among them, and not all the same number of times: an all-black necklace once, a necklace of alternating colours twice, most of them six times. Dividing by six is wrong. The way through is to count, for each move, how many strings it leaves exactly as they were, and to average those counts over the moves. The average is the number of necklaces. The same count says how many shapes of graph there are on four dots, the question 41a left open, and the answer is 11.

The places a move group carries x to, times the moves that keep x, is the size of the group. So the number of classes, things up to the group’s moves, is the average over the moves of how many things each move keeps. Six beads of two colours make 14 necklaces up to turns, 13 up to turns and flips. Graphs on four dots come in 11 shapes.

41·cb19.1Where a Thing Can Go, and What Keeps It

Let G be a group of shuffles of a finite set X (19a). For x in X, its class is the set of all gx, the places the moves carry it to. Its keepers are the moves g with gx = x; they form a part-group, since a keeper after a keeper keeps x, and so does a keeper’s undo.

19·chem used this count on molecules, as the class-times-keepers technique. Here is its proof from the copies of 19d.

gx = hx \iff h^{-1}g\,x = x \iff h^{-1}g \in K_x @@ K_x the keepers of x
\iff g \in hK_x @@ a copy of K_x, as in 19d
\text{the moves carrying } x \text{ to one place } y \text{ are exactly one copy} @@ y = hx for some h
\text{the copies never overlap and each has } |K_x| \text{ members} @@ copies-never-overlap, copies-same-size (19d)
\htmlClass{new}{|G| = |\text{class of } x| \cdot |K_x|} \quad \blacksquare @@ one copy for each place in the class

So the size of a class divides the size of the group: a class of things under the six turns of a six-bead necklace has 1, 2, 3 or 6 members, never 4 or 5.

41·cb19.2Count by Averaging

Average-kept technique: the number of classes is the average, over the moves of G, of the number of things each move keeps.

P = \{(g, x) : gx = x\} @@ pairs of a move and a thing it keeps
|P| = \textstyle\sum_{g \in G} |\{x : gx = x\}| @@ sorted by the move (4k)
|P| = \textstyle\sum_{x \in X} |K_x| = \sum_{x} \frac{|G|}{|\text{class of } x|} @@ sorted by the thing; the class-times-keepers technique
\textstyle\sum_{x \in C} \frac{1}{|C|} = 1 \text{ for each class } C @@ |C| terms of 1/|C|
\htmlClass{new}{|P| = |G| \cdot (\text{number of classes})} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by |G|

One set counted two ways again, as the ends-count technique of 41a counted the ends of links. The classes are hard to see; what each move keeps is easy to count.

41·cb19.3Necklaces

A turn of a six-bead necklace by k places keeps a string exactly when the string repeats every k places, and so every gcd(6, k) places, the greatest common divisor of 4j. It splits the six places into gcd(6, k) rings, each of one colour, so it keeps 2^gcd(6, k) strings.

\text{turns by } 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5:\ \ 2^6, 2^1, 2^2, 2^3, 2^2, 2^1 \text{ strings kept} @@ gcd(6, k) = 6, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1
\text{up to turns: } \tfrac16 (64 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 4 + 2) = \tfrac{84}{6} = 14 @@ the average-kept technique
\text{flips: three through two beads keep } 2^4,\ \text{three through two gaps keep } 2^3 @@ the 12 symmetries of the hexagon, 19f
\htmlClass{new}{\text{up to turns and flips: } \tfrac{1}{12}(84 + 3 \cdot 16 + 3 \cdot 8) = \tfrac{156}{12} = 13} \quad \blacksquare @@

So exactly one pair of the 14 necklaces are mirror images of each other that no turn joins, and a flip makes them one. 41·cb19.6.4 asks a chemist’s version of the same question.

41·cb19.4Shapes of Graph

41a counted 64 graphs on four named dots and left the shapes uncounted. The 24 shuffles of the four dots shuffle the six possible links, and a shuffle keeps a graph exactly when it carries every kept link to a kept link: when the graph is a union of the rings the shuffle makes on the links.

\text{do nothing (1 shuffle): 6 rings on the links, } 2^6 = 64 @@ every link stays
\text{a swap (6): the swapped pair’s link, the other pair’s link, two rings of two: } 2^4 @@ 4 rings
\text{two swaps (3): two links stay, two rings of two: } 2^4;\ \ \text{three-rings (8): two rings of three: } 2^2 @@ 4 rings, 2 rings
\text{four-rings (6): one ring of four links, one of two: } 2^2 @@ 2 rings
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{24}(64 + 96 + 48 + 32 + 24) = \tfrac{264}{24} = 11} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-kept technique

Eleven shapes: none, one link, two apart, two touching, three in a path, three in a star, a triangle, and the complements of the first four: all six links, five, a ring of four, and a triangle with a tail. For n dots the shapes grow like 2^(n(n − 1)/2)/n!: most graphs have no symmetry but doing nothing, and are counted n! times among the named ones.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A group acting on a set is a group action; a class is an orbit, and the keepers are the stabilizer. The class-times-keepers technique is the orbit–stabilizer theorem. The average-kept technique is Burnside's lemma, also called the Cauchy–Frobenius lemma, or the lemma that is not Burnside's. Counting with weights for each colour is Pólya enumeration. The three dichlorobenzenes are ortho, meta and para.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy had the count in 1845 and Ferdinand Georg Frobenius in 1887; William Burnside put it in his book of 1897, and the name stuck to him. J. Howard Redfield published a far-reaching form in 1927, unnoticed, and George Pólya found it again in 1937 and counted chemical compounds with it.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each necklace is six strings, one for each turn. So there are 64/6 necklaces, rounded up, which is 11.” Not allowed, and wrong: 14. The all-black necklace is one string, and the alternating one is two. Rounding cannot repair a count that assumed every class has the same size.
