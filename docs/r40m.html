40m · technique

Keeping things findable

The road map of the United States, as a graph, has about twenty four million dots and fifty eight million links. A recipe that runs on it must be able to find, quickly and over and over, the links at a dot, the unsettled dot that is nearest, and whether a dot has been seen before. Rung 38 built the stores that do such finding: the heap, the search tree, the table found by a scrambling rule. This step puts them under the recipes of the last two steps and counts what each costs. A grid of every pair of dots is the wrong store for a road map: it would have hundreds of millions of millions of entries, nearly all of them empty. A list of neighbours at each dot is right. A heap makes the nearest first cost a little more than the number of links. And then a floor: no recipe that learns about lengths only by comparing them can list the dots in order of distance faster than it could sort them, so the heap is nearly the best there is. Last, a graph too big to write down, the positions of a sliding puzzle, is walked by making the dots as they are reached, with a table to say which have been seen.

Store a thin graph as a list of neighbours at each dot, not as a grid of every pair. A heap of tentative distances makes the nearest first cost about the number of links times log₂ n; pushing a fresh entry in place of lowering an old one changes that by a factor of two at most. Listing dots in order of distance by comparing sums costs at least as much as sorting. A graph made as it is walked needs a table of the dots already seen.

40m1Two ways to store links

A graph on dots named 1 to n can be stored as a grid, n × n, with a 1 at (u, w) when u and w are joined and 0 otherwise; or as neighbour lists, one list at each dot of the dots joined to it.

Two-stores technique: the grid takes n² entries and answers “are u and w joined?” in one look; the neighbour lists take n + 2|E| entries, answer it in at most d(u) looks, and give the links at u in d(u) looks where the grid needs n. The count n + 2|E| is the ends-count technique: each link is in two lists.

n \approx 2.4 \times 10^7,\ \ |E| \approx 5.8 \times 10^7 @@ the road map of the United States as dots and one-way links
\text{grid: } n^2 \approx 5.7 \times 10^{14} \text{ entries} @@ almost all 0
\text{lists: } n + |E| \approx 8.2 \times 10^7 \text{ entries} @@ one-way links are in one list each
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the lists are about 7 million times smaller}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average dot has fewer than 3 links out

A graph is thin when |E| is far below n², as a road map is, and crowded when it is near. Lists suit thin graphs, which is most of them. To answer “are u and w joined?” quickly in a thin graph, put each list in a scrambled table of 38n: on average a few looks, with room still about n + 2|E|.

40m2A heap under the nearest first

The nearest first needs, again and again, the unsettled dot with least tentative distance, and lowers tentative distances as dots are settled. 38n’s heap gives the least of its entries in one look, and takes it out or puts a new one in with about log₂ of its size moves.

Heap-run technique: with neighbour lists and a heap, the nearest first costs about (n + |E|) log₂ n moves; pushing a fresh entry whenever a tentative distance is lowered, and skipping stale entries when they come out, keeps that within a factor of two.

\text{each dot is settled once: one take-out, about } \log_2 n @@ n take-outs
\text{each link is looked at once from its settled end: at most one lowering} @@ 40k’s charge to the links
\text{fresh entries: at most } |E| + 1 \text{ in the heap at any time} @@ one per lowering, and the start
\log_2(|E| + 1) \le \log_2 n^2 = 2 \log_2 n @@ |E| is below n²
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } (n + |E|) \log_2 n \text{, and at most twice that with stale entries}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a stale entry is taken out once and skipped

On a crowded graph, with |E| near n², a plain list of tentative distances, searched in full for the least each time, costs n² looks, and beats the heap’s n² log₂ n. The store follows the graph.

40m3The floor

Can a cleverer store make the nearest first cost about n + |E|, with no log at all? Not if the recipe learns about lengths only by comparing them.

Sorting-inside technique: a recipe that lists the dots in order of distance from r, and learns about lengths only by comparing sums of them, makes at least log₂((n − 1)!) comparisons on some graph with n dots.

\text{a star: } r \text{ joined to } w_1, \dots, w_{n-1} \text{ with lengths } \ell_1, \dots, \ell_{n-1}\text{, all different} @@ any n − 1 numbers
\text{distance of } w_i = \ell_i @@ one link each
\text{listing the } w_i \text{ in order of distance lists the } \ell_i \text{ in order} @@ the list is a sort
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at least } \log_2((n - 1)!) \sim n \log_2 n \text{ comparisons}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the answer-count technique (22l, 38l)

So on thin graphs, where |E| is a small multiple of n, the heap’s (n + |E|) log₂ n is within a fixed factor of the floor. The greedy tree of 40k sorts its links first and meets the same floor for |E| numbers. Recipes that do arithmetic on the lengths, not only comparisons, can step under the floor, and do, for whole-number lengths; the floor is about what a recipe is allowed to learn.

40m4A graph made as it is walked

Some graphs are too large to store, and are given by a rule: the positions of a puzzle are the dots, and two positions are joined when one move turns one into the other. The sliding puzzle of eight numbered tiles in a 3 × 3 frame, with one gap, has 9! = 362 880 arrangements. Half of them can be reached from any one, by a count of 19e’s shuffles.

Seen-set technique: walking out in layers over a graph made by a rule, with the seen dots kept in a scrambled table, costs on average a fixed number of looks for each link followed, and room for every dot seen. At each dot, the rule makes its neighbours; each is looked up in the table, and put in the queue and the table if it is new. The queue of 40k1 stays as it was.

\text{a position has 2, 3 or 4 neighbours: the tiles beside the gap} @@ the rule
\text{reachable from one position: } 9!/2 = 181\,440 @@ a move keeps the parity of the shuffle with the gap’s place, 19e
\text{links followed: at most } 4 \cdot 181\,440 @@ the layer-by-layer technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the farthest positions are 31 moves away}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last layer, found by the walk

The fifteen-tile puzzle in a 4 × 4 frame has 16!/2, about 10¹³, positions, and the walk becomes a question of room, not time. The farthest are 80 moves away; that was settled in 2005, by a walk that kept only its last few layers and a code for each position in a few bits.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The grid is the adjacency matrix, the lists are adjacency lists, and a scrambled table is a hash table. Thin and crowded graphs are sparse and dense. The heap under the nearest first is a binary heap used as a priority queue, and pushing fresh entries is lazy deletion. The sorting-inside technique is the comparison lower bound for Dijkstra's algorithm. The stack walk of 40m.6.1 is depth-first search. Walking a graph made by a rule is implicit graph search.

Who said it first

J. W. J. Williams invented the heap in 1964, for sorting. Michael Fredman and Robert Tarjan gave a heap in 1984 that brings the nearest first to |E| + n log₂ n. Mikkel Thorup found, in 1999, a recipe for whole-number lengths in undirected graphs that takes about |E| steps, by arithmetic on the lengths. The fifteen-tile puzzle was a craze in 1880; William Johnson and William Story proved in 1879 that half its positions cannot be reached. Richard Korf and Peter Schultze walked all of its positions in 2005.

A proof that is not allowed

“The heap takes about log₂ n moves for each operation, and the nearest first does about n + |E| of them, so no recipe can do better than (n + |E|) log₂ n.” Not allowed: that bounds this recipe, from above. A floor for every recipe needs an argument about every recipe, as the star gives, and even that holds only for recipes that learn by comparing.
