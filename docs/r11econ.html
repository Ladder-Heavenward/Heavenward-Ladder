Why Would Oranges Be Left to Rot?

Why would oranges be left to rot? In the hungry years of the 1930s, Californians watched good oranges dumped in heaps, and some sprayed with kerosene so that nobody could take them, while families nearby went without. It looked like madness, or cruelty. The question is hard because the people who did it were not mad. Each step made sense to someone, and the waste came out of the steps together. Economists answer it with two functions of the price: how much buyers will take, and how much growers will send. This page builds both, finds the price where they meet, and shows what a least price set above it does. It reads the result with rung 11's tools: what is sold is continuous in the price, with a joint where the two functions meet; and when picking has a cost, supply has a jump, and a meeting price may not exist at all.

Demand falls as the price rises, supply climbs. They meet at one price, where what growers send equals what buyers take. What is sold is the lesser of the two, a glued function with a joint at that price. A least price set above it sells only what buyers take at that price, while growers send more, and the difference rots. If buyers barely flinch at the price, growers take in more by selling less.

11econ1Demand and Supply Are Functions of the Price

Fix a market, a good and a stretch of time: navel oranges, in boxes, at the wholesale market in Sacramento, for one week. At each price per box, buyers would take some number of boxes, and growers would send some number. Economists call the first demand and the second supply.

From here on, on this page, p names a price in dollars per box, D(p) the boxes buyers would take at p, and S(p) the boxes growers would send at p. Both are functions of p in 9a's sense, on a stretch of prices where they make sense. Here S is the market's supply, not 3c's S(x), the next number.

The commonest guess is that both are linear on the stretch that matters. Demand falls as the price rises: buyers switch to apples, or buy fewer. Supply climbs: at a higher price, growers send fruit they would otherwise sell for juice. Demand falls with the price and supply climbs with it, and each is guessed as a linear function over the prices that matter. This page works one model, made up to be the right size, not measured:

D(p) = 6000 - 100\,p @@ rate −100: each dollar more per box, 100 fewer boxes bought (11e's rate)
S(p) = 1000 + 150\,p @@ rate 150: each dollar more, 150 more boxes sent
6000 - 100\,p = 1000 + 150\,p \iff 250\,p = 5000 @@ where they meet: 5g's techs
p^* = \htmlClass{new}{20},\quad q^* = D(20) = S(20) = \htmlClass{new}{4000} @@ $20 a box, 4000 boxes

Economists draw these with the price up and the boxes across, a habit from the 1890s. This page draws boxes up and the price across, because the boxes are the functions' outputs (9e).

11econ2Where They Meet

Let E(p) = S(p) − D(p), the boxes sent but not bought at p. Here E(p) = 250p − 5000: a polynomial, so continuous at every price (11b's continuous-sums, or 11d). Below $20 it is negative: buyers want more than is sent, and they bid the price up. Above $20 it is positive: boxes go unsold, and sellers cut the price to move them. The economists' guess is that the price drifts to where E is 0. Meeting technique: two linear functions with rates of opposite signs meet at exactly one price. For D(p) = k − bp and S(p) = c + dp with b, d > 0, the equation k − bp = c + dp has the one answer p* = (k − c)/(b + d), since b + d is not 0 (5g). ∎ It is a price that makes sense when k > c, so that p* > 0.

What is sold at a price p is the lesser of the two: buyers cannot take boxes that were not sent, and growers cannot sell boxes nobody takes. Call it Q(p). Below p*, Q(p) = S(p); above it, Q(p) = D(p).

Q(p) = S(p) \text{ for } p \le 20,\quad Q(p) = D(p) \text{ for } p \gt 20 @@ the lesser of the two, on each side
\lim_{p \to 20^-} Q(p) = S(20) = 4000 = D(20) = \lim_{p \to 20^+} Q(p) @@ each piece is a polynomial; substitution
Q \text{ is continuous at } 20 @@ glued-functions: the pieces arrive at one height, and Q(20) is that height
\text{rates } \htmlClass{new}{150} \text{ on the left},\ \htmlClass{new}{-100} \text{ on the right} @@ 11e: a joint. Continuity asks how the pieces arrive, not how they climb

So a small change in price makes a small change in what is sold, on both sides of p*. The most is sold at p* itself, where the joint is.

11econ3A Least Price

Suppose growers agree, or a law says, that no box may sell below f dollars. Call f a price floor. If f ≤ 20 it does nothing: the market price is already above it. If f > 20, the price is f. Buyers take D(f). Growers, offered f, send S(f). The difference has nowhere to go.

D(26) = 6000 - 2600 = 3400 \text{ boxes bought} @@ at the floor
S(26) = 1000 + 3900 = 4900 \text{ boxes sent} @@ growers answer the higher price
E(26) = \htmlClass{new}{1500} \text{ boxes left over} @@ they cannot be sold at $26, and selling them for less breaks the floor

Rot technique: the boxes left over, as a function of the floor, are 0 up to p* and S(f) − D(f) above it, a continuous function with a joint at p*. Call it R(f). For f ≤ 20, R(f) = 0; for f > 20, R(f) = 250f − 5000. At 20 both pieces give 0, so R is continuous there (glued-functions), with rates 0 and 250. ∎ A floor just above p* leaves only a few boxes over; every dollar more leaves 250 more. Nothing jumps.

11econ4Why Growers Would Want It

At the meeting price, growers take in 20 × 4000 = $80 000. At a floor of $26 they sell fewer boxes and take in 26 × 3400 = $88 400. They sell 600 fewer boxes, send 900 more, and are paid more. The takings at a floor f above p* are T(f) = f · D(f) = 6000f − 100f², and 8i's completing the square shows where they are greatest.

T(f) = 6000f - 100f^2 = -100\,(f^2 - 60f) @@ for f ≥ 20
= -100\,\big((f - 30)^2 - 900\big) = \htmlClass{new}{90\,000 - 100\,(f - 30)^2} @@ completing the square
T(f) \le 90\,000,\ \text{ with equality at } f = \htmlClass{keep}{30} @@ a square is never negative (8f)

Food is like that. People need about as many oranges at $26 as at $20, so a higher price loses few sales and each sale brings more. When buyers barely flinch at the price, the growers as a group take in more by selling less, and the boxes they do not sell are the cost of selling less. At a floor of $30 they would take in $90 000 and leave 2500 boxes over.

But each grower alone would rather sell his leftover boxes at $25 than see them rot, and if many do, the floor collapses. So the leftover fruit has to be kept off the market: sold for juice or cattle feed at a much lower price, or destroyed. In the 1930s some was sprayed with kerosene so that it could not be taken and eaten for nothing. From the 1950s into the early 1990s, a committee of growers under a federal order set each week how many navel oranges from California and Arizona could go to the fresh market. The rest went to juice, to feed, or to waste.

11econ5When Picking Has a Cost: A Jump

The model so far lets supply climb a box at a time. Late in the season it does not. The fruit is grown. What remains is the cost of picking and hauling it, say $8 a box. Below $8 a grower loses money on every box picked, so he picks none. Above $8 he picks the lot. Let the crop be 7000 boxes, a bumper year.

S_h(p) = 0 \text{ for } p \lt 8,\quad S_h(p) = 7000 \text{ for } p \gt 8 @@ pick none, or pick all
\lim_{p \to 8^-} S_h(p) = 0 \ne 7000 = \lim_{p \to 8^+} S_h(p) @@ 11f: a jump at 8, of size 7000
p \lt 8:\ S_h = 0 \lt D(p);\quad p \gt 8:\ S_h = 7000 \gt 5200 \ge D(p) @@ D(8) = 5200, and D falls from there
\text{no price with } S_h(p) = D(p) @@ the meeting price does not exist

The market price falls to $8, where a grower does not care whether he picks. Buyers take D(8) = 5200 boxes, and 1800 boxes stay on the trees. No law made that happen, and no committee. The jump in supply did. At p = 8 itself, the model's supply is not one number: anything from 0 to 7000 suits the growers, so it is not a function there, and 9a's demand of one output fails.

Compare the linear model, where E rose from negative to positive with no break, and a meeting price existed. Here E jumps from negative to positive at 8, and none does. Whether a continuous E that is negative at one price and positive at a higher one must be 0 somewhere between is a question rung 12 answers.

11econ7Running It Backwards

Handed p*, q* and the two rates, write D(p) = q* − b(p − p*) and S(p) = q* + d(p − p*). Handed a floor and the boxes left over, R(f) = (b + d)(f − p*) gives f, or the sum of the rates. Handed the takings a group of growers wants, solve T(f) = f · D(f) for f, and read off the boxes it leaves over. Handed a picking cost and a crop, compare the crop with demand at that cost: if the crop is larger, there is no meeting price, and the excess stays on the trees.

Who said it first

Antoine Augustin Cournot wrote demand as a function of the price in 1838. Léon Walras, in 1874, described how prices move toward the one where the two meet, by a groping that cuts a price when goods go unsold and raises it when buyers go without. Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) drew the two curves with the price on the up axis, the habit economists keep. The dumped and sprayed oranges are in chapter 25 of John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath (1939). The weekly limits on navel oranges were set under federal marketing orders for California and Arizona citrus, under a law of 1937; the limits on shipments ended in the early 1990s. The ploughed cotton and the killed pigs of 1933 came under the Agricultural Adjustment Act of that year.

Steinbeck wrote of men with hoses squirting kerosene on the oranges, and of the people who came to take the fruit, and of the anger that grew in them as they watched.

John Steinbeck, The Grapes of Wrath (1939), chapter 25; paraphrase

Accepted names: p* and q* are the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity; E(p) > 0 is excess supply, often called a surplus (a word 16·econ uses differently), and E(p) < 0 is a shortage; a floor is a price floor or price support. Demand that barely changes with the price is inelastic. The jump in supply at the picking cost is the shutdown price, read for a crop already grown.
