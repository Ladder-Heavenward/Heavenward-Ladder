How Big Must Order Be?

How big must order be? 42j proved that at any party of six, three people all know each other or three are all strangers, and that six is the least number that works. For four people, the number is 18, known since 1955. For five people, nobody knows. The answer lies between 43 and 46, and the upper end moved last in 2024. This page builds the party of 17 with no four all acquainted and no four all strangers, from the remainders of squares. It counts why 43 is out of reach of any search, even counted up to symmetry. And it tells the story of the gap 42j left between the lower bound, near 2 to the power k/2, and the upper bound, near 4 to the power k: for 88 years no one could lower the 4, and in 2023 someone did.

Link two of 17 dots when their difference leaves the remainder of a square on dividing by 17: no four are all linked and no four all unlinked, so R(4, 4) = 18. R(5, 5) is between 43 and 46; the colourings of K₄₃ number 2⁹⁰³. 42j gave 2^(k/2) < R(k, k) < 4^k; in 2023 the 4 became 4 − 2⁻⁷, and later 3.8.

42·now2.1A Party of 17 With No Order of Four

Number 17 dots 0 to 16. The squares 1², 2², …, 16² leave the remainders 1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16 on dividing by 17 (4j); call these eight the square remainders. Link two dots when their difference leaves a square remainder. Since 16 = −1 leaves a square remainder, x − y and y − x leave square remainders together, so the links do not depend on order.

Square-party technique: in the 17-dot graph of square remainders, no four dots are all linked and no four are all unlinked.

\text{adding the same number to every dot keeps every link} @@ differences do not change
\text{so a group of four all linked can be moved to contain } 0 \text{ and some } s,\ s \text{ a square remainder} @@ move one of its links to start at 0
x \mapsto -x \text{ keeps links too; so } s \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\} @@ 16 − s for the rest
\text{the other two dots are linked to } 0 \text{ and to } s @@ square remainders both x and x − s
s = 1: \{2, 9, 16\};\ \ s = 2: \{1, 4, 15\};\ \ s = 4: \{2, 8, 13\};\ \ s = 8: \{4, 9, 16\} @@ the dots linked to both
\text{differences in each: } 7, 14, 7;\ \ 3, 14, 11;\ \ 6, 11, 5;\ \ 5, 12, 7 @@ none a square remainder
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no two of them are linked, so no four are all linked}} @@ 
  
3 \cdot \{1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16\} \to \{3, 6, 12, 7, 10, 5, 11, 14\} @@ remainders on dividing by 17
\text{these are exactly the 8 differences that are not square remainders} @@ and 3 times those gives the squares back
x \mapsto 3x \text{ shuffles the 17 dots} @@ 6 · 3 = 18 leaves 1, so x ↦ 6x undoes it
\text{it carries links to non-links and non-links to links} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four all unlinked would go to four all linked}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the step before

So R(4, 4) > 17. The party-number technique of 42j with R(3, 4) = 9, sharpened by a standing question of 42j, gives R(4, 4) ≤ 18. So R(4, 4) = 18 exactly, and a computer could check the 17-dot party too: it has 2380 groups of four, and none is all linked or all unlinked.

42·now2.2Why Five Is Out of Reach

For five, R(5, 5) ≥ 43: a colouring of the links of K₄₂ with no one-colour K₅ was found by computer in 1989. And R(5, 5) ≤ 46, by a computer proof of 2024, which lowered the 48 of 2017. The party-number technique alone gives R(5, 5) ≤ R(4, 5) + R(5, 4) = 25 + 25 = 50, using R(4, 5) = 25, found by computer in 1995.

K_{43} \text{ has } \tbinom{43}{2} = 903 \text{ links} @@ each red or blue
2^{903} \approx 10^{271.8} \text{ colourings} @@ 903 · log₁₀ 2
43! \approx 10^{52.8} \text{ ways to rename the dots} @@ the moves of 42·cb19
\text{each class holds at most } 43! \text{ colourings} @@ the class-times-keepers technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at least } 10^{271.8 - 52.8} = 10^{219} \text{ classes to check}} @@ far beyond any computer

So the search for R(5, 5) cannot be a search through colourings. The upper bound of 2024 came from proving that a colouring of K₄₆ with no one-colour K₅ would have to be built from smaller parties of known kinds, and then checking those by computer, a proof of the kind 42·now asked about. The lower bound would rise only if someone found a colouring of K₄₃ with no one-colour K₅; many believe the answer is 43.

42·now2.3The Gap Between 2 and 4

42j proved both bounds of the gap: the party-number technique gives R(k, k) < 4^(k − 1), and the average-below-one technique of 1947 gives R(k, k) > 2^(k/2). Written as k-th roots, the answer lies between √2 ≈ 1.41 and 4.

1935:\ R(k, k)^{1/k} < 4 @@ the party-number technique
1947:\ R(k, k)^{1/k} > \sqrt 2 @@ the average-below-one technique
1975\text{ to }2020: \text{ only factors smaller than any } c^k @@ both bounds; the bases did not move
2023:\ R(k, k) \le (4 - \varepsilon)^k,\ \ \varepsilon = 2^{-7} @@ the base below 4 for the first time
\htmlClass{new}{\text{later: } R(k, k) \le 3.8^k \text{ for large } k} @@ by a sharper run of the same method
  
\left(\tfrac{3.9921875}{4}\right)^{100} \approx 0.82 @@ 4 − 2⁻⁷, at k = 100
\left(\tfrac{3.8}{4}\right)^{100} \approx 0.0059 @@ 0.95¹⁰⁰
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a base below 4 beats every bound of the form } c \cdot 4^k / k^m} @@ exponentials outgrow powers

The method of 2023 builds the one-colour group greedily, as 42j’s proof does, but keeps a second pile of dots, a “book”, from which many choices can be made at once, and proves that the pile stays large enough. The lower bound has not moved its base since 1947, and no one knows a colouring better than a random one. Whether the answer is near √2 to the k, near 4 to the k, or somewhere between, is open.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

R(s, t) are the Ramsey numbers. The 17-dot graph is the Paley graph of order 17, and the square remainders are the quadratic residues of 17, which rung 43 names properly. The square-party technique is the statement that the Paley graph on 17 vertices has clique and independence number 3. The 2023 upper bound is the exponential improvement for diagonal Ramsey numbers, by the book algorithm.

Who said it first

Robert Greenwood and Andrew Gleason found R(3, 4) = 9, R(4, 4) = 18 and the 17-dot party in 1955. Paul Erdős and George Szekeres gave the upper bound in 1935, and Erdős the random lower bound in 1947. Geoffrey Exoo found the colouring of K₄₂ in 1989; Brendan McKay and Stanisław Radziszowski proved R(4, 5) = 25 in 1995; Vigleik Angeltveit and McKay proved R(5, 5) ≤ 48 in 2017 and ≤ 46 in 2024. Marcelo Campos, Simon Griffiths, Robert Morris and Julian Sahasrabudhe proved the bound (4 − ε)ᵏ in 2023, and P. Gupta, N. Ndiaye, S. Norin and L. Wei sharpened it to 3.8ᵏ. Erdős said that if an alien force demanded R(5, 5) on pain of war, we should set every computer to work on it; if it demanded R(6, 6), we should try to destroy the aliens.

A proof that is not allowed

“R(4, 4) is the next after R(3, 3) = 6, and the numbers go 2, 6, so they grow about three times each time: R(4, 4) ≈ 18, R(5, 5) ≈ 54.” Not allowed: two values fix no rule, and the guess for five is already above the proved bound 46. The value 18 came from a party of 17 and a count, not from a pattern.
