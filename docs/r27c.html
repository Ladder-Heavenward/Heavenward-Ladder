27c · proof

Maps That Keep Adding and Stretching

Some functions between spaces of arrows respect the arrows. Send a sum and you get the sum of what each part was sent to. Stretch first or stretch after, it comes to the same. Turns of the plane do this, and so does taking a slope. Such a map loses some arrows to zero and reaches some part of the far space. This step proves that what it loses and what it reaches always add up to the dimension it started with.

A map that keeps adding and stretching sends a space of dimension n somewhere. The dimension of what it sends to zero, plus the dimension of what it reaches, is n.

27c1What a Map Loses and Reaches

A map L from a space V to a space W is straight if L(u + v) = L(u) + L(v) and L(t·v) = t·L(v) for all arrows and numbers. Accepted name: a linear map. The arrows it sends to 0 are what it loses; the arrows L(v) are what it reaches.

Nothing-sent technique: for a straight map L, what it loses is a space inside V, what it reaches is a space inside W, and L is one-to-one exactly when it loses only 0.

L(0) = L(0\cdot 0) = 0\cdot L(0) = 0 @@ the zero-stretch technique (27a1), twice
L(u) = L(v) = 0 \Rightarrow L(u + v) = 0,\ L(t\cdot u) = 0 @@ L keeps adding and stretching
L(u) + L(v) = L(u+v),\ \ t\cdot L(v) = L(t\cdot v) @@ the same, read from the far side
\text{both are spaces} @@ the inside-space technique (27a2)
L(u) = L(v) \iff L(u - v) = 0 @@ L keeps adding, and −v = (−1)·v
\htmlClass{new}{L \text{ one-to-one} \iff L \text{ loses only } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above

27c2Lost Plus Reached

Lost-plus-reached technique: if V has dimension n and L is straight from V, then the dimension of what L loses plus the dimension of what it reaches is n.

u_1..u_k \text{ a basis of what } L \text{ loses} @@ it is a space inside V (27c1); its dimension k is at most n (27b2)
u_1..u_k, v_1..v_r \text{ a basis of } V,\ k + r = n @@ the grow-to-basis technique (27b3)
L(v_1), \ldots, L(v_r) \text{ build what } L \text{ reaches} @@ L(uᵢ) = 0, and L keeps mixes
\textstyle\sum s_jL(v_j) = 0 \Rightarrow \sum s_jv_j \text{ is lost} \Rightarrow \sum s_jv_j = \sum t_iu_i @@ L keeps mixes; the u’s build what is lost
\Rightarrow \text{all } s_j = 0 @@ the whole list u, v is free
\htmlClass{new}{k + \dim(\text{reached}) = k + r = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the L(vⱼ) are a basis of what is reached

So a straight map from a space to itself of dimension n is one-to-one exactly when it reaches everything. If it loses only 0, what it reaches has dimension n, and the grow-to-basis technique says n free arrows in a space of dimension n already build it.

The accepted names

A straight map is a linear map. What it loses is its kernel (or null space), and the kernel’s dimension is the nullity; what it reaches is its image (or range), and the image’s dimension is the rank. The lost-plus-reached technique is the rank–nullity theorem.

Who said it first

Grassmann had the idea in 1844. The theorem in this form became standard in the 1930s, when Bartel van der Waerden’s Moderne Algebra set linear algebra out from spaces and maps rather than from equations.
