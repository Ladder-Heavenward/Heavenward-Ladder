30h · proof

Averages settle

Toss a fair coin ten times and you may well get seven heads. Toss it ten thousand times and the share of heads will sit very close to one half. Everyone who has gambled or counted knows this. This step proves it, in two strengths. First, a chance variable that is never negative can rarely stray far above its average, and one with a small spread can rarely stray far from its average. From that, the average of many independent tries is very unlikely to be far from the true average once the tries are many. Then a stronger claim: with chance one, the run of averages actually settles at the true average, and stays there. The proof counts how often things can go wrong, and finds that, past some point, they stop going wrong for good.

Strays far above the average are rare, and the rarer the smaller the spread. So the average of many independent copies is very likely near the true average, and with chance one it settles there.

30h1Strays are rare

Stray-bound technique: if X ≥ 0 and a > 0, then P(X ≥ a) ≤ E X / a.

Spread-bound technique: if X has average μ and spread width σ, then P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² for every k > 0.

a \cdot 1_{\{X \ge a\}} \le X @@ where X ≥ a the left side is a ≤ X; elsewhere it is 0 ≤ X
a\,P(X \ge a) \le E\,X @@ average-adds keeps order (30g1)
\htmlClass{new}{P(X \ge a) \le E\,X / a} @@ stray-bound
\{|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\} = \{(X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2\} @@ both sides at least 0
P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le E(X - \mu)^2 / (k^2\sigma^2) = 1/k^2 @@ stray-bound with (X − μ)², a = k²σ²
\htmlClass{new}{P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ spread-bound

The spread-bound needs nothing about the shape of X but its spread. Whatever X is, at most 1 in 4 of its chance lies two widths or more from its average, and at most 1 in 9 lies three widths or more away.

30h2Averages settle, very likely

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent chance variables with the same law: P(Xₖ ∈ A) is the same for every k. Each has average μ and spread σ². Their average after n tries is X̄ₙ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ)/n.

Averages-settle technique: for every ε > 0, P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), which heads to 0 as n grows.

E\,\bar X_n = \mu,\ \ \text{Var}\,\bar X_n = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n @@ average-adds; independent-spreads-add; Var(aX) = a² Var X (30g)
P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \text{Var}\,\bar X_n / \varepsilon^2 @@ spread-bound, with kσ = ε
\htmlClass{new}{P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the challenge (10a): for n past σ²/(ε² δ) the chance is under δ
Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

21a wrote π as a fence with a width you choose. An average from tries is the same. It is never the true μ, and it carries a width: σ/√n. The width shrinks as the tries grow, by the square root.

An average from tries is reported with its width, σ over the root of the number of tries.

A poll of 1000 voters, each for or against, has σ at most 1/2, so a width of at most 0.5/√1000 ≈ 0.016. Report the share with that width or the report is unfinished. Quadruple the poll, and the width only halves.

A poll of 1000 voters in Washington finds 52 percent for a measure. How many voters would the poll need before two widths no longer reach 50 percent?

30h3Only finitely often

Finitely-often technique: if A₁, A₂, … are events and Σ P(Aₙ) is finite, then with chance 1 only finitely many of them happen.

\text{infinitely many } A_k \text{ happen} \Rightarrow \text{some } A_k \text{ with } k \ge n \text{ happens, for every } n @@ the event is inside each Bₙ = A_n ∪ A_{n+1} ∪ ⋯
P(\text{infinitely many}) \le P(B_n) \le \sum_{k \ge n} P(A_k) @@ split Bₙ into Aₙ, A_{n+1} less Aₙ, …: separate, each inside its Aₖ (30b2, 30b3)
\sum_{k \ge n} P(A_k) \to 0 @@ the tail of a settling sum (22d)
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{infinitely many } A_n) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a number below every tail is 0

So chance 1 for “only finitely many” comes from counting alone. Nothing is needed about whether the events are independent.

30h4Averages settle, surely

Surely-settle technique: if X₁, X₂, … are independent with the same law, average μ and E X⁴ finite, then with chance 1, X̄ₙ settles at μ.

Y_k = X_k - \mu,\ \ E\,Y_k = 0,\ \ S_n = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n @@ shift each try to average 0
E\,S_n^4 = \sum_{i, j, k, l} E(Y_i Y_j Y_k Y_l) @@ multiply out; average-adds
\text{a term with a lone index has average } 0 @@ independent-averages-multiply, and E Y = 0
E\,S_n^4 = n\,E\,Y^4 + 3n(n - 1)\,\sigma^4 \le C n^2 @@ i = j = k = l: n terms; two pairs: 3n(n − 1) terms
P(|S_n| \ge n\varepsilon) \le C n^2 / (n\varepsilon)^4 = C/(\varepsilon^4 n^2) @@ stray-bound with S_n⁴ ≥ n⁴ε⁴
\sum_n C/(\varepsilon^4 n^2) < \infty \Rightarrow |\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon \text{ only finitely often, with chance } 1 @@ 22f’s sum of 1/n²; finitely-often
\htmlClass{new}{\bar X_n \to \mu \text{ with chance } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε = 1, 1/2, 1/3, …: countably many events of chance 0

The averages-settle technique says that at any one large n, a stray is unlikely. This one says more: run the tries for ever, and with chance 1 the strays stop. The demand on E X⁴ makes the proof short. The same is true with only E|X| finite, and that proof waits much higher up the ladder.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The stray-bound is Markov’s inequality, and the spread-bound is Chebyshev’s inequality. Averages-settle is the weak law of large numbers, which says X̄ₙ converges to μ in probability. The finitely-often technique is the first Borel–Cantelli lemma. Surely-settle is the strong law of large numbers, which says X̄ₙ converges to μ almost surely. Independent copies with the same law are i.i.d., independent and identically distributed. Believing a coin is due is the gambler’s fallacy.

Who said it first

Jacob Bernoulli proved the first law of large numbers, for coin tosses, in his Ars Conjectandi, published in 1713; he called it his golden theorem. Pafnuty Chebyshev proved the spread-bound in 1867, and his student Andrey Markov the stray-bound. Émile Borel proved the strong law for coin tosses in 1909, and Francesco Cantelli the finitely-often lemma in 1917. Andrey Kolmogorov proved the strong law with only a finite average in 1933.

A proof that is not allowed

“The share of heads settles at 1/2, so after many tosses heads and tails come out even.” Not allowed: the share settles because the difference is divided by n. The difference itself has width √n/2, so it usually grows.
