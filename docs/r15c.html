15c · proof

Integrable, and the sign ∫

15b showed that the lower sums all sit below the upper sums, so there is a gap between them, or a single point where they meet. This step takes the best of each. The lower sums are bounded above, so rung 8's no-holes gives them a least upper bound. The upper sums have a greatest lower bound. Suppose those two numbers are the same. Then exactly one number is above every under-estimate and below every over-estimate, and boxes-trap-the-area leaves the area no choice: it is that number. A long S is the mark for it.

The best under-estimate is the sup of all lower sums. The best over-estimate is the inf of all upper sums. The first is never more than the second. When they are equal, the function is integrable, and the common value is its integral: the area. To check, find one partition whose two sums are as close as you like.

15c1The best estimates

Fix f bounded on [u, v]. The set of its lower sums, one for each partition, is not empty, since {u, v} is a partition. By every-lower-below-every-upper every lower sum is at most U(f, {u, v}). So the set is bounded above, and 8c's no-holes gives it a least upper bound. Likewise the upper sums are bounded below, and greatest-lower-bound gives them a greatest lower bound. From here on the ladder uses no-holes’ accepted name, completeness: a regular number system with no holes is a complete ordered field, and ℝ is one.

From here on, the lower integral of f on [u, v] is the sup of L(f, P) over all partitions P of [u, v], and the upper integral is the inf of U(f, P) over all partitions. Their accepted names are the lower integral and the upper integral. In marks, the lower integral is : the P written low says which letter runs over the partitions, as i does in a Σ. The upper integral is in the same way. Each mark is a name, for one number: the packed form of a search through every partition.

Lower-at-most-upper technique: for f bounded on [u, v], the lower integral is at most the upper integral.

\text{fix a partition } Q @@ any one
L(f, P) \le U(f, Q) \text{ for every } P @@ every-lower-below-every-upper (15b)
\sup_P L(f, P) \le U(f, Q) @@ U(f, Q) is an upper bound of the lower sums; the sup is the least (no-holes)
\text{that is true for every } Q @@ Q was any partition
\htmlClass{new}{\sup_P L(f, P) \le \inf_Q U(f, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lower integral is a lower bound of the upper sums; the inf is the greatest (greatest-lower-bound)

15c2Integrable

From here on, f is integrable on [u, v] when f is bounded there and its lower integral equals its upper integral. The common value is the integral of f from u to v, written ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx, read “the integral from u to v of f of x, d x”. Its accepted names: f is integrable; ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx is a definite integral; f is the integrand. u and v are the bounds of integration, which many books call its limits, a second use of that word. The whole mark ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx is a name, for one number. ∫ with its ends and dx is an operator: carried out on f, it produces that number.

The letter x in ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx is a dummy, like i in a Σ: ∫ᵤᵛ f(t) dt is the same number. The dx is a reminder of the widths Δxᵢ, and of which letter is the input. It is not a number, and nothing here multiplies by it.

Exactly-one-between technique: if f is integrable on [u, v] and a number A satisfies L(f, P) ≤ A ≤ U(f, P) for every partition P, then A = ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

A \ge L(f, P) \text{ for every } P @@ the hypothesis
A \ge \text{the lower integral} @@ A is an upper bound of the lower sums (no-holes)
A \le \text{the upper integral} @@ A is a lower bound of the upper sums (greatest-lower-bound)
\text{lower} = \text{upper} = \textstyle\int_u^v f(x)\,dx @@ integrable
\htmlClass{new}{A = \textstyle\int_u^v f(x)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ three-way-order on ℝ: A is neither less nor more

So boxes-trap-the-area and exactly-one-between together leave the area no room. If f ≥ 0 is integrable, and the region under f has an area obeying the three area axioms, that area is ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

From here on, the area of the region under an integrable f ≥ 0 on [u, v] is ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

One of the inventors of the calculus, in a manuscript of 29 October 1675, proposed writing a long S in place of the word omnia. The sum of all the l's would then be written ∫ l.

15c3The test

The definition asks about every partition at once. The test asks for one.

Gap-closes technique: a bounded f is integrable on [u, v] exactly when, for every ε > 0, some partition P has U(f, P) − L(f, P) < ε. Its accepted name is Riemann's criterion.

In the column below, (I with a bar below) names the lower integral and (I with a bar above) the upper integral. The bars are part of the names, as a low mark is, and claim nothing.

\begin{aligned}&\text{let } \underline{I} \text{ and } \overline{I} \text{ be}\\ &\quad\text{the lower and upper integrals}\end{aligned} @@ lower-at-most-upper: I̲ ≤ Ī
\text{suppose } \underline{I} \lt \overline{I} @@ for contradiction (7e)
\varepsilon = \overline{I} - \underline{I} \gt 0 @@ the gap between them
\text{call } P:\ U(f, P) - L(f, P) \lt \varepsilon @@ the hypothesis, at this ε
L(f, P) \le \underline{I} \le \overline{I} \le U(f, P) @@ sup is an upper bound, inf a lower bound; lower-at-most-upper
\overline{I} - \underline{I} \le U - L \lt \varepsilon = \overline{I} - \underline{I};\ \ \bot @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree; three-way-order on ℝ
\htmlClass{new}{\underline{I} = \overline{I}} @@ refute; lower-at-most-upper and three-way-order on ℝ
  
I = \underline{I} = \overline{I};\ \ \text{fix } \varepsilon \gt 0 @@ integrable
\text{call } P_1:\ L(f, P_1) \gt I - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ I − ε/2 is less than the sup, so no upper bound of the lower sums (no-holes)
\text{call } P_2:\ U(f, P_2) \lt I + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ likewise for the inf (greatest-lower-bound)
P = \text{the common refinement of } P_1 \text{ and } P_2 @@ 15b
U(f, P) - L(f, P) \le U(f, P_2) - L(f, P_1) @@ refining-tightens, twice
\htmlClass{new}{\lt (I + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}) - (I - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}) = \varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two calls

15c4Worked: what passes

f(x) = c \text{ on } [u, v] @@ mᵢ = Mᵢ = c on every piece
L(f, P) = U(f, P) = c \textstyle\sum \Delta x_i = c(v - u) @@ widths add (15a)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_u^v c\,dx = c(v - u)} @@ every lower sum and every upper sum is that number
  
f = 1 \text{ on } [0, 1),\ \ f = 2 \text{ on } [1, 2] @@ one discontinuity, at 1
P_\delta = \{0, 1 - \delta, 1 + \delta, 2\},\ \ 0 \lt \delta \lt 1 @@ a narrow piece around the discontinuity
L = (1 - \delta) + 2\delta \cdot 1 + 2(1 - \delta) = 3 - \delta @@ on the middle piece the least is 1
U = (1 - \delta) + 2\delta \cdot 2 + 2(1 - \delta) = 3 + \delta @@ and the greatest is 2
U - L = 2\delta @@ below any ε, with δ = ε/3
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^2 f(x)\,dx = 3} @@ gap-closes, then exactly-one-between: 3 is between every L and U by every-lower-below-every-upper, as 3 − δ ≤ I ≤ 3 + δ for every δ

L(q, P) = 0,\ \ U(q, P) = 1 \text{ for every } P @@ 15a6
\text{lower integral } 0,\ \ \text{upper integral } 1 @@ the sup of a set of 0s; the inf of a set of 1s
\htmlClass{new}{q \text{ is not integrable on } [0, 1]} @@ 0 ≠ 1

So the squeeze can fail. For 10d's q, any number from 0 to 1, taken as the area under it, breaks none of the three area axioms, and the definition gives it none.

Who said it first

Leibniz, in a manuscript of 29 October 1675, proposed writing a long S in place of the word omnia, so that the sum of all the l's would be written ∫ l.

Leibniz, manuscript “Analysis tetragonistica” (1675); paraphrase

The long S is Gottfried Leibniz’s. The standing question’s two inventors are Isaac Newton (fluxions, 1666) and Leibniz; the Royal Society’s ruling of 1712 is the Commercium Epistolicum.

Accepted names: integrable is also called Riemann integrable, after Bernhard Riemann, and the gap-closes technique is Riemann’s criterion. 10d’s q is Dirichlet’s function.
