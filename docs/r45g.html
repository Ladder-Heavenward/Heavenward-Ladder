45g · proof

A Thing Is Its Arrows

Rung eight made a real number out of the fractions below it, and then everything about the real was read off that set: which fractions it passes, which it does not. A group can be read the same way: a map of groups from the whole numbers into it is fixed by where 1 goes, and 1 can go anywhere, so the maps from the whole numbers are the members. Both are cases of one fact, and it is the fact the rung is named for. Take any thing A of a category, and look at all the arrows into it, from every thing, together with how they change when an arrow is run before them. That collection of collections is a map of categories, and a natural map out of it is fixed by one arrow, the one that does nothing at A. From that one line it follows that two things with matching arrows into them are matched. Nothing about what A is made of is needed. The arrows into it are enough, and they are all there is to know.

The arrows into a thing, from every thing, form a map of categories that turns arrows round. A natural map from it to any such map is fixed by where the do-nothing arrow of the thing goes. If the arrows into two things match naturally, the two are matched: a thing is known by the arrows into it.

45g1The Arrows Into a Thing

Fix a thing A of C. For each thing X let h_A(X) = C(X, A), the set of arrows from X into A. For an arrow f : Y → X, let h_A(f) send u : X → A to u ∘ f : Y → A: run f first. Then h_A(g ∘ f)(u) = u ∘ g ∘ f = h_A(f)(h_A(g)(u)), so h_A is a map of categories from C^op to Set, turning arrows round as 45b3’s readings did. Call it the arrows into A.

Three cases. In Set, the arrows from a set {⋆} of one member into A are the members of A, one for each place ⋆ can go. In Grp, the arrows from ℤ into G are the members of G: ℤ is the free group on one letter, and by 45f1 a map out of it is where the letter goes. In an order, h_y(x) has one member when x ≤ y and none otherwise, so the arrows into y are the things below y. For the line with its order, the fractions below a real α are its cut: 8a built the real as exactly that.

Arrows-from-one technique: in Grp, the product-keeping maps from ℤ into G match with the members of G, by sending each map to where it sends 1.

\mathbb Z = F(\{1\}) @@ the free group on one letter: its reduced words are 1ⁿ, n a whole number (34i1)
\mathbf{Grp}(\mathbb Z, G) \cong \mathbf{Set}(\{1\}, U(G)) @@ the free-forgetful technique (45f1)
\mathbf{Set}(\{1\}, U(G)) \cong U(G) @@ a function from one member is where it sends it
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi \mapsto \varphi(1) \text{ matches } \mathbf{Grp}(\mathbb Z, G) \text{ with the members of } G} \quad \blacksquare @@ and φ(n) = φ(1)ⁿ, by 19b5’s powers

45g2One Arrow Fixes the Rest

Let F be any map of categories from C^op to Set. A natural map α from h_A to F gives, at each X, a function α_X from the arrows X → A to F(X), and its square says α_Y(u ∘ f) = F(f)(α_X(u)) for every f : Y → X.

Arrows-into technique: a natural map from the arrows into A to F is fixed by where it sends the do-nothing arrow of A, and every member of F(A) is where exactly one natural map sends it.

u : X \to A \Rightarrow u = \mathrm{id}_A \circ u = h_A(u)(\mathrm{id}_A) @@ every arrow into A is id_A run after u
\alpha_X(u) = \alpha_X(h_A(u)(\mathrm{id}_A)) = F(u)(\alpha_A(\mathrm{id}_A)) @@ α’s square, for the arrow u : X → A
\alpha \text{ is fixed by } e = \alpha_A(\mathrm{id}_A) \in F(A) @@ the line before, at every X and u
e \in F(A):\ \ \alpha_X(u) = F(u)(e) @@ the other way: define α from any e
\alpha_Y(u \circ f) = F(u \circ f)(e) = F(f)(F(u)(e)) = F(f)(\alpha_X(u)) @@ F turns arrows round: F(u ∘ f) = F(f) ∘ F(u)
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha \leftrightarrow \alpha_A(\mathrm{id}_A) \text{ matches the natural maps } h_A \Rightarrow F \text{ with } F(A)} \quad \blacksquare @@ α_A(id_A) = F(id_A)(e) = e

The proof is five lines, and every line spends only the two rules and the square. Its weight is in the first line: the do-nothing arrow sits inside every collection of arrows into A, and every other arrow is it run after something.

45g3Known by the Arrows Into It

Take F = h_B. A natural map from h_A to h_B is then fixed by an arrow f = α_A(id_A) : A → B, and the formula of 45g2 reads α_X(u) = f ∘ u: run u, then f.

Known-by-arrows technique: if the arrows into A and the arrows into B are naturally matched, A and B are matched, by the arrow the matching sends id_A to.

\alpha : h_A \Rightarrow h_B \text{ a natural matching},\ \ \beta = \alpha^{-1} @@ β is natural (the natural-undo technique, 45c4)
f = \alpha_A(\mathrm{id}_A) : A \to B,\ \ g = \beta_B(\mathrm{id}_B) : B \to A @@ the arrows-into technique, twice
(\beta\alpha)_X(u) = g \circ (f \circ u) @@ α runs f after, β runs g after
\beta\alpha = \text{the do-nothing natural map, which is fixed by } \mathrm{id}_A @@ β is α undone
g \circ f = (\beta\alpha)_A(\mathrm{id}_A) = \mathrm{id}_A,\ \ \text{likewise } f \circ g = \mathrm{id}_B @@ one arrow fixes the natural map, both ways
\htmlClass{new}{A \cong B} \quad \blacksquare @@ f is undone by g (45a3)

Read 45d with it. A product of A and B is a thing P whose arrows into it, from each X, are naturally matched with pairs of arrows X → A, X → B. Two products have naturally matched arrows into them, so they are matched: 45d2’s one-matching technique, now a case of this one. Every universal property says that some thing’s arrows into it, or out of it, are a given collection, and so pins the thing down. In an order, the technique says: if the things below x are the things below y, then x ≤ y and y ≤ x. For the line, two reals with the same fractions below them are the same real, which 8a laid down as the way to read a cut.

The accepted names

h_A is the representable functor Hom(−, A), and a functor matched with one is representable; the arrows-into technique is the Yoneda lemma, and the map A ↦ h_A, which the arrows-into technique shows to be full and faithful, is the Yoneda embedding. Universal properties are representability: a product represents the functor X ↦ C(X, A) × C(X, B). The arrows-from-one technique says ℤ represents the forgetful functor on groups.

Who said it first

Nobuo Yoneda told the lemma to Saunders Mac Lane at the Gare du Nord in Paris in 1954, as Mac Lane recalled; Yoneda published related work on Ext the same year. Alexander Grothendieck made representable functors the language of algebraic geometry from 1960, in the spirit of reading a space by all the maps into it. Richard Dedekind’s cut of 1872 is the oldest case on the ladder of a thing read off the things below it.

A proof that is not allowed

“The arrows into A are matched with the arrows into B, from every X, since both sets have the same size. So A ≅ B.” Not allowed: sizes match sets one at a time. In Set, A = {1, 2} and B = {1, 2, 3} have h_A({⋆}) of size 2 and h_B({⋆}) of size 3, so here they differ; but in Grp, S₃ and ℤ/6 have the same number of maps from ℤ, six each, and are not matched. The technique needs one natural matching, closing every square.
