The Dimension of a Shape

A point, a curve, a surface: dimension seems obvious. For shapes cut out by polynomials it can be defined three ways, and all three agree: the longest chain of whole shapes inside, the number of free coordinates after a good projection, and the number of independent functions. This step defines dimension by chains, finds it by projection, and shows that one equation cuts it by one.

dim X = the largest d with whole shapes X₀ ⊊ X₁ ⊊ ⋯ ⊊ X_d inside X. For a whole X, F[X] is reached from F[y₁, …, y_d] by finitely many roots of polynomials, for d = dim X: a projection onto d-space with finitely many points over each. One equation f ≠ 0 in a whole X of dimension d leaves pieces of dimension d − 1.

45g1Chains of Whole Shapes

\text{point} \subsetneq \text{line} \subsetneq \text{plane} \subsetneq \mathbb C^3 @@ a chain of length 3
\text{prime ideals: } (x, y, z) \supsetneq (x, y) \supsetneq (x) \supsetneq (0) @@ the same chain, reversed (45e1)
\dim \mathbb C^n = n @@ no longer chain; held in trust
\htmlClass{new}{\dim X = \text{the longest chain } X_0 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq X_d \text{ of whole shapes in } X} @@ finite, since chains stop (45e2)

Chain technique: the dimension of a shape is the length of its longest chain of whole shapes, the same as the longest chain of prime ideals in its ring, falling from a largest one; n-space has dimension n.

45g2Finitely Many Over Each Point

(x, y) \mapsto x @@ project to the line
y^2 = 1 - x^2:\ \text{over each } x,\ \text{at most two points} @@ y is a root of a polynomial over F[x]
F[X] = F[x] + F[x]\,y @@ reached from F[x] by one root
\htmlClass{new}{\dim X = 1} @@ one free coordinate

Projection technique: for every whole shape X there are d functions y₁, …, y_d on it, with F[X] reached from F[y₁, …, y_d] by finitely many roots of polynomials; then X projects onto d-space with finitely many points over each, and d = dim X. Held in trust.

45g3One Equation Cuts One Dimension

\text{sphere } x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1:\ \dim 2 @@ project to (x, y)
\text{cut with } z = 0:\ \text{the circle, dim } 1 @@ one less
\text{cut a line with } x = 5:\ \text{a point, dim } 0 @@ one less
\htmlClass{new}{f \ne 0 \text{ on } X,\ V(f) \cap X \ne \varnothing \Rightarrow \text{each piece has dim } d - 1} @@ held in trust

Cut technique: in a whole shape of dimension d, one equation that is neither 0 on it nor never 0 leaves pieces all of dimension d − 1; k equations leave dimension at least d − k.

Accepted names

Dimension by chains is Krull dimension; the projection is Noether normalization; the cut is Krull’s principal ideal theorem. The count of independent functions is the transcendence degree of the field of fractions of F[X].

Who said it first

Wolfgang Krull defined dimension by chains of primes in 1928 and proved the cut that year; Emmy Noether proved the projection in 1926.
