21g · proof

The slopes of sin and cos

Now the circle can say how fast things change as you walk around it. At each moment the height is changing at a rate equal to the across, and the across is changing at a rate equal to minus the height. So sine and cosine chase each other: each one’s rate of change is the other, with a sign. Take the rate of change twice and you get back where you began, turned upside down. That is how a weight on a spring moves, and this step proves that sine and cosine are the only motions that do it.

The rate of change of the sine is the cosine, and the rate of change of the cosine is minus the sine. Anything whose rate of change, taken twice, is minus itself is a mix of the two.

21g1The slope of sin

Slope-of-sin technique: for every θ, the slope of sin at θ is cos θ. Split sin(θ + h) by the sum technique. One piece carries (cos h − 1)/h, which heads to 0. The other carries sin h / h, which heads to 1.

\frac{\sin(\theta + h) - \sin\theta}{h} = \frac{\sin\theta\cos h + \cos\theta\sin h - \sin\theta}{h} @@ the sum technique (21d3)
= \sin\theta \cdot \frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos\theta \cdot \frac{\sin h}{h} @@ regroup
\to \sin\theta \cdot 0 + \cos\theta \cdot 1 @@ the cosine-gap and sine-over-arc techniques (21f)
\htmlClass{new}{\sin'\theta = \cos\theta} \quad \blacksquare

21g2The slope of cos

Slope-of-cos technique: for every θ, the slope of cos at θ is −sin θ. Cos θ is sin(π/2 − θ), and the chain rule brings out a −1.

\cos\theta = \sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) @@ the complement technique (21e3)
\cos'\theta = \sin'(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) \cdot (-1) = \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) \cdot (-1) @@ the chain rule; 21g1
\htmlClass{new}{\cos'\theta = -\sin\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the complement technique again

Both have slopes everywhere, so both are continuous everywhere (13b).

21g3The slope of tan

Slope-of-tan technique: wherever cos θ ≠ 0, the slope of tan at θ is sec² θ. The quotient rule, then the circle technique on top.

\tan'\theta = \frac{\cos\theta \cdot \cos\theta - \sin\theta \cdot (-\sin\theta)}{\cos^2\theta} @@ the quotient rule
= \frac{1}{\cos^2\theta} @@ the circle technique (21c1)

21g4Twice changed, turned over

Swing technique: if f″ = −f everywhere, then f(θ) = f(0) cos θ + f′(0) sin θ. sin and cos both answer f″ = −f. For any answer f, the gap g = f − f(0) cos − f′(0) sin is an answer with g(0) = g′(0) = 0. Its energy g² + g′² has slope 0, so it stays 0.

\sin'' = \cos' = -\sin,\ \ \cos'' = -\sin' = -\cos @@ 21g1, 21g2
g'' = -g,\ \ g(0) = 0,\ \ g'(0) = 0 @@ a difference of answers is an answer
(g^2 + g'^2)' = 2gg' + 2g'g'' = 2gg' - 2g'g = 0 @@ the chain rule, then g″ = −g
g^2 + g'^2 = g(0)^2 + g'(0)^2 = 0 @@ zero slope means flat (14d)
\htmlClass{new}{g = 0,\ \ f = f(0)\cos + f'(0)\sin} \quad \blacksquare @@ squares that add to 0 are each 0

As 17f found for growth, a rule about rates fixes the whole future from two numbers now: where the swing starts, and how fast.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The derivatives of sine and cosine; f″ = −f is the equation of simple harmonic motion, and its answers are the harmonic oscillations. The quantity g² + g′² is the energy of the swing, kept by the motion.

Who said it first

In 21g.4.5, the vibrating-string controversy: Jean d'Alembert (1747), Leonhard Euler (1748) and Daniel Bernoulli (1753), with Lagrange later. It was settled, as far as it was, by Joseph Fourier (1807) and Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet (1829), and it returns on rung 32.
