45c · technique

Shapes Cut Out by Equations

A circle is where x² + y² − 1 is 0; a pair of crossing lines is where xy is 0; the curve (t, t², t³) in space is where y − x² and z − x³ are both 0. Any set of polynomials cuts out a shape: the points where all of them are 0. Shapes and ideals answer each other: an ideal cuts out a shape, and a shape is cut out by the ideal of all polynomials that vanish on it. This step defines both, joins and meets shapes, and finds the shapes of one letter.

V(S) = the points where every f in S is 0; V(S) = V((S)). I(X) = the polynomials that are 0 on X, an ideal. V(fg) = V(f) ∪ V(g); V(I + J) = V(I) ∩ V(J). Bigger ideal, smaller shape. In one letter, the shapes are the finite sets and the whole line.

45c1Zeros of an Ideal

V(S) = \{p : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\} @@ the shape S cuts out
V(S) = V((S)) = V(f_1, \ldots, f_k) @@ the ideal’s members vanish there too; finitely made (45b)
I(X) = \{f : f(p) = 0 \text{ for all } p \in X\} @@ an ideal: sums and multiples of zeros are zeros
\htmlClass{new}{J \subseteq J^{\prime} \Rightarrow V(J) \supseteq V(J^{\prime});\ \ X \subseteq V(I(X))} @@ more equations, fewer points

Zero-shape technique: every set of polynomials cuts out the same shape as the ideal it makes, and so as finitely many polynomials; and every set of points X lies in the shape cut out by I(X), the ideal of what vanishes on it.

45c2Joining and Meeting Shapes

V(f) \cup V(g) = V(fg) @@ fg is 0 exactly where f or g is (44c: no zero products in a field)
V(I) \cup V(J) = V(IJ) @@ products of members
V(I) \cap V(J) = V(I + J) @@ both sets of equations
\htmlClass{new}{V(xy) = \{x\text{-axis}\} \cup \{y\text{-axis}\}} @@ two lines from one equation

Join-meet technique: finite unions and any meets of shapes are shapes; the empty set is V(1) and the whole space is V(0).

45c3Shapes of One Letter

V(I) = V(g),\ \ I = (g) @@ one maker (44c)
g \ne 0:\ V(g) = \text{the roots of } g,\ \text{at most } \deg g @@ a finite set: each root splits off a factor x − r (44c)
g = 0:\ V(g) = \text{the whole line} @@ every point
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shapes in a line: finite sets, and the line}} @@ no interval is a shape

On a line the shapes are the finite sets and the whole line; an interval is no shape, because a polynomial that vanishes on endlessly many points vanishes everywhere.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

A shape cut out by polynomials is an affine algebraic set or affine variety; V is the vanishing locus and I(X) the ideal of X. Shapes as closed sets give the Zariski topology.

Who said it first

René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat described curves by equations in 1637; Oscar Zariski made shapes the closed sets of a topology in 1944.
