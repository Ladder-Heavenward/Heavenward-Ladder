30f · proof

Chance Is Size

The statistics annexes have spoken of chance since rung two: the chance of heads, the chance of a misprint, the chance a forecast is right. They used it carefully, and never said what it is. This step says. A chance is a statement that names a size. The things that might happen make up a set, the events are its sized parts, and the size of the whole is one. Everything the annexes did with chance follows from that: the chance something does not happen, the chance of one thing or another, the chance of one thing given another, and the technique that turns a given chance around. The last of these is how a doctor reads a test result, and this step shows why a good test for a rare condition still raises many false alarms.

A chance space is a set with a sized family of events and a size of total one. From the techniques of size come the chance of not, of or, of one given another, of independent events, and the turned-around chance.

30f1A Chance Space

From here on, a chance space is three things. A set Ω of outcomes: everything that might happen. A family of subsets of Ω called events, which holds Ω and is closed under taking what is left over and under joining countably many. And a size P on the events with P(Ω) = 1, that adds over countably many separate events. P(A) is the chance of A. Its accepted names are probability space and probability. This Ω (capital omega) is not 3a's looped set.

Two examples. A fair die: Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, every subset is an event, and P(A) is the number of faces in A over 6. A dart thrown with no aim within [0, 1]: Ω = [0, 1], the events are the sized sets, and P is the line’s size m. Each single point then has chance 0, yet the dart lands somewhere.

Chance technique: P(not A) = 1 − P(A), and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B).

\Omega = A \cup A^c,\ \text{separate} \Rightarrow 1 = P(A) + P(A^c) @@ countable-adding (30b3), two pieces
A \cup B = A \cup (B \setminus A),\ \ B = (B \setminus A) \cup (A \cap B) @@ separate pieces
P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B \setminus A) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) @@ the size adds on each line
\htmlClass{new}{P(A^c) = 1 - P(A),\ \ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

Not, or and and on events are 1’s not, or and and on claims about the outcome. A ⊆ B gives P(A) ≤ P(B).

30f2Given, and Independent

From here on, for events A and B with P(B) > 0, the chance of A given B is

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B).

It is the chance of A once B is known to have happened: B becomes the whole, and A is measured inside it. Its accepted name is conditional probability.

Given-chance technique: for fixed B with P(B) > 0, A ↦ P(A | B) is a chance on the same events.

P(\Omega \mid B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1 @@ Ω ∩ B = B
A_1, A_2, \ldots \text{ separate} \Rightarrow A_k \cap B \text{ separate} @@ a point in two of them would be in two Aₖ
P\big(\textstyle\bigcup A_k \mid B\big) = \sum P(A_k \cap B)/P(B) = \sum P(A_k \mid B) @@ countable-adding (30b3)
\htmlClass{new}{P(\,\cdot \mid B) \text{ is a chance}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ︿ “chance space”

From here on, A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). When P(B) > 0 this says P(A | B) = P(A): knowing B tells nothing about A. Many events are independent when every finite selection of them multiplies this way.

Independent technique: if A and B are independent, so are A and not B. P(A ∩ Bᶜ) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) = P(A)P(Bᶜ).

30f3The Whole Chance, and Turning It Around

Whole-chance technique: if B₁, B₂, … are separate events of positive chance that fill Ω, then P(A) = Σ P(A | Bₖ) P(Bₖ).

Turn-around technique: if P(A) > 0 and P(B) > 0, then P(B | A) = P(A | B) P(B) / P(A).

A = \bigcup_k (A \cap B_k),\ \text{separate} @@ the Bₖ fill Ω and share no point
P(A) = \sum_k P(A \cap B_k) = \sum_k P(A \mid B_k)P(B_k) @@ countable-adding; given-chance, multiplied out
\htmlClass{new}{P(A) = \textstyle\sum_k P(A \mid B_k)P(B_k)} @@ whole-chance
P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) @@ given-chance, both ways
\htmlClass{new}{P(B \mid A) = P(A \mid B)P(B)/P(A)} \quad \blacksquare @@ turn-around
  
P(\text{has it}) = 0.001,\ \ P(+ \mid \text{has it}) = 0.99,\ \ P(+ \mid \text{does not}) = 0.02 @@ one in a thousand; the test catches 99 in 100; 2 in 100 false alarms
P(+) = 0.99 \cdot 0.001 + 0.02 \cdot 0.999 = 0.02097 @@ whole-chance, with B₁ = has it, B₂ = does not
P(\text{has it} \mid +) = 0.99 \cdot 0.001 / 0.02097 \approx 0.047 @@ turn-around

A positive result raises the chance from 1 in 1000 to about 1 in 21. Most positives are false alarms, because the people without the condition are so many more. The test is good. The condition is rare.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

5e stated laws that hold with a known rate of exceptions. A chance is exactly such a law. “The test raises a false alarm 2 times in 100” is exact, and it is checked against thousands of tests, not against any one.

A chance is a law stated with its exception rate, exact about the many and silent about the one.

No single person is 4.7 percent ill. The 4.7 percent is a claim about everyone who tests positive, kept as exactly as any other measurement, with its exceptions counted in.

A forecast says 30 percent chance of rain in Seattle tomorrow, and it rains. Was the forecast wrong? How many forecasts would you need to check it?

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A chance space is a probability space (Ω, 𝓕, P), the events form a σ-algebra 𝓕, and P is a probability measure. The three demands are Kolmogorov’s axioms. The given chance is conditional probability, the whole-chance technique is the law of total probability, and the turn-around technique is Bayes’ theorem, or Bayes’ rule. Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A) is the base rate fallacy, or the prosecutor’s fallacy in a courtroom.

Who said it first

Gerolamo Cardano, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat counted chances in games of dice between the 1560s and 1654. Thomas Bayes’ essay, published after his death in 1763, turned a given chance around; Pierre-Simon Laplace stated the rule in general in 1774. Andrey Kolmogorov laid chance on measure in 1933, and his axioms are the ones on this page.

A proof that is not allowed

“The test is right 99 times in 100, so a positive result is right 99 times in 100.” Not allowed: 0.99 is P(+ | has it). The question asks for P(has it | +), and turning it around needs P(has it), the rate of the condition, which the claim never used.

Two senses of independent

30f2’s independent is chance independence, not 3f’s independence of a statement from its axioms: it says the chance of both is the product, and no world is built.
