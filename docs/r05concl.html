5·∎ · conclusion

Every subtraction and division has an answer

Among the fractions you can always take one amount away from another, and always divide by anything that is not 0, and each time there is exactly one answer. Dividing by 0 is the one edge, and the rules themselves mark it.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 5f between any two, another; yet holes is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 5 promised numbers in which every subtraction, and every division by a number other than 0, has an answer: not by courtesy, but as an object with a proof that it does the job. 5b built the integers as classes of pairs of numbers and proved the subtraction technique for them. 5c built the fractions as classes of pairs of integers, with no denominator 0, and proved the reciprocal and division techniques.

5d proved the twelve techniques for the fractions, one at a time, on pairs. 5e took the rules alone as its starting point: anything times zero is zero, cancelling, and no number times zero is one. 5f found the fractions crowded and yet holed. This page proves the claim from the twelve techniques, so that no line looks inside a fraction at its pairs.

The claim, in full

Among the fractions, every question “what must be added to y to make x?” has exactly one answer, and so does every question “what must b be multiplied by to make w?”, as long as b is not 0.

Before this rung neither question always had an answer: no number x has 3 + x = 0, and no integer x has 2x = 1.

Let x and y be rationals. Then y + (x − y) = x, and if y + z = x, then z = x − y.

Let w and b be rationals with b ≠ 0. Then b · (w/b) = w, and if bz = w, then z = w/b.

With 0 as the multiplier there is no one answer: 0 · z = 1 has none, and 0 · z = 0 has more than one.

The proof

The first part is why the rung was needed. 5b and 5c proved each gap from the techniques of the rung below.

3 \ne 0 @@ the next-never-empty technique: 3 names S(2)
\neg\exists x \in \mathbb N\;(3 + x = 0) @@ the gap-in-ℕ technique (5b), with 3 for k
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\exists x \in \mathbb Z\;(2x = 1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gap-in-ℤ technique (5c)

The second part is 5b's subtraction column, run on the fractions with the twelve techniques of 5d. Every reason is a rule “on ℚ”, the swap axiom, or a technique proved from them.

\text{let } x, y \in \mathbb Q @@ speak of everything by speaking of anything
y + (x - y) = y + (x + (-y)) @@ ﹀ −
\phantom{y + (x - y)} = y + ((-y) + x) @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ, inside
\phantom{y + (x - y)} = (y + (-y)) + x @@ regroup for plus on ℚ, turned about
\phantom{y + (x - y)} = 0 + x = x + 0 = \htmlClass{new}{x} @@ negative on ℚ; turn-about for plus on ℚ; plus-zero on ℚ: x − y is an answer
\text{assume } y + z = x @@ any answer z at all
(-y) + (y + z) = (-y) + x @@ the swap axiom: the same thing added on the left of each side
((-y) + y) + z = (-y) + x @@ regroup for plus on ℚ, turned about
(y + (-y)) + z = (-y) + x @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ, inside
0 + z = (-y) + x @@ negative on ℚ
\htmlClass{new}{z} = x + (-y) = x - y \quad \blacksquare @@ turn-about for plus on ℚ and plus-zero on ℚ on the left, turn-about for plus on ℚ on the right; ︿ −: the only answer

The integers are among the fractions by the ℤ-inside-ℚ technique, so this covers them too. Where taking away already worked in ℕ, the answer is the old one: if k = b + c, then k − b = c (5b).

The third part is the same shape with multiplying in place of adding, and the reciprocal in place of the negative. The reciprocal exists only because b ≠ 0, and the column says where that is used.

\text{let } a, b \in \mathbb Q,\ \ b \ne 0 @@ speak of everything by speaking of anything
b \cdot (a/b) = b(ab^{-1}) @@ ﹀ the slash: 5c's line for dividing
\phantom{b \cdot (a/b)} = b(b^{-1}a) @@ turn-about for times on ℚ, inside
\phantom{b \cdot (a/b)} = (bb^{-1})a @@ regroup for times on ℚ, turned about
\phantom{b \cdot (a/b)} = 1 \cdot a = a \cdot 1 = \htmlClass{new}{a} @@ reciprocal on ℚ, since b ≠ 0; turn-about for times on ℚ; times-one on ℚ: a/b is an answer
\text{assume } bz = a @@ any answer z at all
bz = b \cdot (a/b) @@ the turn-about technique on b · (a/b) = a, then the chain technique
zb = (a/b)b @@ turn-about for times on ℚ, on each side
\htmlClass{new}{z = a/b} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cancel technique for times on ℚ (5e), since b ≠ 0: the only answer

The last part is the edge. It uses nothing but 5e's techniques, which were proved from the rules alone.

\forall z\;\; 0 \cdot z = 0 @@ the anything-times-zero technique (5e), with turn-about for times on ℚ; speak of everything
\neg\exists c\,(c \cdot 0 = 1) @@ the nothing-times-0-is-1 technique (5e): 0 · z = 1 has no answer
0 \cdot 0 = 0 \land 0 \cdot 1 = 0 @@ use the every of the earlier line, with 0 and with 1; join
1 \ne 0 @@ times-one on ℚ: 1 is not 0
\htmlClass{new}{0 \cdot z = 0 \text{ has two different answers, } 0 \text{ and } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines above

So the condition b ≠ 0 in the claim is not a gap a cleverer list could fill. 5e showed that any patch giving 0 a reciprocal forces 1 = 0, or gives up the spreading rule.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 5g the same change to both sides: Puts the claim to work on questions that arrive wrapped, such as 3x + 5 = 11 − x. Adding anything, and multiplying by anything other than 0, keeps every answer, because each can be undone; multiplying by what might be 0 cannot.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5h fractions by hand, and decimals: Does fractions by hand: lowest terms, one denominator for adding, dividing as multiplying by the reciprocal. A fraction has a finite decimal exactly when its denominator's only primes are 2 and 5.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5i expanding and factoring: Runs the spreading rule both ways. Expanding always works; factoring reads a sum back into the product that made it, and with the zero-product technique it answers questions the claim alone cannot unwrap.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5j ratio, proportion and units: A ratio is a pair that means the same when both parts are scaled, and a proportion is solved by cross-multiplying. Quantities carry units, and changing a unit is multiplying by a fraction that is 1.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·chem how do you count what is too small to see?: Balances a chemical change as whole-number samenesses, one per element, solved together and scaled to the least whole numbers. Then it counts atoms by weighing them, through the mole, with the units carried along.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·bio why do peas come three to one?: Reads chance as a fraction of equally likely cases, and counts the pairs of a cross with the product rule. Out come the pea counts: three to one, and nine to three to three to one.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·stat where is the middle of a list?: The mean shares a total out evenly, a division the claim makes possible. With it come the median, the expected value of a gamble, and the chance of one thing given another, as a fraction.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·cb2 classes of equal pairs: Goes back to 2g's classes with ℤ and ℚ as the examples. They are quotient sets, and what 5b to 5d kept calling honest gets its general name, well defined.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·nav why does a calendar need leap years?: Puts the answers to work on clocks and calendars. Seconds, minutes, hours, days and weeks are places with different bases that repeat out of step, and each leap-year rule is a fraction guessing the year's 365.2422 days, with a drift that division measures.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 5·eng how do gears change speed?: Shows fractions and negatives describing turning machines exactly: teeth times turns is the same on both gears, and two negatives make a positive when a middle gear brings the direction back.its line appears when you finish it.
Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

5e set beside the proofs a rule about real things: it comes with its range, and with the direction it fails past the edge. Water flows downhill until it is a thin film on glass, and then it creeps up the sides.

This claim has such an edge, and it was built rather than decreed. Every division has its one answer except division by 0, and the rules say exactly how it fails there: “0 times what is 1?” has no answer, and “0 times what is 0?” has every answer. An exception of this kind does not weaken the claim; it marks where the claim stops.

Every subtraction, and every division by a number other than 0, now has its one answer. The fractions still have holes, and you are just getting started.
