14c · proof

The mean value technique

In 14b's picture, tilting a function by a straight line broke its equal ends, and the level-ends inputs did not move. At each of them the tilted function’s slope was the tilt, the slope of the tilted chord. Run that backwards. Hand it any function, continuous on a closed interval with a slope inside, and untilt it: subtract the chord through its two ends. What is left has equal ends, so level-ends finds an input inside with slope 0, and there the function's own slope is the chord's slope. Every chord has a tangent line beside it with the same slope. This is the technique rung 14 is built around, because it turns a fact about slopes at single inputs into a fact about two inputs far apart.

Subtract the chord from the function. What is left is zero at both ends. Level-ends gives an input inside where what is left has slope 0. There the function’s own slope is the chord’s slope, so the change across the interval is that slope times the interval’s width.

14c1The technique, every line cited

Average-slope technique (the mean value technique): if f is continuous on [α, β] and has a slope at every input of (α, β), then for some c with α < c < β,

f′(c) = (f(β) − f(α))/(β − α), that is, f(β) − f(α) = f′(c) · (β − α).

Its accepted name is the mean value theorem, since “mean” is an old word for an average and the chord's slope is the average rate of change over [α, β].

m = \tfrac{f(\beta) - f(\alpha)}{\beta - \alpha} @@ the chord's slope (13a); β − α is not 0
\htmlClass{new}{h(x) = f(x) - f(\alpha) - m(x - \alpha)} @@ the helper: f with the chord taken away
h(\alpha) = 0,\ \ h(\beta) = f(\beta) - f(\alpha) - m(\beta - \alpha) = 0 @@ the second by the first line, multiplied out
h \text{ continuous on } [\alpha, \beta] @@ continuous-sums (11b): f less a linear function, side by side at the ends
h'(x) = f'(x) - m \text{ for } \alpha \lt x \lt \beta @@ slopes-add, slope-of-a-line, constant-has-slope-0: the chord part has slope m
\text{call } c \in (\alpha, \beta):\ h'(c) = 0 @@ level-ends (14b)
f'(c) = m \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above, with h′(c) = f′(c) − m

Where each hypothesis was spent: all three in level-ends, which needs them for h. h inherits them from f because a chord is continuous and has slope m everywhere. Rung 7's either-way principle and no-holes: inside level-ends, in highest-and-lowest, and nowhere new. Like level-ends, the technique names no c.

x(t) = t^3 \text{ on } [0, 2] @@ metres and seconds; a position given as an example
m = \tfrac{8 - 0}{2} = 4 @@ the chord slope: on average, 4 metres each second
3c^2 = 4,\ \ c = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \approx 1.15 @@ the slope 3c² (power for slopes) meets the chord slope; average-slope says such a c had to be there

The second form, f(β) − f(α) = f′(c)(β − α), is the one used most. It says the change over a stretch is a slope times the length of the stretch, for some slope the function really has inside. Know something about every slope inside, and you know the same thing about the change.

14c2A bound on the slope bounds the change

Slope-bound technique: suppose f is continuous on [α, β], has a slope at every input of (α, β), and |f′(x)| ≤ K for every x in (α, β). Then |f(y) − f(x)| ≤ K · |y − x| for every x and y in [α, β]. Take x < y. f is continuous on [x, y] with a slope inside, so average-slope gives c between them with f(y) − f(x) = f′(c)(y − x). Take sizes (size-of-a-product, 8h), and |f′(c)| ≤ K. For x = y both sides are 0. ∎ The one-sided forms are proved the same way. f′ ≤ K inside gives f(y) − f(x) ≤ K(y − x) for x < y. And f′ ≥ k gives the change at least k(y − x).

f(x) = \sqrt{x} \text{ on } [100, 101] @@ continuous (root-is-continuous, 11a), slope 1/(2√x) for x > 0 (root-slope, 13h)
\sqrt{101} - 10 = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt c},\ \ 100 \lt c \lt 101 @@ average-slope
10 \lt \sqrt c \lt \sqrt{101} \lt 11 @@ powers-keep-order (8f) turned back: √c ≥ 11 would give c ≥ 121
\tfrac{1}{22} \lt \sqrt{101} - 10 \lt \tfrac{1}{20} @@ reciprocals turn the order about (positive-reciprocal on ℚ read in ℝ)
\htmlClass{new}{10.045 \lt \sqrt{101} \lt 10.05} @@ 1/22 ≈ 0.0455, so the left is at least 10.045

Two bounds from one technique, without a single square root worked out. 12e's bisection, starting from [10, 11], would take eight halvings to get as close.

0 \lt x \lt y:\ \ y^n - x^n = n c^{n-1}(y - x) @@ average-slope on [x, y] for xⁿ, power for slopes; x < c < y
n x^{n-1}(y - x) \lt y^n - x^n \lt n y^{n-1}(y - x) @@ c^(n−1) lies between x^(n−1) and y^(n−1) when n ≥ 2 (powers-keep-order, 8f)

13h's difference-of-powers wrote yⁿ − xⁿ as (y − x) times a sum of n terms, each between x^(n−1) and y^(n−1). Average-slope says the sum is n times one power c^(n−1). Both roads give the squeeze.

14c3The average speed is a speed

From here on, for a position x(t) with a slope at the instant t, its velocity there is that slope, v(t) = x′(t). Its average velocity over [t₁, t₂] is the chord slope (x(t₂) − x(t₁))/(t₂ − t₁). Speed is the size of the velocity, |v(t)|.

A natural premise about a body whose speed only grows says this: it covers a stretch of time at an average velocity between its speeds at the start and the end. 9·phys took it as a premise. 13·phys held it in trust for every such motion, and held in trust that some instant has the average velocity. Here both are proved, and those flags are closed. Let x(t) be a position with a velocity v(t) at every instant of [t₁, t₂], so x is continuous there (slope-means-no-gap). Average-slope gives an instant c strictly inside with

(x(t₂) − x(t₁))/(t₂ − t₁) = v(c), for some t₁ < c < t₂.

If the speed only grows, in 13l's words if v is increasing, then t₁ < c < t₂ gives v(t₁) < v(c) < v(t₂). The premise is true for every such motion. For steady gain of speed it is true with c the halfway instant. Take x(t) = ½gt² + v₀t with g > 0, so v(t) = gt + v₀ (polynomial-slope). Its average velocity over [t₁, t₂] is ½g(t₁ + t₂) + v₀, which is v at (t₁ + t₂)/2.

14c4The tangent step, for any function

13m proved a bound for the tangent step N(x) = x − f(x)/f′(x), near a zero r where the slope is not 0. The error after the step is at most a constant times the square of the old error, so the correct places roughly double. Its proof factored a polynomial at r, by factor, and said that a function which is not a polynomial would have to wait. Average-slope replaces the factoring.

Places-double-for-any-function technique: suppose f has a slope at every input of [r − d, r + d], and f′ has a slope at every input of it too. Suppose f(r) = 0, and there are bounds |f″(x)| ≤ C and |f′(x)| ≥ m > 0 there (m a bound, not a slope). Put K = C/m. If |x − r| ≤ d, then K · |N(x) − r| ≤ (K · |x − r|)². So 13m's places-double can be used as written, with C bounding f″ in place of q′.

From here on, ξ (the Greek letter xi) and η (eta) may name an input that a technique promises without saying which, as c does in average-slope. They are only letters, like c, and claim nothing.

f(x) = f(x) - f(r) = f'(\xi)(x - r) @@ average-slope on the stretch from r to x, ξ strictly between (for x = r all is 0)
N(x) - r = (x - r) - \tfrac{f'(\xi)(x - r)}{f'(x)} @@ the tangent step, with f(x) replaced
= (x - r) \cdot \tfrac{f'(x) - f'(\xi)}{f'(x)} @@ one bottom (fractions-of-rationals read in ℝ)
f'(x) - f'(\xi) = f''(\eta)(x - \xi) @@ average-slope again, for f′, which is continuous by slope-means-no-gap; η between ξ and x
|x - \xi| \le |x - r| @@ ξ is between r and x
|N(x) - r| \le |x - r|^2 \cdot \tfrac{C}{m} @@ the bounds on |f″| and |f′|, size-of-a-product (8h)
K\,|N(x) - r| \le (K\,|x - r|)^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply by K = C/m

Every step of 13m that came after this bound goes through unchanged. That includes the condition K · |x₀ − r| ≤ ½, which keeps each guess inside [r − d, r + d]. That closes what 13m and 13·comp held in trust for functions that are not polynomials.

One builder of the calculus worked from series, without limits. He proved that the change of a function over a stretch lies between the least and the greatest of its slopes on the stretch, times the length. He used it to bound the error of breaking off a series.

14c6Running it backwards

Handed two values of a function and asked about some slope between, divide the change by the step. Average-slope says some slope inside is exactly that. Handed bounds on every slope and asked how far a value can move, multiply the bound by the step (slope-bound). Handed a number hard to compute, find a function and a stretch where it is a change and the slopes are easy, and pin it between two bounds. Handed a proof that needs a factoring, look for an average-slope that replaces it. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Lagrange, building the calculus from series without limits, proved that the change of a function over a stretch lies between the least and the greatest of its slopes on the stretch, times the length. He used it to bound the error of breaking off a series.

Lagrange, Théorie des fonctions analytiques (1797); paraphrase

Some books call the mean value technique Lagrange’s theorem. The tangent step is Newton’s method (or the Newton–Raphson method). The standing question’s quarrel is Giuseppe Peano’s with Philippe Gilbert, in 1884.

Accepted names: the average-slope technique is the mean value theorem; the chord’s slope is the average rate of change.
