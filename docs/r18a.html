18a · proof

Points, Distance, the Displacement Pattern

Rung 9 drew pairs of reals as dots and said no shape was meant. This step gives the pairs a distance, and the shapes come with it. The distance from one pair to another is built from two of rung 5's displacement patterns, one for each coordinate. Then a second definition, a product of two displacements, says when they are perpendicular, and the right-triangle technique is a line of algebra.

The distance between two points is the square root of the square of their horizontal gap plus the square of their vertical gap. The dot of two vectors multiplies their horizontal parts and their vertical parts and adds. Two vectors are perpendicular when their dot is zero. The square of the length of a sum of vectors is the sum of their squared lengths plus twice their dot, so the squares add exactly when the vectors are perpendicular. The point a given fraction of the way from one point to another is the first plus that fraction of the vector between them.

18a1Points and Displacements

From here on, a point is a name for a pair (x, y) of reals, and the plane is ℝ × ℝ, written ℝ². For points P = (x₁, y₁) and Q = (x₂, y₂), the displacement from P to Q is the pair Q − P = (x₂ − x₁, y₂ − y₁). Its accepted name is a vector.

Displacements add and scale coordinate by coordinate: u + v = (u₁ + v₁, u₂ + v₂), tu = (tu₁, tu₂), and P + u is the point reached from P by u. So (Q − P) + (R − Q) = R − P: going from P to Q and then from Q to R is going from P to R. A displacement whose second coordinate is 0 has the accepted name horizontal, and one whose first is 0 vertical. These are 9e's row and column, now with their geometric names.

18a2Distance

From here on, the length of a displacement u is |u| = √(u₁² + u₂²), and the distance from P to Q is d(P, Q) = |Q − P| = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²). Its accepted name is the distance formula. d is an operator: carried out on two points, it produces one number, their distance. |u| is a name, for one number.

Under the root are two displacement patterns, (x − h)² with h = x₁ and with h = y₁. The root exists by 8f's square-root, since a sum of squares is not negative (square-never-negative).

Distance-laws technique: for all points P, Q and displacements u, and every real t: d(P, Q) ≥ 0, with d(P, Q) = 0 only when P = Q; d(P, Q) = d(Q, P); and |tu| = |t| |u|.

18a3Perpendicular

From here on, u · v = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ for displacements u and v. Its accepted name is the dot product. u and v are perpendicular, u ⊥ v, when u · v = 0. The mark ⊥ is not new: since 1b it has named the totally false. From here on it does two jobs, and where it stands tells them apart. Between two names, as in u ⊥ v, it is a statement that the two are perpendicular. Standing alone on a line of an argument, it is still the totally false. The two jobs are tied: 21c1 and 27i2 show how. Three points P, Q, R make a right angle at Q when (P − Q) · (R − Q) = 0. The dot is an operator: carried out on two displacements, it produces a number. u ⊥ v is a statement.

(1, 0) · (0, 1) = 0, so horizontal and vertical are perpendicular. The techniques of the dot product are rung 4's techniques of + and ·, coordinate by coordinate: u · v = v · u, u · (v + w) = u · v + u · w, (tu) · v = t(u · v), and u · u = |u|². Together they are the dot technique.

18a4The Right Triangle

Right-triangle technique: for all displacements u and v, |u + v|² = |u|² + |v|² + 2u · v. So |u + v|² = |u|² + |v|² exactly when u ⊥ v. In a triangle with a right angle at C, the squares of the two sides that meet at C add to the square of the side across from it; and if they add so, the angle at C is right.

The word “square” now has three jobs, and they are related. Rung 4 made it a number times itself, x². A square, the shape, has four equal sides meeting at right angles, and its area is its side squared. And a carpenter's square is a tool for marking a right angle, the meeting this step defines by u · v = 0. They are one idea seen three ways. |u|² is u · u, a number squared. It is the area of the square built on u. And that square's corners are right angles, square in the carpenter's sense. From here on “square” may mean any of the three, and the statement says which.

18a5Dividing a Segment

Part-of-the-way technique: for points P ≠ Q and 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, the point X = P + t(Q − P) has d(P, X) = t · d(P, Q) and d(X, Q) = (1 − t) · d(P, Q). With t = ½ it is the midpoint ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2); with t = m/(m + n) it divides the way from P to Q in the ratio m : n.

18a6Squares, as Shapes

The points (0, 0), (s, 0), (s, s) and (0, s), for s > 0, have four sides of length s and a right angle at each corner (the dot technique). That figure has the accepted name a square, and 5f's name for a number times itself is its area. Square's-area technique: the region 0 ≤ x ≤ s, 0 ≤ y ≤ s has area ∫₀ˢ s dx = s², and the box with edges s has volume s³ (16j's prism); a rectangle with sides p and q along the axes has area pq. The diagonal of the square has length √(s² + s²) = s√2, by right-triangle. So 5f's square and 13g's cube are now shapes, and their names were not chosen by accident.

18a7Points in Space

16j laid down a point of space as a triple (x, y, z). The plane’s distance carries up into space by being used twice. To go from P = (x₁, y₁, z₁) to Q = (x₂, y₂, z₂), first cross the floor to R = (x₂, y₂, z₁), then climb straight up to Q. Across the floor, right-triangle gives d(P, R)² = (x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)².

The climb from R to Q changes only the height, and the walk from P to R changes everything but the height. So the two displacements meet at a right angle, and right-triangle, used a second time on the triangle P, R, Q, gives d(P, Q)² = d(P, R)² + (z₂ − z₁)².

From here on, the distance between points of space is d(P, Q) = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² + (z₂ − z₁)²). Distance in space is the plane’s distance used twice. Its laws are distance-laws, with one more displacement pattern under the root.

From here on, the sphere with centre C and radius r > 0 is the set of points of space at distance r from C. With C = (a, b, c), a point (x, y, z) is on it exactly when (x − a)² + (y − b)² + (z − c)² = r². A ball, or the shell of a planet, is drawn with one line.

Who said it first

The method of coordinates is René Descartes's, set out in La Géométrie of 1637. In 18a.4.6, the French philosopher is Descartes, the English mathematician who wrote the Principia is Isaac Newton, and the figures it imitates are those of Euclid's Elements.

Descartes begins his Geometry by saying that any problem in geometry can be brought to such terms that, to solve it, one needs only to know the lengths of certain straight lines.

René Descartes, La Géométrie (1637); paraphrase

Accepted names: the plane with these coordinates is the Cartesian plane; the distance of 18a2 is the Euclidean distance; the right-triangle technique is Pythagoras' theorem; the clay tablet from Babylon is called Plimpton 322.
