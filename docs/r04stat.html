What Can Counting Alone Say About a List?

What can counting alone say about a list? A list of measurements, such as how many days it rained each week, or how many birds were counted at each stop, is called data. Before there are fractions, three things can be said about data by counting alone. You can say which value comes up most often, how far apart the largest and smallest are, and how many cases there are of each kind. Chance starts the same way, as counting: of the cases that could happen, how many give what you are waiting for.

The tally of a value is how many places in the list hold it, and the tallies add up to the length of the list. A mode is a value with the largest tally. The range is the gap from the least value up to the greatest. When the cases are equally likely, the chance of an event is how many of the cases give it, out of how many cases there are. Of all the ways a handful of coins can fall, the number that show a given count of heads is one of 4k’s choosing numbers.

Data and tallies

From here on, data is a list of numbers x₀, x₁, …, x₍ₙ₋₁₎ of length n: a function from n to ℕ, which is 4i's list with its places counted from 0 instead of from 1. Each xᵢ is a value of the data. For a number v, the tally of v is the number of members of {i ∈ n : xᵢ = v}, the places that hold v. That set is a settled part of n, and a settled part of n has a number of members, by induction on n. The part of 0 is ∅, with 0 members. The part of S(n) is the part of n, with n brought in or not, as the settled statement at n says (bring-one-in, since n is not in n). One-count makes that number the only one.

A tally was once kept with strokes, four upright and the fifth across them, so that the strokes could be counted in fives. Its accepted name is in the cellar. A table of the values with their tallies is a tally table.

Tallies-add-up technique: if the values that appear in a list of length n are v₀, …, v₍ₘ₋₁₎, all different, then the sum of their tallies is n.

n = \{i \in n : x_i = v_0\} \cup \dots \cup \{i \in n : x_i = v_{m-1}\} @@ each place holds some value, and each value is one of the vⱼ
\text{the piles share no place} @@ a place holds one value, and the vⱼ are different
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{j \lt m} (\text{tally of } v_j) = n} \quad \blacksquare @@ separate-piles, once for each pile, by induction on m (induction, sum-one-more)

The greatest, the least, and the gap between

Least-member gave every settled set of numbers that has a member a least member. The greatest needs its own proof, since ℕ itself has no greatest member. 4k4 proved it as greatest-member: every finite set of numbers with at least one member has a greatest member. This page cites it by that name.

From here on, for numbers a ≤ b, b − a names the one number d with a + d = b. Less-is-adding gives such a d when a < b, d = 0 serves when a = b, and cancel for plus makes it the only one. Read it “b minus a”. Rung 5 will give every subtraction an answer; here only the ones with a ≤ b are allowed.

For data with at least one value, its least value and its greatest value exist, by least-member and greatest-member. The range of the data is greatest − least. A mode of the data is a value whose tally is at least the tally of every value. Mode-exists technique: data with at least one value has at least one mode, and it may have more than one. The tallies of the values that appear make a finite set of numbers with a member, so greatest-member gives the greatest tally, and any value with that tally is a mode. The list 1, 1, 2, 2 has two modes. Mode is French for the fashion: the value most often worn.

3, 5, 2, 5, 6, 0, 5, 3 @@ eight weeks of rain days at Eureka, as one student logged them
\text{tallies: } 0{:}\,1,\ 2{:}\,1,\ 3{:}\,2,\ 5{:}\,3,\ 6{:}\,1 @@ they add to 8 (tallies-add-up)
\text{mode } 5;\ \ \text{range } 6 - 0 = 6 @@ mode-exists; greatest-member and least-member

Counting by two questions at once

When each case is sorted by two questions, the tallies go into a grid. It has a row for each answer to the first question and a column for each answer to the second. Call it a two-way table. Two-ways-to-the-total technique: in a two-way table of counts, the row totals and the column totals add to the same number, the number of cases. Each way splits the cases into separate piles, so each adds to the number of cases by separate-piles.

\begin{array}{l|cc|c} & \text{bruised} & \text{sound} & \\ \hline \text{Gravenstein} & 7 & 29 & 36 \\ \text{other} & 4 & 20 & 24 \\ \hline & 11 & 49 & 60 \end{array} @@ 60 apples from an orchard near Sebastopol: 36 + 24 = 60 = 11 + 49
Anexact, yet rigorous · counting

4k found that random things in a crowd pile into a steady shape, and that the spread is part of the shape. A tally table is that pile, written down. Eight weeks of rain days at Eureka make a pile with its peak at 5, the mode, and a width of 6, the range. Neither number says what next week will bring. Both say something about the weeks as a crowd.

The spread is part of the answer, not noise around it.

The range is the crudest width there is: one very wet week or one dry one sets it alone. It is still a width. A report of rain days without one says less than it seems to.

The next year the same student logs eight more weeks at Eureka, and the mode is 2. Did Eureka's weather change?

Chance by counting

From here on, when a set of n cases is taken to be equally likely, and k of them give an event, the chance of the event is k of n. Its accepted name is probability, from the Latin probabilis, “likely”. 5·stat will write it as a fraction.

Which cases are equally likely is not proved. It is a choice about the world, and the counting is only as good as that choice. Two dice, one red and one blue, fall in 6 · 6 = 36 ways by stages-multiply. If each way is taken as equally likely, a total of 7 comes in 6 of 36 and a total of 2 in 1 of 36.

Count-of-successes technique: of the 2ⁿ ways n coins can fall, heads or tails, exactly (n choose k) show k heads, and the counts for k = 0, 1, …, n add to 2ⁿ. A way of falling is a function from n to {heads, tails}, so there are 2ⁿ by counted-functions. Match each way with its set of places that show heads. It is a k-member part of n exactly when the way shows k heads, and each part comes from one way, so choosing-counts-parts counts them. The sum is two-term-power with both numbers 1.

n = 4:\ \ 1,\ 4,\ 6,\ 4,\ 1 @@ row 4 of 4k's table; they add to 16 = 2⁴
\text{two heads in four tosses: } 6 \text{ of } 16 @@ count-of-successes, if each of the 16 ways is equally likely
\text{three dice, total } 10:\ 27 \text{ of } 216;\ \ \text{total } 9:\ 25 \text{ of } 216 @@ count ordered triples, each die told apart

Gamblers say a total of 9 and a total of 10 with three dice should come equally often, since each can be made in six ways from three numbers. Yet long play shows 10 more often. The ways must be counted with each die told apart: 10 then has 27 and 9 has 25.

Who said it first

The gamblers' question was answered by Galileo. The great mathematician of 4·stat.4.5 is Jean d'Alembert, writing in 1754.

Gamblers say a total of 9 and a total of 10 with three dice should come equally often, since each can be made in six ways from three numbers. Yet long play shows 10 more often. The ways must be counted with each die told apart: 10 then has 27 and 9 has 25.

Galileo Galilei, Sopra le scoperte dei dadi (about 1620); paraphrase

Accepted names

A tally is a frequency, and a tally table is a frequency table. The chance of an event, k of n, is its probability.
