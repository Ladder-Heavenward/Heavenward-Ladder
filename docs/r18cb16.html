18·cb16 · newsflash for 16j

A cone, cut by a plane

16j cut solids with flat walls and looked at what each wall cut out. 18e built the ellipse, the parabola and the hyperbola from distances in a flat plane, with no cone anywhere. Here the two meet. Stand two cones point to point, cut them with one tilted plane, and the cut is one of 18e’s curves. Which one depends only on the tilt.

The double cone is two cones tip to tip, every point as far from the axis as from the tips’ level. Cut it with a tilted plane that misses the tip. A plane less steep than the cone’s side cuts an ellipse, a circle when it is level. A plane exactly as steep cuts a parabola. A steeper plane cuts both cones, and the cut is a hyperbola.

18·cb16.1Measuring inside a tilted plane

Stands on 16j1 (points of space) and 18a (distance used twice).

From here on, the double cone is the set of points (x, y, z) with x² + y² = z². Its two halves, z ≥ 0 and z ≤ 0, meet at the tip (0, 0, 0). Its side climbs one unit for each unit out from the z-axis.

The points of the plane z = mx + c are (x, y, mx + c). Seen from straight above, each sits over its shadow (x, y). Shadow technique: distances inside the plane are the shadow’s distances, stretched along x by √(1 + m²). So with u = x√(1 + m²), the plane is measured by (u, y) exactly as the floor is measured by (x, y).

P = (x_1, y_1, mx_1 + c),\ \ Q = (x_2, y_2, mx_2 + c) @@ two points of the plane
|P - Q|^2 = (\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 + m^2(\Delta x)^2 @@ distance in space is the plane’s used twice (18a)
= (1 + m^2)(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 @@ collect
\htmlClass{new}{|P - Q|^2 = (\Delta u)^2 + (\Delta y)^2,\ \ u = x\sqrt{1 + m^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stretch is the same everywhere in the plane

18·cb16.2The tilt decides the curve

Stands on 18·cb16.1 and 18h3 (sorting).

Cone-cut technique: the plane z = mx + c, with c ≠ 0, cuts the double cone in an ellipse when |m| < 1, a parabola when |m| = 1, and a hyperbola when |m| > 1. When m = 0 the ellipse is a circle of radius |c|.

x^2 + y^2 = (mx + c)^2 @@ a point on both: put the plane’s z into the cone
(1 - m^2)x^2 - 2mcx + y^2 - c^2 = 0 @@ multiply out and collect
\tfrac{1 - m^2}{1 + m^2}\,u^2 - \tfrac{2mc}{\sqrt{1 + m^2}}\,u + y^2 - c^2 = 0 @@ in the plane’s own measure: x = u/√(1 + m²) (18·cb16.1)
\text{the } u^2 \text{ term has the sign of } 1 - m^2,\ \text{the } y^2 \text{ term is } + @@ 1 + m² is above 0, so dividing by it keeps signs
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same signs: ellipse; } u^2 \text{ term } 0: \text{ parabola; opposite: hyperbola}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the completed-squares technique (18h3); c ≠ 0 keeps the curve from shrinking to a point or a pair of lines

So the tilt alone sorts the cut. Tip the plane past the slope of the cone’s side and the ellipse opens into a parabola, then splits into the two branches of a hyperbola, one on each half of the cone.

18·cb16.3Three tilts, worked

Stands on 18·cb16.2 and 8i (completing the square).

m = \tfrac12,\ c = 1:\ \ \tfrac34x^2 - x + y^2 = 1 @@ the cone-cut line, before the stretch
\tfrac34\big(x - \tfrac23\big)^2 + y^2 = \tfrac43 @@ completing the square (8i): an ellipse with centre (2/3, 0)
m = 1,\ c = 1:\ \ y^2 = 2x + 1 @@ the x² terms cancel: a parabola, opening along x
m = 2,\ c = 3:\ \ -3x^2 - 12x + y^2 = 9 @@ the cone-cut line
y^2 - 3(x + 2)^2 = -3 @@ completing the square: a hyperbola, crossing y = 0 at x = −1 and x = −3
\htmlClass{new}{(-1, 0, 1),\ \ (-3, 0, -3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ one point on each half of the cone: x² = z² both times

The stretch along x changes the size of each curve but never its kind. A circle seen in the shadow of a tilted plane is an ellipse; the cut itself is the true curve, measured by (u, y).

The accepted names

The curves cut from a cone by a plane are the conic sections. The double cone is a right circular cone with two nappes. The balls of 18·cb16.4.5 are Dandelin spheres.

Who said it first

Menaechmus is said to have found these curves as cuts of a cone in the fourth century BCE. Apollonius of Perga wrote the eight books of the Conics around 200 BCE and gave them the names ellipse, parabola and hyperbola. In 18·cb16.4.5, the geometer is Germinal Pierre Dandelin (1822).
