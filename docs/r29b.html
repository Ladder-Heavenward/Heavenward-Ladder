29b · proof

Triangles on a sphere

A pilot flying from Vancouver to Tokyo does not head straight west, though Tokyo lies a little south of Vancouver’s latitude. The route arcs up past Alaska. On the surface of the earth, the straight lines are the great circles, the circles whose centre is the earth’s centre. This step treats a sphere as a world of its own, with great circles as its lines, and draws triangles there. It finds that any two lines meet, that a triangle’s angles add to more than a straight angle, and that the amount by which they overshoot is exactly the triangle’s area.

On a sphere of radius R, two great circles always meet, and a triangle made of great circles has area equal to R² times the amount by which its angles add to more than π.

29b1Great circles are the lines

Let the sphere have centre O and radius R. A plane through O cuts it in a circle of radius R, a great circle. Take the great circles as the lines of the sphere. 29g will show they are its shortest paths; for now they are its lines by choice. The angle between two great circles where they cross is the angle between their planes, which is the angle between the planes’ normals.

Lines-always-meet technique: two different great circles meet in exactly two points, and the two are opposite each other.

n_1 \cdot x = 0,\ \ n_2 \cdot x = 0 @@ the two planes through O, with normals n₁, n₂ that are not multiples
u = n_1 \times n_2 \ne 0 @@ cross technique (27i): zero only for multiples
x \text{ is on both planes} \iff x = t\,u \text{ for some } t @@ u is at right angles to both normals, and the planes meet in a line through O
|t\,u| = R \iff t = \pm R/|u| @@ the points of that line on the sphere
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the circles meet at } \pm R\,u/|u|,\ \text{two opposite points}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line through O meets the sphere twice

So the sphere has no lines that never meet. And two opposite points, like the poles, lie on endlessly many great circles. The sphere breaks the first of the other axioms of 29a. It is a different geometry, not a counterexample inside the old one.

29b2A slice between two great circles

Two great circles through the poles cut out a slice, like a segment of an orange, with angle α at each pole. Call it a lune.

Lune-area technique: a lune of angle α on a sphere of radius R has area 2αR².

\text{a turn about the polar axis keeps area} @@ a turn is a straight map of space with determinant 1 (28i volume-scale, surfaces by 28n)
n \text{ lunes of angle } 2\pi/n \text{ fill the sphere without overlap} @@ turn one lune by 2π/n, n times
\text{each has area } 4\pi R^2 / n @@ skin technique (21o): the whole sphere is 4πR²
\text{a lune of angle } 2\pi m/n \text{ has area } 4\pi R^2 \cdot m/n @@ m of them side by side
\alpha \text{ between two such angles: its area is between theirs} @@ inside-no-more (15a): a lune inside another has no more area
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = \tfrac{\alpha}{2\pi} \cdot 4\pi R^2 = 2\alpha R^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ fractions m/n squeeze α/2π from both sides

29b3Area is the excess

Draw a triangle ABC on the sphere, with sides on great circles and angles α, β, γ. Each pair of sides, carried on round the sphere, makes a lune at A and the same lune again at the opposite point. The three pairs of lunes together cover the whole sphere. They cover the triangle three times, and the opposite triangle three times, and every other point exactly once.

Excess-area technique: a triangle on a sphere of radius R has area (α + β + γ − π)R². The number α + β + γ − π is its excess.

2\cdot 2\alpha R^2 + 2\cdot 2\beta R^2 + 2\cdot 2\gamma R^2 @@ the three pairs of lunes (lune-area, 29b2)
= 4\pi R^2 + 2\,|T| + 2\,|T'| @@ the sphere once, plus T and its opposite T′ twice more each
|T'| = |T| @@ the map x ↦ −x keeps area and sends T to T′
4R^2(\alpha + \beta + \gamma) = 4\pi R^2 + 4|T| @@ the two lines above
\htmlClass{new}{|T| = (\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi)R^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by 4, take πR² across

Since an area is more than 0, every triangle on a sphere has angles adding to more than π. The blunt case of 29a, refuted there, lives here, because the sphere breaks one of the other axioms.

29b4Same angles, same size

In ℝ², a photograph enlarged is a similar triangle: same angles, bigger sides (18f). On a sphere that cannot happen.

Same-angles-same-size technique: on a sphere, two triangles with the same three angles have the same area, and a small triangle has a small excess.

|T_1| = (\alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi)R^2 = |T_2| @@ excess-area, the same angles in both
|T| \to 0 \Rightarrow \alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi \to 0 @@ excess-area, R fixed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no enlarging keeps the angles; small triangles are nearly flat}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

So on a sphere the angles carry a length scale. Measure the angles of a large enough triangle and you can find R. A surveyor who measured a triangle of mountain tops and found its excess would be measuring the earth’s curvature. 29e asks whether that can be done without leaving the surface at all.

The accepted names

The excess-area technique is Girard’s theorem; α + β + γ − π is the spherical excess. A lune is also called a digon or a spherical biangle. Geometry on the sphere is spherical geometry; with each pair of opposite points counted as one point, so that two points lie on exactly one line, it is elliptic geometry.

Who said it first

Menelaus of Alexandria wrote on spherical triangles in his Sphaerica, around 100 CE, and knew their angles add to more than two right angles. Thomas Harriot found the area as the excess in 1603 but did not publish it; Albert Girard published it in 1629. Carl Friedrich Gauss, running the survey of Hanover from 1818 to 1832, measured the triangle of three mountain tops, Brocken, Hohehagen and Inselsberg, with sides up to about 107 km. Whether he meant it as a test of the geometry of space is still argued.
