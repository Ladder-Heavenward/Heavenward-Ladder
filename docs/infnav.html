What Can a Navigator Leave Out?

What can a navigator leave out? Three navigation annexes, from rung 20 to rung 29, each worked with every number in hand: the runs of a voyage, the formula of a map, the coordinates of a route. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. The facing of a voyage is worked out from the facings of its orders, and the runs can be left out; the place cannot be worked out that way. A map that keeps sizes, followed by a squeeze that keeps sizes, keeps sizes, so one such map gives a family with no new formula. And a route is a row of legs: a place lies on a shortest route exactly when the detour by way of it costs nothing.

facing(g ∘ h) = facing(g) · facing(h); no such rule for place. Stretches of a row multiply: the size map squeezed c across and 1/c up has stretches c sec φ and cos φ/c, both 1 at φ₀ when c = cos φ₀. d(P, Q) = d(P, X) + d(X, Q) exactly when X lies on a shortest route from P to Q.

infnav.1The Facing Leaves the Runs Out

20·nav3 composed the orders of a voyage and found that its facing is the product of the turns, whatever the runs. Read that as a statement about two ways of summing up a pose g: its facing, the turn Rg, and its place, the slide sg.

Facing-arrow technique: facing(g ∘ h) = facing(g) · facing(h): the facing of a row is worked out from the facings of its parts. No rule works out the place of a row from the places of its parts. And a voyage whose turns cancel is a slide.

R_{g \circ h} = R_g R_h @@ the voyage technique (20·nav3)
g = \text{do nothing},\quad g' = \text{starboard } 90:\quad s_g = s_{g'} = (0, 0) @@ two poses with one place
h = \text{ahead } 2:\quad s_{g \circ h} = (0, 2),\quad s_{g' \circ h} = (2, 0) @@ the same places in, and two places out: no rule
\htmlClass{new}{R_g = \text{do-nothing} \ \Rightarrow\ g(P) = P + s_g} \quad \blacksquare @@ a pose is a turn and then a slide (20·nav2)
  
\text{starboard } 90,\ \text{port } 45,\ \text{starboard } 135,\ \text{runs lost}:\quad \text{facing } 180^\circ @@ the heading-sum technique (20·nav1), with no run read
\htmlClass{new}{\text{starboard } 90,\ \text{ahead } 1,\ \text{port } 90,\ \text{astern } 1:\quad \text{a slide by } (1, -1)} @@ the turns cancel (20·nav4); the place needs every order, in order

What hides is the arrangement: the runs. A log with its runs lost still gives the heading. A log with its turns lost gives nothing, since each run is turned by the facing at that moment.

infnav.2A Map After a Map

22·nav4 found the straight-grid map that keeps sizes, y = R sin φ, and showed that it keeps shapes only along the equator. A map is an arrow from the ground to the paper. Follow it by an arrow from the paper to the paper, and the row is again a map. What both keep, the row keeps.

Map-row technique: along a row of maps the stretches multiply. So the size map followed by a squeeze, c across and 1/c up, keeps sizes, and with c = cos φ₀ it keeps shapes along latitude φ₀.

\text{the size map at latitude } \varphi:\quad \text{across by } \sec\varphi,\ \ \text{up by } \cos\varphi @@ the east-stretch technique (22·nav1), and k = cos φ (22·nav4)
\text{the squeeze}:\quad (x, y) \mapsto (cx,\ \tfrac{y}{c}),\quad \text{areas by } c \cdot \tfrac1c = 1 @@ areas scale (15e)
\text{the row}:\quad \text{across by } c\sec\varphi,\ \ \text{up by } \tfrac{\cos\varphi}{c},\ \ \text{areas by } 1 @@ stretches multiply along a row
\htmlClass{new}{c = \cos\varphi_0:\quad \text{both stretches are } 1 \text{ at } \varphi_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ shapes and sizes true along that latitude
  
\varphi_0 = 0^\circ:\quad c = 1,\ \ \text{the world } 2\pi R \text{ by } 2R:\ \ \tfrac{\text{width}}{\text{height}} = \pi @@ 22·nav4’s map
\varphi_0 = 45^\circ:\quad c \approx 0.707,\ \ \tfrac{\text{width}}{\text{height}} = \pi c^2 \approx 1.57 @@ true along 45° N and 45° S
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi_0 = 60^\circ:\quad c = \tfrac12,\ \ \tfrac{\text{width}}{\text{height}} \approx 0.79} @@ taller than it is wide

What hides is the arrangement: the formula of each map. Only its two stretches are used. The heading map has no such family: squeezed, its stretches are c sec φ and sec φ/c, equal only for c = 1. And no row of maps keeps every distance (22·nav4).

infnav.3A Shortest Route Is Made of Shortest Routes

∞9 read a distance as an arrow and the detour rule, d(P, Q) ≤ d(P, X) + d(X, Q), as running arrows in a row. 29·nav1 found the shortest route from Seattle to Tokyo, and 29·nav2 asked when such a route passes over the pole. The detour rule answers with no coordinates.

Parts-shortest technique: X lies on a shortest route from P to Q exactly when d(P, Q) = d(P, X) + d(X, Q), and then the parts of that route on each side of X are shortest too.

\text{a shortest route through } X:\quad d(P, Q) = \ell_1 + \ell_2 \ge d(P, X) + d(X, Q) \ge d(P, Q) @@ its two parts are routes; then the detour rule (∞9)
\ell_1 = d(P, X),\quad \ell_2 = d(X, Q) @@ both “at least” are “is”: the parts are shortest
\htmlClass{new}{d(P, Q) = d(P, X) + d(X, Q) \ \Rightarrow\ \text{shortest routes } P \text{ to } X \text{ and } X \text{ to } Q \text{ in a row are a shortest route}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other way round
  
X = \text{the top of the route},\ 54.3^\circ \text{ N}:\quad 2768 + 4888 = 7656 \text{ km} @@ on the route (29·nav2): the detour costs nothing
X = \text{the pole}:\quad R\,(42.55^\circ + 54.24^\circ) \approx 10\,763 \text{ km} \gt 7656 @@ two shortest routes in a row, and the row is not shortest
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Seattle to Dubai by the pole}:\quad 11\,931 \text{ km, against } 11\,928} @@ 177.7° apart in longitude: 3 km from the shortest (29·nav2)

What hides is the arrangement: the coordinates of the route between. A flight plan from a machine can be checked at any one place on it by three distances. Along the whole of it one number stays the same, the kept-product technique’s cos φ sin h (29·nav2), though the heading changes all the way.

The accepted names

A pose is a rigid motion of the plane, and the poses form the Euclidean group; facing is a homomorphism onto the turns, the slides are its kernel, a normal subgroup, and the poses are a semidirect product of slides and turns. Turn, run, turn back, run back is a commutator. Working out a place from a log of headings and runs is dead reckoning. A map of the globe is a projection; one that keeps sizes is equal-area, one that keeps headings and shapes is conformal, and the heading map is Mercator’s. The family of the second footing is the cylindrical equal-area projections, and φ₀ is the standard parallel: 0° gives Lambert’s, 30° Behrmann’s, 45° the Gall–Peters. The two stretches of a map at a point are its scale factors. That no map keeps every distance is Gauss’s Theorema Egregium, in the form 22·nav4 proved. A shortest route is a geodesic; that its parts are shortest is the principle of optimality; a place with d(P, Q) = d(P, X) + d(X, Q) is between P and Q; and the kept product is Clairaut’s relation.

What the arrows let hide

All three footings hide arrangement: the runs, the formula of the map, the coordinates between. The first also says what cannot hide: the order of turns and runs, for the place. In each a navigator checks an answer without redoing it. The heading is read from the turns. A map’s claim to keep sizes is read from two stretches. A place on a route is tested by three distances. No dated claim is made on this page.
