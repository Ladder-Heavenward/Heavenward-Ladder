How Many Colours Does the Plane Need?

How many colours does the plane need? Colour every point of a flat sheet so that no two points exactly one unit apart share a colour. Rung 42 coloured maps, where only countries sharing a border must differ, and five colours did. Here every point is a country, and every point has a whole circle of neighbours. Seven colours suffice: lay down hexagons a little under one unit across, in a pattern of seven colours that keeps hexagons of one colour more than a unit apart. Three do not: seven points arranged as two thin diamonds force a clash, which was found in 1961. For more than fifty years nobody could close the gap between four and seven. In 2018 a biologist working on ageing, an amateur at this question, built a set of 1581 points that cannot be coloured with four, and a computer checked every way of trying. Others, working together in the open, cut the set down to a few hundred points. So the answer is five, six or seven, and nobody knows which. There is a further twist. Whether the plane can be coloured at all with few colours, without choosing colours point by point with no rule, may depend on which axioms for sets are laid down.

The plane as a graph: every point a dot, a link at distance exactly 1. Hexagons of side s with 0.388 < s < 0.5, coloured by i + 3j mod 7, give seven colours. The spindle, 7 points and 11 links, needs four. A finite set needing k colours forces k; with choice, the plane needs exactly the most any finite set needs. 1581 points need five (2018), checked by computer as clauses; a few hundred since. With choice a graph can need two colours where a world without it needs uncountably many.

sky22.1The Plane as a Graph

Make every point of the plane a dot, and link two dots when their distance (18a) is exactly 1. This is the unit graph of the plane. A colouring, as in 42d4, gives each dot a colour so that linked dots differ. The least number of colours that works is the colour number of the plane. The graph has as many dots as the line has points, every dot has a whole circle of links, and it is far from flat: the links cross everywhere, so 42d’s five-colour technique says nothing about it.

Finite-witness technique: if some finite set of points cannot be coloured with k colours, then the plane cannot be coloured with k colours. A colouring of the plane, kept only on those points, would colour them. So every lower bound is a finite set of points, and the hunt is for clever ones.

\text{an equilateral triangle of side } 1 @@ three dots, each pair linked
\text{two colours on three dots: two share a colour} @@ the pigeonhole of 42i
\text{those two are linked} @@ every pair is
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the plane needs at least } 3} \quad \blacksquare @@ the finite-witness technique

The other way round is harder. If every finite set of points can be coloured with k colours, must the plane be? Held in trust: yes, if the axiom of choice of rung 6 is laid down (a technique proved with 34d’s finitely covered products, which 34d showed spend choice). Then the colour number of the plane is the most that any finite set of points needs, and the question is about finite sets after all.

sky22.2Seven Colours Suffice

Tile the plane by regular hexagons of side s, centres on the triangular grid with spacing √3 · s, each hexagon taking its centre’s grid label (i, j): the centre is i steps along one grid direction plus j along the other, at 60° to it. Give hexagon (i, j) the colour i + 3j on a clock of 7. Give each point on a shared edge to one of its hexagons, so each point gets exactly one colour.

Seven-hexagon technique: with 1/(√21 − 2) < s < 1/2, that is about 0.388 < s < 0.5, the colouring i + 3j mod 7 of the hexagons of side s colours the plane with seven colours, and no two points one unit apart share a colour.

\text{two points of one hexagon are less than } 2s \lt 1 \text{ apart} @@ the widest span is corner to opposite corner, 2s
\text{the six neighbours of } (0, 0) \text{ get } 1, 6, 3, 4, 5, 2 @@ (1,0), (−1,0), (0,1), (0,−1), (1,−1), (−1,1): all different from 0
\text{colour } 0 \text{ again at } (1, 2)\text{: } i + 3j = 7 @@ the nearest same colour, and its five turns about the centre
|(1, 2)| = \sqrt{1 + 2 + 4} \cdot \sqrt3\, s = \sqrt{21}\, s @@ i² + ij + j² on the triangular grid, times the spacing
\text{two hexagons of one colour are at least } \sqrt{21}\,s - 2s \text{ apart} @@ each reaches at most s from its centre
\htmlClass{new}{(\sqrt{21} - 2)\, s \gt 1 \text{ when } s \gt 0.388} \quad \blacksquare @@ no link joins a colour to itself

With s = 0.45: one hexagon spans at most 0.9, and two of one colour are at least 1.16 apart, in fact 1.19. The colouring is 20c’s pattern repeated by slides: the slides by (1, 2) and by (3, −1) carry the colouring to itself, and seven hexagons make one copy of the repeating piece, one of each colour. No colouring of the plane with six has ever been found.

sky22.3The Spindle Needs Four

Two equilateral triangles of side 1 sharing a side make a diamond. Its two tips, the corners off the shared side, are √3 apart. In a colouring with three colours the shared side takes two of them, so each tip must take the third: the two tips of a unit diamond share a colour.

Spindle technique: the seven points A, B, C, D, B′, C′, D′, where A B C and B C D are unit triangles and B′, C′, D′ are B, C, D turned about A by the angle θ with 2√3 sin(θ/2) = 1, have 11 unit links and cannot be coloured with three colours; so the plane needs at least four.

A = (0, 0),\ B = (1, 0),\ C = (\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}),\ D = (\tfrac32, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}) @@ two unit triangles; |AD| = √3
\theta = 2 \arcsin \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt3} \approx 33.56^\circ\text{, so } |DD'| = 2\sqrt3 \sin\tfrac\theta2 = 1 @@ the chord of a circle of radius √3
\text{three colours: } B, C \text{ differ from } A \text{ and from each other} @@ AB, AC, BC are links
D \text{ differs from } B \text{ and } C\text{, so } D \text{ has } A\text{’s colour} @@ only three colours
D' \text{ has } A\text{’s colour, the same way} @@ the turned diamond
\htmlClass{new}{D \text{ and } D' \text{ share a colour, and are 1 apart}} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute: three colours fail

The links: AB, AC, BC, BD, CD and their turned copies AB′, AC′, B′C′, B′D′, C′D′, and DD′: 11. A colouring with four exists, so the spindle alone can give no more. For 57 years after 1961 no finite set of points was found that needs five, and many expected the answer to be four, or that no finite set would show more.

sky22.4Five, by Computer

In 2018 a set of 1581 points was published that cannot be coloured with four colours. It was built from many spindles and pieces of the triangular grid, arranged so that every colouring of one part with four colours forces a pattern that another part forbids. Nobody checked that by hand. The check was a search by machine, the way 42·now.4 checked the four colour technique, and here the search is turned into a question of 41h’s satisfiability.

Colour-clause technique: a set of V points with E unit links can be coloured with k colours exactly when a list of V + kE clauses in Vk true-or-false letters can all be made true: for each point p, “p has colour 1 or 2 or … or k”, and for each link pq and colour c, “not (p has c and q has c)”. A true assignment gives each point some colour, pick one, and no link joins two points of one colour; a colouring gives a true assignment. It is 41k’s reduction run the other way, from colouring to satisfiability.

\text{spindle, } k = 3\text{: } 21 \text{ letters, } 7 + 3 \cdot 11 = 40 \text{ clauses} @@ no assignment makes all 40 true, by sky22.3
\text{the 2018 set, } k = 4\text{: } 4 \cdot 1581 = 6324 \text{ letters} @@ one clause per point, four per link
\text{colourings to try one by one: } 4^{1581} \approx 10^{952} @@ far beyond any machine
\text{a solver learns a new clause from each dead end, and turns colours about} @@ the four colours can be permuted: 24 colourings at once
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no assignment exists: the plane needs at least } 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ the search finished, with nothing found

Within weeks others had run their own searches and found sets of under a thousand points, and a group working in the open online set out to shrink the set further, to a few hundred points. Is a refusal that no person has read a proof? 42·now asked that of the four colour technique; here the record is smaller, and the question is the same.

sky22.5The Axioms May Matter

The finite-witness technique runs one way with no axiom. The way back, from every finite set to the whole plane, spent choice. Choice also built 30a’s set with no size. So ask: what if each colour must be a set with an area-size, as 30 measures it? Held in trust: then at least five colours are needed, a result published decades before the finite sets reached five.

A smaller graph shows choice at work. Link two reals x and y when x − y − √2 or y − x − √2 is a fraction. Even-cycle technique: in this graph every closed walk has an even number of steps; so, with choice, two colours suffice.

\text{a step adds } \pm(\sqrt2 + q),\ q \text{ a fraction} @@ the link, read as a move along the line
\text{a closed walk: } m \text{ steps with } + \text{ and } n \text{ with } -\text{; the moves add to } 0 @@ back where it began
(m - n)\sqrt2 + (\text{a fraction}) = 0 @@ collect the √2 terms
m - n \ne 0 \Rightarrow \sqrt2 \text{ is a fraction} @@ refute: rung 8’s √2
\htmlClass{new}{m = n,\ \text{so the walk has } 2m \text{ steps}} \quad \blacksquare @@ every cycle even

Example: 0, then √2 + 1, then 1 − ½ = ½, then ½ + √2 − ½ = √2, then √2 − √2 − 0 = 0: four steps. Colour each piece of the graph by the parity of the steps from one chosen point of it. The pieces are as many as the reals, and no rule picks a point in each, so the choosing is 6b’s, without a rule. Held in trust, as published in 2003: in a world for sets where every set of reals has a size (built as 6d builds worlds), this graph needs more colours than there are whole numbers. The same graph, with and without choice: 2, or uncountably many. Whether the unit graph of the plane behaves like this is not known.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞22.oq.1Five, six or seven? Every finite set of points found so far can be coloured with five colours, and no colouring of the whole plane with six is known. Closing the gap needs either a finite set that refuses five, or a new way of colouring the plane. The second would have to beat the hexagons, which have stood since the question was asked.
∞22.oq.2How small can a set of points that needs five be? The record has fallen from 1581 to a few hundred. Each step was a search by machine. Whether a set of a hundred points, or a few dozen, could do it, small enough for a person to check line by line, is open; so is whether the smallest one has any structure a person could see.
∞22.oq.3Does the answer depend on the axioms? If every colour must have an area-size, at least five are needed. Is the colour number with sized colours larger than without? An example where the two differ, for the unit graph of the plane itself, would make the question’s answer a matter of which axioms are laid down.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question was asked in 1950, by a student, and passed by word of mouth before it reached print. Seven colours from hexagons were seen early, and the spindle, which needs four, was published in 1961. For the next 57 years the bounds were four and seven, and the question became a favourite example of a simple statement that resists every method.

Some expected the answer to be four. Others expected seven, since the hexagon colouring looks tight. A third view held that the answer might not be fixed by the usual axioms at all: it had been shown that if each colour must have an area-size, five are needed, and in 2003 graphs were built on the line whose colour number really does depend on the axioms for sets. Whether the plane is such a graph is still not known.

In April 2018 a researcher on ageing, working on the question as a hobby, posted a set of 1581 points that cannot be coloured with four. A search by machine from outside the field invited doubt, and the answer was more checking: others ran their own searches, found smaller sets within weeks, and a public collaboration of professionals and amateurs, run on a blog, set out to shrink and understand it.

The collaboration cut the set to a few hundred points, each step again a search by machine. It also asked whether a set that needs six could be found the same way. None has been.

What is argued now: whether the answer is decided by finite sets at all, given the role of choice; whether proofs by solver records are proofs in the full sense, as 42·now asked; and whether the hexagons are best. The question is now more than seventy years old, and the 2018 set is the only move on its bounds since 1961.

The accepted names

The question is the Hadwiger–Nelson problem, and the colour number of the plane is χ(ℝ²), the chromatic number of the plane. The unit graph is the unit distance graph. The spindle is the Moser spindle; the seven-hexagon colouring is usually credited to John Isbell. The way back from finite sets is the de Bruijn–Erdős theorem, which needs the axiom of choice (or a weaker form of it). The colour number with sized colours is the measurable chromatic number. The 1581-point set is the de Grey graph. The colour-clause technique is the standard encoding of graph colouring as Boolean satisfiability (SAT). The world where every set of reals has a size is Solovay’s model.

Who said it first

Edward Nelson posed the question in 1950, as a student at Chicago. The name also honours Hugo Hadwiger. William and Leo Moser published the spindle in 1961. Kenneth Falconer proved the measurable bound (1981). Saharon Shelah and Alexander Soifer gave the graphs whose colour number depends on the axioms in 2003. Aubrey de Grey found the 1581-point set in 2018; Marijn Heule found smaller ones by SAT within weeks; Jaan Parts later cut it to about 510 points (2019 and 2020; whether the smallest known has 510 or 509 points is not checked against two sources here); the collaboration was the Polymath project Polymath16, proposed by Aubrey de Grey.

A proof that is not allowed

“Seven colours work, and every attempt at six has failed, so the plane needs seven.” Not allowed: failure to find a colouring refutes nothing. A lower bound needs a finite set and a finished check; an upper bound needs a colouring. The 2018 set gave the first, for five. Nothing yet gives either for six.
