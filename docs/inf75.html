How Good a Route Can Be Found Fast?

How good a route can be found fast? A van must call at every stop once and come home, and the shortest tour cannot be found by trying every order: the orders outrun any computer, and finding the best is one of the hardest questions of its kind, the kind no fast method is known to settle. So the question changes. Not the best tour, but a tour that is surely close to the best, found fast, with a proof of how close. Without one plain condition even that is out of reach: if a road between two stops can be longer than a detour through a third, no fast method can promise to stay within any fixed factor of the best, unless every question of that hardest kind is fast. With the condition, a cheapest tree joining the stops is already shorter than every tour, and walking round it twice gives a tour at most twice the best. In 1976 a better recipe was found, independently, in two countries: take the cheapest tree, pair up its stops with an odd number of links as cheaply as possible, walk the result and skip the stops already seen. The tour is at most half again as long as the best. For forty four years nobody could promise better. Meanwhile a floor under the best tour was built from prices on the stops and on the ways of splitting them, a program with fractions in place of whole links, and a guess grew that the true tour is never more than a third above that floor. In 2020 the half again was beaten, by a margin smaller than one part in a billion billion billion billion, by picking the tree at random, with chances read off the fractional program and spread as widely as possible. This page builds the condition and why it is needed, the tree under every tour, the tree and the matching with the whole proof of the half again, the prices, the guess of a third, and the random tree, and ends with the guess still open.

Costs obeying c(u, w) ≤ c(u, v) + c(v, w): best tour ≥ cheapest tree T; the odd places of T, paired cheaply in M, cost at most half the best tour; T + M has every count even, so it has a closed tour of its links, and skipping repeats gives a tour ≤ c(T) + c(M) ≤ 3/2 · best (1976). Prices y with y_u + y_v ≤ c(uv) give best ≥ 2Σy; with split limits the fractional program sits under the best, on some lists by a factor near 4/3. In 2020 a random tree with chances from the program gave 3/2 − ε, ε > 10⁻³⁶.

inf75.1Without the Triangle Condition, No Promise

Places 1 to n, and a cost c(u, v) = c(v, u) ≥ 0 for every pair, as in 22·logi. A tour visits every place once and comes home; its cost is the sum over its n links. A method is within r when, on every list of costs, the tour it returns costs at most r times the best tour. 22·logi’s nearest-stop technique is fast and within no fixed r (22·logi3). The first thing to know is that, on arbitrary costs, no fast method is.

No-promise technique: if, for some fixed r ≥ 1, a fast method is within r on every list of costs, then whether a graph has a round of the dots is decided fast, and so every set in NP is fast.

G \text{ with } n \text{ dots};\ \ c(u, v) = 1 \text{ if } u, v \text{ are joined in } G,\ \ c(u, v) = rn + 1 \text{ if not} @@ a list of costs, made fast from G
G \text{ has a round of the dots} \Rightarrow \text{best tour} = n @@ the round is a tour of n links, each of cost 1
\text{the method returns a tour of cost} \le rn @@ within r
\text{a tour with one link outside } G \text{ costs} \ge rn + 1 @@ every link costs at least 1, and that one rn + 1
\text{no round} \Rightarrow \text{every tour costs} \ge rn + 1 @@ every tour then uses a link outside G
\htmlClass{new}{G \text{ has a round} \iff \text{the method's tour costs} \le rn} \quad \blacksquare @@ a fast test; rounds of the dots are NP-complete (42c3, 41h)

The costs built in the proof are lopsided: two links of cost 1 make a detour of 2 round a pair that costs rn + 1 directly. Road distances never do that. The triangle condition asks c(u, w) ≤ c(u, v) + c(v, w) for every three places: going straight is never longer than going through a third. The courier of 22·logi3, with blocks counted east-west plus north-south, obeys it at all 7 × 7 × 7 = 343 triples (checked by script). From here on every list of costs obeys the triangle condition, and the question is how small a fixed r a fast method can reach.

The triangle condition is ∞Η.1’s detour condition: with it, the places and their costs are a category whose arrows are numbers, and going by way of a third place is composing. A tour may then skip any place already passed, since the direct arrow costs no more than the detour, and the doubled tree of inf75.2 uses exactly that. The plan is the costs; the map of the places is let go.

inf75.2A Tree Under Every Tour

Drop any one link of a tour and what is left is a path through every place: in one piece, n − 1 links, so a spanning tree by the any-two-of-three technique. Costs are at least 0, so the cheapest tree, found greedily by 42k3, is a floor.

Tree-under-tour technique: the best tour costs at least the cheapest spanning tree. Shortcut technique: under the triangle condition, a closed walk that passes every place can be cut to a tour, by skipping each place already seen, and the tour costs no more than the walk.

\ldots u \to v \to w \ldots,\ \ v \text{ seen before} @@ a walk, and a place to skip
c(u, w) \le c(u, v) + c(v, w) @@ the triangle condition
\text{skipping } v \text{ never lengthens the walk} @@ the walk keeps every other place and closes the same way
\text{skip until each place appears once} @@ each skip shortens the list by one, so this stops
\text{walk round the cheapest tree } T,\ \text{each link twice} @@ out along a branch and back: a closed walk through every place, cost 2c(T)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tour} \le 2c(T) \le 2 \cdot \text{best}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shortcut and tree-under-tour techniques

Doubled-tree technique: under the triangle condition, a tour at most twice the best is found fast. The courier: greedily the tree takes C–E 2, D–F 3, O–E 3, O–A 4, O–D 4 and B–C 6, so c(T) = 22. The best tour, 32, is above it, as it must be. Walking round the tree from O: O E C B C E O A O D F D O, cost 44. Skipping repeats: O E C B A D F O, cost 3 + 2 + 6 + 15 + 6 + 3 + 7 = 42, within 2 · 32 = 64, and here 42/32 ≈ 1.31. Half the walk is spent coming back along links already used. The next footing pays for only the coming back that parity forces.

inf75.3A Tree and a Matching

In the cheapest tree T, call a place odd when it has an odd count of tree links. By the ends-count technique the counts add to 2(n − 1), so the odd places are even in number. Pair them up, a matching M on the odd places using any pairs, as cheaply as possible. Then T + M, with a link doubled where M repeats a tree link, has an even count at every place and is in one piece, so the even-tour technique gives a closed tour of its links.

Half-tour technique: under the triangle condition, for any even number of places W, the cheapest matching pairing all of W costs at most half the best tour. Tree-and-matching technique: under the triangle condition, the cheapest tree plus the cheapest matching of its odd places, walked and shortcut, gives a tour at most 3/2 times the best.

\text{the best tour, skipping every place not in } W:\ w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_{2m}, w_1 @@ the shortcut technique: this cycle C_W costs at most the best
M_1 = \{w_1w_2, w_3w_4, \ldots\},\ \ M_2 = \{w_2w_3, \ldots, w_{2m}w_1\} @@ its links taken by turns: two matchings of W
c(M_1) + c(M_2) = c(C_W) \le \text{best} @@ every link of C_W is in exactly one
\text{cheapest } M \le \min(c(M_1), c(M_2)) \le \tfrac12\,\text{best} @@ the smaller of two is at most half their sum: the half-tour technique
T + M \text{ walked and shortcut} \le c(T) + c(M) @@ the even-tour and shortcut techniques
\htmlClass{new}{\text{tour} \le \text{best} + \tfrac12\,\text{best} = \tfrac32\,\text{best}} \quad \blacksquare @@ tree-under-tour, and half-tour with W the odd places

The courier. The tree’s counts are O 3, E 2, C 2, D 2, A 1, B 1, F 1: the odd places are O, A, B, F. Their three pairings cost O–A + B–F = 4 + 10 = 14, O–B + A–F = 11 + 5 = 16 and O–F + A–B = 7 + 15 = 22. The best tour O A F D B C E meets them in the order O, A, F, B, and its two matchings are {O–A, F–B} and {A–F, B–O}, 14 + 16 = 30 ≤ 32. Take M = {O–A, B–F}: T + M has O–A twice, and the closed tour O A O D F B C E O costs 22 + 14 = 36. Skipping the second O: O A D F B C E O, cost 4 + 6 + 3 + 10 + 6 + 2 + 3 = 34, against 3/2 · 32 = 48 promised. Both steps are fast: the tree by 42k3, the cheapest matching by the repaired search of 42e3 with costs added (1965, held in trust).

The 3/2 is earned. Lay 2k + 1 places on a strip of triangles of side 1: k + 1 along the bottom at 0, 1, …, k, and k on top halfway between. The zigzag bottom, top, bottom, … uses 2k links of cost 1 and is a cheapest tree, since every link costs at least 1. Its only odd places are its two ends, k apart, so M is one link of cost k and the tour costs 3k. The best tour runs round the edge of the strip, 2k + 1 links of cost 1. At k = 5: 15 against 11; as k grows, 3k/(2k + 1) climbs to 3/2. A different cheapest tree would have done better here; the recipe does not choose.

inf75.4Prices on the Places

Every bound so far compares against the best tour, which nobody knows. A floor that can be computed makes the comparison real. Let x_uv be a share of the link uv, between 0 and 1. A tour is the plan with x = 1 on its n links and 0 elsewhere, and it has shares adding to 2 at every place. Drop the demand that shares be whole: the least of Σ c(uv) x_uv over these fractional plans is a program of 42g, and it sits under every tour. Its twin puts a price y_v on each place’s limit.

Place-price technique: if y_u + y_v ≤ c(u, v) for every pair of places, every tour costs at least 2(y₁ + ⋯ + y_n). It is the price-bound technique read for tours.

\text{tour} = \textstyle\sum_{uv \text{ in the tour}} c(u, v) @@ n links
\ge \textstyle\sum_{uv \text{ in the tour}} (y_u + y_v) @@ the prices are covered on every pair
= \textstyle\sum_v (\text{links of the tour at } v) \cdot y_v @@ each link brings the prices of its two ends
\htmlClass{new}{= 2\textstyle\sum_v y_v} \quad \blacksquare @@ every place has exactly two links of the tour

The courier, with prices O 0, A 4, B 6, C 0, D 2, E 2, F 1: every pair is covered (checked at all 21 pairs; A–O, A–F, A–D, B–C, B–E and D–F are tight), so every tour costs at least 2 · 15 = 30. The two-links floor of 22·logi4 gave 28.5. A price can be negative here, since the shares at a place add to exactly 2.

Fractions can cheat: two separate triangles have shares 2 at every place and are no tour. A tour crosses every split of the places at least twice, out and back, so add the split limits: for every set S with something in it and something outside, the shares of the links crossing S add to at least 2. A tour still obeys every limit, so the program with split limits is still a floor. On the courier its least value is 32, reached by the best tour itself (by script, over all 63 splits), and the twin prices that prove it, prices on places and on splits, certify that 32 is best without trying the 360 tours.

inf75.5The Guess of a Third

Call the least value of the program with split limits the split floor. Every tour is an allowed plan, so the split floor is at most the best tour. How far below can it fall? The courier says not at all. Other lists say more.

Split-floor technique: the split floor is at most the best tour.

x = 1 \text{ on the links of a tour},\ 0 \text{ elsewhere} @@ a plan of whole shares
\text{shares at each place} = 2 @@ two links at every place
\text{a split } S:\ \text{the tour leaves } S \text{ and comes back} @@ a closed walk crosses a split an even number of times, at least once each way
\htmlClass{new}{\text{split floor} \le \text{best tour}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tour is an allowed plan, and its cost is its value

A list where the floor falls short: take two places u and v joined by three paths of k links each, and let the cost of a pair be the fewest links between them along the paths, which obeys the triangle condition. By script: at k = 2, 5 places, best 6, split floor 6; at k = 3, 8 places, best 10, split floor 9; at k = 4, 11 places, best 14, split floor 12. The pattern is 4k − 2 against 3k, held in trust past k = 4, and the ratio climbs towards 4/3. No list is known on which the split floor falls further below the best than 4/3.

So two numbers sit between what is known and what is guessed. The tree-and-matching tour is within 3/2 of the split floor, not only of the best tour, by an argument of 1980 held in trust; so the best tour is never more than half above the split floor. The three paths show it can be nearly a third above. The guess, which has circulated for decades, is that a third is the truth: the best tour is never more than 4/3 times the split floor. If it holds, the floor is the right yardstick, and a method within 4/3 may be found by rounding it.

inf75.6A Random Tree, 2020

The 2020 method keeps the recipe of inf75.3 and changes one step: the tree is not the cheapest, but picked at random, with chances read off a best plan x of the split floor. Then the odd places are matched as before. The cost of a random tree is a number that depends on the choice, and the some-outcome technique of ∞θ finds a tree as cheap as the average.

Average-tree technique: if a random spanning tree holds each link uv with chance (n − 1)/n · x_uv, then its average cost is (n − 1)/n times the plan’s value, and some tree costs at most that.

c(T) = \textstyle\sum_{uv} c(u, v)\,[uv \in T] @@ [·] is 1 when uv is in T, else 0
\operatorname{E}\,c(T) = \textstyle\sum_{uv} c(u, v)\,P(uv \in T) @@ the average-adds technique (30g)
= \tfrac{n - 1}{n} \textstyle\sum_{uv} c(u, v)\,x_{uv} @@ the chances given
\htmlClass{new}{\text{some } T:\ c(T) \le \tfrac{n - 1}{n} \cdot \text{value of } x} \quad \blacksquare @@ the some-outcome technique (∞θ)

Four places, every pair at the same cost, and the plan x = 2/3 on all six links: shares 3 · 2/3 = 2 at each place, and a split of two against two is crossed by four links, 8/3 ≥ 2. By the leaf-code technique there are 4² = 16 spanning trees, and picking one of them with equal chances puts each link in 8 of them: chance 1/2 = (3/4) · (2/3). Equal chances are also the most spread pick, 4 bits by the surprise-average technique. The 2020 method picks, among all ways of picking trees with the chances it needs, the one with the most surprise. The reason is the next step. Of the 16 trees, 12 are paths, with 2 odd places, and 4 are stars, with all 4 odd. A cheap matching needs few odd places, and few odd places needs links that do not crowd at the same places; the most spread pick keeps the links from leaning on each other.

Told, not proved here: with the most spread pick, the paper of 2020 shows that the odd places can be matched, on average, slightly more cheaply than half the floor, and so the whole tour, on average, is at most (3/2 − ε) times the split floor, with ε above 10⁻³⁶. The proof holds in trust a long study of how the chances of links in such a pick depend on each other. A method that is random can be run many times; the guarantee is on the average, which by the some-outcome technique is met by some run.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞金.oq.1Is there a fast method within 4/3 of the best tour on every list of costs that obeys the triangle condition? And is the split floor always within 4/3 of the best tour?
Why it is hard: the second would follow from a way of rounding fractional plans into tours that loses at most a third, and every rounding known loses more on some list; the three paths show 4/3 cannot be beaten by the floor alone.
∞金.oq.2Where between the two known numbers does the true limit of fast methods lie? Unless every set in NP is fast, no fast method is within 123/122, a little under 1.01 (2013); the best method is within 3/2 − ε with ε above 10⁻³⁶ (2020).
Why it is hard: the lower number comes from building lists out of hard puzzles, and the upper from rounding the split floor, and the two kinds of argument have not met in the middle.
∞金.oq.3How much better than 3/2 does the random-tree method really do, in the worst case? The proved gain is tiny, and a 2023 paper gives lists on which the method cannot do much better than it does there; the exact numbers have not been revisited by this page.
Why it is hard: the proof must control how the odd places of a random tree fall, which depends on how the chances of many links lean on each other at once.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The modern question begins with a table of distances and a computation by hand and by program. In 1954 a team at RAND published the best tour through 49 cities of the United States, found with the fractional program and split limits added one at a time, as many as were needed. The split floor was born as a working tool: it gave a number no tour could beat, and a tour that met it was proved best. In 1972 the round of the dots was put on the first list of NP-complete questions, and with it the best tour, so the hope for an exact fast method became the open question of 41·now2.

In 1976 the tree and the matching appeared in a report from Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh, and, independently, in the Soviet Union, in work published in Russian in 1978 that was little known outside it for decades. A historical note of 2020 brought it to wider attention, and the method now often carries both names. The 3/2 was so simple to state and so hard to improve that it became a test of method: special cases fell, the general case did not.

In the plane, with straight-line distances, the best tour is still hard to find, shown in the 1970s, but in the late 1990s methods were found that come within any fixed factor above 1, 1.01 or 1.001, at a cost that grows with the precision; they shared a prize of 2010. The plane is special because a near-best tour can be forced to cross a grid of cells only a few times. In 2013 it was shown that on general lists obeying the triangle condition no such thing is possible: no fast method is within 123/122 unless every set in NP is fast.

Practice and theory pulled apart. Programs built on the split floor and its cousins proved best tours through tens of thousands of places, among them a tour through 85 900 points from a problem in making chips, while the guarantee for fast methods stayed at 3/2. One side reads this as a sign that the worst case is the wrong question for routing, where lists come from real maps and quick techniques like 22·logi’s already do well. The other side answers that a guarantee is what a method offers on a list nobody has seen, and that the split floor, the tool of the practitioners, is exactly what the guarantees are measured against.

In July 2020 three researchers at the University of Washington posted the random tree with the most spread chances, and a gain above 10⁻³⁶. The size of the gain became the story: one blog called it a vast and tiny breakthrough, vast because a barrier of forty four years fell and tiny because of the number. It won a best-paper award at a leading conference of the field in 2021. What is argued now is whether the method can be pushed towards 4/3, or whether a new idea is needed: a 2023 paper found lists on which the random tree itself cannot reach far below 3/2 (its numbers not revisited here).

The accepted names

The question is the travelling salesman problem (TSP); with the triangle condition it is metric TSP, and with straight-line distances Euclidean TSP. A method within r is an r-approximation algorithm. The no-promise technique is the inapproximability of general TSP (Sahni and Gonzalez, 1976). The doubled-tree technique is the tree doubling 2-approximation. The tree-and-matching technique is the Christofides algorithm, also called the Christofides–Serdyukov algorithm. The fractional program with split limits is the subtour elimination LP, or Held–Karp relaxation; its least value is the Held–Karp bound; the ratio of best tour to split floor is its integrality gap, and the guess of a third is the 4/3 conjecture. The bound of 1980 is Wolsey’s analysis. The pick of trees with the most surprise is the maximum entropy distribution over spanning trees; the 2020 method is the algorithm of Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan. The plane methods are polynomial-time approximation schemes (PTAS) for Euclidean TSP.

Who said it first

George Dantzig, Ray Fulkerson and Selmer Johnson solved the 49-city tour at RAND (Operations Research, 1954). Richard Karp listed the Hamiltonian circuit among his 21 NP-complete problems (1972). Nicos Christofides wrote the 3/2 method in a Carnegie Mellon report of 1976; Anatoliy Serdyukov found it independently (published 1978); René van Bevern and Viktoriia Slugina wrote the historical note (2020). Laurence Wolsey showed the method is within 3/2 of the Held–Karp bound (1980). Christos Papadimitriou showed Euclidean TSP NP-hard (1977). Sanjeev Arora and Joseph Mitchell found the Euclidean schemes and shared the 2010 Gödel Prize. Marek Karpinski, Michael Lampis and Richard Schmied gave the 123/122 bound (2013). The 85 900-point tour was certified by the Concorde team of David Applegate, Robert Bixby, Vašek Chvátal, William Cook and others. Anna Karlin, Nathan Klein and Shayan Oveis Gharan posted the 3/2 − ε method in July 2020 (arXiv 2007.01409; best paper at STOC 2021). The blog post “A vast and tiny breakthrough” is by Richard Lipton and Kenneth Regan (October 2020).

A proof that is not allowed

“The cheapest matching of the odd places costs at most half the tree, because the tree joins those places too.” Not allowed: a tree is not a cycle, and the tree’s links do not split into two matchings of the odd places. The proof compares with the best tour, which, cut down to the odd places, is a cycle of even length, and a cycle of even length is two matchings.
