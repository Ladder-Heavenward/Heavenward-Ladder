Why Do Sunflower Seeds Spiral?

Why do sunflower seeds spiral? Look into a ripe sunflower head and the seeds sit in curved arms, some sweeping one way and some the other. Count them, and you find numbers like thirty-four one way and fifty-five the other, again and again, head after head. People have counted them for centuries. What makes it hard is that the plant draws no spirals. It makes one seed at a time, at its centre, and pushes the older ones out. This page shows, with the angles of rung 21, that a fixed turn between one seed and the next is enough to make the arms, that a turn that is a simple fraction of the whole wastes room in spokes, that one turn stays as far as it can from every simple fraction, and why that turn makes arms in counts that are neighbours in the rabbit counts.

Each new seed sits a fixed share of a whole turn past the last, a little farther out. If that share is a simple fraction, the seeds fall on straight spokes with empty wedges between. If it is near a simple fraction, seeds that many apart drift slowly round and make that many arms. The share that splits the turn the way each part splits again stays far from every simple fraction, and its arms come in neighbouring rabbit counts that lean opposite ways.

21bio1A Fixed Turn, Seed After Seed

A sunflower makes its seeds at the centre of the head, one at a time, and pushes the older ones outward. Each new seed starts a fixed share α of a whole turn past the one before. So seed n sits at the turn 2πnα, and only the part of nα past a whole number matters (21b).

Each seed takes about the same room s. So the first n seeds fill a disc of area ns, and πR² = ns (21n). Seed n sits at reach R = c√n, where c = √(s/π).

\text{past whole turns}:\ \ 0.382,\ 0.764,\ 0.146,\ 0.528,\ 0.910 @@ whole turns change nothing (21b)
\text{reach}:\ \ c,\ 1.41c,\ 1.73c,\ 2d,\ 2.24c @@ c√n

Seed-place technique: seed n sits at reach c√n and turn 2πnα. A large head from the sunflower fields of the Sacramento Valley can hold well over a thousand seeds, every one placed by this one rule.

21bio2A Simple Fraction Makes Spokes

Try α = p/q, a fraction in lowest terms. Seed n + q sits at the turn of seed n, plus p whole turns.

(n + q)\tfrac{p}{q} = n\tfrac{p}{q} + p @@ p whole turns change nothing (21b)
\text{seeds } n,\ n + q,\ n + 2q,\ \dots \text{ lie on one ray} @@ so every seed lies on one of q rays
\text{gap between spokes at reach } R:\ \tfrac{2\pi R}{q} @@ the arc of a qth of the turn (21j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the gaps widen as } R \text{ grows; along a spoke the seeds crowd}} @@ √(n + q) − √n = q/(√(n + q) + √n), which shrinks

Spoke technique: a turn of p/q of the whole puts every seed on just q spokes, and the wedges between them widen as the head grows. Near a fraction is nearly as bad. Seeds q apart sit qα less a whole number of a turn apart.

From here on, on this page, the miss of q is qα less the whole number nearest to it.

With α = 0.38, the miss of 8 is 3.04 − 3 = 0.04. Seeds 8 apart sit 0.04 of a turn apart, so they line up in 8 arms that curve slowly round. Since 0.38 = 19/50, far out the arms give way to 50 straight spokes.

21bio3The Turn Farthest From Every Simple Fraction

Every share of the turn has small misses for some q. Which share keeps them as large as it can? A guess: let the first seed split the turn in a ratio that can repeat, so that each part splits again the same way. The larger piece is to the smaller as the whole is to the larger.

\tfrac{1 - \alpha}{\alpha} = \tfrac{1}{1 - \alpha} @@ the guess: larger over smaller is whole over larger
(1 - \alpha)^2 = \alpha,\ \ \alpha^2 - 3\alpha + 1 = 0 @@ clear the fractions
\alpha = \tfrac{3 - \sqrt5}{2} \approx 0.381966 @@ the smaller root (8i); the other is above 1
\htmlClass{new}{0.381966 \times 360^\circ \approx 137.5^\circ} @@ a share of the turn (21i)

Write r = 1/(1 − α) = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.618. Then r² = r + 1, 1/r = r − 1 and α = 2 − r. Test the guess on the rabbit counts F₁, F₂, F₃, … = 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …, each the sum of the two before (9·cb4).

e_k = F_k\,r - F_{k+1} @@ how far F_k r is from the next count
e_{k+1} = F_{k+1}(r - 1) - F_k = \tfrac{F_{k+1}}{r} - F_k = -\tfrac{e_k}{r} @@ F_{k+2} = F_{k+1} + F_k, and r − 1 = 1/r
e_1 = r - 1 = \tfrac1r \Rightarrow e_k = (-1)^{k+1}\,r^{-k} @@ induction (4c)
F_k\,\alpha = 2F_k - F_k\,r = F_{k-2} - e_k @@ α = 2 − r, and 2F_k − F_{k+1} = F_k − F_{k−1} = F_{k−2}
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the miss of } F_k \text{ is } (-1)^k\,r^{-k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ from k = 3 on, it is under ½, so F_{k−2} is the nearest whole

Golden-turn technique: for the golden turn, each miss of a rabbit count is the last one divided by r, with its sign changed. The misses never reach 0. A machine check of every q up to 1000 finds q times the miss never below 0.38 for the golden turn. For √2 − 1 it falls to 0.34; for 0.38 it reaches 0 at q = 50.

21bio4Counting the Spirals Each Way

Follow seeds q apart: n, n + q, n + 2q, …. Each step moves the turn by the miss of q and the reach a little out. So they form an arm that curves slowly round. There are q such arms, one starting at each of the seeds 1 to q.

34\alpha = 12.98684:\ \ \text{miss } -0.01316 @@ just short of 13 turns: each step falls back
55\alpha = 21.00813:\ \ \text{miss } +0.00813 @@ just past 21 turns: each step runs ahead
\tfrac{0.00813}{0.01316} \approx 0.618 = \tfrac1r @@ 21·bio.3
\htmlClass{new}{34 \text{ arms lean one way, } 55 \text{ the other}} @@ the signs of the misses

Spiral-count technique: seeds q apart form q arms, which lean the way the sign of the miss of q says. For the golden turn, neighbouring rabbit counts lean opposite ways.

The arms you see join each seed to its nearest neighbours. By the cosine-side technique (21h), near seed 300 the steps of 34 and 55 are the shortest, about 1.75c and 1.78c. Near seed 1000 the steps of 89 and 55 are, about 1.71c and 1.85c. So a small head shows 34 and 55, and a larger one 55 and 89. Most sunflower heads show one of these pairs; the largest show 89 and 144.

The accepted names

The arrangement of seeds, leaves and scales is phyllotaxis. The golden turn is the golden angle, about 137.5°; r = (1 + √5)/2 is the golden ratio, written φ. The rabbit counts are the Fibonacci numbers, and 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18, … are the Lucas numbers. The arms are parastichies. Seed n at reach c√n and turn 2πnα is Vogel’s model. That no number keeps its misses larger, for large q, than the golden ratio is Hurwitz’s theorem; rung 43 builds the continued fractions behind it.

Who said it first

Karl Schimper and Alexander Braun (1830s) counted the arms on pine cones and found rabbit counts. The brothers Louis and Auguste Bravais (1837) found the turn of about 137.5°. Alan Turing worked on how a growing tip could make the pattern in the last years of his life (1952–54). Helmut Vogel (1979) gave the √n rule. Stéphane Douady and Yves Couder (1992) dropped magnetised drops one at a time into a dish, each pushed away by the others, and watched the golden turn appear with no plant at all: one answer to 21·bio.6.5. A count of several hundred heads grown by the public, published in 2016, found most showed neighbouring rabbit counts, and about one in five did not.
