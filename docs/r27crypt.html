Can a Grid of Numbers Scramble Letters?

Can a grid of numbers scramble letters? Rung four’s shift moved every letter the same number of places, and fell to anyone patient enough to try all twenty five keys. Later pages swapped single letters by a keyword or a machine, and each time the letters’ own habits gave the secret away: some letters are common, and a common letter stays common in disguise. In 1929 a teacher of mathematics in New York asked a new question. What if the letters were taken two or three at a time, as a list of numbers, and the whole list was sent through a grid? Then one letter no longer has one disguise. The same letter turns into different letters depending on its neighbour. What makes the question hard is that a grid of whole numbers is easy to undo with fractions, and fractions are not letters. This page shows when a grid can be undone with letters alone, using rung four’s remainders and rung twenty-seven’s determinant, and counts the keys. Then it takes the enemy’s side, and finds that a short guessed word gives the whole key away.

Number the letters from zero to twenty five, and read the message two letters at a time, each pair a list of two numbers. Send each pair through a grid of whole numbers and keep the remainders by twenty six: that is the secret pair. Remainders can be taken at any stage of adding and multiplying, so the grid works among letters as it does among numbers. To read, send the secret pair through a second grid that undoes the first. That second grid exists exactly when the key’s determinant shares no divisor above one with twenty six. Then it is the key’s grid of stand-ins, times a number that undoes the determinant among letters. Because the scrambling is straight, two pairs of message with their secret pairs, read off a guessed word, are enough to solve for the key.

27crypt1A Pair of Letters Is an Arrow

As in 4·crypt, a names 0, b names 1, and so on to z, which names 25. A message is written in small letters and its secret form in capitals. From here on, on this page, the remainder by 26 of any whole number n is the number r from 0 to 25 with n − r a multiple of 26. For n below 0, add 26 until it is 0 or more: −3 leaves 23. The key is a grid K of whole numbers, two rows and two columns.

m = 26q + r,\ \ n = 26q' + r' @@ division with remainder (4j)
m + n = 26\,(q + q') + (r + r') @@ regroup
m\,n = 26\,(26qq' + qr' + rq') + r\,r' @@ multiply out: the spread technique
\htmlClass{new}{m + n \text{ leaves what } r + r' \text{ leaves};\ \ m\,n \text{ leaves what } r\,r' \text{ leaves}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each pair differs by a multiple of 26, and one-split gives one remainder (4·crypt)

Remainders-pass-through technique: the remainder by 26 of a sum or a product depends only on the remainders of its parts, so remainders may be taken at any stage. So a grid’s action, made of sums of products, can be done among letters. Here is a chinook salmon run reported in pairs.

\text{ch} = (2, 7) @@ c is 2, h is 7
K = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 8 \\ 11 & 11 \end{pmatrix}:\ \ K\,(2, 7) = (14 + 56,\ 22 + 77) = (70, 99) @@ a grid acts on a list (27d1)
70 = 26 \cdot 2 + 18,\ \ 99 = 26 \cdot 3 + 21 @@ division with remainder
(18, 21) = \text{SV} @@ s is 18, v is 21
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chinookrun} \mapsto \text{SV EX CW YL KZ}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same, pair by pair

The doubled o of chinook came out as C and W: a doubled letter no longer shows. The two n’s came out as X and Z. One letter has many disguises, set by its neighbour. But each pair has one disguise: oo always gives CW under this key, so pairs can still be counted, as 4·crypt counted letters.

27crypt2The Way Back Among Letters

Among numbers, K is undone by K⁻¹ = S / det K, where S is the grid of stand-ins (27g). For a grid with two rows, S swaps the corners and turns the other two entries to their opposites. But det K here is −11, and dividing by −11 gives fractions, which are not letters. Among letters, a number that undoes −11 is enough.

S\,K = K\,S = (\det K)\,I,\ \ S = \begin{pmatrix} 11 & -8 \\ -11 & 7 \end{pmatrix} @@ the stand-in technique (27g); for two rows, multiply out both ways
\det K = 77 - 88 = -11,\ \text{which leaves } 15;\ \ 15 \cdot 7 = 105 = 26 \cdot 4 + 1 @@ 15 shares no divisor above 1 with 26, so some e has 15e leaving 1 (4j): e = 7
K' = 7\,S = \begin{pmatrix} 77 & -56 \\ -77 & 49 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 22 \\ 1 & 23 \end{pmatrix} @@ remainders by 26
K'K = 7\,S\,K = 7\,(-11)\,I = -77\,I,\ \ -77 = 26 \cdot (-3) + 1 @@ grouping-free (27d2); remainders pass through
\htmlClass{new}{K'(Kx) \text{ leaves } x:\ \ K'\,(18, 21) = (912, 501) \to (2, 7) = \text{ch}} \quad \blacksquare @@ SV read back

Letter-undo technique: a key grid can be undone among letters exactly when its determinant shares no divisor above 1 with 26; then the key back is e times its grid of stand-ins, where e times the determinant leaves 1. The lines above work one key. The same steps work for any key whose determinant shares no divisor above 1 with 26, since the plus-only combination technique (4j) then gives e. For the other half, suppose some K′ undoes K among letters. The determinant is made of sums of products of entries, so det(K′K) leaves what det I = 1 leaves. By the det-product technique (27e), det K′ · det K = 26q + 1 for some q. A divisor of det K and of 26 divides det K′ · det K and 26q, so it divides 1 (4j).

So a determinant that is not 0 is not enough. Of the 26 remainders, only 12 share no divisor above 1 with 26: the odd ones other than 13. Of the 456 976 grids with entries from 0 to 25, exactly 157 248 can be undone among letters, about a third. That is far more than the 25 shifts of 4·crypt, and far fewer than the keys of 10·crypt’s scrambled alphabets.

27crypt3A Guessed Word Gives the Key Away

Now take the enemy’s side. A marine forecast for the Strait of Georgia is sent with the key above, and the secret text begins QOFJYIPSEX. Forecasts often begin with the words gale warning. Guess that this one does, as 20·crypt guessed a word in a machine’s secret text. Then five pairs of message and secret text are known: ga to QO, le to FJ, wa to YI, rn to PS, in to EX.

K\,P = C,\ \ P = \begin{pmatrix} 11 & 17 \\ 4 & 13 \end{pmatrix},\ \ C = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 15 \\ 9 & 18 \end{pmatrix} @@ le and rn as the columns of P, FJ and PS as those of C; K acts column by column (27d2)
\det P = 143 - 68 = 75,\ \text{which leaves } 23;\ \ 23 \cdot 17 = 391 = 26 \cdot 15 + 1 @@ 23 shares no divisor with 26: e = 17
P' = 17 \begin{pmatrix} 13 & -17 \\ -4 & 11 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 13 & 23 \\ 10 & 5 \end{pmatrix} @@ the letter-undo technique
K = K\,P\,P' = C\,P' = \begin{pmatrix} 215 & 190 \\ 297 & 297 \end{pmatrix} @@ PP′ leaves I; remainders pass through
\htmlClass{new}{K \to \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 8 \\ 11 & 11 \end{pmatrix}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 215 = 26 · 8 + 7, 190 = 26 · 7 + 8, 297 = 26 · 11 + 11

Known-pairs technique: two pairs of message with their secret pairs give the key, as C times the undo of P, whenever the grid P of the two message pairs can be undone among letters. Not every two pairs will do. The first two, ga and le, give det P = 6 · 4 − 11 · 0 = 24, which shares 2 with 26. The enemy simply tries other pairs from the guessed words. With a key of three rows, three pairs are needed: nine letters of guessed message.

The accepted names

The pair scramble is the Hill cipher, and K its key matrix. Attacking it from guessed words is a known-plaintext attack. The key back is the inverse of K among letters; the accepted names for arithmetic with remainders, and for this inverse, wait for rung 43. The 157 248 keys that can be undone are the invertible 2 × 2 matrices over the remainders by 26. A step that mixes a block of letters by a grid is called diffusion.

Who said it first

Lester Hill, of Hunter College in New York, published the cipher in 1929 in the American Mathematical Monthly, and with Louis Weisner patented a machine of gears in 1932 that scrambled six letters at a time. It saw little use: the guessed-word attack was plain from the start. Its idea lives on. The cipher that guards much of today’s web traffic, made a standard in 2001, mixes four bytes at a time by a grid, and adds a step that is not straight, so that no guessed word can be solved for the key by elimination. In 27·crypt.5.5 the two sides are the hope of building safety from size alone, and the reply that straightness itself is the weakness.
