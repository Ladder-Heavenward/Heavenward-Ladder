7·then · newsflash

Is a Number Too Big to Write Down Still a Number?

Is a number too big to write down still a number? Rung 4 built the counting numbers by a next-one technique, and adding, multiplying and powers by repeating. Repeat powers, and repeat that, and a few strokes of a pen name a number whose digits could not be written on every atom there is. Is it a number at all? Rung 4 says yes, and this page proves things about it, down to its last digit. Then it meets a number that rung 4's repeating cannot reach, which exists only because rung 7 says every statement is true or false. In 2024 a team of volunteers found its fifth value. Nobody expects to find its sixth.

Write a power with an arrow; each extra arrow repeats the one before, so two arrows and a 3 mean a power of a power of a power. Every such expression names a counting number, by recursion. Since nine is less than ten and ten less than twenty-seven, a power of three has between about a third and a half as many digits as its power. A busy beaver is the longest-running rulebook of its size that still stops; it is a number because each rulebook stops or does not.

7then1Arrows

Adding repeats the next one, multiplying repeats adding, and a power repeats multiplying: 4g's ladder of operations. In 1976 the next steps were written with arrows.

From here on, for numbers p and b, p↑b names pᵇ. For n ≥ 1, p↑ⁿ⁺¹b names the value at b of the one function fixed by recursion with start p↑ⁿ⁺¹0 = 1 and step p↑ⁿ⁺¹S(b) = p↑ⁿ(p↑ⁿ⁺¹b). Here ↑¹ is ↑, ↑² is written ↑↑ and ↑³ is ↑↑↑. Read p↑↑b “p arrow arrow b”. p↑↑b is a tower of b copies of p, worked out from the top down.

Arrows-name-numbers technique: for every n ≥ 1, and all numbers p and b, p↑ⁿb is a counting number, and only one.

\varphi(1): b \mapsto p^b @@ 4g: powers are defined for every b
\varphi(n) \Rightarrow \varphi(n + 1) @@ recursion, with start 1 and the step x ↦ p↑ⁿx, a function by φ(n)

7then2How Many Digits

Digits-of-a-power technique: for every k ≥ 1, 3²ᵏ < 10ᵏ < 3³ᵏ. So 3ᵐ has more than k digits when m ≥ 3k, and at most k digits when m ≤ 2k. 9 < 10 < 27, and multiplying by numbers other than 0 keeps order (4h's cancel for times and keep-order for plus). So by induction 9ᵏ < 10ᵏ < 27ᵏ; and 9ᵏ = 3²ᵏ, 27ᵏ = 3³ᵏ by 4g's exponent techniques. In 4i's numerals, a number below 10ᵏ has at most k digits, and one at least 10ᵏ has more than k.

m = 3\uparrow\uparrow 3 = 7\,625\,597\,484\,987 @@ so 3↑↑4 = 3ᵐ
m \ge 3 \cdot 2\,541\,865\,828\,329 @@ 4j: division by 3
3^m \text{ has more than } 2\,541\,865\,828\,329 \text{ digits} @@ digits-of-a-power
m \le 2 \cdot 3\,812\,798\,742\,494 @@ division by 2
3^m \text{ has at most } 3\,812\,798\,742\,494 \text{ digits} @@ digits-of-a-power; the exact count, which a later rung's tools find, is 3 638 334 640 025
\text{one digit per mm: } 2.5 \text{ to } 3.8 \text{ million km} @@ six to ten times the way to the Moon

So 3↑↑4 could be written, with a very long strip of paper. 3↑↑5 = 3^(3↑↑4) has more digits than 3↑↑4 has divided by 3. That count of digits itself runs to trillions of digits. The atoms in the part of the universe anyone can see are thought to number about 10⁸⁰, so 3↑↑5 cannot be written even one digit to an atom. And 3↑↑↑3 is a tower of 7 625 597 484 987 threes.

7then3What Is Known About It Anyway

Last-digit technique: for every n ≥ 1 and b ≥ 1, 3↑ⁿb is odd. For b ≥ 2, 3↑↑b leaves remainder 3 on division by 4 and ends in the digit 7. So 3↑↑↑3 ends in 7.

\text{odd} \cdot \text{odd} = \text{odd},\ \ 3 \text{ odd} @@ 4j's even-or-odd line: (2a + 1)(2b + 1) = 2(2ab + a + b) + 1
3^j \text{ is odd for every } j @@ induction on j; so each 3↑ⁿb with b ≥ 1 is a power of 3, odd
3^{2i+1} = 9^i \cdot 3 \text{ leaves } 3 \text{ on division by } 4 @@ 9 leaves 1, so 9ⁱ leaves 1 (remainders multiply, 4j); times 3
3\uparrow\uparrow b = 3^{\text{odd}} \text{ leaves } 3,\ \ \text{so it is } 3^{4j + 3} @@ the exponent 3↑↑(b − 1) is odd, and leaves 3 by the line above when b − 1 ≥ 1
3^{4j+3} = 81^j \cdot 27 @@ 81 ends in 1, so 81ʲ ends in 1; times 27 ends in 7
\htmlClass{new}{3\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow 3 = 3\uparrow\uparrow(3\uparrow\uparrow 3) \text{ ends in } 7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tower's height, 3↑↑3, is at least 2

Every claim about one digit of 3↑↑↑3 is decidable in 7c's sense. The number is fixed by recursion, and the digits of a fixed number can be found by 4i's method, so a search over a finite set (finite-search) answers it. No either-way principle is needed for it to be true or false. Decidable means a method would answer it, not that anyone could carry the method out. Some mathematicians deny that a number no one could ever write down is there at all; one argued so in the 1960s, and another argues so today. In September 2011 a logician, moved by such doubts, announced a proof that rung 4's arithmetic contradicts itself. Within days two others found the gap, and the proof was withdrawn.

7then4A Number the Arrows Cannot Reach

A rulebook machine works on a strip of cells, each holding 0 or 1, all 0 at the start. It has a head over one cell and n states, one of them the first. Its rulebook says, for each state and each mark under the head, what to write, which way to step, and which state to go to next, or to stop. Such machines were first described in 1936. With n states there are only finitely many rulebooks. Some stop after a while and some run for ever. A rulebook machine has the accepted name Turing machine.

Busy-beaver technique: for each n ≥ 1, among the rulebooks with n states that stop, there is a greatest number of steps taken before stopping. It is named in 1962, and the ladder writes it BB(n), for busy beaver: BB(n) is not 3c's next of n.

\text{finitely many rulebooks with } n \text{ states} @@ each of 2n cases picks a mark, a way and a next state or stop: stages-multiply and counted-functions
\text{each rulebook stops} \lor \text{it does not} @@ either-way, rung 7's principle; “stops” is not known to be decidable: the search over steps may never end
\begin{aligned}&\text{the stopping ones form}\\ &\quad\text{a finite set, with a member}\end{aligned} @@ the sift of a finite set; the rulebook that stops at once is in it
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{BB(n) = \text{the greatest of their step counts}}\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ 4·stat's greatest-of-a-finite-set

So BB(n) is a counting number, and the proof names no way to find it. The second line is the one arrows-name-numbers never needed. BB(1) = 1, BB(2) = 6, BB(3) = 21 and BB(4) = 107, the last found in 1983. In 2024 the bbchallenge project, mostly volunteers, proved BB(5) = 47 176 870, with the proof checked by the computer proof assistant Coq. In 2025 a six-state rulebook was shown to stop only after more than 2↑↑↑5 steps, so BB(6) is past that. And rulebooks with fewer than 750 states are known whose stopping the usual axioms of mathematics can neither prove nor refute. So for some n, BB(n) is a number no proof from those axioms will ever pin down. Rung 7's either-way principle still says it has one value.

The cellar · there if you want it

A rulebook for every n?

It has been proved that no single rulebook can work out BB(n) for every n. The proof is about rulebooks that decide whether other rulebooks stop, and it is a long way up the ladder. Nothing on this page leans on it.

Who said it first

The arrows are Donald Knuth’s (1976). Those who deny the unwritable numbers are ultrafinitists: Alexander Esenin-Volpin in the 1960s, and Doron Zeilberger today. The 2011 claim was Edward Nelson’s; Terence Tao and Daniel Tausk found the gap. Rulebook machines are Alan Turing’s (1936). BB(n) is Tibor Radó’s (1962), who also proved that no rulebook computes it; BB(4) was found by Allen Brady in 1983. The paradise line is David Hilbert’s, about Georg Cantor’s set theory.

Accepted names

p↑↑b is tetration, and the arrows are Knuth’s up-arrow notation. A rulebook machine is a Turing machine. BB(n) is the busy beaver number. The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle.
