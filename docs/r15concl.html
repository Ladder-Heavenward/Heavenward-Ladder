15·∎ · conclusion

Area is squeezed between every estimate

Boxes under a graph give too little, boxes over it give too much, and every too-little sits below every too-much. For a continuous function exactly one number is left between them, and that number is the area.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 15f every continuous curve has an area is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 15 promised to build area where no earlier rung gave a number: cut the interval, stand boxes under the graph and over it, and find the one number caught between. 15a laid down the three area axioms, cut the interval into a partition, and defined the lower sum L(f, P) and the upper sum U(f, P). It proved boxes-trap-the-area: any area obeying the axioms lies between them. 15b proved that a finer cut only tightens both, so every lower sum is below every upper sum.

15c took the sup of the lower sums and the inf of the upper sums. It called f integrable when they meet, wrote ∫ for the common value, and gave the gap-closes test. 15d squeezed the first area, ∫₀¹ x dx = ½. 15e proved that areas add, scale and split, and that a smaller function has a smaller area. 15f found one width that serves a continuous function on a whole closed interval, and with it proved that every such function is integrable.

The claim, in full

For a function continuous on a closed interval, exactly one number lies above every lower sum and below every upper sum, and any area the region under it has must be that number.

Let f be continuous on [u, v].

For any two partitions P and Q of [u, v], L(f, P) ≤ U(f, Q).

f is integrable: the sup of its lower sums equals the inf of its upper sums. That number is ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

If a number A has L(f, P) ≤ A ≤ U(f, P) for every partition P, then A = ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

If also f ≥ 0, and the region under f has an area obeying the three area axioms, then that area is ∫ᵤᵛ f(x) dx.

The proof

The first part needs no continuity. It needs only that f is bounded on [u, v], which 12b's bounded-both-ways gives, so that every mᵢ and Mᵢ exists.

R = \text{the common refinement of } P \text{ and } Q @@ 15b: every point of P and of Q, in increasing order
L(f, P) \le L(f, R) @@ refining-tightens (15b): a smaller piece has an inf at least as large
L(f, R) \le U(f, R) @@ lower-below-upper (15a)
U(f, R) \le U(f, Q) @@ refining-tightens, with R refining Q
\htmlClass{new}{L(f, P) \le U(f, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three lines chained: every-lower-below-every-upper

So the lower sums are bounded above and the upper sums below, and completeness gives the sup of the one and the inf of the other. The sup is at most the inf, by lower-at-most-upper. The second part shows they meet.

\text{fix } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ \eta = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2(b - a)} @@ the gap allowed on each piece
\text{call } \delta:\ |s - t| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(s) - f(t)| \lt \eta @@ evenly-continuous (15f): one width for the whole closed interval
P = \text{the equal partition with } \tfrac{b - a}{n} \lt \delta @@ everyday-counting-passes (8e) finds such an n
M_i - m_i \lt \eta \text{ on every piece} @@ highest-and-lowest (12c) on each piece: both are values of f at inputs closer than δ
U(f, P) - L(f, P) \lt \eta\,(b - a) \lt \varepsilon @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree; the widths add to b − a
\htmlClass{new}{\sup_P L(f, P) = \inf_Q U(f, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ gap-closes (15c): f is integrable, and the common value is ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx

The integral itself lies between every lower and every upper sum: it is the sup of the one and the inf of the other. The third part says nothing else does.

A \ge L(f, P) \text{ and } A \le U(f, P) \text{ for every } P @@ the hypothesis
A \ge \sup_P L(f, P) @@ A is an upper bound of the lower sums, and the sup is the least (no-holes, 8c)
A \le \inf_Q U(f, Q) @@ A is a lower bound of the upper sums, and the inf is the greatest (greatest-lower-bound)
\htmlClass{new}{A = \textstyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second part: both bounds are the integral; three-way-order on ℝ. This is exactly-one-between (15c)
  
A = \text{the area of the region under } f \text{ on } [a, b] @@ the hypothesis: f ≥ 0, and the area obeys the three area axioms
L(f, P) \le A \le U(f, P) \text{ for every } P @@ boxes-trap-the-area (15a): box-area, side-by-side and inside-no-more
\htmlClass{new}{A = \textstyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ the third part

The three axioms never said which number the area is. They only said it sits between the boxes, and the squeeze leaves it one place to sit. For 10d's q the squeeze fails: every lower sum is 0 and every upper sum is 1, and the axioms leave the area undecided.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 15g sums and Σ by hand: It gives Σ its rules and the sum that collapses, then the sums of whole numbers, of their squares and of their cubes, which the squeezes for x² and x³ need.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15h sums of thin strips by hand: It shows that a sum using any height from each piece lands between the lower and upper sums, so it closes in on the same number. With 15g's formulas it finds the areas under x² and x³ exactly.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15i geometric sums: It cuts at points that shrink by a fixed factor, so the boxes shrink by a fixed factor too, and finds the area under every whole power at once. Then it asks when such a sum, never stopping, has a total.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15·chem why does a squeezed gas push back?: It states the law that ties a gas's pressure, volume and temperature, reads it again for the pressure across a membrane, and finds the work a gas does pushing out as the area under its pressure curve.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15·econ what is a payment next year worth today?: 15i's geometric sums price loans, annuities and payments forever, and in-between-value with halving traps the one true rate hidden in a deal.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15·eng how hard does water push on a dam?: It traps the push of still water between strip sums, so the push is an area, and places the whole push one third of the way up, which is why dams thicken toward the bottom.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15·phys where is the balance point of a shape?: It finds the balance point of a plate as one area shared by another, and shows that pieces combine by weighted averages and that a plate can balance on a point that is not on it.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 15·stat how likely is landing in a range?: a histogram whose bars are drawn as areas heads to a curve as the bins narrow, so the chance of landing in a range is the area over it, and the running total is the area so far.its line appears when you finish it.

That is the first area you have measured from nothing but boxes and a squeeze. It is an early step, and a sound one to stand on.
