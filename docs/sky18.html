How Big Can a Set With No Line Be?

How big can a set with no line be? On a grid where each coordinate is an hour of the clock of three, a line is three points evenly spaced, and a set with no line is called a cap. A well known card game with shapes, colours, counts and shadings is played on such a grid of four coordinates, and its largest cap has 20 cards, a fact proved in 1970. As the number of coordinates grows, how large a share of the grid can a cap hold? The turnings of ∞17 showed that the share must shrink, but only slowly, like one over the number of coordinates. In May 2016 a short paper about a grid on the clock of four, followed within days by two papers that carried it to the clock of three, proved that a cap holds an exponentially small share: at most about 2.756 to the power of the number of coordinates, out of 3 to that power. The tool is old and plain. A polynomial that is zero at too many points must be zero everywhere, so a set that avoids a pattern can be caught inside the zeros of a polynomial of small degree, and that limits its size. The same tool had already settled, in 2008, the question of how small a set on a finite grid can be if it holds a line in every direction, and in 2010 it was the key to counting the distances among points in the plane. This page builds the tool, proves the direction question and the cap bound in full, proves the counting of joints of lines that the distance proof rests on, and states what is still open, including whether the same tool can reach the needle question of ∞20.

A nonzero polynomial of degree d in n variables over a field of q members is 0 at no more than dqⁿ⁻¹ points; and fewer than C(n + d, n) points lie in the zeros of some nonzero polynomial of degree d. So a set holding a line in every direction has at least C(q + n − 1, n) ≥ qⁿ/n! points. In (ℤ/3)ⁿ, a cap A makes δ(x + y + z = 0) on A³ a diagonal, of slice rank |A|; the polynomial Π(1 − (xᵢ + yᵢ + zᵢ)²) has slice rank at most 3M, M the count of exponent lists with sum at most 2n/3. So |A| ≤ 3 · 2.756ⁿ.

sky18.1Few Zeros in Many Variables

Over a field F, a nonzero polynomial in one variable of degree d has at most d zeros: if p(r) = 0 then x − r divides p (the remainder-is-value technique), and degrees add, so there is room for at most d such factors. In n variables a polynomial can have infinitely many zeros, xy has the whole of both axes, but on a finite grid it cannot have too many.

Few-zeros technique: if F has q members and P is a nonzero polynomial in n variables of degree at most d, then P is 0 at no more than d qⁿ⁻¹ of the qⁿ points of Fⁿ.

n = 1:\ \text{at most } d = d\,q^0 \text{ zeros} @@ the remainder-is-value technique, degrees add
P = \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{k} x_n^i P_i(x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}),\ \ P_k \ne 0,\ \deg P_k \le d - k @@ sort by the power of the last variable
P_k(x') = 0 \text{ at no more than } (d - k)q^{n-2} \text{ points } x' \text{; times } q \text{ values of } x_n @@ the claim for n − 1 variables
P_k(x') \ne 0:\ P(x', x_n) \text{ is a nonzero polynomial in } x_n \text{ of degree } k\text{: at most } k \text{ zeros} @@ for each of at most qⁿ⁻¹ points x′
\htmlClass{new}{(d - k)q^{n-1} + k\,q^{n-1} = d\,q^{n-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on n

On the grid of the clock of 5, P = xy − 1 has degree 2, and the technique allows at most 2 · 5 = 10 zeros; there are 4, the pairs (1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2), (4, 4). A polynomial of degree below q is therefore never 0 at every point of the grid unless it is the zero polynomial: d qⁿ⁻¹ < qⁿ. That is the form the next footing spends.

Room-for-a-polynomial technique: if S is a set of fewer than C(n + d, n) points of Fⁿ, some nonzero polynomial of degree at most d is 0 at every point of S. The monomials of degree at most d number C(n + d, n) (choices of exponents adding to at most d, by 4k’s count). Sending a polynomial to its list of values on S is a straight map from a space of dimension C(n + d, n) to one of dimension |S|; it reaches at most |S| dimensions, so by the lost-plus-reached technique it loses something other than 0.

sky18.2A Line in Every Direction

Let F be a field of q members (44g). A line in Fⁿ is {x + tv : t ∈ F} for a point x and a direction v ≠ 0. A set K holds a line in every direction if for each v ≠ 0 some line with direction v lies inside K. How small can such a set be? The question, asked in the late 1990s, was whether every such set holds a fixed share of the grid when n is fixed and q grows. It was answered yes in 2008, with a proof of under a page.

Line-in-every-direction technique: a set K ⊆ Fⁿ holding a line in every direction has at least C(q + n − 1, n) ≥ qⁿ/n! points.

|K| \lt \tbinom{q + n - 1}{n} \Rightarrow P \ne 0,\ \deg P = d \le q - 1,\ P = 0 \text{ on } K @@ the room-for-a-polynomial technique, degree q − 1
d \ge 1;\ P_d \text{ the part of } P \text{ of degree exactly } d,\ P_d \ne 0 @@ a nonzero constant is not 0 on K
P(x + tv) = P_d(v)\,t^d + (\text{lower powers of } t) @@ only the top part reaches tᵈ
P(x + tv) = 0 \text{ for all } q \text{ values of } t\text{, degree } \le q - 1 \Rightarrow \text{ all its coefficients are } 0 @@ the line lies in K; the few-zeros technique in one variable
P_d(v) = 0 \text{ for every } v \ne 0, \text{ and } P_d(0) = 0 @@ every direction; d ≥ 1
P_d = 0 \text{ at all } q^n \text{ points, with } d\,q^{n-1} \lt q^n @@ the few-zeros technique
\htmlClass{new}{\bot:\ |K| \ge \tbinom{q + n - 1}{n} \ge \tfrac{q^n}{n!}} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; (q + n − 1)⋯q ≥ qⁿ

On the grid of the clock of 3, with n = 2, there are 4 directions, and the technique gives at least C(4, 2) = 6 points. A search of every set finds the smallest is 7, for instance the first two columns and one more point. The proof never looked at which lines K holds. It caught K inside the zeros of a polynomial too small to hold a line in every direction.

sky18.3Caps on the Clock of Three

Now take F = ℤ/3 and the grid (ℤ/3)ⁿ of 3ⁿ points. A line is {x, x + d, x + 2d}, d ≠ 0: a run of three of ∞17 on a grid that wraps round. A cap is a set with no line. On the clock of three, lines have a symmetric form.

Line-sum technique: three different points x, y, z of (ℤ/3)ⁿ make a line exactly when x + y + z = 0; and if x, y, z lie in a cap and x + y + z = 0, then x = y = z.

y = x + d,\ z = x + 2d \Rightarrow x + y + z = 3x + 3d = 0 @@ 3 = 0 on the clock of three
x + y + z = 0 \Rightarrow z = -x - y = (2y - x) - 3y = y + (y - x) @@ 3y = 0: z − y = y − x, so x, y, z is a line with d = y − x
x = y \Rightarrow z = -2x = x @@ two equal forces all three equal
\htmlClass{new}{x, y, z \in A \text{ a cap},\ x + y + z = 0 \Rightarrow x = y = z} \quad \blacksquare @@ three different ones would be a line in A

A search finds the largest caps for n = 1, 2, 3: 2, 4 and 9 points, out of 3, 9 and 27. For n = 4, the grid of the card game, the largest has 20 of 81. The turnings of sky17.4 work on (ℤ/3)ⁿ with planes in place of runs, and give a share at most about C/n; the shares 2/3, 4/9, 9/27, 20/81 fall, but no fixed power of one over n says how fast. The next two footings show the share falls like (2.756/3)ⁿ.

sky18.4A Diagonal Cannot Be Thin

Take a function T(x, y, z) of three points of a set A, with values in ℤ/3. Call it a slice when it is one function of a single variable times a function of the other two, in any of the three ways: f(x)g(y, z), f(y)g(x, z) or f(z)g(x, y). The slice rank of T is the least number of slices that add up to T. A diagonal is T(x, y, z) = c(x) when x = y = z and 0 otherwise.

Diagonal-slice technique: a diagonal on A with c(x) ≠ 0 for every x has slice rank |A|. At most |A| slices suffice, one c(x)[y = x][z = x] for each x. The point is that fewer will not do.

T = \textstyle\sum_{i \le r_1} f_i(x) g_i(y, z) + \sum_{j \le r_2} f'_j(y) g'_j(x, z) + \sum_{k \le r_3} f''_k(z) g''_k(x, y) @@ suppose r₁ + r₂ + r₃ slices
W = \{w : \textstyle\sum_x w(x) f_i(x) = 0 \text{ for } i \le r_1\},\ \dim W \ge |A| - r_1 @@ r₁ conditions: the lost-plus-reached technique
\text{some } w \in W \text{ is nonzero at } \ge |A| - r_1 \text{ points} @@ the basis of W in steps: add its members, each is 1 where the others are 0
\textstyle\sum_x w(x) T(x, y, z) = w(y)c(y)[y = z] @@ a diagonal grid in y, z: rank = the points where w ≠ 0
= 0 + \textstyle\sum_j f'_j(y) h_j(z) + \sum_k h'_k(y) f''_k(z) @@ the first kind is killed by w; the rest have rank 1 each
|A| - r_1 \le r_2 + r_3 @@ the rank of a sum is at most the sum of the ranks (27c)
\htmlClass{new}{r_1 + r_2 + r_3 \ge |A|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the diagonal-slice technique

For a grid of two variables, slice rank is the rank of 27c, and a diagonal grid with nonzero entries has full rank. The technique says the same holds with three variables, if rank is measured by slices. On A = {0, 1, 2}, the diagonal is three slices and not two.

sky18.5The Cap Bound

Let A be a cap in (ℤ/3)ⁿ and T(x, y, z) = 1 if x + y + z = 0 and 0 otherwise, for x, y, z in A. By the line-sum technique, T is the diagonal with c = 1, of slice rank |A|. Now write T as a polynomial. On ℤ/3, 1 − w² is 1 at w = 0 and 0 at w = 1, 2.

Cap-bound technique: a cap in (ℤ/3)ⁿ has at most 3M points, where M is the number of exponent lists (e₁, …, eₙ), each eᵢ in {0, 1, 2}, with e₁ + ⋯ + eₙ ≤ 2n/3; and 3M ≤ 3 · 2.756ⁿ.

T(x, y, z) = \textstyle\prod_{i=1}^n \big(1 - (x_i + y_i + z_i)^2\big) @@ 1 exactly when every coordinate of x + y + z is 0
T = \textstyle\sum \text{coefficient} \cdot x^\alpha y^\beta z^\gamma,\ \ |\alpha| + |\beta| + |\gamma| \le 2n @@ multiply out; each factor has degree 2 and each variable power at most 2
\text{in each term, one of } |\alpha|, |\beta|, |\gamma| \text{ is at most } \tfrac{2n}{3} @@ the smallest of three is at most their average
\text{terms with } |\alpha| \le \tfrac{2n}{3}\text{, grouped by } \alpha\text{: } \textstyle\sum_\alpha x^\alpha g_\alpha(y, z)\text{, at most } M \text{ slices} @@ likewise for β and γ
|A| \le 3M @@ the diagonal-slice technique
M \le \textstyle\sum_{e} s^{\,e_1 + \cdots + e_n - 2n/3} = \Big(\tfrac{1 + s + s^2}{s^{2/3}}\Big)^n,\ 0 \lt s \le 1 @@ each counted list has a power ≥ 1, the rest ≥ 0; the sum splits coordinate by coordinate
\htmlClass{new}{s = 0.5931:\ |A| \le 3 \cdot 2.7552^n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the least of (1 + s + s²)/s^(2/3), at 4s² + s − 2 = 0

The numbers: for n = 6, M = 168 and the bound is 504, against 729 points; the largest cap in six coordinates is 112. For n = 20 the bound is 11% of the grid, for n = 100 about 0.005%. The share falls exponentially. The best caps known from below hold about 2.22ⁿ points, so the truth for the base lies between about 2.22 and 2.756.

sky18.6Joints, and Distances in the Plane

Over the real numbers the same tool counts how lines in space can cross. A joint of a set of lines in space is a point on three of them whose directions do not lie in one plane. The grid of k × k × k points with the 3k² lines parallel to the axes through it has k³ joints: with k = 10, 300 lines and 1000 joints. The question was whether N lines can have many more than N^(3/2) joints.

Joints technique: N lines in space have at most 7N^(3/2) joints.

J \text{ joints};\ P \ne 0 \text{ of least degree } d \text{ with } P = 0 \text{ on them};\ d \le (6J)^{1/3} + 1 @@ the room-for-a-polynomial technique: C(d + 3, 3) > d³/6
\text{if every line held more than } d \text{ joints, } P = 0 \text{ on every line} @@ P along a line has degree ≤ d and more zeros
\text{at a joint, the slopes of } P \text{ along three directions not in a plane are } 0\text{, so every partial slope is } 0 @@ P(p + tv) ≡ 0 has slope 0 at t = 0 (28c); three such equations
\text{some partial slope of } P \text{ is a nonzero polynomial of degree} \lt d \text{, zero at every joint} @@ P is not constant; over the reals it does not vanish on differentiating
\text{so some line holds at most } d \text{ joints: remove it, and repeat} @@ refute, least degree; a joint is lost only with a line it lies on
J \le N\big((6J)^{1/3} + 1\big) \le 2N(6J)^{1/3} @@ each line removed takes at most d joints
\htmlClass{new}{J \le 2^{3/2}\sqrt6\,N^{3/2} \le 7N^{3/2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ raise J^(2/3) ≤ 2 · 6^(1/3) N to the power 3/2

The grid has 1000 joints for 300 lines, and the bound allows 36 000: the power 3/2 is right, the constant generous. In 2010 the same polynomial ideas, with a polynomial that cuts space into cells, settled a question of 1946 nearly exactly: N points in the plane always make at least about N/ln N different distances. A square grid of N points makes about N/√(ln N); for a 10 × 10 grid, 50 different distances among 100 points. The proof turns pairs of points with equal distances into lines in space and counts how often the lines meet. It is stated here, not proved.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞18.oq.1What is the true base for caps? The largest cap in (ℤ/3)ⁿ has between about 2.22ⁿ and 2.756ⁿ points. The upper base comes from counting monomials; the lower comes from building caps in a few coordinates and multiplying them. Where between the two is the truth?
∞18.oq.2How many pairs of N points in the plane can be exactly a unit apart? A square grid, suitably scaled, gives slightly more than N pairs times any fixed number; the best ceiling, from 1984, is about N^(4/3). The question was asked in 1946. Nobody has closed the gap, and the polynomial tool that settled the different-distances question has not done it.
∞18.oq.3Does the tool reach the needle of ∞20? Over a finite field, a set with a line in every direction holds a fixed share of the grid, by sky18.2. In the plane and in space, a set holding a needle in every direction can have area or volume 0, and the question becomes one of dimension. The finite-field proof does not carry over, because a real polynomial of low degree can vanish on a whole curve. How much of the finite-field idea survives in the real question is open, and ∞20 tells the case of space.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Caps were studied as objects of finite geometry long before the card game gave them a wide audience; the largest cap in four coordinates, 20 points, was found and proved best in 1970. The largest are now known for five coordinates (45) and six (112). For larger n nobody knows the exact answer.

The upper bound was the hard side. In 1995 the turnings of ∞17, with planes in place of runs, gave a share at most about 1/n; in 2012 this was improved to about 1/n^(1 + c) for a small c, by a long and delicate analysis. Whether the share falls exponentially, like a fixed number below 1 to the power n, was open.

In May 2016 three authors, in Atlanta, Haifa and Budapest, posted a short paper proving an exponential bound for the grid of the clock of four, using polynomials. Within days two mathematicians, working apart, each adapted it to the clock of three; they wrote a joint paper of a few pages, published in 2017. Much of the reaction was surprise at how short the proof was. Soon after, a simpler form by slice rank appeared in a blog post, and that is the form of this page.

The polynomial method had come back into combinatorics a decade earlier. In 2008 a graduate student settled the finite-field direction question in a proof of under a page; before that, the best bounds had come from intricate combinatorics and were far weaker. The joints question was settled the same year with polynomials, and in 2010 the different-distances question of 1946 was settled up to a factor of √(ln N), with polynomials that cut space into cells.

What is argued now is how far the method reaches. On the clock of three it gives an exponential ceiling, but no one knows whether 2.756 is near the truth or far above it, and the best caps built by hand and by computer search sit near 2.22. Over the real numbers the finite-field proofs were hoped to point the way to the needle question; how much they did is told in ∞20.

A note on the record: that the slice-rank form first appeared in a blog post has not been revisited against two independent sources for this page; the other dates and numbers here have been.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The few-zeros technique is the Schwartz–Zippel lemma (in this form, also due to Ore and to DeMillo–Lipton). The room-for-a-polynomial technique is parameter counting, or polynomial interpolation through a point set. A set holding a line in every direction is a Kakeya set, and the line-in-every-direction technique is Dvir’s theorem, the finite field Kakeya conjecture. Caps are cap sets; the card game is SET, and the largest caps for n = 4, 5, 6 have 20 (Pellegrino, 1970), 45 and 112 points. The diagonal-slice technique is Tao’s slice-rank lemma; the cap-bound technique is the Ellenberg–Gijswijt bound r₃(𝔽₃ⁿ) ≤ 3 · 2.756ⁿ, after Croot–Lev–Pach for (ℤ/4)ⁿ. The turnings bound for caps is Meshulam’s theorem, improved by Bateman and Katz. The joints technique is the joints theorem of Guth and Katz, in the short proof of Kaplan–Sharir–Shustin and Quilodrán; the different-distances result is the Guth–Katz bound on the Erdős distinct distances problem, and ∞18.oq.2 is the Erdős unit distance problem, with the Spencer–Szemerédi–Trotter bound.

Who said it first

Thomas Wolff asked the finite field Kakeya question; Zeev Dvir proved it in 2008. Roy Meshulam proved the 1/n bound for caps in 1995, and Michael Bateman and Nets Katz improved it in 2012. Ernie Croot, Vsevolod Lev and Péter Pál Pach posted the (ℤ/4)ⁿ bound in May 2016; Jordan Ellenberg and Dion Gijswijt carried it to caps; Terence Tao gave the slice-rank form. Larry Guth and Nets Katz settled the joints problem in 2008 and the distinct distances problem in 2010; Haim Kaplan, Micha Sharir and Eugenii Shustin, and René Quilodrán, gave the short joints proof. Paul Erdős posed both distance problems in 1946. Joel Spencer, Endre Szemerédi and William Trotter proved the N^(4/3) bound in 1984.

A proof that is not allowed

“A cap avoids x + y + z = 0, so the polynomial Π(1 − (xᵢ + yᵢ + zᵢ)²) is 0 on A³ off the diagonal, and a polynomial of degree 2n with that many zeros must be 0; so A is small.” Not allowed: the few-zeros technique counts zeros on the whole grid, not on A³, and the polynomial is not 0 on the diagonal. What makes the proof work is a count of rank, not of zeros: the polynomial is built from few slices, and a diagonal needs many.
