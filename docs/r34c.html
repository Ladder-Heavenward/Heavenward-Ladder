34c · proof

In one piece

Rung 12 proved that a continuous curve on a closed stretch cannot skip a height. The proof leaned on the least upper bound, and it looked like a fact about numbers. This step finds what it was really about. A space is in one piece when it cannot be split into two open parts that share nothing. Stretches of the line are in one piece, by the same least upper bound argument. Continuous maps carry one piece to one piece. Put those two facts together and the in-between values of rung 12 fall out in two lines, for any space in one piece and any continuous map from it to the line. A second way to be in one piece is to be joined by paths, the way a hiker joins two trailheads. Being joined by paths gives one piece. The other way round fails, and the curve that shows it wiggles faster and faster as it nears a line it never reaches by walking.

A space is in one piece when no two separate open parts, neither of them empty, make up the whole of it. Every stretch of the line is in one piece, and a part of the line is in one piece exactly when it is a stretch. The image of a space in one piece under a continuous map is in one piece, so a continuous real map on such a space takes every value between two it takes. A space whose points can all be joined by paths is in one piece. A set squeezed between a set in one piece and its closure is in one piece, so a curve can be in one piece and still not be joined by paths.

34c1One piece

Two parts U and V of a space X split X when both are open, neither is empty, they share no point, and together they make up X. X is in one piece when nothing splits it. Each of U and V is then also closed, being the other’s leftover, so X is in one piece exactly when ∅ and X are its only parts that are both open and closed. Its accepted name is connected.

The line with 0 taken out is split by the negatives and the positives. A space with the scattered family and two points is split by its two points. A space with the lumped family is in one piece, whatever it holds.

Stretch-one-piece technique: every stretch of the line, open, closed or half-open, bounded or not, is in one piece.

I = U \cup V \text{ split},\ \ p \in U,\ q \in V,\ \ p < q @@ suppose a split; name the points so that p is left of q
s = \sup(U \cap [p, q]) \in [p, q] \subseteq I @@ a member, p, and a bound, q (8c); I is a stretch, so it holds [p, q]
s \in U \Rightarrow s < q,\ \ (s - r, s + r) \cap I \subseteq U @@ q is in V; U is open in I (34b5)
\text{then } U \cap [p, q] \text{ holds points above } s @@ a false line: s is an upper bound
s \in V \Rightarrow s > p,\ \ (s - r, s + r) \cap I \subseteq V @@ p is in U; V is open in I
\text{then } s - r \text{ bounds } U \cap [p, q] \text{ above} @@ a false line: s is the least upper bound
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no split: } I \text{ is in one piece}} \quad \blacksquare @@ s is in U or in V, and both cases fail

This is the least upper bound argument of 12a4, with the function taken away. What it used about the line is that it has no holes. The fractions are split by those below √2 and those above.

34c2One piece goes through continuous maps

Image-one-piece technique: if X is in one piece and f is continuous from X, then f(X), as a part of the space f maps into, is in one piece.

f(X) = U' \cup V' \text{ split} @@ suppose a split of the image, as a part
U' = U \cap f(X),\ \ V' = V \cap f(X) \text{ with } U, V \text{ open} @@ open in a part (34b5)
X = f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V) @@ every point lands in U′ or V′
f^{-1}(U), f^{-1}(V) \text{ open, not empty, sharing no point} @@ continuity; U′ and V′ are not empty; they share no point
\htmlClass{new}{f(X) \text{ is in one piece}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a split of X: a false line

One-piece-is-a-stretch technique: a part of the line is in one piece exactly when it is a stretch: whenever it holds p and q, it holds everything between. One way is the stretch-one-piece technique. The other way: if p < c < q with p and q in the part and c not, then the parts below c and above c split it.

f([v, w]) \text{ is in one piece} @@ the image-one-piece technique, with the stretch-one-piece technique for [v, w]
\htmlClass{new}{f(v) < y < f(w) \Rightarrow y \in f([v, w])} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-piece-is-a-stretch: the image holds f(v) and f(w), so everything between

That is the in-between-value technique of 12a, and it now holds for any space in one piece, not only [v, w]. The temperature along a ridge, across a lake, or over the whole skin of the earth takes every value between two it takes. Rung 12’s proof was this one, with a number line where the space should be.

34c3Joined by paths

A path in X from x to y is a continuous map γ from [0, 1] to X with γ(0) = x and γ(1) = y: 18i’s curve traced in time, carried into any space. X is joined by paths when every two of its points have a path between them.

Path-one-piece technique: a space joined by paths is in one piece.

X = U \cup V \text{ split},\ \ x \in U,\ y \in V @@ suppose a split
\gamma : [0, 1] \to X,\ \ \gamma(0) = x,\ \gamma(1) = y @@ X is joined by paths
[0, 1] = \gamma^{-1}(U) \cup \gamma^{-1}(V) @@ every γ(t) is in U or in V
\gamma^{-1}(U) \ni 0,\ \ \gamma^{-1}(V) \ni 1,\ \text{open, sharing nothing} @@ continuity; U and V share nothing
\htmlClass{new}{[0, 1] \text{ is split}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a false line, by the stretch-one-piece technique

Every space of arrows is joined by paths: (1 − t)x + ty runs from x to y. So are balls, the circle, the sphere, and the plane with a point taken out, by going round it. The line with a point taken out is not even in one piece.

34c4One piece, with no path

The closure of a part A of X is the set of points every open set about which meets A: A with its edge added. With a distance, it holds every limit of a settling list from A.

Closure-one-piece technique: if A is in one piece, and A ⊆ B ⊆ the closure of A, then B is in one piece.

B = U \cup V \text{ split, as a part} @@ suppose a split
A \subseteq U \text{ or } A \subseteq V,\ \text{say } A \subseteq U @@ else U ∩ A and V ∩ A split A
b \in V,\ \ V = V' \cap B,\ \ V' \text{ open in } X @@ V is not empty; open in a part
V' \text{ meets } A \subseteq U @@ b is in the closure of A, and V′ is an open set about b
\htmlClass{new}{U \cap V \ne \emptyset} \quad \blacksquare @@ the point met lies in B, in V′, so in V, and in U: a false line

Now the curve. Let S be the points (x, sin(1/x)) for 0 < x ≤ 1, and let T be S with the segment from (0, −1) to (0, 1) added. S is the image of (0, 1] under a continuous map, so it is in one piece. As x heads to 0, sin(1/x) passes every height in [−1, 1] endlessly often, so every point of the segment is in the closure of S. By the technique, T is in one piece.

Sine-curve technique: T is in one piece and is not joined by paths.

\gamma(t) = (x(t), y(t)) \text{ a path in } T,\ \ \gamma(0) = (0, 0),\ \gamma(1) = (1, \sin 1) @@ suppose a path from the segment to the curve
t_0 = \sup\{t : x(t) = 0\},\ \ x(t_0) = 0,\ \ x(t) > 0 \text{ for } t > t_0 @@ the last time on the segment; the segment is closed and x is continuous (34a3)
\text{for every } \delta > 0:\ x \text{ takes every value in } (0, x(t_0 + \delta)] \text{ on } (t_0, t_0 + \delta] @@ the in-between-value technique, on the coordinate x
\text{so } y = \sin(1/x) \text{ takes both } 1 \text{ and } -1 \text{ on } (t_0, t_0 + \delta] @@ 1/x passes π/2 + 2πk and 3π/2 + 2πk there
\htmlClass{new}{y \text{ is not continuous at } t_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ near t₀ it takes values 2 apart; no path

A hiker on T can stand on the segment and see the curve crowd in, close enough to touch at every distance, and never walk onto it. One piece is a property of the open sets. Joined by paths asks for a walk. They are different questions, and for open parts of the plane they turn out to agree.

The accepted names

A space in one piece is connected; joined by paths is path-connected; the pieces are connected components. A stretch of the line is an interval, and the one-piece-is-a-stretch technique is the classification of connected subsets of ℝ. The closure is the topological closure, written with a bar. The space T is the topologist’s sine curve. The in-between-value technique, read this way, is the statement that a continuous image of a connected space is connected.

Who said it first

Georg Cantor defined connected sets by chains of short hops in 1883, which made the fractions connected; he asked the sets to be closed as well. Camille Jordan used the idea for closed bounded sets in 1893. N. J. Lennes in 1905 and 1911, and Felix Hausdorff in 1914, gave the definition by splits used here. The sine curve was a standard example by the 1920s.

A proof that is not allowed

“T is in one piece. A space in one piece is joined by paths, because if it were not, the points joined to a fixed point by paths and the rest would split it.” Not allowed: the points joined to a fixed point need not make an open set. On T, the points joined by paths to (0, 0) are the segment, which is closed and not open in T. The argument works for open parts of the plane, where every point has a ball, and balls are joined by paths.
