7·crypt · cryptography

Can I prove I know a secret without telling it?

Can I prove I know a secret without telling it? Every time you type a password, you hand the secret over to prove you have it. Whoever receives it, and anyone listening in, now has it too. People asked whether there was another way, and the question sounds impossible. To convince someone you know a thing, surely you have to show them the thing. What makes it hard is that the checker must end up sure, and also end up knowing nothing new. This page answers with a cave, a locked door and a coin. The checker never sees the secret. Instead he sets a challenge, again and again, that only someone with the secret can meet every time. Rung 7 does the heavy lifting: whether she knows the secret is true or false, even though the checker cannot look inside her head, so he can reason in each case. The page ends with the strangest part. The checker becomes sure, and yet he could not convince anyone else, because the record of what he saw is something he could have made up on his own.

Set a challenge the prover can meet every time if she knows the secret, and only half the time if she does not. Let a fair coin choose the challenge, after she has committed. Split on whether she knows: in one case she always passes, in the other she passes many rounds only by a long run of luck, whose chance halves with every round. Then ask what the checker saw. If he could have produced the same record alone, with the same chances, the record told him nothing about the secret.

The cave

A cave has one mouth. A little way in, the passage splits in two, a left passage and a right passage. Deep inside, the two passages join again, but where they join stands a locked door. The door opens only to someone who says the secret word. So from the left passage you can reach the right one only through the door.

The prover says she knows the word. The checker wants to be convinced, and she will not say the word. So they play rounds. In each round the checker waits outside, where he cannot see the fork. The prover walks in and takes the left passage or the right one. Then the checker walks to the fork, tosses a fair coin, and calls out a side: left or right. The prover must come out of the side he calls.

If she knows the word, she can always do it. If she is in the called passage, she walks back out. If not, she opens the door and comes out the other side. If she does not know the word, she is stuck in the passage she chose. She comes out the called side only when she happened to choose it.

From here on, on this page, a cheat is a prover who does not know the word, and a round is passed when the prover comes out of the side called.

One round, then many

In one round, the coin has two equally likely calls. A cheat's side is fixed before the call, and exactly one of the two calls matches it. So a cheat passes one round with chance 1/2, by 5·stat's chance as a fraction. Half the time is no proof of anything. So they play again.

Cheat's-chance technique: a cheat passes n rounds in a row with chance 1/2ⁿ, however she plays. She may choose each side by any plan, and her plan may use the calls she has heard so far. The proof counts lists of calls.

2^n \text{ lists of calls, equally likely} @@ counted-functions (4k): a call for each of n rounds, from {left, right}; the coin is fair
\text{her side in round } i \text{ is fixed by the calls before it} @@ her plan: whatever it is, the earlier calls settle it
n = 0:\ \text{one list, the empty one, and it passes} @@ induction on n (4c)
\text{a list of } S(n) \text{ passes} \iff \text{its first } n \text{ pass and the last matches} @@ the rounds are passed in turn
\text{exactly one first part passes, and one of two last calls fits it} @@ the induction, then her side for round S(n) is fixed
\htmlClass{new}{1 \text{ list of } 2^n:\ P(\text{pass}) = \tfrac{1}{2^n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ chance as a fraction

A cheat who tosses a coin of her own to choose her sides does no better. For each way her own coin falls, she passes exactly one list in 2ⁿ, so her chance is 1/2ⁿ whatever her coin does. Ten rounds bring a cheat's chance down to 1/1024. Twenty bring it to 1/1 048 576, about one in a million. Each round halves it.

One failed round settles the matter the other way. A prover who knows the word passes every round. So a prover who fails a round does not know it, by 4e's refutation: suppose she knows; then she passes; she failed; a collision. One failure refutes the claim; no run of passes proves it outright.

She knows, or she does not

The checker cannot look inside her head. Before rung 7, a proof could split into cases only on a statement someone could settle. “She knows the word” is not one the checker can settle, and that is the whole point. With 7a's either-way principle, it is true or false all the same, and 7d's proof by cases may split on it.

\text{she knows} \lor \lnot(\text{she knows}) @@ the either-way principle
\text{case she knows}:\ \text{she passes all } n \text{ rounds} @@ she opens the door whenever she must
\text{case she does not}:\ P(\text{she passes all } n) = \tfrac{1}{2^n} @@ the cheat's-chance technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{she passed } 20:\ \text{she knows, or a } \tfrac{1}{1\,048\,576} \text{ chance came off}} @@ reasoning by cases: the two cases, with what each allows

That is the verdict. It is not “she knows”. It is an or, and one side of the or has a chance attached that the checker chose himself. He can make it as small as he likes by playing more rounds.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The checker's verdict is a rule he lives by: after 20 passed rounds, treat her as knowing the word. It is not certain. It can be wrong, and the page knows exactly how often and which way. It passes a cheat with chance 1/1 048 576. It never fails a prover who knows. The error runs in one direction only.

State the rule, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

The range is part of the rule. The 1/2ⁿ holds only in a cave where the door is the one way between the passages, and only when the calls are fair tosses made after she has chosen. A checker who always calls left, or who calls the side she did not come out of last time, can be read, and a cheat who reads him passes every round. A second, hidden tunnel does the same. Past the edge, the rule fails toward letting cheats through.

A thief tries the cave a million times, once with each of a million stolen claims, and the checker plays 20 rounds each time. How many of the million would you expect to get through?

What the checker learns

The checker is convinced. What has he learned about the word? He saw a list of calls, and the prover coming out of the side called, every time. Could he have produced that same record without her?

He could. Let a friend who does not know the word stand in. The checker films each round. In each round the friend guesses a side, and the checker tosses his coin. When the call misses, they cut that piece of film and play the round again. When it hits, they keep it. After 20 kept rounds the film shows 20 fair calls, each one met. Nothing-learned technique: the record of a real proof could be made, with the same chances, by someone who does not know the word.

\text{real: each call is a fair toss, and is met} @@ she knows the word, so every call is met
\text{faked: a kept round's call is a fair toss that matched a guess} @@ guess and toss each fair; the misses are cut
P(\text{left} \mid \text{kept}) = \tfrac{1/4}{1/2} = \tfrac12 @@ of the 4 equally likely (guess, call) pairs, 2 match, and 1 of them is left (5·stat: chance given that)
\text{each kept round: left or right, } \tfrac12 \text{ each, met} @@ the same in both records
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every list of } n \text{ calls: } \tfrac{1}{2^n} \text{ in both, each met}} \quad \blacksquare @@ chance-product, round by round

So the record carries nothing that needs the word. Whatever the checker could work out from it, he could have worked out from a film he made himself. A proof can convince the one who checks it and tell him nothing else.

It has an odd side effect. Show the film to a friend in Salem, and the friend shrugs. The film could have been cut, and it would look exactly the same. What convinced the checker was not the record. It was that he tossed the coins himself, live, after she had gone in. The proof lives in the doing, and does not travel.

Cards, and keys

The cave is a story, but the shape is everywhere. A friend cannot tell red from green, and holds two cards that look the same to him. You say one is red and one is green. He shows you a card, hides both behind his back, tosses a coin, and swaps the cards or not. Then he shows you a card again, and you say whether he swapped. If the cards were really alike to you too, you would be guessing, and you would be right with chance 1/2. After 15 rounds of being right, the chance of guessing your way through is 1/32 768. He is convinced the cards differ, and he still has no idea which one is red.

Computers use the same idea to show they hold a secret key without sending it. A login of this kind never puts the password on the wire. Since 2016 the digital currency Zcash has used proofs of this family to show that a payment is valid without showing who paid whom, or how much. 4·crypt's shift was beaten by trying all 25 keys. Here there is nothing to try: the checker never sees a single letter of the secret.

The basement · there if you want it

Who said it first

Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff set out proofs that convince and reveal nothing in a paper first circulated in 1985; Goldwasser and Micali received the Turing Award for this line of work in 2012. The cave is from a 1989 paper by Jean-Jacques Quisquater, Louis Guillou and others, “How to explain zero-knowledge protocols to your children”, where the prover and checker are Peggy and Victor and the cave is Ali Baba's. The red and green cards are a common classroom version. The test of a stand-in whose misses are cut is the idea behind the formal definition, where the stand-in is called a simulator. Michael Rabin's test of whether a number is prime (1976, 1980), which can be wrong with a chance you choose, drew the first loud argument over whether a check with a chance of error is a proof. Zcash launched in October 2016.

Accepted names

A proof that convinces and tells nothing more is a zero-knowledge proof. The prover and checker are the prover and the verifier, and the rounds of question and answer make an interactive proof. The call is the challenge. That a knower always passes is completeness; that a cheat rarely passes is soundness, and 1/2ⁿ is the soundness error. The stand-in who makes a cut film is a simulator. A checker who keeps to the rules, tossing fair coins after the prover has chosen, is an honest verifier.
