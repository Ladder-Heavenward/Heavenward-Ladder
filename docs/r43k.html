43k · technique

Continued Fractions

Run the oldest algorithm on a fraction and write down only the quotients. The fraction comes back as a whole number plus one over a whole number plus one over another, a staircase of fractions inside fractions. Do the same to a number that is no fraction, the root of two, and the staircase never ends, and its steps repeat: one, then two, two, two, for ever. Cut the staircase short and you get fractions, and they are rung five’s near misses for the root of two: three halves, seven fifths, seventeen twelfths. They come closer than any fraction with a smaller bottom can. Cut the staircase for pi and you get twenty two sevenths, rung five’s first guess, and then three hundred and fifty five over one hundred and thirteen, good to seven places. Cut it for how many octaves make a twelfth on a string and you get the reason a piano has twelve keys to the octave. And the same staircase for the root of a whole number finds the whole numbers whose squares differ by one times that number, which a great mathematician once set as a challenge to the English.

The quotients of the gcd algorithm write a number as a staircase of fractions. Cut it short and the fractions, the convergents, obey a two-term recursion, and neighbours cross-multiply to differ by one. Each convergent is within one over its bottom squared. A repeating staircase is a root of a quadratic, and the convergents of the root of n find x and y with x squared minus n y squared equal to one.

43k1The gcd Algorithm, Written as a Fraction

4j12 ran on 1071 and 462: 1071 = 462 · 2 + 147, 462 = 147 · 3 + 21, 147 = 21 · 7. Divide each line by its divisor and fold: 1071/462 = 2 + 147/462 = 2 + 1/(462/147) = 2 + 1/(3 + 1/7). From here on, [a₀; a₁, …, a_k] names a₀ + 1/(a₁ + 1/(⋯ + 1/a_k)), the staircase with steps a₀, a₁, …, a_k. Cut after a_j, it gives the convergent p_j/q_j. So 1071/462 = [2; 3, 7], with convergents 2, 7/3 and 51/22.

Neighbour technique: with p₋₁ = 1, q₋₁ = 0, p₀ = a₀ and q₀ = 1, the convergents obey p_j = a_j p_(j−1) + p_(j−2) and q_j = a_j q_(j−1) + q_(j−2), and neighbours cross-multiply to differ by one: p_j q_(j−1) − p_(j−1) q_j = (−1)^(j−1).

[a_0; a_1] = a_0 + \tfrac1{a_1} = \tfrac{a_1 a_0 + 1}{a_1} @@ the recursion for j = 1, with p₋₁ = 1, q₋₁ = 0
[a_0; \ldots, a_j, a_{j+1}] = [a_0; \ldots, a_j + \tfrac1{a_{j+1}}] @@ a last step split in two
\text{so } p_{j+1}/q_{j+1} = \tfrac{(a_j + 1/a_{j+1})p_{j-1} + p_{j-2}}{(a_j + 1/a_{j+1})q_{j-1} + q_{j-2}} = \tfrac{a_{j+1}p_j + p_{j-1}}{a_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1}} @@ induction on j: the recursion holds for any real last step; multiply top and bottom by a_{j+1}
p_{j+1}q_j - p_j q_{j+1} = (a_{j+1}p_j + p_{j-1})q_j - p_j(a_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1}) @@ the recursion
\htmlClass{new}{= -(p_j q_{j-1} - p_{j-1}q_j) = (-1)^{j}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the a_{j+1} terms cancel; induction from p₀q₋₁ − p₋₁q₀ = −1

So every convergent is in lowest terms, since any common divisor of p_j and q_j divides ±1. And neighbours differ by 1/(q_j q_(j−1)): 7/3 − 2 = 1/3, and 51/22 − 7/3 = 1/66.

43k2Staircases That Never End

For a real x, take a₀ = ⌊x⌋; if x is not a₀, take x₁ = 1/(x − a₀), which is above 1, and go on with x₁. For a fraction this is the gcd algorithm, and it stops. For a number that is no fraction it never stops, since a stop would write x as a fraction.

\sqrt2 = 1 + (\sqrt2 - 1),\ \ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2 - 1} = \sqrt2 + 1 = 2 + (\sqrt2 - 1) @@ difference of squares: (√2 − 1)(√2 + 1) = 1
\sqrt2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, \ldots]:\ \ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ \tfrac75,\ \tfrac{17}{12},\ \tfrac{41}{29},\ \ldots @@ the step repeats; the recursion (43k1)
\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2} = [1; 1, 1, 1, \ldots]:\ \ 1,\ 2,\ \tfrac32,\ \tfrac53,\ \tfrac85,\ \ldots @@ x = 1 + 1/x (5g): the step is its own staircase
\pi = [3; 7, 15, 1, 292, \ldots]:\ \ 3,\ \tfrac{22}7,\ \tfrac{333}{106},\ \tfrac{355}{113},\ \ldots @@ from π’s decimal; the steps never repeat, as far as anyone knows
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{22}{7} - \pi \approx 0.00126,\ \ \tfrac{355}{113} - \pi \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-7}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the large step 292 makes the convergent before it very good

The convergents of √2 are 5f’s near misses, and 8·cb5’s how-near technique measured them. 7f’s climb (p, q) ↦ (p + 2q, p + q) is the step from each convergent of √2 to the next: 3/2 to 7/5 to 17/12. The golden number’s convergents are ratios of neighbours in the list 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …, and every step is 1, the smallest a step can be. 5b named 22/7 as π’s first guess; it is the first convergent.

43k3How Close a Convergent Comes

Convergent-bound technique: if x is no fraction, its convergents alternate below and above x, and |x − p_j/q_j| < 1/(q_j q_(j+1)) ≤ 1/q_j².

x = [a_0; \ldots, a_j, x_{j+1}] = \tfrac{x_{j+1}p_j + p_{j-1}}{x_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1}} @@ the recursion with the real last step x_{j+1} (43k1)
x - \tfrac{p_j}{q_j} = \tfrac{p_{j-1}q_j - p_j q_{j-1}}{q_j(x_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1})} = \tfrac{(-1)^{j}}{q_j(x_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1})} @@ common bottom; the neighbour technique
x_{j+1} \gt a_{j+1} \Rightarrow x_{j+1}q_j + q_{j-1} \gt q_{j+1} @@ x_{j+1} is a_{j+1} plus a positive part
\htmlClass{new}{|x - p_j/q_j| \lt \tfrac{1}{q_j q_{j+1}} \le \tfrac1{q_j^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sign (−1)ʲ alternates; q_{j+1} ≥ q_j

More is true, and held in trust: a convergent is the best fraction with a bottom no larger, and any fraction p/q with |x − p/q| < 1/(2q²) is a convergent. So no fraction with a bottom below 16 604 comes nearer π than 355/113; the first that does is 52 163/16 604, and the next convergent is 103 993/33 102. And the golden number, with every step 1, is the number whose convergents come least close: its q_(j+1) grows as slowly as any can.

43k4Twelve Notes to the Octave

6·phys stacked fifths, each the ratio 3/2, and found that no stack lands on a stack of octaves. In equal steps, k fifths come near j octaves when k log₂(3/2) is near j, so when log₂ 3 = 1 + log₂(3/2) is near (j + k)/k. The convergents of log₂ 3 say which numbers of equal steps to the octave fit a fifth well.

\log_2 3 \approx 1.5849625 = [1; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, \ldots] @@ the steps of 43k2, from the decimal
1,\ 2,\ \tfrac32,\ \tfrac85,\ \tfrac{19}{12},\ \tfrac{65}{41},\ \tfrac{84}{53},\ \ldots @@ the neighbour recursion
\tfrac{19}{12}:\ \ 12 \log_2 3 \approx 19.020 @@ 12 steps to the octave: the twelfth, 3 : 1, is 19 steps, 0.02 of a step flat
\tfrac{84}{53}:\ \ 53 \log_2 3 \approx 84.003 @@ 53 steps: off by three thousandths of a step
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the bottoms } 5, 12, 41, 53 \text{ are the equal tunings that fit the fifth best for their size}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the convergent-bound technique

So a piano’s twelve keys to the octave are a convergent: its fifth, seven equal steps, misses 3/2 by about 2 hundredths of a step, the comma 8·cb6 shared out. Tunings of 53 steps were proposed for that reason, and the theory of some music of Turkey uses them; their fifth misses by about 3 thousandths of a step, and they are much harder to play.

43k5Squares That Differ by One Times n

For a whole number n that is not a square, look for whole numbers x and y with x² − ny² = 1. 8·cb5.4.1 climbed for n = 3. The staircase of √n always repeats, and the convergent just before the end of a block gives x² − ny² = ±1. That is held in trust here, proved in 1770 and the years after.

Unit-climb technique: if x₁² − ny₁² = 1 with x₁, y₁ > 0, then the pairs given by x_k + y_k√n = (x₁ + y₁√n)ᵏ all have x_k² − ny_k² = 1. For (x + y√n)(x − y√n) = x² − ny², and a product of two such numbers is one of the same kind, with the sizes multiplying.

\sqrt7 = [2; 1, 1, 1, 4, \ldots]:\ \ 2,\ 3,\ \tfrac52,\ \tfrac83 @@ the block 1, 1, 1, 4 repeats
8^2 - 7 \cdot 3^2 = 64 - 63 = 1 @@ the convergent before the block’s end
(8 + 3\sqrt7)^2 = 127 + 48\sqrt7:\ \ 127^2 - 7 \cdot 48^2 = 1 @@ the unit-climb technique, k = 2
\sqrt{61}:\ \text{a block of } 11 \text{ steps},\ \ 29718^2 - 61 \cdot 3805^2 = -1 @@ an odd block gives −1 first
\htmlClass{new}{(29718 + 3805\sqrt{61})^2 = 1\,766\,319\,049 + 226\,153\,980\sqrt{61}} \quad \blacksquare @@ squaring turns −1 into 1: the least answer for 61

A search by trying y = 1, 2, 3, … for 61 would need over two hundred million tries. The staircase needs eleven steps. The size of the least answer jumps about from one n to the next, with the length of the block, and no simple rule gives the block length from n.

The accepted names

The staircase is a continued fraction, its steps partial quotients, and the cut-off fractions convergents. The neighbour technique is the determinant identity of convergents. The convergent-bound technique is Dirichlet’s approximation for convergents, and “within 1/(2q²) means a convergent” is Legendre’s theorem; the golden number’s worst approximability is Hurwitz’s theorem (1891), with the constant √5. x² − ny² = 1 is Pell’s equation, misnamed by Euler; the least answer is the fundamental solution, and the unit-climb technique generates all positive ones. A repeating staircase is exactly a quadratic irrational: Euler and Lagrange. 53 equal steps is Mercator’s comma tuning.

Who said it first

Brahmagupta (628) solved x² − 92y² = 1 by composing answers, and Bhāskara II’s cyclic method (1150) solved x² − 61y² = 1. Pierre de Fermat set the case 61 as a challenge to English mathematicians in 1657; William Brouncker and John Wallis answered it. Christiaan Huygens used continued fractions to design the gear ratios of a planetarium (1680s). Leonhard Euler named the subject; Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved in 1768 and 1770 that the equation always has an answer and that quadratic roots repeat. 355/113 was found by Zu Chongzhi in the fifth century.

A proof that is not allowed

“The convergents of √2 come within 1/q² and π’s do too, so π, like √2, is a root of a quadratic.” Not allowed: every number that is no fraction has convergents within 1/q². What marks a quadratic root is a staircase that repeats, and π’s, as far as anyone has computed, does not; π is not a quadratic root, by its proof much higher up the ladder.
