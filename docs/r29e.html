29e · proof

Curvature Lives in the Ruler

Roll a sheet of paper into a tube. It bends, but nothing on it stretches: every line drawn on it keeps its length. Now try to wrap the same sheet smoothly round a ball. It wrinkles or tears. Some bending can be done without changing the ruler, and some cannot. This step measures how a curve bends and how a surface bends, and then proves something surprising. One number, the product of a surface’s two main bends, can be found from the ruler alone, with no look at the space around the surface. The tube has that number 0, like the flat sheet. The ball has 1/R². That is why the sheet will not wrap it.

A surface bends in two main amounts at each point, and their product K can be computed from E, F, G and their slopes alone. Surfaces with the same ruler have the same K.

29e1How a Curve Bends

Trace a curve in space at speed 1: γ(s), with s the distance travelled. Its direction T = γ′(s) has length 1. How fast T turns is how sharply the curve bends. Call κ = |T′(s)| the bend of the curve. Accepted name: curvature.

Curve-bend technique: T′ is at right angles to T, a straight line has bend 0, and a circle of radius r has bend 1/r at every point.

T \cdot T = 1 \Rightarrow 2\,T \cdot T^{\prime} = 0 @@ moving-dot (27i): the slope of a constant is 0
\gamma = p + s\,v,\ |v| = 1 \Rightarrow T^{\prime} = 0 @@ a line at speed 1
\gamma(s) = \big(r\cos\tfrac{s}{r},\ r\sin\tfrac{s}{r}\big) @@ the circle at speed 1 (21)
T^{\prime}(s) = -\tfrac{1}{r}\big(\cos\tfrac{s}{r},\ \sin\tfrac{s}{r}\big) @@ slope twice, the chain technique (13, 21)
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa = |T^{\prime}| = 1/r} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bracket has length 1

So a tight curve bends a lot, and a wide one a little. A road engineer reads the bend off a design drawing as one over the radius of the circle that best fits the road there.

29e2How a Surface Bends Along a Direction

On a patch r(u, v), let n = (ru × rv)/|ru × rv|, the unit arrow standing out of the surface. Put L = ruu · n, M = ruv · n, N = rvv · n. The grid [[L, M], [M, N]] is the bending grid.

Normal-bend technique: every curve on the surface through a point, at speed 1, in the direction (u′, v′), bends toward n by exactly L u′² + 2M u′v′ + N v′². The curves that leave in the same direction all agree.

\gamma(s) = r(u(s), v(s)),\ \ \gamma^{\prime} = u^{\prime} r_u + v^{\prime} r_v @@ chains (28c)
\gamma^{\prime\prime} = u^{\prime\prime} r_u + v^{\prime\prime} r_v + u^{\prime 2} r_{uu} + 2u^{\prime}v^{\prime} r_{uv} + v^{\prime 2} r_{vv} @@ chains again, mixed slopes agree (28d)
r_u \cdot n = r_v \cdot n = 0 @@ n is at right angles to both (cross technique)
\htmlClass{new}{\gamma^{\prime\prime} \cdot n = L u^{\prime 2} + 2M u^{\prime}v^{\prime} + N v^{\prime 2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first two terms drop out; L, M, N by name

29e3Two Main Bends, and Their Product

Choose coordinates at a point p so that the surface is a graph over its flat map at p: z = f(x, y), with f(0, 0) = 0 and both partial slopes 0 there (28b, 28f). Then at p, E = G = 1, F = 0, n = (0, 0, 1), and L = fxx, M = fxy, N = fyy. The direction at angle θ has normal bend b(θ) = L cos² θ + 2M cos θ sin θ + N sin² θ.

Surface-bend technique: the normal bends at p range between a largest κ₁ and a smallest κ₂, the main bends, and κ₁κ₂ = LN − M². In any coordinates, this product is K = (LN − M²)/(EG − F²).

b(\theta) = \tfrac{L+N}{2} + \tfrac{L-N}{2}\cos 2\theta + M \sin 2\theta @@ double technique (21): cos² θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2, sin² θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2
\text{largest and least: } \tfrac{L+N}{2} \pm \sqrt{\big(\tfrac{L-N}{2}\big)^2 + M^2} @@ a cos t + b sin t = √(a² + b²) cos(t − t₀), by the difference technique (21) with (a, b) out and round (21·cb20)
\kappa_1 \kappa_2 = \big(\tfrac{L+N}{2}\big)^2 - \big(\tfrac{L-N}{2}\big)^2 - M^2 = LN - M^2 @@ difference of squares
\text{in other coordinates, } g \mapsto J^{\mathsf T} g J \text{ and the bending grid the same way} @@ ruler-change (29d3); for the bending grid, chains give the second slopes as Jᵀ-mixes plus arrows along r_u and r_v, which n drops
\htmlClass{new}{K = \frac{LN - M^2}{EG - F^2} = \kappa_1\kappa_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ det-product (27e), side-turn (27g): det(JᵀAJ)/det(JᵀgJ) = det A / det g

K is the surface bend. Its sign is 28l’s test. Positive: both main bends the same way, a bowl or a dome, like a ball, K = 1/R². Zero: one main bend is 0, like a tube. Negative: they bend opposite ways, a saddle, like z = xy, where K = −1 at the centre.

29e4The Remarkable Technique

L, M and N look outside the surface: they use n. So does K, it seems. It does not.

Remarkable technique: K can be computed from E, F, G and their first and second partial slopes alone.

W = |r_u \times r_v| = \sqrt{EG - F^2},\ \ L = \frac{\det[\,r_{uu}, r_u, r_v\,]}{W} @@ ruler-area (29d2); a · (b × c) written out is the 3 × 3 determinant (27)
(LN - M^2)\,W^2 = \det[\,r_{uu}, r_u, r_v\,]\det[\,r_{vv}, r_u, r_v\,] - \det[\,r_{uv}, r_u, r_v\,]^2 @@ the same for M and N
= \det P - \det Q,\ \ P = A^{\mathsf T}B,\ \ Q = C^{\mathsf T}C @@ det-product (27e) and side-turn (27g); A = [r_uu r_u r_v], B = [r_vv r_u r_v], C = [r_uv r_u r_v]
r_{uu}\cdot r_u = \tfrac12 E_u,\ \ r_{uv}\cdot r_u = \tfrac12 E_v,\ \ r_{uv}\cdot r_v = \tfrac12 G_u,\ \ r_{vv}\cdot r_v = \tfrac12 G_v @@ moving-dot (27i): E_u = 2 r_uu · r_u, and so on
r_{uu}\cdot r_v = F_u - \tfrac12 E_v,\ \ r_{vv}\cdot r_u = F_v - \tfrac12 G_u @@ F_u = r_uu · r_v + r_u · r_uv
r_{uu}\cdot r_{vv} - r_{uv}\cdot r_{uv} = F_{uv} - \tfrac12 E_{vv} - \tfrac12 G_{uu} @@ slope of the last line’s parts; the r_u · r_uvv terms cancel (28d)
P,\ Q \text{ share the lower right } 2\times 2 \text{ block } [[E, F], [F, G]] @@ every other entry is in the three lines above, except the two corners
\det P - \det Q = (P_{11} - Q_{11})(EG - F^2) + (\text{entries from the lines above}) @@ expand both along the first row: the corners meet the same cofactor
\htmlClass{new}{K = \frac{\det P - \det Q}{(EG - F^2)^2} \text{ is built from } E, F, G \text{ and their slopes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the corner difference is the line before last

So a creature in the surface, with a tape measure and nothing else, can find K. Two surfaces with the same ruler have the same K at matching points. The tube and the flat sheet share a ruler, and both have K = 0. A ball has K = 1/R², so no piece of it has the flat sheet’s ruler. That is why every flat map of the earth stretches something.

29e5A Ruler With One Long Direction

Many rulers can be written with E = 1 and F = 0, so ds² = du² + G dv². Then u is distance along curves that leave at right angles to one line, or out from one point. Put g = √G.

Straight-out technique: for the ruler ds² = du² + g² dv², the surface bend is K = −guu/g.

E = 1,\ F = 0:\ \ P_{12} = P_{13} = 0,\ \ Q_{12} = 0,\ \ Q_{13} = \tfrac12 G_u @@ the corner identities of 29e4
\det P = P_{11}\,G,\ \ \det Q = Q_{11}\,G - \tfrac14 G_u^2 @@ expand along the first row
\det P - \det Q = -\tfrac12 G\,G_{uu} + \tfrac14 G_u^2 @@ the corner difference is −½G_uu
G = g^2:\ \ -\tfrac12 g^2(2g_u^2 + 2g\,g_{uu}) + g^2 g_u^2 = -g^3 g_{uu} @@ slopes of g², twice
\htmlClass{new}{K = \frac{-g^3 g_{uu}}{g^4} = -\frac{g_{uu}}{g}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remarkable technique, EG − F² = g²
  
\text{flat, from a point: } g = u \Rightarrow K = 0 @@ polar coordinates (29d3)
\text{sphere, from the pole: } g = R\sin(u/R) \Rightarrow K = 1/R^2 @@ u = R × (the angle from the pole)
\text{disc plane, from the centre: } g = \tfrac12(e^{u} - e^{-u}) \Rightarrow K = -1 @@ 29d4 with ρ in terms of distance u; g is its own second slope

Flat, round and saddle again, as in 29c. The flat plane has K = 0, the sphere K > 0 and the disc plane K = −1 at every point. The three geometries of the parallel question are the three signs of the surface bend.

The accepted names

The bend of a curve is its curvature κ, and 1/κ its radius of curvature. The bending grid is the second fundamental form, with coefficients L, M, N (also written e, f, g). The normal bend is the normal curvature, the main bends are the principal curvatures, and the formula for them in a direction is Euler’s formula (1760). K is the Gaussian curvature. The remarkable technique is Gauss’s Theorema Egregium, “the remarkable theorem”; the formula for K from det P − det Q is the Brioschi formula. Surfaces with the same ruler are isometric.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler found how the normal curvature varies with direction in 1760. Carl Friedrich Gauss defined the curvature of a surface by the area its normals sweep, proved that it depends only on E, F, G, and named the result the Theorema Egregium in his Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas of 1827. Francesco Brioschi put the formula in its determinant form in the 1850s.
