31f · proof

Turn, Stretch, Turn

Symmetric maps have axes. Most maps are not symmetric: a shear has too few stretched vectors, a quarter turn has none that are real, and a map from one space to another of a different dimension has no stretches at all, since a vector and its image do not live in the same place. Yet every map, of any shape, has a pair of right-angled bases that it carries one to the other, stretching as it goes. Find them by feeding the map to its own transpose, which makes a symmetric map whose stretches are never below zero. Their roots are the stretches of the original map. So every grid is a turn, a plain stretch, and another turn. The largest stretch is the most the map can lengthen any vector, and keeping only the few largest stretches gives the closest simple map there is. That is how a large table of numbers is stored small.

Every grid A carries a right-angled basis of its input space to a right-angled basis of its output space, stretching the kth vector by σₖ ≥ 0. The σs are the roots of the stretches of AᵀA. The largest is the most A lengthens any vector, and keeping the r largest gives the closest grid built from r pieces.

31f1Feed a Map Its Own Transpose

Never-negative technique: the stretches of AᵀA are never below 0, and Av = 0 exactly when AᵀAv = 0.

A^{\mathsf T}Av = \lambda v,\ \ |v| = 1 @@ an axis of AᵀA (31d3)
\lambda = \lambda\,v\cdot v = v^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Av = (Av)\cdot(Av) = |Av|^2 @@ the dot as a row times a column
\lambda \ge 0 @@ a squared length
A^{\mathsf T}Av = 0 \Rightarrow |Av|^2 = v^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Av = 0 @@ the same line, for any v
\htmlClass{new}{A^{\mathsf T}A \text{ loses exactly what } A \text{ loses}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and Av = 0 gives AᵀAv = 0 at once

From here on, the stretch sizes of A are σₖ = √λₖ, for the stretches λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λₙ ≥ 0 of AᵀA, written in falling order. The number of σₖ above 0 is the dimension of what A reaches, by lost plus reached (27c2).

31f2Every Grid Is a Turn, a Stretch and a Turn

Turn-stretch-turn technique: for every real grid A with m rows and n columns there are right-angled bases v₁, …, vₙ of the inputs and u₁, …, uₘ of the outputs with Avₖ = σₖuₖ; as grids, A = UΣVᵀ, with U and V length-keepers and Σ zero except for σ₁, σ₂, … down its middle. This Σ (capital sigma) is not 4k's adding mark.

v_1, \ldots, v_n \text{ right-angled axes of } A^{\mathsf T}A @@ the spectral technique (31d3), stretches λ₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λₙ
\sigma_k > 0:\ \ u_k = Av_k/\sigma_k @@ |Avₖ|² = λₖ = σₖ² (31f1), so |uₖ| = 1
u_j\cdot u_k = \tfrac{(Av_j)\cdot(Av_k)}{\sigma_j\sigma_k} = \tfrac{v_j^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Av_k}{\sigma_j\sigma_k} = \tfrac{\lambda_k\,v_j\cdot v_k}{\sigma_j\sigma_k} = 0 @@ for j ≠ k: the vs are at right angles
\sigma_k = 0:\ \ Av_k = 0 = \sigma_ku_k \text{ for any } u_k @@ grow the uₖ found so far to a right-angled basis (27b3, then 31c1)
\htmlClass{new}{AV = U\Sigma,\ \ A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}} \quad \blacksquare @@ column k of each side is Avₖ = σₖuₖ; Vᵀ undoes V (27m1)
  
A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix},\ \ A^{\mathsf T}A = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25\end{pmatrix} @@ symmetric, though A is not
\lambda = 45 \text{ along } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1),\ \ \lambda = 5 \text{ along } \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1) @@ the stretch-test technique (27f1)
\sigma_1 = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.708,\ \ \sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236 @@ the roots
u_1 = \tfrac{Av_1}{\sigma_1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3),\ \ u_2 = \tfrac{Av_2}{\sigma_2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3, -1) @@ Av₁ = (3, 9)/√2, Av₂ = (3, −1)/√2
\htmlClass{new}{u_1\cdot u_2 = \tfrac{3 - 3}{10} = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the right angles survive the map

31f3The Most a Map Can Stretch

Greatest-stretch technique: the most A lengthens any vector is σ₁: |Ax| ≤ σ₁|x| for every x, with equality at x = v₁. From here on, σ₁ is the stretch size of A, written ‖A‖.

x = y_1v_1 + \cdots + y_nv_n @@ the vₖ are a basis; |x|² = y₁² + ⋯ + yₙ² (27i2)
|Ax|^2 = |\textstyle\sum_k y_k\sigma_ku_k|^2 = \sum_k \sigma_k^2y_k^2 @@ the uₖ are right-angled: the square-sum technique (31a1)
\le \sigma_1^2(y_1^2 + \cdots + y_n^2) = \sigma_1^2|x|^2 @@ σ₁ is the largest
\htmlClass{new}{|Ax| \le \sigma_1|x|,\ \ |Av_1| = \sigma_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ take roots

31f4Keep the Largest Pieces

Keep-the-largest technique: ‖A − Aᵣ‖ = σᵣ₊₁, and every grid B that reaches at most r dimensions has ‖A − B‖ ≥ σᵣ₊₁: no simpler grid is closer.

A - A_r = \textstyle\sum_{k > r}\sigma_ku_kv_k^{\mathsf T},\ \ \|A - A_r\| = \sigma_{r+1} @@ itself turned, stretched and turned, with σᵣ₊₁ its largest (31f3)
B \text{ reaches at most } r \Rightarrow B \text{ loses at least } n - r \text{ dimensions} @@ lost plus reached (27c2)
\text{some } x \ne 0 \text{ in what } B \text{ loses and in what } v_1, \ldots, v_{r+1} \text{ build} @@ (n − r) + (r + 1) > n (27b2)
|(A - B)x|^2 = |Ax|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{k \le r+1}\sigma_k^2y_k^2 \ge \sigma_{r+1}^2|x|^2 @@ Bx = 0; x uses only v₁, …, vᵣ₊₁
\htmlClass{new}{\|A - B\| \ge \sigma_{r+1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by |x|
  
A_1 = \sigma_1u_1v_1^{\mathsf T} = 3\sqrt5\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}(1\ \ 1) = \tfrac32\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3\end{pmatrix} @@ the largest piece
A - A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5\end{pmatrix} @@ what is left
\htmlClass{new}{\|A - A_1\| = \sqrt5 = \sigma_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ its AᵀA has rows (2.5, −2.5), (−2.5, 2.5), stretches 5 and 0

A table of numbers 1000 by 1000 needs a million numbers. Kept to its 20 largest pieces it needs 20 × (1000 + 1000 + 1) = 40 020, and the keep-the-largest technique says exactly how much is lost: σ₂₁. Whether σ₂₁ is small is a fact about the table, not about the technique.

The accepted names

Turn-stretch-turn is the singular value decomposition (SVD), the σₖ are the singular values, and the vₖ and uₖ are the right and left singular vectors. The stretch size ‖A‖ = σ₁ is the operator norm (or spectral norm) of A. The ratio σ₁/σₙ is the condition number. The keep-the-largest technique is the Eckart–Young theorem, and Aᵣ is the truncated SVD, the best rank-r approximation. The number of σₖ above 0 is the rank.

Who said it first

Eugenio Beltrami in 1873 and Camille Jordan in 1874 found the decomposition for square grids, independently. James Joseph Sylvester followed in 1889, and Erhard Schmidt carried it to maps on functions in 1907. Carl Eckart and Gale Young proved the keep-the-largest technique in 1936, writing about psychological tests. Gene Golub and William Kahan gave the machine method in 1965, and it is now one of the most used computations there is.

A proof that is not allowed

“A sends v₁ to σ₁u₁ and v₂ to σ₂u₂, so it sends v₁ + v₂ to (σ₁ + σ₂)(u₁ + u₂).” Not allowed: a straight map sends v₁ + v₂ to σ₁u₁ + σ₂u₂. The stretches go with their own vectors and do not add.
