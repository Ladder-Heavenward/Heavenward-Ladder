How Do Gears Change Speed?

How do gears change speed? A motor spins fast and a clock hand creeps. A rider's legs turn the pedals about once a second, and the back wheel must turn faster than that to go anywhere. Between the thing that turns and the thing that must turn lies a row of toothed wheels, and people have cut them by hand for more than two thousand years. What makes the question look hard is that gears come in every size and are packed into every kind of machine. What makes it easy, once you look, is that teeth can be counted. A tooth of one gear pushes exactly one gap of the next, so the two gears pass the same number of teeth. Everything on this page follows from that one count, written as a fraction from rung 5. A gear in the middle of a row turns out to change the direction and nothing else. Gears on one shaft multiply. A bicycle's chain plays the same game with links, and its gears are a short table of fractions.

Count the teeth on the gear that drives and on the gear that is driven. The driven gear turns the first count over the second count of a turn for each turn of the driver, and it turns the other way. Along a row of gears, multiply the fractions: the teeth of a gear in the middle cancel, and each meshing reverses the direction once. Gears fixed on one shaft turn together, so their fractions multiply without cancelling. A chain carries the count the same way, without reversing.

5eng1Teeth Are Counted

Two gears mesh when the teeth of one sit in the gaps of the other. Turn one, and its teeth push the other round. The gear you turn is the driver, and the one it turns is the driven gear. Each gear has a whole number of teeth, all the same shape and the same distance apart.

Take a driver with 12 teeth and a driven gear with 36. Turn the driver once. Its 12 teeth pass the place where the gears meet, and each one pushes one gap of the big gear past that place. So 12 of the big gear's 36 gaps go by: a third of a turn. Turn the driver three times and the big gear makes exactly one turn.

From here on, on this page, a number of turns is a rational with a sign: positive the way a clock's hands go, negative the other way. For a gear named G, n_G is its turns, and g is its count of teeth.

Meshing technique: if a gear with g teeth drives a gear with h teeth, the driven gear makes g/h of a turn the other way for each turn of the driver. The proof is the count of teeth, done on both gears at once.

\text{G turns } n:\ g \cdot n \text{ teeth of G pass the mesh} @@ g teeth go by in each whole turn, and n turns are n of those
\text{each pushes one gap of H past it} @@ teeth and gaps are matched, one for one (4k): one-count gives the same number
h \cdot m = g \cdot n @@ H turns m the other way, and h gaps go by in each of its turns
m = \tfrac{g}{h}\, n @@ multiplying-keeps-answers (5g): times the reciprocal of h, and h ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{n_H = -\tfrac{g}{h}\, n_G} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other way is a negative (5b)

From here on, on this page, the gear ratio of a pair is the fraction g/h: the driven gear's turns for one turn of the driver, sign aside.

The line h · m = g · n says the product of teeth and turns is the same on both gears. That is 5j's inverse proportion: a gear with twice the teeth turns half as fast. A small gear driving a big one slows the turning down. A big gear driving a small one speeds it up. The ratio 12/36 is 1/3, by the scaling technique, so the 36 turns a third as fast. Only the fraction matters: a 20 driving a 60 does the same job.

5eng2A Gear in the Middle

Put a third gear between the two. G drives H, and H drives I. Each meshing is the meshing technique again, and the two lines chain together. Middle-gear technique: a gear in the middle of a row changes the direction, and not the ratio.

n_H = -\tfrac{g}{h}\, n_G @@ meshing: G drives H
n_I = -\tfrac{h}{i}\, n_H @@ meshing: H drives I
n_I = \left(-\tfrac{h}{i}\right)\left(-\tfrac{g}{h}\right) n_G @@ put the first line into the second
n_I = \tfrac{h \cdot g}{i \cdot h}\, n_G @@ two-negatives (5e); fractions multiply (5c)
\htmlClass{new}{n_I = \tfrac{g}{i}\, n_G} \quad \blacksquare @@ scaling technique: h ≠ 0, so it cancels

The middle gear's teeth have gone. H could have 20 teeth or 200, and I would turn the same. What H does is turn I the same way as G. Engineers put such a gear in for exactly that, or to bridge a gap between two shafts. The same holds down a longer row, by induction on the number of meshes (4c): each new meshing multiplies by a negative fraction whose top cancels the bottom before it. Along a row, the last gear turns the first's teeth over its own teeth, the same way after an even number of meshes and the other way after an odd number.

5eng3Gears on One Shaft Multiply

To get a big change of speed, a single pair is clumsy. A ratio of 1/12 from one pair needs a gear with twelve times the teeth of the other. So engineers fix two gears of different sizes on one shaft. They turn together, so they make the same turns. The first is driven by one pair, and the second drives the next pair. Now nothing cancels. Chain technique: gears in a chain multiply their fractions.

A clock does this. The minute hand must turn 12 times for each turn of the hour hand, the same way. On the minute hand's shaft, a 10 drives a 30. On the 30's shaft, an 8 drives a 32, and the 32 carries the hour hand.

n_{30} = -\tfrac{10}{30}\, n_{\text{min}} = -\tfrac13\, n_{\text{min}} @@ meshing; lowest terms (5h)
n_{8} = n_{30} @@ one shaft: the two gears turn together
n_{\text{hour}} = -\tfrac{8}{32}\, n_{8} = -\tfrac14\, n_8 @@ meshing again
n_{\text{hour}} = \left(-\tfrac14\right)\left(-\tfrac13\right) n_{\text{min}} @@ put the lines together
\htmlClass{new}{n_{\text{hour}} = \tfrac{1}{12}\, n_{\text{min}}} @@ two-negatives; 12 = 4 · 3, so two modest pairs do it

Two meshes, so the hour hand goes the same way as the minute hand. The 12 was split into its factors, 3 and 4, and each pair took one. That is how every big ratio is built: split it into factors, one small pair for each.

5eng4Which Teeth Meet

Teeth wear where they touch. If one tooth is chipped, it marks every tooth it meets. With 12 and 36 teeth, a tooth of the big gear meets the same tooth of the small gear every turn of the big gear, and only that one, since 36 = 3 · 12. A wear mark is passed back and forth between the same two teeth for ever. So engineers pick counts that share no factor but 1, such as 13 and 36. Every-tooth technique: if the two counts share no factor but 1, each tooth of one gear meets every tooth of the other.

\text{gcd}(g, h) = 1;\ \text{fix one tooth of H} @@ the counts share no factor but 1
\text{it meets G's teeth after } 0, h, 2h, \ldots, (g-1)h \text{ teeth pass} @@ once for each turn of H; g turns of H
\text{suppose it meets the same tooth after } jh \text{ and } kh,\ j \lt k \lt g @@ to be refuted
g \mid (k - j)h @@ between them, whole turns of G: a multiple of g teeth
g \mid k - j @@ factor-that-shares-nothing (5h)
0 \lt k - j \lt g:\ \text{a collision} @@ divisor-not-past (4j): a divisor of k − j other than 0 is at most k − j
\htmlClass{new}{g \text{ different teeth of G: all of them}} \quad \blacksquare @@ G has only g teeth

The pairs come back in step after lcm(13, 36) = 468 teeth have passed, which is 13 turns of the big gear. That is 5h's lcm, met in metal. The accepted name for the extra tooth that breaks the factor is a hunting tooth.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

Gears are not the only way to pass turning on. A belt round two smooth wheels does it too, and it is quieter and cheaper. But a belt grips by rubbing, and rubbing slips. A belt built for a ratio of 1/3 does not quite give it. The driven wheel falls short by a little, often 1 to 3 percent, more when it is loaded hard. So a belt's ratio is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured statement, with its range and its exceptions. It is the first law on the ladder: a regularity measured, not proved, and trusted only inside the range where the measuring was done. With a motor at 1200 turns a minute, the 1/3 belt gives not 400 but something from about 388 to 396.

State the law, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

Teeth do not slip. The meshing technique is a count, so it holds exactly as long as the teeth hold. Past that edge it does not drift by a percent: a tooth breaks, and the ratio fails all at once. That is why a clock, a gearbox or a printer's paper feed uses teeth, and a fan or a washing machine can use a belt.

5eng5A Bicycle's Gears

On a bicycle the pedals turn a toothed wheel at the front, the chainring. A chain carries the turning back to a small toothed wheel, the cog, fixed to the back wheel. Each tooth of the chainring pulls one link of the chain, and each link pulls one tooth of the cog. So the same count passes at both ends. With f teeth in front and r behind, f · n_pedals = r · n_wheel. A chain does not cross between the wheels as two meshing gears do, so both turn the same way. The back wheel turns f/r times for each turn of the pedals.

A common road bicycle has chainrings of 50 and 34 teeth, and eleven cogs from 11 to 32 teeth. A road tyre of the usual size rolls about 2.136 m along the road in one turn of the wheel. A rider turns the pedals about 90 times a minute. Each pairing gives a distance for each turn of the pedals, and that times 90 is the distance in a minute.

\tfrac{50}{11} \cdot 2.136\ \text{m} \approx 9.71\ \text{m} @@ top gear: the wheel turns 50/11 times for each turn of the pedals
9.71\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{turn}} \cdot 90\ \tfrac{\text{turn}}{\text{min}} \cdot \tfrac{60\ \text{min}}{1\ \text{h}} \approx \htmlClass{new}{52.4\ \tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}} @@ the turns cancel, then 1000 m = 1 km
\tfrac{34}{32} \cdot 2.136\ \text{m} \approx 2.27\ \text{m} @@ bottom gear, for a climb
2.27 \cdot 90 \cdot 60\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{h}} \approx \htmlClass{new}{12.3\ \tfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}}} @@ the same chain of units

On the flat road beside the Willamette, a strong rider may sit in 50/11 and go near 50 km/h. On the road up to Hurricane Ridge the same rider shifts to 34/32. Her legs keep the same 90 turns a minute, and the bicycle goes about a quarter as fast. The gears let the legs keep a comfortable pace while the road changes. Between the two ends lie 22 pairings, and some nearly repeat: 50/16 and 34/11 differ by less than 2 percent.

Who said it first

The oldest gear train known is the Antikythera mechanism, found in 1901 in a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera and made around the second or first century BC. It has at least 30 bronze gears, and their tooth counts carry the cycles of the moon and the sun. Hero of Alexandria, in the first century, described gears that trade speed for force. In the 1090s Su Song built a water-driven clock tower at Kaifeng with a train of gears. Galileo, in his short book on machines (around 1600), set down the law that what a machine gains in force it pays back in speed. Millwrights chose tooth counts that share no factor so that wear would spread, and the extra tooth that does it took the name hunting tooth. The chain-driven bicycle with two wheels of about the same size, John Kemp Starley's Rover of 1885, made the chainring and cog what they are today.

Accepted names

The small gear of a pair is the pinion, and the large one the wheel or gear. The page's gear ratio g/h is also called the speed ratio; many books quote the gear ratio the other way up, as h : g, the driven teeth to the driver's, so that 12 driving 36 is “a reduction of 3 to 1”. A gear in the middle of a row is an idler gear. A row of gears is a gear train; one with two gears on a shaft is a compound gear train. Teeth on a straight bar make a rack, and with its gear a rack and pinion. On a bicycle the cogs together are the cassette, the pedal rate is the cadence, and the distance for one turn of the pedals is the development. A belt with teeth is a timing belt.
