Rung 19 · theory

Undoing the moves

Rung 18 built a place: the plane, with distances. This rung builds the other half of geometry, the moves. A move might shuffle three cards, turn a wheel, or lift a square card and lay it back in its outline. Forget what is being moved and keep only how the moves combine. Three demands are left: two moves in a row make a move, every move can be undone, and doing nothing counts as a move. Anything that meets them is called a group. Five facts follow from the demands alone, and they hold for every group there is.

Shuffling a deck, turning a key, flipping a card: you have made moves all your life. This rung keeps only how they combine. You will find the bare pattern harder than any one example, and better.

A group is a statement that names a set with one way of combining two members into a third, which may be regrouped freely, has a member that does nothing, and has an undo for every member. Then the do-nothing member is the only one, each member’s undo is the only one, undoing two moves in a row means undoing the second one first, the size of a part-group divides the size of the group, and every shuffle is even or odd for good.

The class · read it through, then go to 19a

What it means to say it

Until now the ladder has studied things: sets, numbers, functions, points. A group is something else. It is a family of moves, and all the ladder asks of it is how its members combine. A shuffle of three cards and a turn of a wheel by a third are very different things, but if they combine the same way they make the same group. The claim of this rung is that three demands about combining, with brackets that do not matter, already force a great deal.

Notice what the demands leave out. They do not ask that ab = ba, and for most moves it is false: put on socks and then shoes, and you get a different result from shoes and then socks. They do not ask what the moves move. Everything the rung proves holds for cards, wheels, numbers under adding, and the corners of a square, all at once.

Where it comes from

In 1771 a mathematician in Berlin studied how the values of a formula change when its letters are shuffled, and his counts already showed the dividing the rung proves. An Italian and a French mathematician then studied shuffles for their own sake. A young Frenchman, before his death in 1832, used groups of shuffles to decide which equations have formulas for their roots, and he first used the word group. In 1854 an English mathematician defined a group by how its members combine and nothing else. A French treatise of 1870 made groups a subject. A German mathematician’s proposal of 1872 is where rung 20 goes next, and rung 20's survey tells it.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the ones before.

First, the definition. A group is a statement that names a set with one way of combining two members, in which brackets do not matter, one member does nothing, and every member can be undone (19a3).

Second, there is only one way to do nothing (19b1), and only one way to undo each move (19b2). So you can cancel: ab = ac gives b = c (19b3), and every equation ax = b has exactly one answer (19b3).

Third, to undo two moves you undo the last one first: (ab)⁻¹ = b⁻¹a⁻¹ (19c1). Socks, then shoes; shoes off, then socks.

Fourth, a group inside a finite group has a size that divides the size of the whole. Its shifted copies are all the same size (19d2), never overlap, and cover everything (19d3), so the sizes divide (19d4). So a move done over and over comes back to doing nothing after a number of steps that divides the size of the group (19d5).

Fifth, every shuffle can be made from swaps (19e1), in many ways, but the number of swaps is always even or always odd (19e4). Count the pairs out of order: each swap changes that count by an odd number (19e3).

What follows

The moves that leave a figure looking the same form a group, and they can be counted by hand: label the corners, send one corner somewhere, and the rest is forced. A square has 8, a triangle 6, a regular n-gon 2n. Every technique of the rung can be checked on them. Some puzzles can be proved impossible: the sliding-tile puzzle with 14 and 15 exchanged needs an odd shuffle that its moves can never make.

What you can do because you know it

You can say exactly what a group is, and test whether a family of moves or numbers is one. You can compose shuffles, undo them, and find their orders. You can solve ax = b in any group, and undo a string of moves in the right order. You can list the part-groups of a small group and say which sizes are impossible. You can tell whether a shuffle is even or odd, and prove a puzzle cannot be solved. You can count the symmetries of a polygon or a solid by hand.

That is the first algebra of moves. People who have spent their lives on groups still call themselves beginners, and you have only just begun.

And 19·comp asks how many ways a puzzle cube can be scrambled, treating each twist as a shuffle of the stickers and finding three things no twist can change.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 9’s functions, composing and running backwards, rung 4’s counting and induction, 2g’s classes, and 18a’s points in space. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that moves combine, undo, and include doing nothing, and what you can do because you know it.
