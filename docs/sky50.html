Can a Protein’s Shape Be Read From Its Letters?

Can a protein’s shape be read from its letters? A protein is a chain of small pieces joined end to end, and each piece is one of twenty kinds, so the chain can be written as a word in an alphabet of twenty letters. In water the chain does not stay a loose string. It folds into one shape, the same shape every time, and the shape is what the protein does: a pocket that holds one sugar, a hinge that opens a channel, a clamp that grips another chain. The letters are cheap to read; the shape is costly to see. So the question is whether the letters alone fix the shape, and if they do, whether anyone can compute it. In 1969 a chemist counted the shapes a chain could take and found far more than could ever be tried one by one, though a small chain folds in a few thousandths of a second. Earlier work, written up in 1973, had shown that a chain pulled apart and let go folds back into its working shape with no help, so the letters carry everything needed. Then the problem sat for half a century. A contest begun in 1994 asked groups every two years to predict shapes that had been measured and not yet shown, and for most of that time the predictions were rough. In 2020 a layered learning machine of the kind on the ninth blue sky page, trained on the shapes already known, placed most chains to within about the width of an atom. Its makers shared the chemistry prize of 2024 with a designer of new proteins. This page counts the shapes, counts the wrong ways a chain could close its bridges, finds how deep the bottom of the energy must be for one shape to win, rebuilds a shape from a grid of distances, and says what the machine is trained on, what it gives, and what it still cannot do.

A chain of n links, each turning one of k ways, has kⁿ shapes; with 200 turning angles of 3 settings that is 3²⁰⁰ ≈ 2.7 × 10⁹⁵, more than the atoms anyone can see. Eight bridge-forming letters can pair in 7 · 5 · 3 · 1 = 105 ways, and the refolded chain picks the working one. One shape of energy −ε against W shapes of energy 0 holds the share 1/(1 + We^(−ε/kT)), so it wins once ε > kT ln W. Two distances fix a point up to a mirror, so a grid of distances fixes a shape; the 2018 machine predicted that grid and walked downhill to a shape, the 2020 machine gives the positions with an error near 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

sky50.1Too Many Shapes to Try

Model a chain as n joints, each of which can be set one of k ways. A shape is a choice of setting at every joint: a function from the n joints to the k settings. 4k9 counted those functions.

Shape-count technique: a chain of n joints, each set one of k ways independently, has exactly kⁿ shapes.

\text{shape} = \text{function } \{1, \dots, n\} \to \{1, \dots, k\} @@ one setting per joint
\#\{\text{functions}\} = k^n @@ 4k9, counted functions
\htmlClass{new}{k^n \text{ shapes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ independence is the model’s assumption

Each piece of a protein chain has two angles of turn along its backbone, so a chain of 100 pieces has 200 of them. Give each angle only 3 settings, a coarse guess, and the count is 3²⁰⁰.

3^{200} \approx 2.66 \times 10^{95} @@ 96 digits, checked by script
10^{95} > 10^{80} @@ 7·then: the atoms anyone can see number about 10⁸⁰
3^{200} / 10^{12} \approx 2.66 \times 10^{83}\ \text{s} @@ trying a million million shapes a second
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 8.4 \times 10^{75}\ \text{years}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a year is about 3.16 × 10⁷ seconds

The count of 1969 was cruder still, about 10³⁰⁰. Small chains, measured, fold in a few millionths to a few thousandths of a second. So the chain is not trying shapes one by one. The count is not wrong; the model of a blind search is. Something steers the chain, and the rest of this page is about what.

sky50.2The Letters Decide

Some letters of a protein can join to each other in pairs across the chain, a bridge between two far-apart pieces. In the 1950s and 1960s a laboratory took an enzyme called ribonuclease, with eight such letters joined in four bridges. It broke the bridges and pulled the chain loose with a chemical, so the protein stopped working. Then it took the chemical away and let the bridges close again. The protein worked again.

How surprising is that? Count the ways eight letters can close four bridges.

Pairing-count technique: 2m things can be split into m pairs in exactly (2m − 1)(2m − 3) ⋯ 3 · 1 ways.

m = 1:\ 1 \text{ way} @@ two things, one pair
\text{the first thing has } 2m - 1 \text{ possible partners} @@ any other thing
\text{the other } 2m - 2 \text{ make } (2m - 3) \cdots 1 \text{ pairings} @@ the claim for m − 1
\htmlClass{new}{(2m - 1)(2m - 3) \cdots 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ 4k6, stages multiply
  
m = 4:\ 7 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 105 @@ checked by listing them all, by script
\text{one of them works} @@ the working protein has one pattern of bridges
\htmlClass{new}{\text{closing at random: right 1 time in 105}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ten letters would give 945

When the bridges were closed while the chain was still held loose, the pairings came out scrambled and the protein mostly did not work. When the chain was let fold first, it chose the one right pattern out of 105. The conclusion, written up in 1973: in its usual surroundings the working shape is fixed by the letters alone, and it is the shape of lowest energy, in the sense of 30·phys.1’s free energy. That is the claim the whole field has stood on since. Whether every chain obeys it, and in which surroundings, is not revisited on this page.

sky50.3Rules Between Neighbours

Why count only the angles of turn, and not the lengths? Because joined atoms sit near the bottom of an energy curve (14·chem), and the bottom is steep. 14·chem fitted the twelve-six curve V(r) = ε((r*/r)¹² − 2(r*/r)⁶) to hydrogen, bottom at r* = 74 pm and depth ε = 436 kJ/mol, and found its bend at the bottom, V″(r*) = 72ε/r*².

Stiff-bond technique: on the twelve-six curve, stretching a bond from r* by a fraction f of r* costs about 36εf² of energy, for small f.

V(r^* + h) \approx V(r^*) + \tfrac12 V''(r^*)\, h^2 @@ the slope is 0 at the bottom
\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{72\varepsilon}{r^{*2}} \cdot (f r^*)^2 = 36\varepsilon f^2 @@ h = f r*
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cost} \approx 36\varepsilon f^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the curve’s bend sets the stiffness
  
f = 0.01:\ 36 \cdot 436 \cdot 10^{-4} \approx 1.6\ \text{kJ/mol} @@ a 1% stretch
f = 0.05:\ 36 \cdot 436 \cdot 0.0025 \approx 39\ \text{kJ/mol} @@ a 5% stretch
RT = 8.314 \times 310\ \text{J/mol} \approx 2.6\ \text{kJ/mol} @@ the warmth of a body, 30·phys’s kT for a mole
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a 5\% stretch costs about } 15\, RT} \quad \blacksquare @@ the warmth almost never pays it

So the warmth of a body shakes each bond by a few percent and no more. The lengths are fixed, near enough, and so are the angles between neighbouring bonds, by the same kind of curve. Turning about a bond changes no length at all, and that freedom is what is left. These local rules are what any folding computation obeys first. They do not choose the shape: every one of the 3²⁰⁰ shapes of sky50.1 obeys them.

sky50.4A Landscape With One Deep Bottom

Give every shape an energy. The working shape, by sky50.2, is the lowest. 30·phys.2 shared a body among states in proportion to e^(−E/kT). Take the crudest landscape: one folded shape of energy −ε, and W loose shapes of energy 0.

Fold-share technique: with one shape of energy −ε and W shapes of energy 0, the folded share is 1/(1 + We^(−ε/kT)). It passes one half exactly when ε > kT ln W.

Z = e^{\varepsilon/kT} + W @@ 30·phys.2, the sum over states
\text{share} = \frac{e^{\varepsilon/kT}}{e^{\varepsilon/kT} + W} = \frac{1}{1 + W e^{-\varepsilon/kT}} @@ divide through
\text{share} > \tfrac12 \iff W e^{-\varepsilon/kT} < 1 @@ the bottom of the fraction below 2
\htmlClass{new}{\iff \varepsilon > kT \ln W} \quad \blacksquare @@ take logarithms
  
\ln W = 200 \ln 3 \approx 219.7 @@ sky50.1
\varepsilon/kT = 210:\ \text{share} \approx 6 \times 10^{-5} @@ loose
\varepsilon/kT = 230:\ \text{share} \approx 0.99997 @@ folded
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a change of 20 in } \varepsilon/kT \text{ flips it}} \quad \blacksquare @@ warmth melts the fold

The crowd of loose shapes is the many ways of 30·phys.1, and the fold wins only when its depth beats their number. That explains why a protein comes undone when heated. It does not explain speed: a share is about where the chain ends, not how it gets there. The answer argued since the 1990s is the shape of the landscape. If the energy falls, broadly, toward the folded shape from almost everywhere, a funnel, then the chain runs downhill as 28o1’s walk does, jostled by the warmth, and never meets most of the 3²⁰⁰ shapes. A landscape of random heights would trap it. The letters of real proteins are, on this view, the rare letters whose landscape is a funnel, and evolution kept them.

sky50.5A Shape as a Grid of Distances

A shape can be given without saying where it is: list the distance (18a2) between every two pieces. A chain of 100 pieces has 100 · 99/2 = 4950 such distances. Moving or turning the whole chain changes none of them. Does the grid fix the shape?

Two-distances technique: in the plane, given points A ≠ B and lengths r and s, there are at most two points at distance r from A and s from B, and when there are two they are mirror images across the line AB.

A = (0, 0),\ B = (c, 0),\ c > 0 @@ place the line AB as the first axis
x^2 + y^2 = r^2,\quad (x - c)^2 + y^2 = s^2 @@ the two distances, squared
2cx - c^2 = r^2 - s^2 @@ subtract: the squares of y cancel
x = \frac{r^2 - s^2 + c^2}{2c},\quad y^2 = r^2 - x^2 @@ one x; y is a root or its negative
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y) \text{ and } (x, -y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ none, one, or a mirror pair
  
c = 4,\ r = 5,\ s = 3:\ x = \frac{25 - 9 + 16}{8} = 4 @@ the subtraction
y^2 = 25 - 16 = 9 @@ y = 3 or −3
\htmlClass{new}{P = (4, 3) \text{ or } (4, -3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 18a4’s 3, 4, 5 triangle

So a grid of exact distances rebuilds a chain point by point, once a third point off the line has chosen between the mirror images; in space it takes three points to place the fourth, with a mirror across their plane, held in trust here. A predicted grid is never exact. Then score a guess for the points by how far its distances miss the grid, and walk downhill on the score. For the point above, the score (|P|² − 25)² + (|P − B|² − 9)² is 274 at (3, 1), and a walk with step 0.005 reached (4.00, 3.00) within 100 steps, by script. The machine of 2018 worked this way: it predicted, for every pair of pieces, a spread of likely distances, built a score from them, and walked downhill to a shape.

sky50.6The Machine of 2020

The machine is of sky9.4’s kind: layers of simple units whose links are learned by walking downhill on an error. What it learns from is the record of shapes measured by experiment, about 170 000 of them when it was trained. What it reads is the letters of a chain, together with the letters of related chains in other living things, lined up against it. Two places that change together across those relatives are likely to touch, which is a hint about the grid of sky50.5. A chain with few known relatives gives few hints, and the machine is then much less sure.

What it gives is not a grid then a walk, as the 2018 machine did, but the positions of the atoms directly, with a grid of distances as a side output and its own estimate of how far each part can be trusted. In the contest of 2020 its median score was 92.4 out of 100, where about 90 counts as matching experiment, and its typical error was about 1.6 ångström, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

1.6\ \text{\AA} = 160\ \text{pm} @@ 1 ångström is 100 pm
160 / 74 \approx 2.2 @@ about two hydrogen bond lengths (14·chem)
160 / 382 \approx 0.42 @@ under half the spacing of two clinging argon atoms (14·chem)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about the width of an atom}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the makers’ own comparison

In 2021 the method and its code were published. In 2022 its predicted shapes for more than 200 million chains, nearly every chain whose letters are known, were put in a free public library. A version of 2024 predicts how chains sit together with each other and with other molecules. The machine reads the letters and gives a shape; it does not fold anything, and it says nothing of the path of sky50.4. It learned the answer from answers, which is the anexact rigour of counting: a rule read from many cases, with a stated share of misses, not a derivation from the energy.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞50.oq.1How does a chain move? A working protein is not one still shape: it bends, opens and closes, and some parts never settle. The machine of 2020 gives one shape per chain. Whether a machine can predict the several shapes a chain moves between, and how much time it spends in each, is asked now, and the data to learn it from are far thinner than the 170 000 still shapes.
∞50.oq.2What of a chain with no known relatives? The machine’s best hints come from relatives that changed together. For a chain that is alone, or newly made, those hints vanish and the predictions worsen. Can the shape be computed from the letters and the energy alone, as sky50.4 says it is fixed, without leaning on evolution’s record?
∞50.oq.3Can the question be run backwards? Given a shape wanted, a pocket for one drug or a lock for one virus, find letters that fold into it. Half of the 2024 chemistry prize went to this, and designed proteins now exist that nothing in nature made. How often a design folds as planned, and how to check before making it, is the live question; so is who should be allowed to design what.
∞50.oq.4How do chains fit together? Many proteins work in pairs or crowds, and the 2024 version predicts how they sit together; how far to trust those predictions is still being measured. A machine that says reliably whether two chains bind at all, and how firmly, would read much of a cell’s wiring from its letters. Nobody yet knows how far the method of learning from known shapes can go here.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

That a protein has one definite shape, fixed by its letters, was not obvious. Through the 1950s and 1960s a laboratory studying ribonuclease found that the chain, unfolded with its bridges broken, would fold back to a working protein by itself. The work was recognised with the chemistry prize of 1972, for the connection between the order of the pieces and the working shape, and written up in 1973 as the principle that the shape is set by the letters.

In 1969 a chemist made the count of sky50.1, about 10³⁰⁰ shapes, and asked how a chain could ever find the right one. His own answer was that it does not search: it follows a path, guided by quick local contacts. The puzzle took his name and several generations of answers. In the 1990s the energy landscape view, with its funnel, became the usual frame, though how rugged real funnels are, and which paths chains take, is still studied.

In 1994 a contest began, run every two years: groups were given the letters of chains whose shapes had just been measured and not yet released, and their predictions were scored blind. For two decades the scores crept up. In 2018 a team from a company entered a learning machine that predicted distances, and the 2020 machine came from the same team.

In November 2020 the new machine scored near the accuracy of experiment on most targets, and the organisers called a fifty-year problem largely solved. Not everyone agreed. Critics pointed out that the machine predicts a still shape, not folding; that it rests on the very experiments it was said to replace; and that the contest’s targets were not every kind of protein. The code and method were published in July 2021, and the library of predicted shapes followed in 2022.

In May 2024 the third version, which also places other molecules beside the chain, was published without its code, and many scientists objected that a journal should not print a method nobody can check; the code was released that November. In October 2024 the chemistry prize went half to the design of new proteins and half to the prediction of their shapes. What is argued now is what the machines know: whether they have learned anything of the physics or only the record, and whether a field that once measured shapes should trust shapes it has not measured.

The accepted names

The pieces of the chain are amino acids, twenty kinds, and the list of letters is the protein’s sequence or primary structure; the shape is the tertiary structure, or native fold. The count of sky50.1 is Levinthal’s paradox, and the two angles of turn are the backbone dihedral angles φ and ψ, plotted on the Ramachandran plot. The bridges of sky50.2 are disulfide bonds between cysteines, and the experiment is Anfinsen’s refolding of ribonuclease A with urea and β-mercaptoethanol; the conclusion is Anfinsen’s thermodynamic hypothesis, or Anfinsen’s dogma, Exceptions discussed in the literature are chaperone-assisted folding and intrinsically disordered proteins. The pairing count (2m − 1)!! is the double factorial. The landscape view is energy landscape theory, with its folding funnel. The grid of distances is a distance matrix, and its predicted spread a distogram. The contest is CASP, the Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction, scored by GDT (global distance test). The machines are AlphaFold (2018), AlphaFold 2 (2020) and AlphaFold 3 (2024), from DeepMind; their library is the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, with EMBL-EBI; the related chains lined up are a multiple sequence alignment, and the record of measured shapes is the Protein Data Bank.

Who said it first

Cyrus Levinthal made the count in 1969. Christian B. Anfinsen received the 1972 chemistry prize “for his work on ribonuclease, especially concerning the connection between the amino acid sequence and the biologically active conformation”, and wrote “Principles that govern the folding of protein chains” in Science in 1973. José Onuchic, Peter Wolynes and others set out the energy landscape view in the 1990s (“Theory of protein folding: the energy landscape perspective”, 1997). John Moult and Krzysztof Fidelis founded CASP in 1994. The 2024 chemistry prize went one half to David Baker “for computational protein design” and the other half jointly to Demis Hassabis and John M. Jumper “for protein structure prediction”.
