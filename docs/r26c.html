26c · proof

The logarithm of −1

On the line, the logarithm undoes the growth function, and minus one has no logarithm, because growth is never negative. In the plane, growth turns, and a half turn is minus one. So minus one has a logarithm after all: i times pi. And i times three pi, and i times minus pi, and endlessly many more, one for every whole number of turns. This step finds every logarithm of every number but zero, picks one of them to call the main one, and finds the price: the old law that the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms holds only up to whole turns.

Every complex number but zero has endlessly many logarithms, a whole turn of two pi i apart. Picking the one whose turn lies in one half turn either side of zero gives a single main value.

26c1Every logarithm

From here on, a logarithm of z is any w with e^w = z, and log z names all of them at once.

Plane-log technique: z = 0 has no logarithm; every z ≠ 0, written r e^(iθ), has exactly the logarithms ln r + i(θ + 2πk), one for each whole number k.

e^w \ne 0 \text{ for every } w @@ the growth-and-turn technique (26b2)
z = re^{i\theta},\ r = |z| > 0 @@ out and round (21·cb20.1), with 26a3
e^{\ln r + i\theta} = e^{\ln r}e^{i\theta} = re^{i\theta} = z @@ 26b1; exp undoes ln on the line (17e)
e^w = e^{w_0} \iff w - w_0 = 2\pi i k @@ the whole-turn technique (26b3)
\htmlClass{new}{\log z = \ln|z| + i(\theta + 2\pi k),\ k \in \mathbb{Z}} \quad \blacksquare

With z = −1: r = 1 and θ = π, so log(−1) = iπ + 2πik. The question that rung 17 had to leave without an answer has endlessly many.

26c2The main value

From here on, for z ≠ 0, Log z = ln|z| + iθ with −π < θ ≤ π: the main logarithm. Accepted name: the principal value of the logarithm.

Main-log technique: e^(Log z) = z for every z ≠ 0, and Log agrees with ln on the positive floor; but Log(zw) = Log z + Log w only when the turns of z and w add to an angle in (−π, π].

e^{\operatorname{Log} z} = e^{\ln|z|}e^{i\theta} = z @@ 26c1 with k = 0
x > 0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{Log} x = \ln x + i\cdot 0 @@ the turn of a positive real is 0
\operatorname{Log}(-1) + \operatorname{Log}(-1) = i\pi + i\pi = 2\pi i @@ θ = π for −1
\operatorname{Log}((-1)(-1)) = \operatorname{Log} 1 = 0 @@ 1 has turn 0
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{Log}(zw) = \operatorname{Log} z + \operatorname{Log} w + 2\pi i k,\ k \in \{-1, 0, 1\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ turns add (21·cb20.2); bringing the sum back into (−π, π] takes at most one whole turn

So the main logarithm keeps the old law only up to a whole turn. Just across the negative floor, its turn jumps from near π to near −π. Any choice of a single logarithm must jump somewhere. A loop round 0 always comes back one whole turn out.

26c3Powers with any exponent

From here on, for z ≠ 0 and any complex w, z^w names every number e^(w log z), one for each logarithm of z.

Many-powers technique: zⁿ has one value for whole n; z^(1/n) has exactly n values; and z^w has endlessly many when w is not a fraction.

z^w = e^{w(\operatorname{Log} z + 2\pi i k)} = e^{w\operatorname{Log} z}\,e^{2\pi i k w} @@ 26c1; 26b1
w = n \in \mathbb{Z}:\ e^{2\pi i k n} = 1 @@ whole turns (26b3): one value
w = \tfrac1n:\ e^{2\pi i k/n} \text{ repeats when } k \text{ grows by } n @@ the even-spacing technique (21·cb20.3): n values
e^{2\pi i k w} = e^{2\pi i k' w} \Rightarrow (k - k')w \in \mathbb{Z} @@ 26b3
\htmlClass{new}{w \notin \mathbb{Q} \Rightarrow \text{endlessly many values}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (k − k′)w whole forces k = k′

The strangest case: i^i = e^(i · i(π/2 + 2πk)) = e^(−π/2 − 2πk). Every value of i to the power i is a real number, and the main one is e^(−π/2) ≈ 0.2079.

26c.4.5seamIn 1712 two of the first masters of the calculus argued by letter over this number. One held that it could not be real. The other held that it was 0: (−1)² = 1, so twice it is log 1 = 0; and log(−x) has the same slope as log x. In 1747 a third man took up the second side, in letters to a fourth. In 1749 the fourth answered both: every number other than 0 has endlessly many logarithms, and not one of those of −1 is 0.
Find every logarithm of 1, and every logarithm of −1 doubled. Find whether 0 is among the second.
Find the line of the second side’s argument that fails, and how log(−x) and log x can share a slope and still differ by more than 0.
Were the two sides answering the same question? If −1 has endlessly many logarithms, is log(−1) a number at all?

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

log z is the multivalued complex logarithm and Log z its principal branch; the jump along the negative real axis is its branch cut, and 0 its branch point. z^w = e^(w log z) is the complex power. The plane-log technique is often written log z = ln|z| + i arg z.

Who said it first

Gottfried Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli argued from 1712 about the logarithm of −1: Leibniz held it imaginary, Bernoulli held it equal to log 1 = 0. Leonhard Euler settled it in 1749, showing every number has endlessly many logarithms. Euler also found that i^i is real, in 1746.

In 26c.4.5, the two of 1712 are Leibniz, who held the logarithm of −1 imaginary, and Johann Bernoulli, who held it 0. The third man is Jean le Rond d’Alembert, who took Bernoulli’s side in letters to Euler of 1747 and 1748. Euler’s answer is his paper of 1749 on the controversy between Leibniz and Bernoulli over the logarithms of negative and imaginary numbers, printed in 1751.
