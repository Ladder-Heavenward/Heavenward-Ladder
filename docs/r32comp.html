How does a photo shrink to a small file?

How does a photo shrink to a small file? A phone photo of the Golden Gate in fog has about twelve million pixels, and each has three brightnesses, one each for red, green and blue, each a whole number from 0 to 255. Stored as it is, that is thirty-six million bytes. The file the phone actually keeps is about three million, and nobody looking at it can see what is gone. Something has been thrown away, nine parts in ten, and yet the picture survives. What makes the question hard is that no single pixel can be dropped: a missing pixel is a hole anyone can see. The answer this page gives is to stop storing pixels at all. A short row of pixels is a sum of turnings, and in a photo of the world almost all of a row’s variation sits in its first few turnings. Store those carefully, store the rest roughly or not at all, and the rebuilt row is close to the original in energy. Then the page asks what is lost, and why the eye forgives it.

Reflect a row of eight pixels end to end and it repeats without a jump, so it is a sum of eight cosines, at right angles, and the books balance exactly in energy. For rows of real photos, nearly all the variation sits in the first few cosines: that is a law, measured, not proved. Round each cosine’s amount to a step, coarse for the fast cosines and fine for the slow ones, and most fast ones become zero, which costs almost nothing to store. The error in the row has exactly the energy of the rounding. The eye sees fine stripes poorly, so the coarse steps go where it looks least.

32comp1A row of pixels as cosines

Take a row of N = 8 brightnesses x₀, …, x₇. 32k’s stepped transform would treat the row as repeating, and the last pixel would jump back to the first: a jump, with amounts falling like 1/n (32h3). Reflect the row end to end first, x₀ … x₇ x₇ … x₀, and it repeats with no jump. The reflected row is even about its middle, so it needs cosines alone (32a3).

Mirrored-row technique: a row of N pixels, reflected end to end, is a sum of the N cosines cos(π(2j + 1)k/(2N)), k = 0 to N − 1, which are at right angles; with sizes set to 1, the energy of the row is the sum of the energies of its amounts.

X_k = s_k\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_j\cos\tfrac{\pi(2j + 1)k}{2N},\ \ s_0 = \sqrt{1/N},\ s_k = \sqrt{2/N} @@ the amount of the kth cosine, with its size set to 1
\text{the cosines are the floor parts of 32k’s turnings on the reflected row of } 2N @@ the sampled-turnings technique (32k1), read for an even list
\text{at right angles, each of size 1} @@ 32k1’s geometric sums, paired off by the reflection
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_j x_j^2 = \sum_k X_k^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy-kept technique (32b3), for a right-angled basis of 8

The fast transform of 32k runs the mirrored row in a few dozen steps. A photo is cut into blocks of 8 by 8, and each block into 64 products of a row cosine and a column cosine: the same technique in two directions.

32comp2Most of a row in a few terms

A row of eight grey levels from a photo, a stretch of sky shading into a cable: 52, 55, 61, 66, 70, 61, 64, 73.

X = (177.5,\ -14.4,\ -5.7,\ -6.7,\ 7.1,\ -3.1,\ -0.7,\ 2.4) @@ the mirrored-row technique, by machine
\textstyle\sum x_j^2 = \sum X_k^2 = 31\,852 @@ the books balance
X_0^2 = 31\,500.5 @@ X₀ is the average brightness, 62.75, times √8
\text{variation: } 31\,852 - 31\,500.5 = 351.5,\ \ X_1^2 + X_2^2 + X_3^2 = 286 @@ the first three slow cosines
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the first three cosines hold about } 81\% \text{ of the row’s variation}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 286/351.5

Nothing proves that this happens. A row of random grey levels spreads its energy evenly over all eight cosines, and the trick would fail. Photos of the world are smooth almost everywhere: sky, skin and walls change slowly, and edges are few. So the energy in front is a law, a measured regularity, true of the photos people take and false of noise; and the file format is a bet on it, a guess under the small case that has been checked on billions of pictures.

32comp3Rounding the amounts

Each amount X_k is rounded to the nearest whole multiple of a step q_k: stored is the whole number round(X_k/q_k), and the rebuilt amount is that times q_k. The steps are small for slow cosines and large for fast ones.

Rounding-error technique: rounding each amount to a whole multiple of its step q_k moves it by at most q_k/2, and the energy of the error in the row is exactly the sum of the squared moves.

|X_k - q_k\,\text{round}(X_k/q_k)| \le q_k/2 @@ the nearest whole number is at most ½ away
\text{error row} = \textstyle\sum_k (\text{moved amount})\times(\text{kth cosine}) @@ amounts subtract
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_j(\text{error}_j)^2 = \sum_k(\text{move}_k)^2 \le \sum_k q_k^2/4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the mirrored-row technique, for the error row
  
q = (16,\ 11,\ 10,\ 16,\ 24,\ 40,\ 51,\ 61) @@ a step table of the kind cameras use, coarser to the right
\text{stored: } (11,\ -1,\ -1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0) @@ round(X_k/q_k): five of eight are 0
\text{rebuilt row: } 52.2,\ 55.7,\ 61.1,\ 65.8,\ 67.9,\ 67.2,\ 64.9,\ 63.0 @@ the cosines, added back
\htmlClass{new}{\text{error energy } 144,\ \text{about } 4.2 \text{ grey levels per pixel}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rounding-error technique: √(144/8)

Eight numbers became three small ones and a run of zeros, and a run of zeros is stored as a count. That is most of the shrinking. The error is 4 levels out of 255 on average, largest at the dip in the fifth pixel, where the row turned sharply: the fast cosines that made the dip were rounded away.

32comp4Why the eye forgives

The eye sees coarse stripes well and fine ones poorly. Measured on many people, the faintest stripes anyone can see are at about 3 to 5 stripes per degree of view; by about 50 stripes per degree, even full black-on-white stripes blur to grey. A photo on a phone at arm’s length puts about 40 to 60 pixels in a degree, so the fastest cosine of an 8-pixel block, nearly 4 stripes in 8 pixels, sits at 20 or more stripes per degree, where the eye needs strong contrast to see anything.

So the steps q_k are set by a measured law of the eye, not by a technique: coarse where the eye is weak. The errors of 32·comp.3 are real, and measurable on the stored numbers, but they sit where the eye does not look well. Two losses are not forgiven. Edges ring a little, as 32c’s overshoot said a cut-off sum must, and at strong compression the 8-by-8 blocks show their seams, since each block is rounded on its own.

The accepted names

The mirrored-row technique is the discrete cosine transform (DCT-II, orthonormal), the transform of the JPEG standard; its two-direction form on 8 × 8 blocks is the 2-D DCT. Rounding to steps is quantization, the step table a quantization matrix, and storing runs of zeros run-length coding, followed by Huffman coding. The eye’s law is the contrast sensitivity function. The rings at edges and the block seams are ringing and blocking artefacts. A format that rounds is lossy; one that does not is lossless. Newer formats use transforms on blocks of many sizes, or the brief waves of 31·now.

Who said it first

Nasir Ahmed, with T. Natarajan and K. R. Rao, introduced the discrete cosine transform in 1974. The Joint Photographic Experts Group fixed the JPEG standard in 1992; its step tables came from tests of how much rounding viewers noticed. Fergus Campbell and John Robson measured the eye’s sensitivity to stripes in 1968. The sky row of this page is the first row of the 8 × 8 block used as the worked example in the textbooks on JPEG since the standard appeared.

A proof that is not allowed

“The fast cosines of every row are small, so dropping them loses nothing.” Not allowed: smallness is measured on photos, not proved, and even on photos it fails at edges. The rounding-error technique says exactly what is lost; whether the loss matters is a question about the eye.
