28h · proof

Bowls, and the Price of a Constraint

28g found the best point on a constraint by a multiplier. But a level point may be a top, a bottom or a pass, and a bottom may be only the lowest nearby. For one great family, functions shaped like bowls, every level point is the lowest point of all, and the multiplier gains a meaning: it is the price of loosening the constraint. This step defines bowls, proves that their level points are the lowest, and proves that every price gives a floor under the best value, the floor that is reached exactly in the bowl case.

f is a bowl if every chord lies on or above its graph; then its graph lies above every flat map touching it, so a point with zero uphill vector is the lowest anywhere. For the least f(x) with g(x) ≤ 0, every price λ ≥ 0 gives a floor: min over x of f + λg is at most the best value. For bowls the best floor equals it.

28h1Bowls

From here on, f is a bowl if f(tx + (1 − t)y) ≤ t f(x) + (1 − t) f(y) for all x, y and 0 ≤ t ≤ 1: every chord on or above the graph.

Bowl technique: if f is a bowl with an uphill vector, then f(y) ≥ f(x) + ∇f(x)·(y − x) for all x, y; so a point where ∇f = 0 is the lowest of all.

f(x + t(y - x)) \le f(x) + t\big(f(y) - f(x)\big) @@ the chord, at share t
\dfrac{f(x + t(y-x)) - f(x)}{t} \le f(y) - f(x) @@ take f(x) away, divide by t > 0
t \to 0:\ \nabla f(x)\cdot(y - x) \le f(y) - f(x) @@ the slope along y − x (28c)
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla f(x) = 0 \Rightarrow f(y) \ge f(x) \text{ for every } y} \quad \blacksquare @@ no lower point anywhere

28h2Every Price Gives a Floor

Make f(x) least among x with g(x) ≤ 0; call the best value p*. For a price λ ≥ 0, let d(λ) be the least value of f(x) + λg(x) over all x, constraint or not.

x \text{ allowed} \Rightarrow g(x) \le 0 \Rightarrow f(x) + \lambda g(x) \le f(x) @@ λ ≥ 0
d(\lambda) \le f(x) + \lambda g(x) \le f(x) @@ d is the least over all x
d(\lambda) \le p^* \text{ for every } \lambda \ge 0 @@ least over allowed x
\htmlClass{new}{\max_{\lambda \ge 0} d(\lambda) \le p^*} \quad \blacksquare @@ the best floor is still a floor

Floor technique: for every price λ ≥ 0, the unconstrained least of f + λg is a floor under the constrained best. It holds for any f and g, bowls or not.

28h3In a Bowl the Floor Is Reached

f = x^2,\ g = 1 - x \le 0 @@ p* = 1, at x = 1
f + \lambda g = x^2 + \lambda(1 - x):\ \text{least at } x = \tfrac{\lambda}{2} @@ slope zero; a bowl
d(\lambda) = \lambda - \tfrac{\lambda^2}{4}:\ \text{largest at } \lambda = 2,\ d(2) = 1 @@ the best floor
\htmlClass{new}{\max d = p^* = 1} @@ no gap

When f and g are bowls and some point satisfies g < 0 strictly, the best floor equals the best value, and the best price λ is 28g’s multiplier. The proof is held in trust for a later rung. The price has a meaning: loosen the constraint to x ≥ 1 − ε and the best value falls by about λε = 2ε. Factories, networks and machine learning all solve their problems by finding these prices.

Accepted names

A bowl is a convex function; d(λ) is the dual function; the floor technique is weak duality, its equality strong duality under Slater’s condition; the price is a shadow price or Lagrange multiplier, and the conditions together are the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions.

Who said it first

William Karush (1939) and Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker (1951) gave the conditions with inequality constraints; Morton Slater gave his condition in 1950. John von Neumann saw duality in linear programming in 1947.
