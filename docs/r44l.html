44l · technique

What can’t be built, by hand

Deciding whether a length can be built with compass and straightedge comes down to one polynomial. Find a polynomial with rational coefficients that the length is a root of. Show it cannot be factored. Read its degree. If the degree is not a power of two, the length cannot be built, and that settles it. If it is a power of two, more is needed, and the group of the polynomial decides. This step runs the test on angles, on regular figures and on lengths, and finds the whole list of regular figures up to twenty sides that can be built. It ends with the pentagon, built line by line, as the smallest case where the test says yes for a reason that is not obvious.

Find the polynomial, prove it irreducible, read the degree. A degree that is not a power of two forbids the construction. An angle can be cut in three exactly when the cubic for the cosine of its third factors over the field of its own cosine. A regular figure can be built exactly when the count of hours of the clock of its number of sides that share no factor with that number is a power of two.

44l1The test

Unbuildable-test technique: to show a number cannot be built, find a polynomial with rational coefficients it is a root of, show the polynomial irreducible, and check that its degree is not a power of 2.

c = \cos 40^\circ:\ \ 4c^3 - 3c = \cos 120^\circ = -\tfrac12 @@ cos 3θ = 4cos³θ − 3cosθ (21d)
y = 2c:\ \ y^3 - 3y + 1 = 0 @@ multiply by 2
y = \pm 1:\ \ -1,\ 3 \ne 0 @@ the whole-answer technique: no rational root, so irreducible
\htmlClass{new}{[\mathbb Q(\cos 40^\circ) : \mathbb Q] = 3:\ \ \text{the 9-gon cannot be built}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the degree-three technique

So 120° cannot be cut in three either, and since 40° is a third of 120°, the angle a 9-gon needs is out of reach.

44l2Which angles cut in three

Third-of-angle technique: an angle θ whose cosine k is buildable can be cut in three exactly when 4x³ − 3x − k has a root in ℚ(k)’s tower of square roots; when k is rational, exactly when 4x³ − 3x − k has a rational root. If it has none, it is irreducible over ℚ and the degree is 3.

90^\circ:\ \ 4x^3 - 3x = 0,\ \ x = \cos 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} @@ k = 0: x(4x² − 3); 30° is built from the equilateral triangle (18j)
180^\circ:\ \ 4x^3 - 3x + 1 = (x + 1)(2x - 1)^2,\ \ x = \tfrac12 @@ k = −1; 60° is built
60^\circ:\ \ 8x^3 - 6x - 1,\ \ \text{no rational root} @@ 44i2
\htmlClass{new}{\arccos\tfrac14:\ \ 16x^3 - 12x - 1,\ \text{roots } \tfrac{u}{v},\ u \mid 1,\ v \mid 16:\ \text{none}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ±1, ±1/2, ±1/4, ±1/8, ±1/16 checked: no third of this angle

For a cubic with top coefficient not 1, the whole-answer technique becomes: a rational root u/v in lowest terms has u dividing the last coefficient and v dividing the first. The proof is the same lines, read at both ends.

44l3Which regular figures

Write φ(n) for the number of hours of the clock of n that share no factor with n, the units of 42d. The turn by an n-th has a field of degree φ(n), held in trust for n not prime: 44i proved it for 17, and the same test proves it for every prime.

Regular-figure technique: the regular n-gon can be built exactly when φ(n) is a power of 2. The field of the turn is a splitting field with a turn-about group of φ(n) members, so the two-group technique gives one way and the power-of-two technique the other.

\varphi(n) = 2, 2, 4, 2, 6, 4, 6, 4, 10, 4, 12, 6, 8, 8, 16, 6, 18, 8 @@ n = 3, 4, …, 20, counted
\text{powers of 2}:\ 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 16, 17, 20 @@ build
\text{not}:\ 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 18, 19 @@ cannot
\htmlClass{new}{n = 2^k p_1 \cdots p_j,\ \ p_i \text{ different primes } 2^{2^m} + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ φ(pᵉ) = pᵉ⁻¹(p − 1) is a power of 2 only for e = 1 and p − 1 a power of 2, and then p = 2^(2^m) + 1

The primes 2^(2^m) + 1 known are 3, 5, 17, 257 and 65 537. 2^32 + 1 = 641 × 6 700 417, and no other prime of the form has been found.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

φ is Euler’s totient function. The regular-figure technique is the Gauss–Wantzel theorem, and the primes 2^(2^m) + 1 are the Fermat primes. That the field of the turn by an n-th has degree φ(n) is the irreducibility of the cyclotomic polynomials. The marked straightedge’s move is a neusis.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat conjectured in 1640 that every 2^(2^m) + 1 is prime; Leonhard Euler found the factor 641 of 2^32 + 1 in 1732. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the sufficient condition for the n-gon in 1801 and stated the necessary one; Pierre Wantzel proved it in 1837. Archimedes is credited with the trisection by a marked straightedge. The construction of the pentagon on this step is close to the one in Euclid’s Elements, Book IV.

A proof that is not allowed

“60° cannot be cut in three, so no angle can.” Not allowed: the test is about one angle at a time. 90°, 180° and every angle whose cubic factors can be cut in three. Impossibility in general means: no recipe works for every angle, which one counterexample, 60°, proves.
