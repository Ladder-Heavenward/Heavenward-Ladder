Why Does Riding Faster Take So Much More Push?

Why does riding faster take so much more push? A cyclist who speeds up from 20 to 30 kilometres an hour needs more than three times the power, almost all of it to shove the air aside. This page finds how drag grows with speed without solving any flow at all, only by asking that the units on both sides agree. Rung 17’s logs turn the powers into sums, and the sums into a few small equations. Then the thickness of the fluid joins in, and one number made from four quantities says which story of drag applies, from a cyclist in air to a bead sinking in syrup.

List the quantities drag can depend on and their units. Write drag as a constant times a product of their powers; take logs, so that the powers add, and match the power of each unit on both sides. Solve the small equations for the powers. With one more quantity, find the product of powers that has no units at all; drag divided by its units can depend only on that.

17eng1What Drag Can Depend On

Zoe rides into still air. The air pushes back with a force F, the drag. It can depend on how dense the air is, how fast she goes, and how big she is across. Each comes with a unit made of kilograms, metres and seconds.

From here on, on this page, the powers of a quantity are how many times each of kilograms, metres and seconds appears in its unit: a force has kilograms to the 1, metres to the 1 and seconds to the −2. 5j’s units check says both sides of a true equation carry the same unit.

17eng2Matching the Units

Guess that drag is a constant C, with no unit, times powers of the three: F = C ρa vb Lc. Take logs of the units: the log of a product is a sum (17c), so the powers of each unit add.

\text{kg}^1\text{m}^1\text{s}^{-2} = (\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-3})^a (\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-1})^b\,\text{m}^c @@ units check (5j)
\ln \text{ of each side: } \ln\text{kg} + \ln\text{m} - 2\ln\text{s} = a\ln\text{kg} + (b + c - 3a)\ln\text{m} - b\ln\text{s} @@ log of a product, of a power (17c)
\text{kg: } a = 1,\ \ \text{s: } b = 2 @@ match each unit
\text{m: } -3 + 2 + c = 1,\ \ c = 2 @@ take from both sides (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{F = C\,\rho\, v^2 L^2} @@ drag grows as the square of the speed

Units technique: if a quantity depends on a few others by a product of powers, matching the power of each unit on both sides fixes the powers. No flow was solved: only C is left for an experiment to measure.

17eng3From Push to Power

On a log scale, drag against speed is a straight line of slope 2: doubling the speed adds 2 ln 2 to ln F, so drag grows four times. The power Zoe must give is force times speed.

P = F v = C\rho L^2 v^3 @@ force times speed
\dfrac{P_{30}}{P_{20}} = \left(\tfrac{30}{20}\right)^3 @@ C, ρ and L cancel
\htmlClass{new}{= 1.5^3 \approx 3.4} @@ half again the speed, more than three times the power

Against the air, the push grows as the square of the speed and the power as the cube. That is why a racing cyclist crouches: a smaller L, and a smaller C, buy more speed than stronger legs.

17eng4When Thickness Matters

Air is thin, but syrup is thick: its thickness, the viscosity μ, has unit kg / (m · s). Add it, and five quantities share three units. Look for a product of powers with no unit at all.

\rho^{a} v^{b} L^{c} \mu^{-1}:\ \text{kg: } a - 1 = 0 @@ match each unit to 0
\text{s: } -b + 1 = 0,\ \ \text{m: } -3a + b + c + 1 = 0 @@ so a = 1, b = 1
c = 1 @@ take from both sides (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{Re = \dfrac{\rho v L}{\mu}} @@ no unit: the same in any system of units

Five quantities and three units leave two numbers with no unit: F/(ρv²L²) and Re. When n quantities share k units, any law among them is a law among n − k numbers with no unit. So C is not a constant after all: it can depend on Re, and only on Re.

17eng5Slow Flow and Small Models

For the bead, Re is tiny: the fluid’s thickness rules and its density hardly matters. Leave ρ out and match the units again.

F = C\,\mu^a v^b L^c @@ ρ left out
\text{kg: } a = 1,\ \ \text{s: } -a - b = -2 @@ so b = 1
\text{m: } -a + b + c = 1 @@ so c = 1
\htmlClass{new}{F = C\,\mu v L} @@ in slow flow, drag grows only as the speed

The same law guides models. A model car at a tenth of the size, tested in the same air, behaves like the real one when its Re is the same, which needs ten times the speed. A model tells the truth about the full size when every number with no unit matches.

Accepted names

Matching the powers of units is dimensional analysis, and the powers of a quantity are its dimensions. C, written with a ½ in front by engineers, is the drag coefficient, and Re is the Reynolds number. The rule that n quantities in k units give n − k numbers with no unit is the Buckingham π theorem. Slow-flow drag on a sphere is Stokes’ law, F = 6πμRv, and matching every number with no unit is similitude.

Who said it first

Osborne Reynolds measured flow in pipes in 1883 and found that one number with no unit decides it. George Stokes found the slow-flow drag on a sphere in 1851. Edgar Buckingham stated the theorem in 1914, and Lord Rayleigh urged the method on engineers in “The Principle of Similitude”, 1915.
