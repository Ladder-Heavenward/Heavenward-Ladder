39·now3 · research front

Can a computer tell if a knot is untied?

Can a computer tell if a knot is untied? 34m drew knots, gave three moves that never change the knot, and held in trust that any two drawings of one knot are joined by a string of them. So a machine can try strings of moves. If the drawing is the plain ring in disguise, the search finds the string. If it is knotted, the search runs for ever, and 39d warned what that means. This page shows how a bound on the number of moves, proved in 2015, turns the search into a recipe that always stops, and puts untied in NP. It shows a short certificate the other way for many knots, by colouring strands with any number of colours, and the knots that every such count misses. It ends at the open question: whether a fast test exists.

The drawings of the ring can be listed by trying every string of moves. A bound of (236n)¹¹ moves for a drawing with n crossings makes untied decided, and the string of moves is a certificate, so untied is in NP. Colourings with p colours give a certificate of knottedness for many knots but not all. Whether untied is fast is open.

39now31Listing the drawings of the ring

A drawing with n crossings is a finite thing: which crossings are joined to which, in what order, and which strand is on top. Up to bending the paper, it has finitely many places where a twist, a poke or a slide can be made.

Move-search technique: the drawings of the unknot are listed by a machine.

\htmlClass{new}{D \text{ is }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{printed, }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{so }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{unknot }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{drawings }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{are }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{listed}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 39d’s word: listed

Listing is not deciding. Given a drawing D, the machine can wait for D to be printed. If D is the unknot, it will be. If D is knotted, it never will be, and no moment comes when waiting longer is pointless, unless there is a bound on how long a string must be. The bound cannot simply be the crossings of D: some drawings of the ring cannot be untied without first adding crossings, as a tangled garden hose sometimes has to be made worse before it comes free.

39now32A bound on the moves

In 2015 it was proved, by a long argument about surfaces whose edge is the knot, that a drawing of the unknot with n crossings can be turned into the ring by at most (236n)¹¹ moves. That bound is held in trust here.

Bounded-untying technique: whether a drawing is the unknot is decided, and it is in NP.

The first proof that untied is decided, in 1961, went another way: it searched among surfaces whose edge is the knot for a disc, and showed the search could be bounded. It was so long that it was decades before anyone wrote it as a program. Programs used today, which simplify drawings and search among surfaces, settle drawings with hundreds of crossings in seconds. None is proved fast.

39now33Colours as a certificate of a knot

34m coloured strands with three colours, and 34m.5.4 wrote the rule with labels 0, 1, 2: at each crossing the three labels add to a multiple of 3. Write it instead as: twice the over label, less the two under labels, is a multiple of 3. That is the same rule, since the two differ by three times the over label, and it works for any number of labels. For a whole number p ≥ 3, a p-colouring labels each strand by 0, 1, …, p − 1 so that at every crossing twice the over label less the two under labels is a multiple of p. Let cₚ(D) be the number of them.

Many-colour technique: cₚ(D) is the same for every drawing of a knot, and cₚ is p for the unknot; so a p-colouring that is not all alike is a certificate that a drawing is knotted.

\text{twist: }\allowbreak \text{over } a\text{, }\allowbreak \text{under }\allowbreak \text{pieces } a \text{ and } b\text{: } 2a - a - b = a - b @@ a multiple of p, and 0 ≤ a, b < p, so b = a: one label in, one out
\text{poke: }\allowbreak \text{over } r\text{, }\allowbreak \text{outer }\allowbreak \text{pieces } q \text{ and } q\prime @@ the middle piece is forced: the remainder of 2r − q on dividing by p
q\prime = 2r - (2r - q) = q \text{, }\allowbreak \text{up }\allowbreak \text{to }\allowbreak \text{a }\allowbreak \text{multiple }\allowbreak \text{of } p @@ the second crossing; so the outer pieces match and join as one strand
\text{slide: }\allowbreak \text{over } c\text{; } b \text{ over } a @@ the moving strand passes under both

So many knots carry a short certificate of being knotted: one colouring. Not all. There is a knot with ten crossings that has only its p all-alike colourings for every p, so every colour count calls it the ring. 34m’s finer count X does see it, but X is not known to see every knot: there are different knots with the same X, such as the star with five crossings of 34m.5.2 and a knot with ten crossings. A certificate for every knot came later: in 2014, one was shown to exist assuming an unproved hypothesis about where the zeros of certain sums lie, and a proof without that hypothesis was announced in 2016. With both, untied sits in NP and its outside sits in NP too, which by 39·now2 makes it unlikely to be among the hardest questions of NP.

39now34What is open

Untied is decided, has a certificate both ways, and is settled fast in practice. Whether it is fast, by a method with a fixed power of n as its bound on every drawing, is open. In 2021 a method was announced whose bound is n raised to a power that grows like the number of digits of n: slower than any fixed power, far faster than 2ⁿ. As far as this page knows, its full proof is still being checked.

  • Whether 34m’s count X equals 1 only for the unknot, the question of 34m.5.3, is open. A finer count built from the same openings of crossings was proved in 2011 to see the unknot exactly, by an argument from another part of mathematics.
  • Telling whether two drawings show the same knot, not only whether one is the ring, is decided too, by a bound on moves proved in 2014. That bound is a tower of powers of 2, far beyond (236n)¹¹.
  • Tables of knots are now made by machine. There are 249 knots with 3 to 10 crossings that are not two knots joined end to end, and 1,701,936 with at most 16, counted in 1998. Each table is checked by computing counts of the kind on this page, until every pair is told apart.
  • One dimension up, the answer turns. In 1958 it was shown that no machine decides whether two shapes in four dimensions, given by lists of their pieces, are the same, by carrying into shapes a question that 39e’s reduction-carries technique had already shown no machine decides.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Deciding whether a drawing is the unknot is the unknot recognition problem. The move-search technique is a search through Reidemeister moves, and it shows the unknot is recursively enumerable. The bound (236n)¹¹ is Marc Lackenby’s (2015), and with it the bounded-untying technique shows unknot recognition is in NP, first proved by Joel Hass, Jeffrey Lagarias and Nicholas Pippenger (1999) with normal surfaces. The 1961 recipe is Wolfgang Haken’s, by normal surfaces. A p-colouring is a Fox p-colouring; nontrivial ones exist exactly when p shares a factor with the knot’s determinant, 3 for the trefoil and 5 for the figure-eight. The ten-crossing knot with determinant 1 is 10₁₂₄, the torus knot T(3,5). The cinquefoil 5₁ and 10₁₃₂ share a Jones polynomial. The certificate of knottedness of 2014 is Greg Kuperberg’s, assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis; Lackenby announced one without it in 2016, putting unknot recognition in NP ∩ co-NP. The 2021 announcement, a quasi-polynomial-time algorithm, is Lackenby’s. The finer count of 2011 is Khovanov homology, shown to detect the unknot by Peter Kronheimer and Tomasz Mrowka. The bound for any two knots is Alexander Coward and Lackenby’s (2014). The table to 16 crossings is by Jim Hoste, Morwen Thistlethwaite and Jeff Weeks (1998). The four-dimensional result is A. A. Markov’s (1958).

Who said it first

Max Dehn asked in 1910 for a way to decide whether a knot is trivial, among his problems about groups. Hellmuth Kneser and Haken built the theory of normal surfaces that made the first answer possible. Programs used today include SnapPy and Regina, the second by Benjamin Burton, who in 2020 extended the knot tables to 19 crossings.

A proof that is not allowed

“A computer ran the move search on this drawing for a week and never reached the ring, so the knot is knotted.” Not allowed: the search lists the ring’s drawings and says nothing when it has not yet printed one. Only a bound on the moves, or a certificate of knottedness such as a colouring, turns a week of failure into a proof.
