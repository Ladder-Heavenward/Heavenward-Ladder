Can a Line’s Output Predict How Many Shifts Meet Quota?

Can a line’s output predict how many shifts meet quota? A factory line has a quota of 400 parts a shift. Over a month, some shifts make it and some fall short. A manager who knows only the line’s average output wants to know what share of shifts will meet quota. This page writes that share as a function of one number, the ratio of average output to quota, finds a function of rung 9 that fits it well, sees what it says about lucky and unlucky months, and finds why a steady line gains far more from a small rise in output than an unsteady one.

Let r be average output over quota. The share of shifts that meet quota is close to rᵏ/(rᵏ + 1), where k measures how steady the line is: big for a steady line, small for an unsteady one. At r = 1 the share is one half, whatever k is.

9stat21Each Shift’s Output Against Its Quota

Evita manages two lines, each with a quota of 400 parts a shift. Over a month of 40 shifts, line 1 made 404 parts a shift on average and met quota on 22 shifts. Line 2 averaged the same 404, but met quota on only 21.

From here on, on this page, a line’s ratio r is its average output over its quota, and its hit share is the share of shifts that met quota.

r = \tfrac{404}{400} = 1.01 @@ both lines
\text{hit share, line 1: } \tfrac{22}{40} = 0.55 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hit share, line 2: } \tfrac{21}{40} = 0.525} @@ same ratio, fewer hits

The ratio alone does not settle the hit share: how much the output wanders from shift to shift matters too, as 8·stat’s spread would say.

9stat22The Hit Share as a Function of the Ratio

Across many months and many lines, the hit share rises with the ratio: well below quota almost no shift makes it, well above almost every shift does, and at r = 1 about half do. So for a given line, the hit share is close to a function of r (9a).

h(1) = \tfrac{1}{2} @@ average output at quota: half the shifts above
h(r) \to 0 \text{ as } r \to 0 @@ far below quota, no hits
h(r) \to 1 \text{ as } r \text{ grows} @@ far above, all hits
\htmlClass{new}{h(1/r) = 1 - h(r)} @@ below quota by a ratio mirrors above by it

The last line says a line averaging 400/1.05 parts misses as often as a line averaging 400 × 1.05 hits: the function treats “times 1.05” and “divided by 1.05” as mirror images.

9stat23The Ratio Technique

One family of rational functions in rᵏ (9g) does all four, for any positive k.

h(r) = \dfrac{r^k}{r^k + 1} @@ k > 0, fitted to the line
h(1) = \tfrac{1}{1 + 1} = \tfrac{1}{2} @@ the middle
h(1/r) = \dfrac{r^{-k}}{r^{-k} + 1} = \dfrac{1}{1 + r^k} = 1 - h(r) @@ multiply top and bottom by rᵏ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{line 1, } k = 20:\ \dfrac{1.01^{20}}{1.01^{20} + 1} \approx \dfrac{1.22}{2.22} \approx 0.55} @@ fits its 22 of 40

Output-ratio technique: a line’s share of shifts meeting quota is close to rᵏ/(rᵏ + 1), where r is average output over quota and k is fitted from the line’s past months. The same shape was first used in sport, to predict a team’s share of wins from its points scored over points allowed. Here it is carried over to a factory, where it does the same job.

9stat24Lucky and Unlucky Lines

A month is a small sample. Once k is fitted from many months, the technique gives what a line’s hit share should be for its ratio, and a month can come in above or below it by luck.

monthratiotechnique, k = 20shifts met (of 40)reading
March1.010.55, 22 shifts275 lucky shifts
April1.010.55, 22 shifts184 unlucky shifts
May1.030.64, 26 shifts26as expected

A month that beats the technique by a lot is more likely luck than a better line; the next month usually comes back toward the technique. Evita does not reward March’s crew or punish April’s on hit share alone: the ratio, which counts every part, is the steadier measure.

9stat25Steady and Unsteady Lines

k says how steady a line is. A steady line’s shifts all land close to its average, so a small rise in average output moves many shifts from just under quota to just over. An unsteady line’s shifts scatter widely, so the same rise moves few.

\text{steady, } k = 20:\ \dfrac{1.05^{20}}{1.05^{20} + 1} \approx \dfrac{2.65}{3.65} \approx 0.73 @@ from 0.50: 23 points
\text{unsteady, } k = 4:\ \dfrac{1.05^{4}}{1.05^{4} + 1} \approx \dfrac{1.22}{2.22} \approx 0.55 @@ from 0.50: 5 points
\htmlClass{new}{\text{big } k \text{: a steep step at } r = 1;\ \text{small } k \text{: a gentle slope}} @@ k sets the steepness

For a steady line, a few percent more output turns many missed quotas into met ones; for an unsteady line, it is better to cut the scatter first. A plant engineer comparing two lines with the same ratio fits k to each from a year of shift records and spends the improvement budget where k is small.

Accepted names

The ratio technique is the factory form of Pythagorean expectation, so called because its first form used squares. Fitting k from data is calibration, and a month’s hit share drifting back toward the technique is regression to the mean.

Who said it first

The win-share formula was published by a sports writer in the early 1980s with k = 2, and analysts later fitted other values for other sports, near 14 for basketball. No published source applying it to shifts meeting quota has been found; this page carries the sports form over (not yet revisited).
