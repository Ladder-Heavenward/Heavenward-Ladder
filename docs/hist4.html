The Age of Rigour

The gaps are closed one by one: what a limit is, what a function is, what a number is, what a proof may use. On this page mathematics turns to look at its own foundations, and physics and chemistry take on the same form.

81 climbers, in order of birth. Each of them climbed the same ladder you have climbed, and built part of it on the way. Under each name are the techniques they advanced, credited to them, and the climbers they met: who argued with whom, over what, and how it was resolved.

1750–1800Lorenzo Mascheroni

Showed in 1797 that the compass alone reaches every point the straightedge and compass reach, and computed Euler's constant, partly wrongly.

Built into Constructions With Straightedge and Compass · Sums Against Areas · Is the Gap Between the Harmonic Sum and the Log a Fraction?

1752–1833Adrien-Marie Legendre

Found his polynomials in 1782 studying the pull of a planet that is not a ball, named Γ in 1811, counted squares by least squares in 1805, and gave the theory of numbers its first textbook.

Advanced Legendre’s no-more-than-straight technique

Met Carl Friedrich Gauss over least squares. Legendre printed the method first; Gauss printed it in 1809 and claimed he had used it since 1795. Resolved Never between them; Gauss acknowledged Legendre's book and still claimed priority. Both names stand on the method now.

1755–1836Marc-Antoine Parseval

Stated in 1799, without proof, that the squares of a wave's coefficients add up to the square of the wave.

Advanced Parseval’s energy-sum technique · Parseval’s energy-kept technique · Parseval’s transform-energy technique · Parseval’s dropped-energy technique

1765–1822Paolo Ruffini

Argued in 1799, before anyone had the word group, that the shuffles of five letters do not come apart as those of four do, so the quintic has no formula.

Built into Undoing the Moves · The Symmetries of the Roots · A₅ Is Simple · No Formula

Met Niels Henrik Abel over whether the quintic has a formula. Ruffini argued in 1799 that it has none. Resolved Abel proved it in 1824, in a pamphlet printed at his own cost; the technique carries both names.

1766–1844John Dalton

The atomic theory, in Manchester, and the refusal of Avogadro's claim of equal volumes.

Built into How Do You Count What Is Too Small to See? · Does Every Gas Follow the Same Law? · Can Every Reaction Be Balanced?

1768–1822Jean-Robert Argand

A bookkeeper in Paris. In 1806 he drew the complex numbers as points and their products as turns, and in 1814 gave the proof by least size that every polynomial has a root.

Built into Finding Every Root · The Pairs Looked Back On · The Size of a Product · Every Polynomial Has a Root

1768–1830Joseph Fourier

The builder of the mathematics of heat. His paper of 1807 wrote any pieced function as one sum of waves; Lagrange doubted it, and the proof waited for Dirichlet in 1829.

Advanced Fourier’s proportion · Fourier’s energy-kept technique · Fourier’s blend-coefficients technique · Fourier’s undo-transform technique · Fourier’s slope-to-frequency technique · Fourier’s slide-and-stretch technique · Fourier’s rate-to-algebra technique · Fourier’s halving-transform technique · Fourier’s one-out technique

Met Joseph-Louis Lagrange, Pierre-Simon Laplace and Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet over whether any function can be written as a sum of waves. Fourier's memoir on heat was read in December 1807; Lagrange and Laplace objected to its series, and the prize committee of 1811 found it wanting in rigour. Resolved Dirichlet proved when the series converge, and gave the series the precision the committee had found missing.

1773–1829Thomas Young

Showed the two-slit bands to the Royal Society in 1803 and argued that light is a wave, measured the stiffness of a material as one number in 1807, and found which harmonics a plucked string lacks.

Advanced Thomas Young’s two-slit technique

Met Christiaan Huygens, Isaac Newton, Léon Foucault and Albert Einstein over what light is. Huygens argued in 1678 that light is a wave; Newton held it to be a stream of particles, and in England few dared contradict Newton. Resolved Young explained interference by waves in 1803, against severe criticism. Foucault's measurement in 1850, that light is slower in water than in air, was the last nail in the particle theory's coffin. Einstein's light quanta of 1905 brought particles back beside the wave.

1776–1856Amedeo Avogadro

Teaching at Vercelli in 1811, he claimed that equal volumes of gas hold equal numbers of molecules; Dalton refused it, and it waited fifty years.

Advanced Avogadro’s equal-counts technique

1777–1855Carl Friedrich Gauss

The builder who hid his scaffolding. The book on numbers of 1801, the proof that every polynomial has a root, least squares, the bell curve, the other geometry kept private, the complex plane named in 1831, the curvature of surfaces, the best points for sums, and the integral that depends only on what the path goes round.

Met Adrien-Marie Legendre over least squares. Legendre printed the method first; Gauss printed it in 1809 and claimed he had used it since 1795. Resolved Never between them; Gauss acknowledged Legendre's book and still claimed priority. Both names stand on the method now.

Resolved Gauss thought highly of his work. Beltrami gave the geometry a concrete surface in 1868, a key step in its acceptance.

1786–1849James Thomson

Brother of Lord Kelvin. He first printed the word radian in 1873, and predicted in 1849 that pressure melts ice.

1789–1857Augustin-Louis Cauchy

Lecturer at the École Polytechnique. The Cours d'analyse of 1821 made the limit the meaning of a sum, defined the integral as a limit in 1823, proved the mean value technique, the dot-bound and the shuffles of letters, and built the calculus of complex functions. He is on more pages of this ladder than anyone but Euler.

Met Niels Henrik Abel over Abel's Paris memoir of 1826. Cauchy, its referee, set it aside and it was forgotten. Resolved Only after Abel's death was it looked for again: Cauchy found it in 1830.

1789–1854Georg Ohm

The proportion law of current and voltage in 1827, and the acoustic law of 1843 that the ear takes a sound apart into its simple tones.

Advanced Ohm’s proportion law

1790–1868August Möbius

Gave areas a sign by the direction they are traced, proved the inversion formula of the 1830s argument, and left the band with one side.

1791–1820Alexis Thérèse Petit

With Dulong he measured cooling over large gaps in 1817 and found Newton's law too small at high heat, and gave the 3R law for heat capacity in 1819.

1791–1871Charles Babbage

Of the Analytical Society, who brought Leibniz's d-mark to Cambridge, designed engines that were never built, and broke the Vigenère cipher around 1854 and left the method in his notes.

1791–1858George Peacock

Of the Analytical Society at Cambridge. He laid down the permanence of equivalent forms: techniques true for whole numbers are kept when the letters stand for more.

1791–1867Michael Faraday

Found induction in 1831, counted electrolysis by the charge in 1833, and proposed the wet surface layer under skates in 1850.

1792–1856Nikolai Lobachevsky

1793–1841George Green

A miller's son from Nottingham who taught himself. His essay of 1828, sold to fifty-one subscribers, holds the identities that trade a volume for its surface.

1796–1878Irénée-Jules Bienaymé

1802–1829Niels Henrik Abel

Dead at twenty-six. He proved in 1824 that the quintic has no formula, wrote the footnote on Cauchy's sum of continuous functions, and said of Gauss that he was like the fox, who wipes out his tracks with his tail. Turn-about groups bear his name.

1805–1859Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet

Proved Fourier's claim in 1829 with the function that is 1 on fractions and 0 elsewhere, cut the knot of primes in clock positions in 1837, and lectured on numbers in the notes Dedekind kept.

1805–1865William Rowan Hamilton

Defined complex numbers as pairs of reals in 1837, carved i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1 into Broom Bridge in Dublin in 1843, and wrote mechanics as the flow of one function.

1806–1871Augustus De Morgan

Stated the not-through techniques in 1847, which Ockham had in words, and gave induction its name.

1809–1882Charles Darwin

The theory of evolution, and the naturalist who wanted far more time for it than Kelvin's cooling earth would give.

Built into Why Are Cells so Small? · How Sour Is It, and How Long Until Half Is Gone? · How Does Heat Creep Into the Ground? · How Are All Living Things Related?

Resolved Radioactivity overturned the estimate: the Earth has a heat source Thomson left out, and Thomson opposed Rutherford's radioactivity too.

1809–1877Hermann Grassmann

A schoolteacher in Stettin. He was the first to define adding by its value at the start and its step and prove arithmetic from that by induction, and his Ausdehnungslehre of 1844 had the vector before anyone wanted it.

1809–1882Joseph Liouville

1810–1893Ernst Kummer

1814–1897James Joseph Sylvester

1814–1848Pierre Wantzel

1815–1864George Boole

In 1854 he wrote the ways a statement can come out as marks and the joining words as signs between them, and logic became an algebra. Shannon showed in 1937 that switches work it out.

1815–1897Karl Weierstrass

The teacher in Berlin. His lectures gave the limit its epsilons and deltas, uniform convergence, the continuous function with no slope anywhere, the product for the sine on firm ground, and the approximation technique of 1885.

Advanced Weierstrass’s bounded · Weierstrass’s highest-and-lowest technique · Weierstrass’s at-once comparison technique · Weierstrass’s average-polynomial technique · Weierstrass’s turnings-near-everything technique

1823–1891Leopold Kronecker

Met Max Planck over whether the second law of heat is absolute. Planck held that it was. Resolved To find his radiation law in 1900, Planck had to accept Boltzmann's reading of the law as statistical, and used his methods; he called it an act of despair.

1845–1918Georg Cantor

Turned collections into mathematical objects, definite and separate. He proved in 1874 that the reals outrun the whole numbers, in 1877 that the square and the line have one size, and that the parts of a set outrun its members; Kronecker fought him.

Met Leopold Kronecker, Richard Dedekind and David Hilbert over whether completed infinities exist. Kronecker held that mathematics should rest on whole numbers and finite steps, and tried to stop Cantor's paper of 1877 in Crelle's Journal. Resolved Dedekind stepped in and the paper appeared, though Cantor never sent Crelle another. Set theory won: it was largely accepted, with Hilbert its strongest supporter.

Met David Hilbert, Kurt Gödel and Paul Cohen over the continuum hypothesis. Cantor proposed it and could not prove it; Hilbert put it first among his problems in 1900. Resolved Gödel showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved, and Cohen in 1963 that it cannot be proved. Gödel wrote to Cohen that his proof was a delight to read.

1848–1925Gottlob Frege

Built the numbers as following in a series and logic as a language of marks; Russell's letter of 1902 brought the foundation down.

Met Bertrand Russell and Ernst Zermelo over Frege's Basic Law V. Russell's letter of 16 June 1902, arriving as Frege's second volume was at the printer, showed that the law gives a contradiction. Resolved Frege conceded in an appendix that the law was false. Russell built his theory of types, and Zermelo's axioms of 1908 let a set be cut only from a set already given.
