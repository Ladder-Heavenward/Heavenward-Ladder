How Can a Boat Sail Toward the Wind?

How can a boat sail toward the wind? A sail is pushed by the wind, so it seems a boat could only go where the wind blows. Yet sailing boats make their way upwind, zigzagging at an angle to it. The sail is a wing standing on end, and its push is not along the wind but mostly across it. This page adds the boat’s motion to the wind as 21·phys’s arrows, splits the sail’s force into lift and drag, lets the keel take the sideways part, measures progress toward the wind with a cosine, and finds the best angle to sail with 14h.

The wind the boat feels is the true wind minus the boat’s own motion, as arrows. The sail’s force is lift, across that wind, plus drag, along it. Split it along the boat’s heading and across: the keel resists the across part, and the along part drives the boat. Progress toward the wind is boat speed times the cosine of the angle off the wind.

21phys21The Wind the Boat Feels

Sarah sails a small keelboat. The true wind blows at 6 m/s from the north. She heads northeast, 45° off the wind, at 3 m/s. The wind she feels on board is the true wind with her own motion taken away, as arrows add (21·phys).

\text{true wind (blowing toward): } (0, -6) @@ from the north
\text{boat: } 3(\cos 45°, \sin 45°) \approx (2.12, 2.12) @@ heading northeast (21b)
\text{felt wind} = (0, -6) - (2.12, 2.12) = (-2.12, -8.12) @@ arrows subtract
\text{size} = \sqrt{2.12^2 + 8.12^2} \approx 8.4 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{it comes from about } 30° \text{ off her heading, not } 45°} @@ stronger, and further ahead

From here on, on this page, the felt wind is this difference. Sailing toward the wind makes the felt wind stronger and swings it toward the bow.

21phys22Lift and Drag on a Sail

A sail set at a slight angle to the felt wind bends the air around it, like a wing. The air pushes back on the sail with a force that is split two ways: lift, at right angles to the felt wind, and drag, along it. A good sail, well trimmed, makes about four times as much lift as drag.

The lift is what lets a boat go against the wind: it points across the felt wind, and so partly forward along the boat’s heading.

21phys23The Keel Takes the Sideways Part

Split the sail’s force along the boat’s heading and across it. Let β be the angle between the heading and where the felt wind comes from: about 30° here.

\text{forward} = L\sin\beta - D\cos\beta @@ lift leans forward, drag pulls back
= 4\sin 30.3° - \cos 30.3° \approx 2.02 - 0.86 = 1.16 @@ 
\text{sideways} = L\cos\beta + D\sin\beta \approx 3.45 + 0.50 = 3.96 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{forward} > 0 \text{ only if } L/D > \dfrac{\cos\beta}{\sin\beta}} @@ about 1.7 at 30°

The sideways push is more than three times the forward push. The keel, a deep fin under the boat, is a second wing working in water: as the boat slips slightly sideways, the keel makes lift that balances the sail’s sideways push, so the boat moves forward. Without a keel, the boat would be blown sideways.

21phys24Speed Made Good Toward the Wind

Heading 45° off the wind, Sarah goes 3 m/s, but not all of it toward the wind. What counts for reaching a point upwind is the part of her speed along the wind’s direction.

\text{made good} = V\cos\theta,\ \ \theta \text{ the angle off the wind} @@ the part along the wind (21b)
3 \cos 45° \approx 2.1 \text{ m/s} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{to a point upwind: zigzag, each leg at } \theta \text{ off the wind}} @@ tacking from one side to the other

Made-good technique: a boat’s progress toward the wind is its speed times the cosine of its angle off the wind; it reaches a point upwind by zigzagging, each leg at the angle that makes this greatest. Sailing straight at the wind gives no speed at all; sailing across it gives speed but no progress.

21phys25The Best Angle to Tack

A boat’s speed depends on its angle off the wind: 0 straight into it, and faster as it bears away. For Sarah’s boat a simple fit is V(θ) = V₀(1 − cos θ) for angles up to a right angle. Find the angle where the made good speed is greatest (14h).

M(\theta) = V_0 (1 - \cos\theta)\cos\theta @@ speed made good
M\prime(\theta) = V_0 \sin\theta\,(2\cos\theta - 1) = 0 @@ slopes of sin and cos (21g)
\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}:\ \theta = 60° @@ sin θ = 0 gives no speed
\htmlClass{new}{M = V_0 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{V_0}{4}} @@ the best for this boat

Pointing closer to the wind trades speed for direction; the best angle is where the gain in direction just pays for the loss in speed. Better sails and keels raise L/D and let a boat point closer: racing boats tack at about 40° to 45° off the wind. A racing crew reads speed made good straight off an instrument and steers to keep it greatest.

Accepted names

The felt wind is the apparent wind; speed made good toward the wind is velocity made good, VMG. Zigzagging upwind is beating or tacking, and sailing as close as the boat can is close-hauled. The chart of boat speed against angle is a polar diagram.

Who said it first

Sailors have tacked upwind for thousands of years. The explanation of sails as wings making lift came with the aerodynamics of the early twentieth century; modern racing yachts are designed with these forces worked out by computer.
