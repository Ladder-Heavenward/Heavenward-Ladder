How Does a Machine Guess the Next Word?

How does a machine guess the next word? The programs that write and answer in plain language do one thing over and over: given the words so far, give each possible next word a chance, and pick one. Everything else is how those chances are made. This page builds chances by counting pairs of words, scores a model by logs of its chances, finds why counting cannot go far, turns words into arrows whose angles say how alike they are, as rung 31 gives functions angles, and lets each word weigh the earlier ones by a dot product.

Give the next word a chance from what came before. Counting pairs gives P(next | last); a sentence’s chance is the product, and its log a sum; score a model by the average log chance it gives to text it has not seen. Make each word an arrow, so alike words point alike and the cosine of their angle measures it. To use the whole past, weigh each earlier word by e to its dot product with a question arrow, divided by the total, and average.

31comp1Chances From Counting Pairs

Zoe gives a program three sentences to learn from: “the cat sat”, “the cat ran”, “the dog sat”. From here on, on this page, a model gives every word a chance of coming next, given the words so far.

\text{after “the”: cat } 2,\ \text{dog } 1 @@ count the pairs
P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = \tfrac23,\ \ P(\text{dog} \mid \text{the}) = \tfrac13 @@ each count over the total
P(\text{sat} \mid \text{cat}) = \tfrac12,\ \ P(\text{ran} \mid \text{cat}) = \tfrac12 @@ after “cat”: sat 1, ran 1
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{the cat sat}) = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac13} @@ each word given the one before

To write, the program starts with “the”, picks the next word by these chances, then the next, and so on. This tiny model can never write “the dog ran”: it never saw “dog ran”, and pairs it has not seen get chance 0.

31comp2Scoring a Model With Logs

A good model gives high chances to real text it has not seen. A sentence’s chance is a product of many small chances, too small to compare easily, so take logs (17c): the product becomes a sum.

\ln P(\text{sentence}) = \textstyle\sum \ln P(\text{word} \mid \text{before}) @@ the log-of-a-product technique (17c)
\text{score} = -\tfrac{1}{n} \textstyle\sum \ln P @@ the average surprise per word
\text{“the cat sat”: } -\tfrac12 (\ln \tfrac23 + \ln \tfrac12) \approx 0.55 @@ two words after “the”
\htmlClass{new}{e^{0.55} \approx 1.7} @@ as if choosing among 1.7 equal words each time

Score technique: score a model by the average of −ln P over words of fresh text; e to that score is the number of equal choices it is, in effect, still guessing among. A word it gave chance 0 makes the score endless, so every model must leave some chance for the unseen.

31comp3Words as Arrows

Counting cannot know that “dog” is like “cat”, so “the dog ran” stays impossible. Instead give each word an arrow of numbers, chosen so that words used in alike places point alike. Rung 31’s product measures how alike.

\text{cat} = (1,\ 0.9,\ 0),\ \text{dog} = (0.9,\ 1,\ 0.1),\ \text{stone} = (0,\ 0.1,\ 1) @@ toy arrows
\cos(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = \dfrac{\text{cat} \cdot \text{dog}}{|\text{cat}|\,|\text{dog}|} \approx 0.99 @@ angle from one product (31a)
\cos(\text{cat}, \text{stone}) \approx 0.07 @@ nearly at right angles
\htmlClass{new}{\text{what follows cat can follow dog}} @@ the model shares what it learns

Arrow technique: give each word an arrow so that words used alike have a small angle between them; the model then treats alike words alike, and can give “the dog ran” a sensible chance without ever seeing it. Real models use arrows of thousands of numbers, learned from the text itself.

31comp4Weighing the Earlier Words

The next word can depend on a word far back: “the keys that I lost yesterday are…”. So each position makes a question arrow q, and each earlier word offers a key arrow k and a value arrow v. The dot product q · k says how much that word matters now.

q \cdot k_1 = 2,\ q \cdot k_2 = 0,\ q \cdot k_3 = 1 @@ how much each earlier word matters
w_i = e^{q \cdot k_i} \big/ \textstyle\sum_j e^{q \cdot k_j} @@ positive, adding to 1
w \approx (0.67,\ 0.09,\ 0.24) @@ the first word counts most
\htmlClass{new}{\text{blend} = w_1 v_1 + w_2 v_2 + w_3 v_3} @@ a weighted average of the past

Weighing technique: each position weighs every earlier word by e to the dot product of its question with that word’s key, divided by the total, and averages their values. Stacked many times over, these blends are where the model keeps track of who did what, many words back.

31comp5Learning the Numbers, and What They Are

Every arrow and every grid in such a model is a number to be chosen, billions of them. They are chosen to make the score of 31comp2 small on a vast amount of text, by 28q’s gradient descent: nudge each number a little downhill, again and again.

\text{goal: make } -\tfrac1n \textstyle\sum \ln P \text{ small on real text} @@ the score
\text{nudge every number against the slope} @@ gradient descent (28q)
\text{repeat over trillions of words} @@ the scale of real training
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the result gives likely continuations, not looked-up facts}} @@ fluent is not the same as true

A language model is a machine for likely next words, trained to make real text unsurprising. It knows a fact only as far as that fact made text likely, so it can write something fluent, confident and false. Checking what it writes stays the reader’s job.

Accepted names

A model of chances for the next word is a language model; one from pairs is a bigram model, and from longer runs an n-gram model. e to the average surprise is perplexity, and the average surprise is cross-entropy. Word arrows are word embeddings and their likeness cosine similarity. Weighing earlier words is attention, e over the total the softmax, and models built of stacked attention transformers. A fluent false answer is often called a hallucination.

Who said it first

Claude Shannon estimated chances of the next letter and word in English in 1948 and 1951. Tomas Mikolov and others learned word arrows from text in 2013, and Ashish Vaswani and others built models from stacked attention in 2017.
