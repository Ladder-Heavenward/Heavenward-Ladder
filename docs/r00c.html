0c · proof

Make your case

When guesses clash, who is right? There is always an argument. We don't fear a guess being wrong, because losing an argument is only the next step toward stronger guesses. Not shouting, but method and technique. Every step offers more tools for your toolbox, and the ladder goes somewhere very high.

0a gave the ladder names and statements, and 0b gave sameness its mark and its axioms, arguing in plain words that sameness turns about and that it chains. This step says what an argument is for: it starts from guesses and rules out the ones that do not fit. It gives the ground floor's arguments their form, one step at a time, and then argues 0b's turn-about and chain techniques again as plain sentences in a row, one move to a sentence.

Line up the guesses. Say one thing in a sentence, and say why you may. Every sentence leans only on sentences before it. When the guesses that do not fit are ruled out and the last sentence is the thing you wanted, end the argument.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0a (letters as names, statements, the bracket, statements named whole, the rule for bringing in a mark) and 0b (the mark =, the axioms self-sameness and swap, the techniques turn-about and chain stated and argued in words, the illegal swaps). Every page above this one argues in the form built here; rung 1, next, adds the joining words and their moves.

0c1Ruling guesses out

Stands on 0a3 (what a statement is).

Here is an example. The last slice of pie is gone, and it was Ann, or Ben, or the dog. Each guess is a statement that might be so. Then come statements you can lean on. Ann was at work all evening, so it was not Ann. The dog cannot reach the counter, so it was not the dog. Each statement you can lean on rules a guess out, until what is left is the guess that fits: it was Ben.

An argument is a relationship of statements that ends by ruling out the guesses that do not fit the techniques. The pie argument used a move this rung does not have yet: ruling out every option of an “or” but one. That move gets its technique on rung 1. The shape is already plain, though: guesses first, then statements that rule them out, then what is left.

There is more than one kind of argument. A proof is one kind of argument: it rules guesses out with techniques that never fail, so what is left cannot fail either. An equation is another; the ladder reaches it later. Statistics is a third: it rules out the guesses the data make too unlikely, and says how sure it is. A lab experiment is a fourth: it rules out the guesses the world itself refuses. The ladder can give you proofs, equations and statistics. A lab experiment needs a lab, and the ladder cannot give you one.

0c2Sentences in a row

Stands on 0c1 (ruling guesses out).

On the ground floor an argument has one form. It is plain sentences in a row, one claim each, each with its reason. Each sentence is a statement, in 0a's sense: something so or not so. With it goes its reason, which says why it may be said. Read the argument from the start and each sentence is justified by what came before it. The last sentence, its foot, is what the argument leaves standing.

Here is one, so you can see the shape. Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (by what “widow” means). Two sentences, each with its reason. The foot is “Ann was married”, and the argument leaves it standing.

The ladder also calls each sentence a line, as in a line of a poem. A given line is a given sentence.

From here on, to parse an argument is to read it from the start, one sentence at a time, checking each against its reason and the sentences before it. Parsing is the ladder's gold standard. An argument that parses all the way to its foot has done its work. One that fails to parse at some sentence has met one of two things: a mistake, or an edge, the frontier of what anyone yet knows how to argue. Mistakes and edges are both interesting. A mistake shows where your reasoning slipped. An edge shows where everyone's does.

0c3The kinds of reason

Stands on 0c2 (sentences in a row), 0b4 and 0b5 (the axioms).

A reason is always one of three kinds: a given, an axiom by name, or a tech by name. Every reason on every page of the ladder is one of the three. Each is tagged below with a line from this argument: Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog. By the swap axiom, on Rex = the dog on the porch, the dog on the porch is a puppy.

  • given: the sentence was laid down at the start, as the thing we are supposing for the length of this argument. “Rex is a puppy. That is given.”
  • an axiom, by name: the sentence is an axiom an earlier page laid down, filled with names. Or it is what is so by the axiom, given sentences before it. “By the swap axiom, on what we have.”
  • a tech, by name: the sentence was got from sentences before it by a tech, short for technique. The reason names the tech and the sentences it used. “So Rex is a dog”, by what “puppy” means: that tech is called unpack, in 0c7. A tech comes built or proved:
    • built: laid down by us, because what the words mean keeps it working, as unpack and pack are in this step;
    • proved: argued once from what came before, and cited by name from then on, as the turn-about and chain techniques are later in this step. Citing its name stands in for telling its argument again.

A given is a statement laid down at the start, that the argument supposes for its length. The axioms are givens of another kind: statements that are so in every case, laid down and never argued, standing behind every argument from the rung where you learned them. A challenge may put an axiom off limits; then you may not cite that axiom, but it is still so.

Where do givens come from? From observation, common sense, measurements, names, lists, and axioms. For example: “given, bread is made of wheat”, or the list of kids on the soccer team. Techniques already proved are givens of that kind too. More widely, a given is any statement you have enough confidence in to start an argument from, so that you can be confident in where it ends.

“It's obvious” is close to how a given is got. But a given is said at the start, before the argument takes its first step. You only say the givens that matter to the argument in front of you; the rest stand behind it unsaid.

Using a tech is a move: the step an argument takes. Nothing but a given, an axiom or a tech earns a sentence. “Obvious”, “everyone knows” and looking out of the window are close to how givens are got. Said at the start, as givens, they are honest. Said in the middle of an argument, each is a given brought in out of order, and it breaks the parse.

0c4Guesses as givens

Stands on 0c1 (ruling guesses out) and 0c3 (the kinds of reason).

Some givens are guesses, and they have kinds of their own. Each is laid down at the start like any given, but it is laid down to be tried, not because you are confident in it. The pie argument of 0c1 had three guesses, each tried in turn. Here are the kinds:

  • a supposition: a guess kept for a while, tried and not claimed, and dropped before the end (1a);
  • a case: one guess among a few that the givens leave open, each tried in turn. Ann, Ben and the dog were the cases of the pie;
  • a very large group of cases: a guess about every member of a group at once, too many to try one by one. Example: “every dog at the park last Saturday was brown”;
  • a wild conjecture: a guess with nothing behind it yet, laid down to see where it goes. It is honest as long as the argument says so: what it reaches is true only if the conjecture is.

Cases are how a guess is tried in a series: one, then the next, then the next. A few cases can each be tried. A very large group cannot, and that difference matters as soon as you ask what an argument may conclude from cases. 0c5 asks it.

0c5“True”, on the ladder

Stands on 0c3 (the kinds of reason) and 0c4 (guesses as givens).

In life a statement is true when what it says is so, and you check by looking. An argument cannot look, and does not try. In an argument, “true” means earned. A statement counts as true when it stands in an argument with its reason, and the reason is a given, an axiom or a tech. Counting only earned statements as true is strict on purpose. Every true statement can be traced back, sentence by sentence, to givens, axioms and named techs. And you can check each step without taking anyone's word.

An argument in which every sentence is earned this way is induction over the arrangement: if the givens are true, the foot cannot fail to be true. Everyday reasoning has another kind, induction over cases: from cases to a rule. For example, the sun has come up every morning you have seen, so it will come up tomorrow. Induction over cases can be very strong evidence, and statistics and the lab live on it: they rule guesses out too, though never every one. But in an arrangement, a guess from cases never earns a line, however many cases stand behind it. A thousand grey cats do not earn “every cat is grey”. One cat that is not grey would undo “every cat is grey”, and the argument cannot look to see whether that cat exists.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger

Both kinds of induction are anexact, yet rigorous. Anexact, because neither one has to touch the world: an arrangement holds whether or not its things exist, and cases never reach every case. Rigorous, because each keeps exact books. No one has watched one raindrop fall through a whole storm, yet the year's rainfall is known to the millimetre: measuring says in numbers how wrong it might be.

The books are a ledger, and the column form already is one. In the Rex argument of 0c3, every sentence is paid for by its reason, and nothing is claimed that was not paid for. Scientists keep a ledger too: every bit of stuff that goes into a burning candle comes out as smoke, gas and heat, and nothing vanishes.

A description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every sentence in it is true.

A candle burns down, and the wax is gone. Where would you look for it, if nothing vanishes?

0c6The end mark

Stands on 0c2 (sentences in a row) and 0a10 (how a mark is brought in).

An argument needs a way to say it is finished. Here is its mark, brought in by 0a's rule:

The end mark ∎ is a statement, named whole in 0a's way: “the argument is complete”.

Typing an answer, with no key for the end mark? Write the word end in its place. It means the same thing, and the ladder reads it the same way.

The shortest argument of all is one given sentence and the end mark: “Ann is a widow. That is given. ∎” That one-sentence argument proves its given, as given. The foot stands exactly where the given stands, and is exactly as so or not so as the given was when it entered. It looks like nothing, and it is the identity of a statement, as the self-sameness axiom is the identity of a name. (4d earns this claim.)

0a promised that a letter names one thing for the length of an argument, and is free when the argument is over, and it left the ending to this step. The end mark is where an argument is over, and 0a's flag on freeing a letter is closed. A letter named inside an argument keeps its meaning down to the end mark and no further. So the same letter can name different things in different arguments, as long as each argument ends before the next begins. Here are two cats, with a single letter, in an argument each:

Call the grey cat on the mat c. So c is the grey cat, until the end mark. c is asleep; that is given. ∎ The end mark frees c.

Call the ginger cat on the wall c. Now c is the ginger cat, in this argument only. c is hungry; that is given. ∎ And c is free again.

Inside either argument, c names one cat throughout, so every sentence can be checked. What you may not do is let a sentence in the second argument lean on a sentence in the first: across the end mark, c is a different cat.

0c7Unpack, pack and transform

Stands on 0c3 (the kinds of reason), 0c6 (the end mark) and 0a6 (four shapes of a claim).

0b's axioms say what is so of sameness, and citing them is a move. The techs that make moves were not found in dusty old books. They were built: people noticed which statements kept doing work for them, and made them shorthand. To prove the point, we build three ourselves, here, out of plain English: unpack, pack and transform. Each gets a name, so that a reason can cite it.

  • Unpack: when a sentence says something of a thing using a word, you may say what that word means, said of the same thing. Example: “puppy” means a young dog, so from “Rex is a puppy” you may say “Rex is a dog”, and you may say “Rex is young”. The reason names the word unpacked.
  • Pack: unpack run the other way. When sentences say of a thing everything a word means, you may say the word, said of that thing. Example: from “Rex is a dog” and “Rex is young” you may say “Rex is a puppy”. The reason names the word packed and the sentences it used.
  • Transform: when a sentence says something of a thing in one of 0a's four shapes, you may say the same claim in another shape. Example: from “Rex is spotted” you may say “Rex has spots”. The claim is the same; only the shape has changed. From “spotted Rex is running” you may say “Rex is spotted”, taking the descriptive apart. The reason names the shapes: “transform, predicate to tentative”.

A transform is any move that changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. Changing the shape is the first kind of transform: it says no more and no less of the thing than the sentence it came from. Transform rests on 0a's possibility axiom: the shapes are ways of writing one claim, so moving between them loses nothing.

“Is” does two jobs in English. In “Mike is the boy in the red cap” it is sameness, =, and the swap axiom and the chain technique carry it. In “Rex is a puppy” it says what kind of thing Rex is. A sentence of the “is a kind of” shape is unpacked, never chained, and two unpacks in a row carry Rex down a line of kinds.

The signs for unpack and pack are drawn from what unpack and pack do. ﹀ is a wide cup opening downward: the meaning pours out of the word. ︿ is the same cup turned over: the meaning is gathered back under the word. Each sign is a name: the name of a tech, packed into one scribble. Unpack and pack are techs on a word and its meaning, so a reason may cite them as it cites any tech.

Typing an answer, with no key for ﹀ and ︿? Write the words unpack and pack in their place. The ladder reads them the same way.

Marks are shorthand for words, and every mark on the ladder was brought in by a “from here on” line saying what it means. So when a later page unpacks a mark, that is the unpack tech again, with the “from here on” line as the meaning it unpacks.

The shortest argument that moves has a given sentence, a sentence got from it, and the end mark. Here it is, each sentence with its reason:

Ann is a widow. That is given. A widow is a woman whose husband has died, so Ann was married (﹀ “widow”). ∎

And here is a longer one, in which the unpack is made again on its own result. Each sentence makes a move of its own and names the sentence it used. The ladder argues this finely so that nothing can hide; you will not have to.

Rex is a puppy. That is given. A puppy is a young dog, so Rex is a dog (﹀ “puppy”, on the sentence before). A dog is an animal, so Rex is an animal (﹀ “dog”, on the sentence before). ∎

0c8Illegal sentences

Stands on 0c3 (the kinds of reason) and 0c7 (unpack and pack).

An argument can look like a proof and not be one. Each failing sentence fails for a reason you can name. A sentence may have no reason with it. It may be a question or a command still in its own shape. Each carries a statement, but it must say that statement before it stands on a line. Its reason may be “obvious”: a given brought in out of order, after the argument has started. Or its reason may cite a sentence that comes after it.

That last one is the subtle one. Reasons point back, always, so that tracing a sentence back through its reasons ends at givens and axioms and does not go round. An argument in which a sentence cites a sentence after it has not proved anything; it has only arranged its wishes in order.

The word “wish” has a meaning here. A wish is a sentence written with no reason behind it: no given, no axiom, no tech earns it. It is the inverse of a guess: not an intuition that something is so, but a desire that it should be so, and it pretends a line already is so, though it was never earned. A wish stays a wish however true its sentence is.

Example. Given “p is a hen”. “Hen” means an adult female chicken, so “p is female” is an unpack. “p lays eggs” is true of most hens, but it is not what the word means, so writing it with “﹀ hen” beside it is a wish; it needs its own given. A wish has two repairs. Earn it from sentences before it, or move it to the start as a given. A given is honest, but then the argument proves its foot only if the given is true.

\text{Rex is brown.} @@ no reason at all: a line with nothing to its right has not been earned, however true it happens to be
\text{Is Rex a dog?} @@ a question in its own shape: it keeps “Rex is a dog” open. On a line, keeping a statement open is a supposition (1a): suppose Rex is a dog
\text{Shut the gate.} @@ a command in its own shape: it carries “if the gate is open, close it”, and must say so before it stands on a line
\text{Rex is a good dog.} \;\;\text{(obvious)} @@ “obvious” in the middle is a given out of order, and it breaks the parse. Say “given Rex is a good dog” at the start instead
\begin{aligned}&\text{Rex is an animal.} \;\\\ &\quad ;\text{(from the line below)}\end{aligned} @@ a reason may cite only lines above it; a line that leans on a later line is leaning on nothing yet

One more fails in a quieter way: a letter that drifts. Suppose a second naming were let in, before the first argument ended. Then:

\text{Call the grey cat } c. @@ naming: c = the grey cat, until the end mark
\text{Call the ginger cat } c. @@ not allowed: c already names the grey cat in this argument, and a letter names one thing until the end mark
c = \text{grey cat} @@ the first naming
c = \text{ginger cat} @@ the second naming
\text{grey cat} = c @@ the turn-about technique, on “c = grey cat”
\text{grey cat} = \text{ginger cat} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chain technique, on the two lines above: every move legal, and the argument proves two cats are one cat

Each move after the two namings is a fair move. That is what makes the drift dangerous: nothing in the chain looks wrong. Ending the first argument before naming again, as the two cats of 0c6 did, is the whole repair.

0c9Finding an argument from its foot

Stands on 0c7 (unpack and pack).

Parse an argument from the start and each sentence is justified by what came before it. Read it from the foot and it answers a different question: what would I need to have, to earn this? Reading from the foot is how arguments are found. Wanting “Rex is an animal”, you ask what word unpacks to “animal”, and “dog” does. Wanting “Rex is a dog”, you ask what unpacks to “dog”, and “puppy” does, which is what you were given. Finding runs backward; writing runs forward. Finding backward and writing forward are both worth practising, and the questions ask for both.

The wording of a reason is free. “Unpack ‘puppy’”, “what puppy means”, “a puppy is a dog”: any of these three, written with the sentence, is a reason. Each tells a reader exactly which move was made and on what. What a reason may not be is absent, or a wish.

0c10More than one given

Stands on 0c3 (the kinds of reason) and 0b5 (swap equals).

The next argument uses two givens. The arguments about Ann, Rex and the two cats each started from a single given. An argument may start from as many as it needs. Each is said at the start, with the reason “given”. Together the givens are everything the argument supposes, and their order does not matter. A move may then lean on more than one sentence before it, and when it does, its reason names every sentence it used. Unpack works on one sentence at a time. A swap cited to the swap axiom, from 0b, is the earliest move that needs two: a sameness, and a statement about one of its names. A given that nothing later uses is allowed, and harmless: it is supposed, and simply not needed. What an argument proves is always “from these givens, this foot”. So the givens belong to the result as much as the foot does: change them, and you have a different proof.

Now the arguments themselves, one move to a sentence. 0b laid down that a fact carried across a sameness is still so, and argued in words that sameness turns about and that it chains. It flagged turn-about and chain as argued and not yet set out one move at a time, and named this step as the place they are. Here they are, each with its reason, and with them 0b's flag on turn-about and chain is closed. The axioms are cited by name. First, a swap cited to the swap axiom, on the cat: Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat, and Tibbles is grey.

t = m. That is given: Tibbles is the cat asleep on the mat. t is grey. That is given too: a statement about t. Call “… is grey” P, so it is P(t). By the swap axiom, on t = m and P(t), P(m), with m said where t stood: m is grey. ∎

0c11The turn-about technique, argued

Stands on 0c10 (more than one given) and 0b6 (sameness turns about).

The tech is 0b's turn-about technique: from a sameness, you may say it turned about. The argument on 0b swapped twice, choosing a different place each time. With t for a and m for b, this is Tibbles turned about: t = m, so m = t. Here it is in letters, one move to a sentence:

a = b. That is given. Read a = b as a statement about a, with its gap at the left place. By the swap axiom, swap b in there: b = b. Now read b = b as a statement about b, with its gap at the right place only. By the swap axiom the other way, on a = b, swap a in there: b = a. ∎

And the second road, with the self-sameness axiom in place of the first swap:

a = b. That is given. By the self-sameness axiom, filled with the name a, a = a. Read a = a as a statement about a, with its gap at the left place only. By the swap axiom, on a = b, swap b in there: b = a. ∎

The thought that took thinking is the choice of places, and the two roads above show exactly which places were chosen. In the first, the swaps went to the left place and then the right. In the second, the sentence from the self-sameness axiom cost nothing and was something to swap inside. Every sentence now has its reason, and the turn-about technique is shown sound. Being proved changes what the turn-about technique is. Having been proved, a technique may be cited by name as a reason, exactly as an axiom may. Its argument need not be told again. “By the turn-about technique” is a reason because the two roads above are real.

0c12The chain technique, argued

Stands on 0c10 (more than one given) and 0b7 (sameness chains).

The tech is 0b's chain technique: from two samenesses that share a name, you may say one. The argument read the sentence in hand as a claim about the shared name, b.

a = b. That is given. b = c. That is given too. Read a = b as a statement about b, with its gap at the right place. By the swap axiom, on b = c, swap c in where b stood: a = c. ∎

Longer chains need no new technique; you cite the chain technique, and then cite it again on its own result. Here it is on three samenesses in a row:

a = b, b = c and c = d. All three are given. By the chain technique, on a = b and b = c, a = c. By the chain technique again, on a = c and c = d, a = d. ∎

The chain technique is why a letter may not drift inside an argument. Let one letter name two things in one argument, and the chain technique makes them the same thing. That is the two-cats argument of 0c8: every move fair, and the second naming the fault.

0c13Choosing the places, one sentence each

Stands on 0c10 (more than one given) and 0b8 (swapping at the places you choose).

0b's Ann is the baker, and “x lives next door to x's sister” is about x at both of its places. Each choice of places is its own move, and each cites the swap axiom:

x = b. That is given: Ann is the baker. x lives next door to x's sister. That is given too. By the swap axiom, swapping at the left place: b lives next door to x's sister. By the swap axiom, swapping at the right place: x lives next door to b's sister. By the swap axiom, at both places: b lives next door to b's sister. ∎

Notice that each of the three last sentences leans on the same two givens, and on nothing else. None of them leans on another. The order they are written in is free, because a swap at one place never needs the swap at the other first. Which you write depends on which you mean to use next.

0c14Found from the foot, told from the start

Stands on 0c9 (finding an argument from its foot), 0c11 and 0c12 (turn-about and chain, argued).

0b found a route to d = x from x = b, b = c, c = d by starting from what it wanted. Nothing given has d on the left, so the last sentence must turn x = d about, and x = d is the chain. Found from the foot, told from the start:

x = b, b = c and c = d. All three are given. By the chain technique, on x = b and b = c, x = c. By the chain technique, on x = c and c = d, x = d. By the turn-about technique, on x = d, d = x. ∎

The finished argument never shows the search. And there is another argument, which turns each given about and chains those; it is longer, and also a proof.

Special challenges

You can say what an argument is, on the ground floor: a relationship of statements that ends by ruling out the guesses that do not fit the techniques. Its form here is plain sentences in a row, one claim each, each with its reason. The reasons are givens, axioms by name, techs by name, built or proved, each leaning only on sentences before it. The argument is closed with the end mark, and you can parse one, sentence by sentence. You have an axiom with nothing before it (self-sameness), and an axiom by which a fact carried across a sameness is still so (swap). You have techniques argued from them (turn-about and chain), which may now be cited as reasons themselves. And you have a gallery of the sentences and swaps that only look licensed. Those axioms, techniques and illegal sentences, in the form of an argument, are the ground floor.

Most questions on the ladder name a foot and ask about earning it. Two other kinds of ask come up in the challenges, and they always use the same words. A challenge may put something off limits: “The self-sameness axiom is off limits”, “supposing is off limits”. Then the argument may not use it, even where it would help; the point is to see what can be done without it. And a challenge may ask you to go as far as you can. You do not know what you can earn until you have made the argument. So an argument goes as far as its moves will take it. Sometimes the honest answer is an argument that stops short: here is the last sentence I can earn, and here is the step no move allows. An argument that stops in the right place, and says why, is a complete answer to that question.

The proof itself is only partly built until 4d builds it. So the builds and closers wait here for whoever wants them. Argue them in plain words for now.

Who said it first

Pascal described a perfect method: define every word, and prove every claim. Then he admitted it cannot be done. Each definition uses words, and each proof uses claims, so the work never ends unless you stop at words too plain to define and claims too plain to prove.

Pascal, “On the Geometrical Spirit” (c. 1657); paraphrase
