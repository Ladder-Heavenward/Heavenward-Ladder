Guess, Work

Every statement so far has been a guess at how things are. This step names the two ways a guess gets worked: from what is said, and from what is seen. Neither one is exact, and both are rigorous.

A short word can be correct about more than it could ever count. Working from what is said is deduction; working from what is seen is induction.

0c1A Word Instead of a Count

“It is raining” is correct even when “a hundred and ten thousand raindrops are going to hit the roof in the next minute” is not. The first statement counts nothing. The word “raining” holds every drop at once, however many fall, so the statement does not have to be exact to be correct.

The second statement is exact, and that is why it is so easy to get wrong. One drop more or less and it fails. The short word gives up the detail and keeps the claim.

0c2Working From What Is Said

Deduction works from statements already written, and writes only what they let you write. 0b's swap is one: from “Tibbles is the cat on the mat” and “Tibbles is grey”, you may write “the cat on the mat is grey”. Nothing is looked at. The work is all in the writing.

The guess is in what you start from. If “Tibbles is grey” was a poor guess, deduction carries it forward faithfully, and the cat on the mat is just as poorly guessed.

0c3Working From What Is Seen

Induction works the other way: from cases to one word for all of them. Drops on the window, on the street, on your sleeve, and you say “it is raining”. The word gathers the drops you saw and all the ones you didn't.

More cases make the guess harder to shake, but they never finish it. One dry patch under a tree does not undo “it is raining”. A dry sky would.

0c4Rigor Out of Guesswork

Both kinds of work end in short words used as if they were exact. That is the economy of language: a word packed from many cases says no more than the cases can carry, so it stays correct while the details go wrong. It works so well that we don't even notice how messy the guess is.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the word that holds

A guess is rigorous when its words claim only what its cases can carry. “It is raining” is anexact, because it never counts the drops. It is rigorous, because it claims nothing the drops could undo.

The ladder keeps this habit on purpose. When it later writes “every” with the mess taken out, it is this same word-for-all-the-cases, made exact.

Accepted names

Working from what is said is deduction; working from cases to a rule is induction. Philosophers call the trouble that more cases never finish a guess the problem of induction.
