Why Does the Bell Curve Keep Turning Up?

Why does the bell curve keep turning up? Heights of adults in Oregon, errors in a surveyor’s chain across the Willamette Valley, the weight of a bag of rice off a packing line in Sacramento, the average of a poll: plot any of them and the same shape appears, high in the middle and thinning at the edges in the same way. The things measured have nothing to do with each other. Rung 30 proved that a rescaled sum of many independent parts heads to the bell, but its proof asked each part to have an average-function, and many measured things have none. What makes the question hard is that the bell seems to come from nowhere: no single part is a bell. This page answers with turnings. Every chance variable has a turning average, which always exists. Turning averages of independent parts multiply. Near zero every one of them looks the same, up to its spread. Multiply many, and the bell’s own turning average is what comes out. And the page ends with a case where the bell does not turn up at all, and why.

Average a turning of a chance variable: the result exists for every variable, starts at one and has size at most one. For independent parts, the turning average of the sum is the product of theirs. Near zero, every turning average with a finite spread is one, plus i times the average times t, minus half the second power average times t squared. Rescale a sum, multiply its parts’ turning averages, and the product heads to e to the minus t squared over two, which is the bell’s own. Where the spread is endless, as for the spots of a lighthouse beam on a beach, the average of many is no narrower than one.

32stat1The Turning Average

For a chance variable X and a real t, the turning average of X is ψ(t) = E e^(itX), the average of a turning of speed t read at X. When X has a density f, it is ∫ f(x)e^(itx) dx, the transform of 32e read at the frequency −t/(2π).

Turning-average technique: every chance variable has a turning average; it has ψ(0) = 1, size at most 1, and is continuous in t.

|e^{itX}| = 1 @@ a turning has size 1 (26b)
\psi(t) = \operatorname{E}\cos tX + i\operatorname{E}\sin tX \text{ exists} @@ bounded sized functions have averages (30g)
|\psi(t)| \le \operatorname{E}|e^{itX}| = 1,\ \ \psi(0) = \operatorname{E}1 = 1 @@ the size of an average is at most the average of the size
\htmlClass{new}{t_k \to t \Rightarrow \psi(t_k) \to \psi(t)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the fenced-limit technique (30d), with fence 1

30j’s average-function E e^(tX) can be endless at every t ≠ 0, as it is for the spots of 32·stat.5. The turning average never is: that is what the i buys.

32stat2Sums Multiply, and Every Start Looks Alike

Sum-turnings technique: for independent X and Y, the turning average of X + Y is the product of theirs; and if X has average μ and finite E X², then near 0, ψ(t) = 1 + iμt − ½E X² t² + r(t), with r(t)/t² heading to 0.

e^{it(X + Y)} = e^{itX}e^{itY} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
\operatorname{E}(e^{itX}e^{itY}) = \operatorname{E}e^{itX}\cdot\operatorname{E}e^{itY} @@ the independent-averages-multiply technique (30g), for the floor and upright parts, multiplied out
e^{iu} = 1 + iu - \tfrac12u^2 + \rho(u),\ \ |\rho(u)| \le \min(u^2,\ |u|^3/6) @@ the remainder (23b), for the floor and upright parts
\operatorname{E}|\rho(tX)|/t^2 \le \operatorname{E}\min(X^2,\ |t||X|^3/6) \to 0 @@ the fenced-limit technique (30d), with fence X²
\htmlClass{new}{\psi_{X+Y} = \psi_X\psi_Y,\ \ \psi(t) = 1 + i\mu t - \tfrac12\operatorname{E}X^2\,t^2 + o(t^2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ average the expansion at u = tX

So near 0 a turning average sees only the average and the spread. Every other feature of the chance law, its lumps and its lean, is pushed out to larger t.

32stat3The Bell’s Own Turning Average

The bell of 30j has density φ(x) = e^(−x²/2)/√(2π).

Bell-turning technique: the bell’s turning average is e^(−t²/2): a bell again.

\psi(t) = \int\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\,e^{itx}\,dx @@ the turning average of a density
x = \sqrt{2\pi}\,y:\ \ \psi(t) = \int e^{-\pi y^2}\,e^{-2\pi iy(-t/\sqrt{2\pi})}\,dy @@ slide the variable
= e^{-\pi(t/\sqrt{2\pi})^2} @@ the own-transform technique (32e2)
\htmlClass{new}{\psi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ π t²/(2π) = t²/2

A bell with spread σ² has e^(−σ²t²/2). Multiply two, for independent bells: e^(−(σ₁² + σ₂²)t²/2), a bell whose spread is the sum of theirs. A sum of independent bells is a bell, with no limit needed.

32stat4Every Rescaled Sum Heads to the Bell

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent copies of X, with average μ and finite spread σ² > 0. Nothing more is asked: no average-function. Let Zₙ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ − nμ)/(σ√n).

Turning-limit technique: the turning average of Zₙ heads to e^(−t²/2), the bell’s, at every t.

Y = \tfrac{X - \mu}{\sigma}:\ \ \operatorname{E}Y = 0,\ \ \operatorname{E}Y^2 = 1 @@ 30g
\psi_{Z_n}(t) = \psi_Y\big(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt n}\big)^n @@ the sum-turnings technique, n times
\psi_Y\big(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt n}\big) = 1 - \tfrac{t^2}{2n} + o\big(\tfrac1n\big) @@ the start, at t/√n
\big(1 - \tfrac{t^2/2 + o(1)}{n}\big)^n \to e^{-t^2/2} @@ (1 − λ/N)ᴺ heads to e^(−λ) (22j), with λ heading to t²/2
\htmlClass{new}{\psi_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{-t^2/2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell-turning technique

Rung 30 held one step in trust on 30j: that matching limits of average-functions give matching limits of chances. The same trust carries over to turning averages, and it is still held: by the undo-transform technique of 32e a chance law with a density is fixed by its turning average, but that matching limits give matching chances is proved much higher up the ladder. What this page adds is that the trust no longer needs the average-function at all, only a finite spread.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

A packing line in Sacramento fills bags of rice. Each bag’s weight is the sum of thousands of small pours, each a little more or less than planned, for reasons no one tracks: a grain that bounces, a gust from a door, the wear of a valve.

The bell is the shape a sum passes through once its parts have faded into their averages and spreads.

Near 0 the turning average of each pour sees only its average and spread. Multiply thousands of them, and every other feature fades from view; what is left is the bell. The weights are anexact, and no one knows any pour’s story. The bell is rigorous, because the step from many parts to one shape is a technique.

If one valve on the line stuck now and then and dumped a whole extra cup, would the weights still form a bell? What would you check first?

32stat5When the Bell Does Not Turn Up

A lighthouse stands 1 km off the straight beach at Long Beach, Washington. Its beam points at a random angle, all angles facing the shore equally likely, and lights a spot on the sand at a distance X from the point nearest the light. Then X = tan Θ with Θ spread evenly on (−π/2, π/2), and X has density 1/(π(1 + x²)).

Heavy-tail technique: for the lighthouse spots, ψ(t) = e^(−|t|), so the average of n independent spots has the same chances as a single spot: averaging does not narrow it.

\int e^{-|x|}e^{-2\pi i x\xi}\,dx = \tfrac{2}{1 + 4\pi^2\xi^2} @@ the build standing question of 32e: split at 0
\int\tfrac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\,e^{itx}\,dx = e^{-|t|} @@ the undo-transform technique (32e3), slid to the variable t
\psi_{\bar X_n}(t) = \psi\big(\tfrac tn\big)^n = \big(e^{-|t|/n}\big)^n = e^{-|t|} @@ the sum-turnings technique, then divide by n
\htmlClass{new}{\bar X_n \text{ has the same turning average as one spot}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and so the same chances (32e3)

The spots have no average: ∫|x|/(π(1 + x²)) is endless (16i). 30h’s averages-settle technique needs an average, and the turning-limit technique a spread. Without them, an average of a thousand spots is no better than one. Some measured things are built from rare huge events, floods and market crashes among them, and for those the bell turns up less than people expect.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The turning average is the characteristic function φ_X(t) = E e^(itX). The sum-turnings technique gives the multiplicativity of characteristic functions for independent sums and their second-order Taylor expansion. The turning-limit technique is the central limit theorem (Lindeberg–Lévy) proved by characteristic functions; the step still held in trust is Lévy’s continuity theorem. The lighthouse spots follow the Cauchy distribution, and the laws with e^(−|t|^α) are the stable laws, α the stability index; α = 2 is the normal law. Laws whose tails thin like a power are heavy-tailed.

Who said it first

Pierre-Simon Laplace used turning averages for sums of errors in 1810. Augustin-Louis Cauchy found in 1853 the law that bears his name and that averaging does not narrow it, against Irénée-Jules Bienaymé. Aleksandr Lyapunov proved the central limit theorem by these averages in 1901, and Jarl Lindeberg in 1922 and Paul Lévy in the 1920s and 1930s gave its classic forms; Lévy also found the stable laws. In 32·stat.7.5, Adolphe Quetelet held the bell to be nature’s law of the average man (1835), and Francis Galton called it a law of frequency of error that reigns with serenity; Henri Poincaré reported the quip that everyone believes in it, the experimenters because they think it a theorem of mathematics, the mathematicians because they think it a fact of observation.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each spot is a tangent of an even angle, so the average of many is the tangent of an average angle, and the average angle heads to 0.” Not allowed: the average of tangents is not the tangent of the average. The angles average to a bell around 0, but the spots do not.
