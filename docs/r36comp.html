36·comp · computing

The circle and the cylinder in a machine

The circle and the cylinder in a machine: what does a computer keep when it keeps a colour? A photo of the Oregon coast on a phone holds millions of places, and each place holds a colour in a handful of whole numbers. A colour picker shows a ring of hues and a slider for brightness. A picture program offers multiply, screen, dodge, burn and difference as buttons. Every one of these is the rung’s arithmetic, written down for a machine that can only hold whole numbers in a fixed range. What makes it hard is that the machine’s numbers are not the eye’s numbers. They are amounts of three chosen lights, so they live inside a gamut triangle; they come in steps, so every blend is rounded; and the machine draws its circle of hue with its own choice of where 0 sits. This page reads the machine’s choices with the rung’s tools.

A screen keeps each place as three whole numbers from 0 to 255, the amounts of its three primaries, and divides by 255 to get the rung’s coordinates in the cube from 0 to 1. Hue, saturation and brightness are read from the largest and smallest of the three, with red at 0 by the machine’s own bend, and a grey gets no hue, so the program writes 0. The blends are the rung’s functions done in whole numbers and rounded. And a fourth number, which machines also call alpha, says how much of a layer covers what is beneath: a point on the segment from the lower colour to the upper. Bytes are written in sixteens, two digits each. A picture is a function from places to colours; sampled, fine stripes disguise themselves as broad ones, and averaging over each place is the cure that works well and not exactly.

36comp1Three whole numbers

A screen has three primaries, a red, a green and a blue light, and each place on the screen mixes them. The machine keeps the three amounts as whole numbers from 0 to 255, one byte each, since a byte is eight places of 4i’s writing in twos and 2⁸ = 256. Dividing each by 255 gives three numbers in [0, 1]: the amounts u, v, w of 36f2’s gamut, scaled so that the brightest the screen can make is 1.

Three-bytes technique: a screen that keeps three bytes a place can show exactly 256³ = 16 777 216 colours, and every one of them lies in the gamut of its three primaries. Proof: 256 choices for each of three amounts, and the counting of 4’s products; each colour is a mix with non-negative amounts, so it is in the gamut by 36f2.

So a screen shows many colours and not every colour: 36f5’s “the screen shows every colour” is wrong twice over, once for the triangle and once for the steps.

36comp2The machine’s cylinder

Colour pickers show the cylinder of 36e. The machine reads it off the three amounts r, g, b in [0, 1]. Let M be the largest of the three and m the smallest. The brightness is M. The saturation is (M − m)/M, the share of the brightness that is not grey. The hue is read from which amount is largest and how the other two compare, one sixth of the circle at a time.

Hue-sector technique: when red is the largest amount and blue the smallest, the machine’s hue is 60° × (g − b)/(M − m), and a grey, with M = m, has no hue, so the program writes 0. The other five sixths of the circle have formulas of the same shape. The machine’s bend puts red at 0, not purple; 36d1 said the bend is a choice and another bend gives other numbers.

(R, G, B) = (255, 128, 0) \Rightarrow (r, g, b) = (1,\ \allowbreak 0.502,\ \allowbreak 0) @@ divide by 255
M = 1,\ \allowbreak \ m = 0 @@ red largest, blue smallest
\text{brightness } 1,\ \allowbreak \ \text{saturation } (1 - 0)/1 = 1 @@ the definitions
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hue} = 60^\circ \times 0.502 \approx 30^\circ} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hue-sector technique

At a grey the formula reads 0/0, 36e3’s axis met in a program. Programs do not stop there; they write hue 0 and saturation 0, and the grey-axis technique says that is harmless, since at r = 0 any θ names the same grey. A grey that is nudged by one step can then jump to any hue at all: the edge of 36e.4.2, on a screen.

36comp3Blends in whole numbers

The blend buttons of a picture program are 36h’s functions, done on bytes. Multiply of bytes c and d is c·d/255, which is 36h1’s pigment mix c·d on [0, 1] written in steps of 1/255. Screen is 255 − (255 − c)(255 − d)/255. Darken, lighten and difference are the least, the greatest and the gap, and need no rounding.

Byte-blend technique: multiply and screen on bytes give whole numbers only after rounding, and each rounding moves the result by at most half a step, 1/510 of the whole range. One example first: multiply of 200 and 100 is 20 000/255 ≈ 78.43, kept as 78; screen of 200 and 100 is 255 − 55·155/255 ≈ 221.57, kept as 222.

So a chain of blends can drift: each rounding is at most half a step, and the moves of a chain add at most, so ten blends in a row can drift by up to five steps. That is why programs that care keep more than one byte a primary while they work.

36comp4The other alpha

Machines keep a fourth number for each place of a layer, and call it alpha too. It is not the rung’s brightness. It says how much of the place the layer covers: 1 covers fully, 0 leaves the layer invisible, ½ lets half of what lies beneath show through. 36e6 said where the other use lives; it lives here.

Over-blend technique: a layer of colour c with alpha α laid over a colour d gives α·c + (1 − α)·d in each coordinate, the point a share α of the way from d to c. Proof: 18a5’s dividing a segment, with t = α, in each coordinate.

c = (255, 0, 0),\ \allowbreak \ d = (0, 0, 255),\ \allowbreak \ \alpha = 0.25 @@ the layer, the ground, and how much the layer covers
0.25\,(255, 0, 0) + 0.75\,(0, 0, 255) = (63.75,\ \allowbreak 0,\ \allowbreak 191.25) @@ the over-blend technique, in each coordinate
\htmlClass{new}{(64, 0, 191)} \quad \blacksquare @@ rounded to bytes

The over-blend is a mix of lights at strengths α and 1 − α, a chord of 36d4, and so it desaturates between two different hues. A red layer faded over blue passes through dull purples, never through the full purple of the ring.

36comp5Sixteen marks for a byte

4i wrote every number in tens, and showed in 4i14 that any base above 1 works as well, twos among them. A byte is eight places in twos, and eight is a long row to read. Machines and the people who program them write bytes in sixteens instead, because 16 = 2⁴: four places in twos make exactly one place in sixteens.

Sixteens need sixteen digits, and the ten of 4i run out at 9. From here on, inside a numeral written in sixteens, the marks A, B, C, D, E and F are digits, worth ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen and fifteen. Outside such a numeral the letters keep every job they had. A colour written for a machine puts the mark # first, and here # says only that three bytes in sixteens follow, red’s, then green’s, then blue’s.

Two-digit technique: every byte, from 0 to 255, is written in sixteens with exactly two digits, and each digit is four places of the byte in twos. Proof: by the split-by-a-base technique (4i6) a byte is 16q + d with d below 16, and q is below 16 because the byte is below 256 = 16²; the one-split technique (4i7) makes the two digits the only ones. And 16q + d, with q and d each below 2⁴, is q’s four places in twos followed by d’s.

255 = 15 \cdot 16 + 15 \to \text{FF} @@ split by sixteen (4i6): digits fifteen and fifteen
128 = 8 \cdot 16 + 0 \to 80 @@ in twos 1000 0000: four places, then four places
0 \to 00 @@ two digits always, so the three bytes can be read off without a gap
\htmlClass{new}{(255, 128, 0) = \#\text{FF}8000} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two-digit technique, three times

So six digits name each of the 16 777 216 colours of the three-bytes technique, and a grey is any colour whose three pairs are the same, as #808080 is: 36e3’s axis, in a name.

36comp6A picture is a function

A screen’s places stand in rows and columns. Number the columns 0 to w − 1 and the rows 0 to h − 1; each place is a pair (i, j). Here w and h count columns and rows; they are not 36f’s amount w or 36d’s h. From here on, a picture is a function P from the pairs to the colours a screen keeps: P(i, j) is the three bytes of the place in column i and row j. It is 9a’s function, nothing more, and every fact about functions is a fact about pictures.

Picture-count technique: a picture of w columns and h rows is kept in 3wh bytes, and there are exactly 256^(3wh) pictures of that size. Proof: three bytes for each of the wh places; one choice of 256³ for each place, and 4’s products. A picture of 1920 columns and 1080 rows has 2 073 600 places and takes 6 220 800 bytes, before any squeezing.

Seen as functions, the picture program’s work sorts itself. A blend of two pictures is done place by place, P and Q to the picture whose value at (i, j) is the blend of P(i, j) and Q(i, j): 36i3’s seen mix was taken the same way. Flipping a picture left to right is composing it with the map (i, j) to (w − 1 − i, j), which is one-to-one and onto, so nothing is lost; doing it twice is the identity. Shrinking a picture to half its width maps two columns to one, so it is not one-to-one, and what it forgets cannot come back. And the scene in front of a camera is a function too, from points of a rectangle of the plane to the colours of 36e. A picture keeps that function at finitely many points.

36comp7Stripes in disguise

A camera takes the scene at one point per place, the place’s centre. That is sampling, and 32g counted what samples can and cannot keep: sampled at the rate r, a turning of frequency ξ and one of ξ + r give the same samples (the same-samples technique). A picture samples at one place per place, so its rate is 1 per place in each direction.

Stripe technique: a scene of even stripes repeating every p places, with p below 2, is sampled as stripes repeating every 1/|1/p − k| places, for the whole number k that brings 1/p − k between −½ and ½. Proof: the stripes have frequency 1/p per place; by the same-samples technique with r = 1, the samples are those of 1/p − k for every whole k, and the one between −½ and ½ is the frequency the picture shows. One example: stripes every 0.9 places have frequency 1.111…, read as 0.111…, so the picture shows broad stripes every 9 places that are not in the scene at all.

That is why a striped shirt on a screen can crawl with broad bands, and a brick wall can grow curves. A sharp edge has the same trouble in another form. A step from one colour to another has frequencies without end, so no rate is enough for 32g’s samples-are-coefficients technique, and the edge comes out as a staircase, sharp where the scene was sharp and stepped where the scene was straight.

36comp8Averaging over the place

The cure is to stop reading the scene at one point. From here on, the averaged picture gives each place the average of the scene over the whole square of the place, coordinate by coordinate, as the mix of 36c2 at the strengths of the shares of the square. An edge that crosses a place then gives it a colour between the two sides, in proportion to what each side covers.

Coverage technique: if a straight edge splits a place so that a share s of its square is colour c and the rest is colour d, the averaged picture gives the place s·c + (1 − s)·d, the over-blend of 36·comp4 with alpha s. Proof: the average over the square is the sum over its two parts of each part’s colour times its share (5d spreading), and the shares add to 1.

A machine cannot average over a square exactly, so it guesses. It takes k columns and k rows of samples inside each place and averages them. The counted share can then take only k² + 1 values, 0, 1/k², …, 1, so an edge shows at most k² + 1 shades between c and d; four by four gives 17. And averaging over the square is a filter before sampling: it weakens the frequencies above ½, the ones that disguise themselves, but it does not take them to 0. So averaged pictures still alias, much less. It is a method that works well and is not exact, and the machines say so by offering more samples for more time.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Three bytes a place is 24-bit colour, or true colour, in RGB. The machine’s cylinder is HSV, hue, saturation and value, and its cousin HSL; the hue-sector formula is the hexcone model. The blend buttons are blend modes: multiply, screen, darken, lighten, difference, linear dodge and linear burn. The machine’s alpha is the alpha channel, and the over-blend is the over operator of compositing. Keeping more than a byte a primary while working is high bit depth. Writing in sixteens is hexadecimal, and #FF8000 is a hex colour code. A picture as a function is a raster image, and its places are pixels. The disguise of the stripe technique is aliasing, and the broad false bands are moiré; the staircase of an edge is called jaggies. Averaging over the place is anti-aliasing, the k by k average is supersampling, and the average over the square is a box filter.

Who said it first

Alvy Ray Smith gave the hexcone formulas for hue, saturation and value in 1978. Alvy Ray Smith and Ed Catmull added the alpha channel in the 1970s, and Thomas Porter and Tom Duff set out the over operator and its companions in 1984. The sRGB primaries used by most screens were fixed in 1996. Edwin Catmull drew anti-aliased pictures by coverage in 1978, and Franklin Crow set out the causes of aliasing in pictures in 1977.
