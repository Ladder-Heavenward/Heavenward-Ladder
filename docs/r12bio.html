How Hard Does a Muscle Pull to Hold a Cup?

How hard does a muscle pull to hold a cup? Hold a full mug with your forearm level and your elbow at a right angle. The mug weighs a few newtons. The muscle on the front of your upper arm, attached just past the elbow, pulls with many times that. This page treats the forearm as 12·phys’s lever turning about the elbow, finds the muscle’s pull from the balance of turning effects, finds the push on the elbow joint, sees how bending the arm changes the pull, and finds what the body gains from such a poor lever.

The forearm turns about the elbow. The mug pulls down far from the elbow; the muscle pulls up close to it. Balance the turning effects: muscle pull × its distance = load × its distance, so the muscle pulls the load times the ratio of the distances. The elbow pushes back with the difference.

12bio1The Forearm as a Lever

Sarah holds a mug of tea, 0.5 kg, in her hand, forearm level, elbow bent at a right angle. Her forearm turns about the elbow joint, the pivot. Her biceps, the muscle on the front of the upper arm, is fixed to the forearm about 4 cm from the elbow; her hand is about 33 cm from it.

From here on, on this page, the arm of a force is its distance from the elbow along the forearm, and its turning effect is the force times its arm (12·phys). Weight is mass times about 9.8 N per kg (12·phys), so the mug weighs about 4.9 N.

12bio2The Muscle Pulls Many Times the Load

Ignore the forearm’s own weight for now. The forearm is still, so the turning effects about the elbow balance (12·phys).

F_{\text{muscle}} \cdot 4 = 4.9 \cdot 33 @@ turning effects balance about the elbow
F_{\text{muscle}} = 4.9 \cdot \tfrac{33}{4} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{F_{\text{muscle}} \approx 40 \text{ N}} @@ about eight times the mug

Joint-lever technique: a muscle attached close to a joint pulls the load times the ratio of the load’s arm to its own; for the forearm that ratio is about 8. The forearm itself, about 1.5 kg acting about 15 cm from the elbow, adds 1.5 × 9.8 × 15/4 ≈ 55 N more: holding the empty arm out costs more than the mug.

12bio3The Push on the Joint Is Bigger Still

The forearm isn’t moving up or down either, so the up and down forces balance too. The muscle pulls up hard; the mug pulls down a little; the upper arm’s bone must push down on the forearm at the elbow to make up the difference.

\text{up} = \text{down} @@ still, so the forces balance
F_{\text{muscle}} = F_{\text{joint}} + 4.9 @@ mug and joint push down
\htmlClass{new}{F_{\text{joint}} \approx 40 - 4.9 \approx 35 \text{ N}} @@ mug alone; about 90 N with the forearm’s weight

Holding a small load pulls the muscle and presses the joint far harder than the load itself. That is why joints wear: the cartilage in an elbow or knee carries many times the weights we lift. A physiotherapist planning exercises after a joint injury works these forces out to keep the joint’s push low.

12bio4Bending the Arm Changes the Lever Arm

When the forearm is not level, gravity still pulls straight down, so what counts is each force’s distance from the elbow measured level, across. Tilt the forearm up or down by an angle and both the mug’s and the muscle’s level distances shrink.

\text{level forearm: mug’s arm } 33 \text{ cm} @@ greatest
\text{forearm lowered halfway to straight: mug’s arm about } 23 \text{ cm} @@ the load turns the arm less
\text{the muscle’s line of pull also changes} @@ its arm shrinks as the arm straightens
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the pull needed depends on the angle}} @@ which is why a weight feels heavier at some angles

A trainer knows a curl is hardest with the forearm level, where the load’s arm is longest, and easiest near the top and bottom. Measurements of the biceps’ arm in living people, taken from scans at many elbow angles, show it largest near a right angle too.

12bio5What the Short Lever Buys: Speed at the Hand

Why would the body attach a muscle where it must pull eight times the load? Because the lever trades force for distance (12·phys). When the biceps shortens by 1 cm, the hand moves about 33/4 ≈ 8 cm, in the same time.

\text{muscle shortens } 1 \text{ cm} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{hand moves } \tfrac{33}{4} \approx 8 \text{ cm} @@ distances in the arm ratio
\text{muscle shortens at } 10 \text{ cm/s} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{hand moves at about } 80 \text{ cm/s} @@ speeds in the same ratio
\htmlClass{new}{\text{force} \times 8,\ \ \text{distance and speed} \div 8} @@ the work done is the same

A muscle can shorten only a few centimetres and only so fast; attaching it near the joint turns that small, slow shortening into a wide, fast swing of the hand. Throwing, reaching and hitting all need speed at the hand more than force. Muscles are strong enough to pay the price.

Accepted names

A lever with the effort between the pivot and the load is a third-class lever. A force’s turning effect is its moment or torque, and a muscle’s arm is its moment arm. The ratio of output to input force is the mechanical advantage, here about 1/8.

Who said it first

Giovanni Alfonso Borelli worked out the forces in muscles and joints as levers in De motu animalium, published in 1680 and 1681. The forearm dimensions here are typical textbook values.
