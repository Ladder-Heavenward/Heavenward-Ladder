28c · proof

The chain rule along several roads

On rung 13 a chain of two functions had a slope that was the product of their slopes. Here a change can travel by several roads at once. Turn up the heat in a tyre and the pressure rises directly, and also because the rubber stretches and the air has more room. This step proves that the roads add up in exactly the way grids multiply. The best flat map of a chain is the chain of the best flat maps.

If g has a best flat map at a, and f has one at g(a), then doing g then f has a best flat map at a, and its slope grid is the product of the two slope grids.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 28b (the best flat map), 27d (doing one grid then another is multiplying) and 13e (the chain technique for slopes). 28e runs a chain backwards.

28c1Straight maps stretch by a bounded amount

Stands on 27d1, 18b1, 27i1.

A chain feeds the step h through two maps. To control the leftover, one fact about straight maps is needed: they never stretch an arrow by more than a fixed factor.

Stretch-bound technique: if A is a grid with m rows and n columns, and c is the square root of the sum of the squares of its entries, then |Ah| ≤ c|h| for every h.

(Ah)_i = \text{row}_i \cdot h @@ the columns technique (27d), read along a row
|(Ah)_i| \le |\text{row}_i|\,|h| @@ the dot-bound technique (18b, in lists of n numbers by 27i1)
|Ah|^2 = \textstyle\sum_i (Ah)_i^2 \le \sum_i |\text{row}_i|^2\,|h|^2 @@ square and add
\htmlClass{new}{|Ah| \le c\,|h|,\ \ c^2 = \textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rows together hold every entry once

28c2Chains of flat maps

Stands on 28b1, 28c1, 27d2.

Chain-of-flat-maps technique: if g has best flat map B at a, and f has best flat map A at g(a), then f ∘ g has best flat map AB at a. Its slope grid is the product of the slope grids.

g(a+h) = g(a) + Bh + r(h),\ \ \ f(g(a)+k) = f(g(a)) + Ak + s(k) @@ the two best flat maps (28b1)
k = Bh + r(h) @@ the step that g hands on to f
f(g(a+h)) = f(g(a)) + ABh + A\,r(h) + s(k) @@ substitute, and A is straight (27c)
|A\,r(h)| \le c_A |r(h)|,\ \ \text{so } \dfrac{|A\,r(h)|}{|h|} \to 0 @@ the stretch-bound technique (28c1)
|k| \le c_B|h| + |r(h)| \le (c_B + 1)|h| \text{ for small } h @@ 28c1, and |r(h)| ≤ |h| once h is small
\dfrac{|s(k)|}{|h|} \le (c_B + 1)\dfrac{|s(k)|}{|k|} \to 0 \ \ (k \ne 0) @@ k → 0 as h → 0; when k = 0, s(k) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{D(f\circ g)(a) = Df(g(a))\,Dg(a)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the leftover shrinks faster than h; doing B then A is AB (27d)

With one input and one output each, the grids are single numbers and this is 13e’s chain technique. The new thing is that the product of grids is a sum over the roads: entry (i, j) of AB adds, over every middle quantity, the slope along one road times the slope along the next.

28c3Several roads to one output

Stands on 28c2.

The most used case: a single output f(x, y) along a moving point (x(t), y(t)).

Roads-add technique: if x(t) and y(t) have slopes at t and f has a best flat map at (x(t), y(t)), then the slope of f(x(t), y(t)) is fx · x′(t) + fy · y′(t).

Dg(t) = \begin{pmatrix} x'(t) \\ y'(t) \end{pmatrix},\ \ Df = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \end{pmatrix} @@ the slope-grid technique (28b2), g(t) = (x(t), y(t))
\htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{d}{dt} f(x(t),y(t)) = f_x\,x'(t) + f_y\,y'(t)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c2): a row times a column

Each term is one road: how fast x moves, times how much f cares about x; then the same for y. The total change is the sum over the roads.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The chain-of-flat-maps technique is the multivariable chain rule. The stretch-bound number c is the Frobenius (or Hilbert–Schmidt) norm of the matrix; the least such factor is the operator norm. The roads-add technique is the chain rule for a total derivative, often drawn as a tree diagram of variables.

Who said it first

Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli used the chain rule for one variable in the 1690s. Euler used the several-roads form freely in the 1750s in his work on fluids. The clean statement with linear maps and matrices belongs to the twentieth century, through William Henry Young (1910) and Jean Dieudonné’s Foundations of Modern Analysis (1960).
