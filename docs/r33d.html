33d · proof

When every point is the average of its neighbours

Leave a metal plate long enough with its rim held at fixed temperatures, some parts warm and some cold, and it stops changing. Heat still flows through it, from the warm stretch of rim to the cold one, but every point takes in exactly what it gives out. The heat rule with nothing changing in time says that the second slopes in the two directions cancel. This step finds a great many such still states at once, in a place the ladder has already been: the real part of any function with a complex slope is one. Then it proves the property in the title. The temperature at any point of a still plate is the plain average of the temperatures round any circle about it. From that, the hottest point of a still plate is on its rim, the rim fixes everything inside, and on a round plate the inside can be written down from the rim by a series.

A still state of heat has its two second slopes cancelling, and the real part of every function with a complex slope is one. Its value at any point is the average round any circle about the point, so it is greatest and least on the rim, the rim fixes the inside, and on a disc a series builds the inside from the rim.

33d1Still states, and where to find them

A plate that has stopped changing has u_t = 0, so by the heat rule in a block (33a1), u_xx + u_yy = 0. A function with continuous second slopes and Δu = 0 on an open set is balanced there. Its accepted name is harmonic. x² − y² is balanced: its second slopes are 2 and −2. x² + y² is not.

Real-part technique: if f = u + iv has a complex slope at every point of an open set, then u and v are both balanced there, and u_x = v_y, u_y = −v_x.

f^{\prime} = u_x + i\,v_x @@ the slope along the floor, h = t real (26d)
f^{\prime} = \tfrac{1}{i}(u_y + i\,v_y) = v_y - i\,u_y @@ the two-ways technique (26d): along the upright the slope is i times f′
u_x = v_y,\ \ u_y = -v_x @@ the same number two ways: match the real and imaginary parts
u, v \text{ have continuous slopes of every order} @@ f is a power series near each point (26g), and so is f′
u_{xx} = v_{yx},\ \ u_{yy} = -v_{xy} @@ take the slope of the first pair along x, the second along y
\htmlClass{new}{u_{xx} + u_{yy} = v_{yx} - v_{xy} = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d); the same lines run for v

So every function of rung 26 hands over two balanced functions. From z² come x² − y² and 2xy. From e^z come eˣ cos y and eˣ sin y. From the logarithm, away from 0, comes ln √(x² + y²): the log of the distance from 0, balanced everywhere but at 0.

33d2The average round a circle

Let u be balanced on an open set holding the closed disc of radius r about the point w = (w₁, w₂). For 0 < ρ ≤ r, let φ(ρ) be the average of u round the circle of radius ρ about w:

\(\varphi(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} u(w_1 + \rho\cos\theta,\ w_2 + \rho\sin\theta)\,d\theta.\)

Circle-average technique: the value of a balanced function at a point is the average of its values round every circle about that point that lies, with its inside, where the function is balanced.

\varphi^{\prime}(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} (u_x\cos\theta + u_y\sin\theta)\,d\theta @@ the slope under the total, as on 33a1; roads add (28c) for the slope along ρ
\varphi^{\prime}(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi\rho}\times\big(\text{flux of } \nabla u \text{ out of the circle of radius } \rho\big) @@ the flux across the circle traced as (w₁ + ρ cos θ, w₂ + ρ sin θ): x′ = −ρ sin θ, y′ = ρ cos θ (28j1)
\varphi^{\prime}(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi\rho}\textstyle\iint_{\text{disc}} (u_{xx} + u_{yy}) = 0 @@ the spread technique (28j) on the disc, which is slanted both ways
\varphi(\rho) = \varphi(r) \text{ for every } 0 < \rho \le r @@ zero slope means flat (14d)
\varphi(\rho) \to u(w) \text{ as } \rho \to 0 @@ u is continuous at w, so close to u(w) all round a small circle
\htmlClass{new}{u(w) = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} u(w_1 + r\cos\theta,\ w_2 + r\sin\theta)\,d\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ a constant that heads to u(w) is u(w)

This is the title of the step. Every point of a still plate is the plain average of its neighbours, on every circle at once. The heat rule says why in words: a point warmer than the average round it would be losing heat, and a still plate loses none.

33d3The rim fixes the inside

Let D be a bounded open set in the plane, and let u be continuous on D with its edge, and balanced in D.

Edge-rules-inside technique: the greatest and the least values of u are taken on the edge of D.

v = u + \varepsilon(x^2 + y^2),\ \ \Delta v = 4\varepsilon > 0 @@ the tilt of 33b1, now bending up both ways
v \text{ has a greatest value at some } p \text{ in } D \text{ or on its edge} @@ closed and bounded (25d)
p \in D \Rightarrow v_{xx}(p) \le 0,\ v_{yy}(p) \le 0,\ \Delta v(p) \le 0 @@ a top along each knob (14a, 14f): a clash with 4ε > 0
\max u \le \max v = \max_{\text{edge}} v \le \max_{\text{edge}} u + \varepsilon\rho^2 @@ D lies within distance ρ of 0
\htmlClass{new}{\max_{D} u = \max_{\text{edge}} u} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε was any positive number; for the least value use −u

One-still-state technique: two functions continuous on D with its edge, balanced in D, and equal on the edge, are equal in D. If they differ by at most δ on the edge, they differ by at most δ in D. The proof is the lines of 33b2 with the edge in place of the floor and walls.

So the rim of a still plate fixes every temperature inside. Nothing about the past is needed. That is a different kind of question from the heat and the string, which needed a start: here there is no start, only a boundary all round.

33d4A round plate from its rim

Let the plate be the disc of radius 1 about 0, and write points by reach r and turn θ (21r). Let the rim’s temperature be g(θ) = a₀/2 + Σ (a_n cos nθ + b_n sin nθ), its series (32a), with Σ(|a_n| + |b_n|) settling.

Disc-series technique: the one balanced function on the disc that matches g on the rim is u = a₀/2 + Σ rⁿ(a_n cos nθ + b_n sin nθ).

r^n\cos n\theta = \operatorname{Re} z^n,\ \ r^n\sin n\theta = \operatorname{Im} z^n @@ z = re^(iθ), so zⁿ = rⁿe^(inθ) (26a)
\text{each term is balanced} @@ the real-part technique, on zⁿ and on −izⁿ
\text{the sum settles at once on } r \le 1 \text{ and is continuous there} @@ the at-once comparison technique, Mₙ = |aₙ| + |bₙ|, and kept continuity (23d)
\text{for } r \le r_0 < 1, \text{ the first and second slopes of the terms settle at once} @@ they are at most n²r₀ⁿ⁻²(|aₙ| + |bₙ|), and n²r₀ⁿ shrinks fast (22e)
u \text{ is balanced inside, and } u = g \text{ on the rim} @@ slopes of the sum are sums of slopes, as on 33a3; at r = 1 the sum is g’s series
\htmlClass{new}{u \text{ is the only such function}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-still-state technique

Each wiggle of the rim is passed inward and shrinks by rⁿ: the finer the wiggle, the faster it is lost. At the centre only a₀/2 is left, the rim’s average, as the circle-average technique said it must be. One example first. A steel disc of radius R has its rim held at 40 + 10 cos θ − 6 sin 2θ degrees. Inside, with r/R for r, the temperature is 40 + 10(r/R) cos θ − 6(r/R)² sin 2θ, which in x and y is 40 + 10x/R − 12xy/R².

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A balanced function is a harmonic function, and Δu = 0 is Laplace’s equation. The pair u_x = v_y, u_y = −v_x is the Cauchy–Riemann equations, and v is a harmonic conjugate of u. The circle-average technique is the mean value property. The edge-rules-inside technique is the weak maximum principle, and the stronger form of the reroute is the strong maximum principle. Finding the balanced function with given edge values is the Dirichlet problem, and the one-still-state technique is its uniqueness. The disc series, summed in one total, is Poisson’s integral formula.

Who said it first

Pierre-Simon Laplace wrote Δu = 0 for the pull of gravity in 1782. Jean le Rond d’Alembert found the pair of equations for the flow of a fluid in 1752, and Leonhard Euler and later Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Bernhard Riemann used them for complex functions. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the mean value property for the potential of gravity in 1840. Siméon Denis Poisson gave the formula for the disc in 1820, and Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet and Riemann argued over whether every edge always has a balanced inside; Karl Weierstrass found the flaw in the argument of 1857 that it always has, and David Hilbert repaired it in 1900.

A proof that is not allowed

“A balanced function has u_xx = −u_yy. Where it bends down along x it bends up along y, so every point inside is a saddle, and a saddle is never a top. So the greatest value is on the edge.” Not allowed: at a top both second slopes can be 0, as for a constant, and a point with both 0 need not be a saddle. The tilt is there to close that gap.
