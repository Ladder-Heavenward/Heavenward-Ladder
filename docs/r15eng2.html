How Does a Cam Lift a Valve Smoothly?

How does a cam lift a valve smoothly? In a car engine, each valve is pushed open by a cam, an egg-shaped lump on a turning shaft, thousands of times a minute. The cam’s shape decides the valve’s lift at every angle of the shaft. A careless shape opens the valve with a bang that wears the engine and makes it shake. This page writes lift as a function of angle, reads speed and acceleration as 13’s slopes, finds what makes a knock, builds a lift back from a chosen acceleration with 15’s areas, and finds a shape whose acceleration never jumps.

Lift s(θ) as a function of the shaft’s angle; its slope is the valve’s speed, the slope of that its acceleration. A jump in speed is a hammer blow; a jump in acceleration is a knock. Choose the acceleration first, smooth and starting and ending at 0, and add up areas to get speed and then lift.

15eng21Lift as a Function of the Turning Angle

Jean is designing the cam for a small engine’s valve. The valve sits closed, then must rise by h = 10 mm while the shaft turns through an angle β, then come back down. Measure the angle in turns, and let u = θ/β run from 0 to 1 over the rise.

From here on, on this page, the lift s is the valve’s height above closed, as a function of u. The simplest rise is a straight line: s = h·u. It reaches 10 mm at u = 1.

Before the rise the valve is still, s = 0; after it, the valve is held open, s = h. The cam’s shape joins these still stretches with the rise.

15eng22Speed and Acceleration as Slopes

The shaft turns steadily, so the valve’s speed is the slope of s in u, times a fixed factor, and its acceleration is the slope of the speed (13a). For the straight-line rise:

s\prime(u) = h \text{ during the rise, } 0 \text{ before and after} @@ the speed jumps from 0 to h at u = 0
\text{a jump in speed has no slope at the joint (13f)} @@ a joint has no slope
\htmlClass{new}{\text{acceleration at the joint: without bound}} @@ a hammer blow

A jump in speed means the valve is struck: in an instant, it goes from still to moving. A real engine with this cam would hammer its valves and wear them out within hours.

15eng23Jerk: A Jump in Acceleration Is a Knock

Try accelerating steadily for the first half of the rise and slowing steadily for the second. The speed now rises and falls without jumps; but the acceleration jumps from 0 to +a at the start, from +a to −a in the middle, and from −a to 0 at the end.

s\prime\prime(u) = +4h \text{ on } [0, \tfrac{1}{2}],\ \ -4h \text{ on } [\tfrac{1}{2}, 1] @@ chosen so the lift ends at h
s\prime(u):\ 0 \to 2h \to 0 @@ continuous, no blow
\htmlClass{new}{s\prime\prime \text{ jumps three times: by } 4h,\ 8h,\ 4h} @@ each jump is a knock

The slope of acceleration is called jerk. Where acceleration jumps, the force on the valve and its spring changes in an instant, which sets the parts ringing. At high engine speeds the ringing makes the valve bounce off its seat.

15eng24Lift Built Back From a Chosen Acceleration, by Areas

Turn the design around: choose the acceleration, then find the speed and lift. Speed gained over a stretch of u is the area under the acceleration over that stretch, and lift gained is the area under the speed (15e: areas add).

\text{speed at } u = \tfrac{1}{2}:\ \text{area of a rectangle } 4h \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 2h @@ area under the acceleration
\text{lift at } u = \tfrac{1}{2}:\ \text{area of a triangle } \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2h = \tfrac{h}{2} @@ area under the speed
\text{second half the mirror image: } \tfrac{h}{2} \text{ more} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lift at } u = 1:\ h} @@ the acceleration was chosen right

Area-built profile technique: choose the valve’s acceleration over the rise, add up the area under it to get the speed, and the area under the speed to get the lift; the cam’s shape is then cut to that lift. The acceleration must have total area 0 (the valve ends still) and give lift h. Engineers design cams this way because acceleration is what the valve spring must control.

15eng25A Lift With No Jumps

For no knocks, the acceleration must start at 0, end at 0, and change without jumps. One polynomial does it: choose the lift s = h(10u³ − 15u⁴ + 6u⁵) and read its slopes with the power technique (13d).

s(u) = h\,(10u^3 - 15u^4 + 6u^5) @@ s(0) = 0, s(1) = h
s\prime(u) = 30h\,u^2 (1 - u)^2 @@ speed: 0 at both ends
s\prime\prime(u) = 60h\,u(1 - u)(1 - 2u) @@ acceleration: 0 at both ends

The smooth rise asks for a higher peak acceleration than the steady one, but never changes it in an instant, so the valve and spring never ring. A test engineer running such a cam on a rig at high speed, with a laser tracking the valve, sees the valve follow the cam’s lift exactly, with no bounce at the seat.

Accepted names

The valve’s mover is the follower; the still stretches are dwells; the lift against angle is the displacement diagram. The steady-then-slowing rise is parabolic or constant acceleration motion, and the smooth polynomial is the 3-4-5 polynomial. Another smooth rise, the cycloidal, needs sines, which arrive at rung 21.

Who said it first

Polynomial cam profiles came into use for high-speed machinery in the mid-twentieth century and are now standard in courses on mechanisms; who first proposed the 3-4-5 rise has not yet been revisited.
