Can a Wire Carry Current With No Loss at Room Temperature?

Can a wire carry current with no loss at room temperature? A copper wire warms as it carries current, and about one part in twenty of the power sent down a country’s lines is lost that way. In 1911 a laboratory in Leiden cooled mercury in liquid helium and watched its resistance vanish near four degrees above the coldest, not fall to small but vanish. For seventy five years no metal did it above twenty three degrees. Then in 1986 a copper oxide from a lab near Zurich did it at thirty five, and within a year another did it in liquid nitrogen, which is cheap. In 2015 a hydrogen compound squeezed between diamonds did it at about minus seventy degrees Celsius. In 2020 and in 2023 three claims reached room temperature, and all three fell. This page builds what a lossless current is, why the real test is not the resistance but what the metal does to a magnet, why electrons that push each other apart travel in pairs, why hydrogen and pressure help, and how each claim was checked and why it failed.

A ring with no resistance keeps its current: I = I₀e^(−Rt/L) bounds R by the loss seen. Zero resistance alone freezes the field inside a metal; the metals that lose their resistance push the field out, to within λ = √(m/(μ₀ne²)), whichever way they are cooled. Their electrons go in pairs bound by 2Δ ≈ 3.5kT_c, the flux in a ring comes in steps h/2e, and when the glue is the lattice’s waves T_c falls as one over the square root of the atoms’ mass. A claim is checked by both sweeps of the magnet, the fall of T_c in a field, the jump in the heat, and another laboratory. A step in a signal after a chosen background proves nothing.

sky6.1A Ring That Keeps Its Current

43·phys.7 gave a metal’s resistivity as m/(ne²τ), and 43·phys.8 split it into a shaking part, which falls on cooling, and a stray part, which does not. Cooled copper keeps its stray part. In 1911 mercury, cooled below 4.2 K, lost all of it at once. A meter reads only down to its floor, so the test of nothing is a ring: start a current round a closed loop and watch whether it dies.

Ring-decay technique: a current round a closed ring of resistance R, whose own flux is Φ = LI, dies as I₀e^(−Rt/L). So if a share below s is lost in a time t, then R < sL/t. L is fixed by the ring’s shape, held in trust: for a ring 10 cm across of wire 1 mm thick, L ≈ 0.29 μH.

RI = -\tfrac{d\Phi}{dt} @@ the push round the loop is −dΦ/dt (24·then); 7·phys’s proportion law
\Phi = LI \Rightarrow \tfrac{dI}{dt} = -\tfrac{R}{L}I @@ the ring’s own flux, in proportion to its current
I = I_0e^{-Rt/L} @@ 24h’s shift-to-rest technique, with the rest at 0
1 - e^{-Rt/L} \approx Rt/L \text{ for small } Rt/L @@ eˣ ≈ 1 + x near 0 (17)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lost share} < s \Rightarrow R < sL/t} \quad \blacksquare @@ the loss grows with R

A copper ring of that size at room temperature has R = 6.7 mΩ, and its current dies in L/R = 44 μs; cooled to 77 K, in 0.35 ms. Watch a ring for a year, see less than one part in 100 000 lost, and R < 9 × 10⁻²⁰ Ω: a resistivity below 2 × 10⁻²⁵ ohm metres, ten million billion times below cold copper. Rings of this kind have been kept going for years with no loss anyone could find. The coil of a hospital scanner (28·phys.5) is closed on itself in the same way and runs for years on a current set once.

sky6.2Zero Resistance Freezes the Field

Suppose a metal has no resistance at all and ask what that alone implies. With no knocks, any push E inside would speed the electrons up without end (43·phys.7, with τ endless), so a steady state has E = 0 inside. 34·phys.4 gave the swirl of E as −∂B/∂t.

Frozen-flux technique: inside a body with no resistance, the magnetic field cannot change: it keeps whatever value it had when the resistance went.

E = 0 \text{ inside} @@ no resistance: a push would grow the current without end
\text{swirl of } E = -\partial B/\partial t @@ 34·phys.4
\partial B/\partial t = 0 \text{ inside} @@ the swirl of 0 is 0
\htmlClass{new}{B_{\text{inside}}(t) = B_{\text{inside}}(\text{when the resistance went})} \quad \blacksquare @@ currents in the skin hold it

So history decides. Cool such a body with no magnet near, then bring a magnet up: the field inside stays 0, and currents in its skin cancel the magnet’s field there. Cool it with the magnet already near: the field inside stays at its value, and if the magnet is then taken away, the field stays still, held by currents in the skin. Two bodies of the same stuff at the same temperature in the same room would differ inside. In 1933 a lab in Berlin cooled tin and lead in a field and measured the field just outside. On cooling through the point where the resistance went, the field was pushed out. Zero resistance does not do that.

sky6.3The Field Is Pushed Out

Take a thick slab with its face at x = 0, a field B along its face that may vary with depth x, and currents j across. Its free-running electrons, n in each unit volume, have m v′ = −eE, so ∂j/∂t = (ne²/m)E. 28·phys.5’s swirl of B = μ₀J reads −dB/dx = μ₀j, and 34·phys.4’s swirl of E reads dE/dx = −∂B/∂t. Together they keep a bracket fixed in time: ∂/∂t (dj/dx + (ne²/m)B) = 0.

Kept at its starting value, the bracket is the frozen field of sky6.2. The 1935 rule lays down that it is 0, whatever the history: it is earned from above, since without it 1933’s push-out cannot happen. Field-out technique: if dj/dx + (ne²/m)B = 0, then a field B₀ at the face dies inside as B₀e^(−x/λ), with λ = √(m/(μ₀ne²)), however the slab was cooled.

\tfrac{dj}{dx} = -\tfrac{ne^2}{m}B @@ the 1935 rule
-\tfrac{d^2B}{dx^2} = \mu_0\tfrac{dj}{dx} = -\tfrac{\mu_0ne^2}{m}B @@ the slope of −dB/dx = μ₀j
B'' = B/\lambda^2,\ \ \lambda^2 = \tfrac{m}{\mu_0ne^2} @@ a model equation of 24h with roots ±1/λ
B = B_0e^{-x/\lambda} @@ the root −1/λ alone stays bounded deep in a thick slab
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no field beyond a few } \lambda \text{, cooled in a field or not}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the push-out

Aluminium, three electrons an atom: n = 1.81 × 10²⁹ per cubic metre, λ = 12.5 nm, against about 16 nm measured. Lead, four an atom: λ = 14.6 nm against about 37 nm measured; not every electron runs free, and those that do are heavier in a lattice than alone. One micrometre in, a field over λ = 37 nm is down by e^(−27), about 2 × 10⁻¹². A metal that pushes out the field is a new state of matter, not a very good conductor; its accepted name is a superconductor, and the temperature where the state begins is T_c.

sky6.4Electrons in Pairs

Electrons push each other apart, yet this state is built from pairs of them. In plain words: an electron passing through the lattice tugs the positive atoms toward it. The atoms are slow (sky5.2: a few THz at most), so their crowding outlasts the electron, and a second electron coming later is drawn into the crowded wake. Through the lattice’s waves of sky05, two electrons near the top of 43·phys.4’s ball, with opposite k and opposite spin, can be held together. A pair has the sign +1 of 38·phys.4, so all the pairs can share one wave, and to slow the current a knock must break a pair. The theory of 1957 found the cost.

Pair-gap technique: if freeing an electron from its pair costs Δ, then at temperature T a share of about e^(−Δ/kT) of the electrons is free, and their heat falls in the same way far below T_c. The pair theory gives 2Δ ≈ 3.53 kT_c, held in trust.

\text{share free} \propto e^{-\Delta/kT} @@ the energy-share technique (30·phys.1); each freed electron costs Δ
\text{aluminium: } T_c = 1.18\text{ K} \Rightarrow 2\Delta \approx 3.53kT_c = 0.36\text{ meV} @@ measured: 0.34 meV
\text{lead: } T_c = 7.19\text{ K} \Rightarrow 2\Delta \approx 2.19\text{ meV} @@ measured: about 2.7 meV; lead holds its pairs harder than the theory’s simplest form
\htmlClass{new}{\text{aluminium at } 0.1\text{ K}:\ e^{-\Delta/kT} = e^{-20.8} \approx 9 \times 10^{-10}} \quad \blacksquare @@ almost nothing free

The heat of the free electrons then falls as the frozen shake of 30·phys.5 did, not as the T of 43·phys.9; measured in the 1950s, that fall was the first sign of the gap. The pairs show in a ring too. Their shared wave must come back to itself round the ring, as 43·phys.1’s waves did on the clock, and that holds the flux through the ring to whole steps of h/q for carriers of charge q (held in trust). The step measured in 1961 was 2.07 × 10⁻¹⁵ webers, which is h/2e: the carriers have charge 2f. A ring 10 μm square traps the field in steps of 21 μT, near the Earth’s 54 μT in Seattle. The qubits of 41·phys.5 are built largely of aluminium, and this gap is why they work.

sky6.5Light Atoms, and a Hard Squeeze

If the glue is the lattice’s waves, the scale of T_c is the scale of their top frequency, 2√(κ/M) by sky5.2, times a factor set by the springs and the electrons, held in trust. Swap one isotope for another and the springs stay; only the mass M of each atom changes.

Isotope-pace technique: if the pairs are glued by the lattice’s waves, then crystals that differ only in the mass of their atoms have T_c in proportion to 1/√M.

\omega_{\text{top}} = 2\sqrt{\kappa/M} @@ the shortest-wave technique
\kappa \text{ the same for every isotope} @@ the springs are made by the electrons, which do not see the mass
T_c = c\,\hbar\omega_{\text{top}}/k,\ \ c \text{ set by springs and electrons} @@ the pair theory, held in trust
\htmlClass{new}{T_c \propto M^{-1/2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ mercury 198 against 204: √(204/198) = 1.015, a change of 1.5%

Shifts of that size were measured in mercury in 1950, the first proof that the lattice takes part. Hydrogen is the lightest atom, so its waves are the fastest, and a solid rich in hydrogen with stiff springs could pair at high T_c, if it could be made a metal. Pressure does that. In 2015 a lab in Mainz squeezed hydrogen and sulfur to 155 GPa, a million and a half atmospheres, between two diamonds, in a speck tens of micrometres across, and found T_c = 203 K. Swapping hydrogen for its double-mass isotope should give 203/√2 = 144 K, and T_c did fall, by roughly that much. In 2019 a hydride of lanthanum at about 170 GPa reached about 250 K, in two labs. The copper oxides of 1986 to 1993 are different: 35 K, then 93 K, above the 77 K of liquid nitrogen, then 133 K, the record at ordinary pressure ever since. Swapping their oxygen for a heavier isotope moves the best of them by a few tenths of a kelvin, far less than the 6% the technique gives. Their glue is mostly not the lattice, and what it is remains argued.

sky6.6How a Claim Is Checked

The frozen-flux and field-out techniques together give the test that separates the two. Cool with no field and bring up a magnet: both keep the field out. Cool in a field: a body with no resistance keeps it all, and a superconductor keeps none beyond λ. The second sweep is the one that counts. A push-out also lifts. A body that pushes out a share |χ| of a field, small, has energy −χB²/(2μ₀) per unit volume, held in trust from the field’s own energy B²/(2μ₀) per unit volume.

Float technique: a body of density ρ that pushes out a small share |χ| of a field floats where B |dB/dz| ≥ μ₀ρg/|χ|. A body that pushes out the whole field floats once the field B at its face has B²/(2μ₀) ≥ ρgt, its weight per unit area, for thickness t.

\text{force per volume} = -\tfrac{d}{dz}\big(-\tfrac{\chi B^2}{2\mu_0}\big) = \tfrac{\chi}{\mu_0}B\tfrac{dB}{dz} @@ the slope of the energy; χ < 0 pushes toward weaker field
\tfrac{\lvert\chi\rvert}{\mu_0}B\lvert dB/dz\rvert \ge \rho g @@ against the weight of each unit volume
\text{water: } \lvert\chi\rvert = 9.0 \times 10^{-6} \Rightarrow 1370\ \text{T}^2/\text{m};\ \ \text{graphite: } 4.5 \times 10^{-4} \Rightarrow 60 @@ ρ = 1000 and 2200 kg per cubic metre
\text{whole push-out, } t = 1\text{ mm},\ \rho = 7000:\ \ B \ge \sqrt{2\mu_0\rho gt} = 0.013\ \text{T} @@ the field presses on the face it cannot enter
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a superconductor floats on a weak magnet; so, near a strong one, do frogs}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a frog floated in a 16 T magnet in Nijmegen in 1997

So floating shows a strong push-out, or a magnet inside, and proves neither zero resistance nor a whole push-out. The other checks have numbers too. T_c must fall in a field: lead’s field of collapse is about 0.080 T near 0 K and 0.053 T at 4.2 K, falling about as 1 − (T/T_c)², a measured law. The electrons’ heat must jump at T_c: in aluminium by about 2.3 mJ per mole per kelvin on top of 1.6, by the pair theory, held in trust. And a second laboratory, with a sample it made itself, must see the same.

sky6.7Three Claims, Tested

A magnet test on a speck between diamonds measures a small push-out on top of a large signal from everything else: the diamonds, the gasket, the metal of the cell. The everything else, the background, must be taken away, and how it is found matters.

Chosen-background technique: for any measured signal s(T) and any step u(T), the background b = s − u leaves s − b = u. So a step left after taking away a background proves a step in the sample only if the background was measured, or fixed, before the signal was seen.

s(T) \text{ measured},\ u(T) \text{ any step} @@ for instance u = −1 below 288 K and 0 above
b := s - u @@ smooth wherever s is, apart from the step of u
s - b = u @@ subtract
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the step is in } b \text{, not in the data}} \quad \blacksquare @@ unless b came from a run without the sample

2020: a compound of carbon, sulfur and hydrogen, at 267 GPa, about 73% of the pressure at the centre of the Earth, was claimed at 288 K, 15 °C. Its magnet data were shown after a background had been taken away. When the raw data were released in 2021, others argued that the background had been chosen rather than measured. The journal’s editors found the way it was taken away non-standard and withdrew the paper in September 2022, against the wishes of its authors. 2023, March: a hydride of lutetium with some nitrogen, at only 1 GPa, ten thousand atmospheres, claimed at 294 K, 21 °C. Within weeks a lab in Nanjing made the compound and found no drop down to about 40 K, and others agreed; one lab saw a drop in samples sent by the original group. Most of its authors asked the journal to withdraw it, and it did, in November 2023. 2023, July: a lead phosphate with copper in it, from a lab in Seoul, claimed at ordinary pressure, posted with a video of a flake with one edge lifted from a magnet. Labs on three continents made it within weeks. Its drop in resistance came near 104 °C, where a copper sulfide in the samples changes its crystal; pure crystals grown in Stuttgart in August were clear purple insulators; the lifted edge fitted a weak push-out with bits of magnet. Each claim failed a check of sky6.6.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞6.oq.1What glues the pairs in the copper oxides? Forty years after 1986 there is no agreed answer. The pairs there are known, from tests of the 1990s, to have a shape with four lobes of opposite sign, not the round shape of the lattice glue, and many think the glue is the electrons’ own magnetism. The simplest model of electrons hopping on a square grid and pushing each other apart can be written in a line, and it cannot yet be solved. Large computations since 2019 disagree on whether that model superconducts at all, or does so only with a small change.
∞6.oq.2Can a hydride’s high T_c be kept at ordinary pressure? The pressure makes hydrogen a metal and holds its atoms close. Calculations since about 2024 propose hydrogen compounds that would hold together at ordinary pressure with T_c far above that of the old metals, though below the squeezed records. None has been made and confirmed. Whether a crystal can be made under pressure and then let out, keeping its state, as diamond keeps its form after it is made deep underground, is open.
∞6.oq.3Is there a highest T_c? In the 1970s it was argued that lattice glue cannot pass about 30 to 40 K at ordinary pressure, and the copper oxides passed it with another glue. No law known now forbids a superconductor at 300 K and ordinary pressure, and none shows how to make one. Whether the gap 2Δ, the stiffness of the springs and the density of electrons can all be large at once without the crystal falling into some other state is argued.
∞6.oq.4Are the nickel oxides of 2023 the copper oxides again? In 2023 a nickel oxide squeezed to about 14 GPa was found to superconduct near 80 K, and in 2025 thin films of related oxides near 40 K at ordinary pressure. Whether their glue is the copper oxides’ glue, and whether two families with one cause would at last pin it down, is being argued now.
∞6.oq.5How should a field check a claim from a speck no one else can easily make? The hydride specks are tens of micrometres across, at pressures few laboratories reach. Should a journal require the raw data and the background runs before it publishes, and should groups share samples? In 2023 the claim from Seoul was tested in public within three weeks by labs posting results openly, while the 2020 claim took two years to withdraw through the journal. Which way of checking does less harm is argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1908 Leiden became the one place in the world with liquid helium, and it stayed the only one until 1923. Its lab measured how metals lose resistance as they cool, a question with two sides at the time: some expected the resistance to fall smoothly to 0, others that the electrons would freeze in place and the resistance grow without end. In 1911 mercury did neither. Near 4.2 K its resistance dropped, all at once, below anything the meter could read. The first suspicion in the lab was a short circuit. Tin and lead followed within two years.

Explaining it took 46 years, and several of the great physicists of the century tried and failed. The push-out of 1933 showed it was a new state and not a perfect metal; the rule of 1935 described it; the isotope shifts of 1950 showed the lattice takes part; the pair theory of 1957 gave the gap and the numbers. The theory also seemed to set a ceiling: arguments in the 1960s and 1970s put the most that lattice glue could give at about 30 to 40 K, and the record from 1973, 23 K in a niobium compound, stood for thirteen years.

In 1986 a lab of a computer company near Zurich reported a copper oxide losing its resistance near 35 K, in a modest journal and with modest words. Few believed it until a group in Tokyo confirmed it late that year. In early 1987 groups in Alabama and Houston passed 90 K. At a meeting in New York in March 1987, thousands of physicists stayed past three in the morning to hear the results, and the meeting was nicknamed the Woodstock of physics. The 1986 discovery won a Nobel prize in 1987, one of the fastest ever given. Governments and companies spent heavily on wires that would carry power with no loss. The oxides are brittle ceramics, and it took twenty years to make useful tape of them. Tapes are now used in some cables and in the magnets of fusion experiments, a 20 T magnet in 2021 among them. But how the oxides work is still argued, and the record at ordinary pressure, 133 K from 1993, has not moved.

Hydrogen was proposed as a road to high T_c in 1968: as a metal, its light atoms would give fast waves and strong glue. Pure hydrogen needs too much pressure, and in 2004 the proposal moved to compounds rich in hydrogen. A calculation in 2014 predicted a sulfur hydride near 200 K; Mainz measured 203 K in 2015, and a lanthanum hydride reached about 250 K in 2019. Those results have been repeated by several labs. A few critics still argue that the magnet evidence from specks so small is too weak, and since 2021 they and the labs have argued in print over each new measurement, including ones of trapped flux in 2023.

The 2020 claim of 288 K came from a group at the University of Rochester and was published in a leading journal. Outside physicists asked for the raw magnet data, argued that its background was built rather than measured, and in 2022 the journal withdrew the paper. The 2023 claim at 1 GPa came from the same group and was published in the same journal, and was withdrawn the same year at the request of most of its authors. Another paper from the group, with data that matched an earlier paper on a different material, was also withdrawn. In 2024 an investigation at the university found misconduct, and the group’s leader left. A company formed around the claims had drawn investors. Other labs spent months of their own money and time on the claims.

The Seoul claim of July 2023 went the other way: two preprints, a video, and within days labs and amateurs around the world were baking the compound and posting what they found. Shares in companies with any link to superconductors jumped on the stock market in Seoul and fell back. By mid August the drop in resistance had been traced to copper sulfide and pure crystals were insulators, and in December 2023 a committee of the Korean society for superconductivity found no evidence for the claim. Some of the original makers have since argued for related compounds. So the record now, as far as this page knows, is about 250 K under pressures near those at the centre of the Earth, and 133 K at ordinary pressure, with the nickel oxides since 2023 a new family under study. A room-temperature superconductor at ordinary pressure has not been made, and no known law forbids one.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The state is superconductivity, and T_c the critical temperature; a current that keeps going round a ring is a persistent current, and L the inductance. A body with zero resistance and nothing more is a perfect conductor. The push-out of the field is the Meissner effect, or the Meissner–Ochsenfeld effect; the 1935 rule is the London equation, and λ the London penetration depth. A body that pushes out a share of a field is diamagnetic, and χ its magnetic susceptibility, with χ = −1 for a whole push-out. The pairs are Cooper pairs, the theory of 1957 is BCS theory, Δ the superconducting gap and 2Δ ≈ 3.53kT_c its weak-coupling ratio; the glue through the lattice is the electron–phonon interaction. The flux step h/2e is the flux quantum Φ₀. The isotope-pace technique is the isotope effect with exponent ½. The two sweeps of the magnet are zero-field-cooled and field-cooled measurements; the field of collapse is the critical field, and its law 1 − (T/T_c)² is the empirical parabolic law. The copper oxides are the cuprates, among them La₂₋ₓBaₓCuO₄ (1986), YBa₂Cu₃O₇ (1987) and HgBa₂Ca₂Cu₃O₈ (1993); the shape with four lobes is d-wave pairing, and the simplest model on the square grid is the Hubbard model. The squeezed hydrides are H₃S and LaH₁₀, made in diamond anvil cells. The 2020 compound is called CSH, the 2023 one Lu–N–H, and the Seoul compound LK-99, Pb₁₀₋ₓCuₓ(PO₄)₆O, with Cu₂S its impurity. The nickel oxide of 2023 is La₃Ni₂O₇.

Who said it first

Heike Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908 and found the loss of resistance in mercury in 1911, with Gilles Holst and Cornelis Dorsman doing much of the measuring. Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld found the push-out in 1933, and Fritz and Heinz London gave the rule in 1935. Emanuel Maxwell, and separately Charles Reynolds, Bernard Serin, William Wright and Louis Nesbitt, measured the isotope shift in mercury in 1950, and Herbert Fröhlich proposed the lattice glue the same year. Leon Cooper found the pair in 1956, and John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and Robert Schrieffer gave the theory in 1957. Bascom Deaver and William Fairbank, and separately Robert Doll and Martin Näbauer, measured the flux step in 1961. Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller at IBM Zurich found the copper oxide of 1986; Paul Chu and Maw-Kuen Wu led the groups that passed 90 K in 1987; Andreas Schilling and colleagues at ETH Zurich found the mercury oxide of 1993. Neil Ashcroft proposed hydrogen in 1968 and hydrogen-rich compounds in 2004. Mikhail Eremets and Alexander Drozdov measured H₃S in 2015, after a prediction by Defang Duan, Yanming Ma and colleagues in 2014; Russell Hemley and Maddury Somayazulu led one of the two groups that measured LaH₁₀ in 2019. Ranga Dias and Ashkan Salamat led the 2020 and 2023 claims, and Jorge Hirsch and Dirk van der Marel led the analysis of the 2020 data. Sukbae Lee, Ji-Hoon Kim and colleagues posted the Seoul claim. Andre Geim floated the frog in 1997. Dates and figures for the claims of 2020 to 2023 are as reported in the journals and the press at the time.

A proof that is not allowed

“The resistance fell to zero within the precision of our meter at 294 K, so the sample is a superconductor at room temperature.” Not allowed: a meter reads down to its floor, and a drop to the floor is also what a crack closing, a change of crystal in an impurity, or a short across the contacts gives. The claim needs the field-cooled push-out, the fall of T_c in a field, and another laboratory, and the 2023 claims had none of these.
