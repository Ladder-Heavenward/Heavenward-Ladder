33c · proof

The Wave Equation

Pluck a guitar string and it does not sink back to rest like a cooling rod. It swings, and the shape you gave it runs along the string, bounces off the ends, and comes back. The rule for the string looks almost like the heat rule. The difference is one more slope in time: the string is pulled by its tension and pushed by its own weight, and what the pull changes is not its height but how fast its height changes. That one slope changes everything. On a long string, any shape at all splits into two copies of half its height, one running left and one running right, each at a fixed speed and neither changing shape. On a string tied at both ends, the swings are sums of standing sine shapes, each with its own pitch, and those pitches are whole multiples of the lowest. And nothing fades: a total of the string’s motion and its stretch stays exactly the same for as long as the rule holds.

A string’s acceleration is a fixed multiple of its second slope along the string. Every answer on a long string is two shapes running opposite ways at one speed. A string tied at both ends sings in whole multiples of one pitch, and its energy is kept.

33c1The String’s Rule

A string is stretched along the x-axis with tension T, and it has mass ρ per metre. Let y(x, t) be its height above the axis at the point x and time t. Two laws held in trust. First, a body’s mass times its acceleration is the force on it, the measured law of motion. Second, the tension pulls along the string, so its upward part at x is T times the sine of the string’s angle there. For small swings that is very nearly T y_x(x, t), since for a small angle the sine and the tangent are nearly equal (21f).

String-rule technique: if a string’s swings are small, so that its tension pulls upward by T times its slope, then y_tt = c² y_xx, with c = √(T/ρ).

\text{force up on } [x, x + h] = T\,y_x(x + h, t) - T\,y_x(x, t) @@ the pull at the right end, less the pull at the left end (the second law)
\text{mass times acceleration} = \textstyle\int_x^{x+h} \rho\,y_{tt}(s, t)\,ds = \rho h\,y_{tt}(x^*, t) @@ the law of motion, piece by piece; the mean value technique (14c) as on 33a1
\rho\,y_{tt}(x^*, t) = T\,\tfrac{y_x(x + h, t) - y_x(x, t)}{h} @@ divide by h
\htmlClass{new}{y_{tt} = c^2\,y_{xx},\ \ c = \sqrt{T/\rho}} \quad \blacksquare @@ let h head to 0

Its accepted name is the wave equation. The heat rule tied the first rate in time to the second in space. The string ties the second to the second, and c has the units of a speed. For the high string of a guitar, tension 70 N and mass 0.38 grams a metre, c = √(70/0.00038) ≈ 429 m/s.

33c2Two Shapes Running Apart

Take a string so long that its ends never matter. Start it at height f(x) with upward speed g(x).

Two-waves technique: every answer of y_tt = c²y_xx on the whole line with continuous second slopes is F(x − ct) + G(x + ct), a shape running right and a shape running left at speed c; and the answer with start f and speed g is

\(y(x, t) = \tfrac12\big(f(x - ct) + f(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{1}{2c}\int_{x - ct}^{x + ct} g(s)\,ds.\)

\xi = x - ct,\ \ \eta = x + ct,\ \ y(x, t) = Y(\xi, \eta) @@ new knobs: one rides with each wave
y_{xx} = Y_{\xi\xi} + 2Y_{\xi\eta} + Y_{\eta\eta},\ \ y_{tt} = c^2(Y_{\xi\xi} - 2Y_{\xi\eta} + Y_{\eta\eta}) @@ the chain rule along several roads (28c), twice; the swap-knobs technique (28d) for the mixed slope
y_{tt} - c^2y_{xx} = -4c^2\,Y_{\xi\eta} = 0 @@ subtract
Y_\xi \text{ does not move with } \eta,\ \ Y_\xi = F^{\prime}(\xi) @@ the zero-slope-is-constant technique (14d) along η; F an area function of it (16b)
Y = F(\xi) + G(\eta) @@ Y − F(ξ) has slope 0 along ξ (14d again)
F + G = f,\ \ -cF^{\prime} + cG^{\prime} = g @@ the start: t = 0, so ξ = η = x
\htmlClass{new}{y = \tfrac12\big(f(x - ct) + f(x + ct)\big) + \tfrac{1}{2c}\textstyle\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct} g} \quad \blacksquare @@ solve the pair: G − F = (1/c)∫g up to a constant, which cancels

Read off what the formula says. The height at x at time t uses only the start between x − ct and x + ct. A pluck at one point is felt at distance d after exactly d/c seconds, and not a moment before. The heat rule put some heat everywhere at once; the string carries its news at a fixed speed. And the shapes F and G keep their form as they run. Nothing smooths them: a kink in the start is still a kink a mile down the string.

33c3The String Tied at Both Ends

Tie the string at x = 0 and x = L: y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0. Look for answers X(x)T(t) that keep their shape, as 33a2 did. The same lines give X″ = −λX with both ends 0, so X = sin(nπx/L) and λ = (nπ/L)². In time, T″ = −c²λT, which the steady-swing technique (24h) solves.

String-tones technique: the answers of the string’s rule that keep their shape, tied at both ends, are sin(nπx/L) times a swing with frequency nc/(2L), for n = 1, 2, 3, …; so every tone of the string is a whole multiple of the lowest.

X(x) = \sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L} @@ the lines of the cooling-shape technique (33a2), word for word in x
T^{\prime\prime} = -\big(\tfrac{n\pi c}{L}\big)^2 T @@ put XT into y_tt = c²y_xx and divide by XT
T = A_n\cos\tfrac{n\pi ct}{L} + B_n\sin\tfrac{n\pi ct}{L} @@ the steady-swing technique (24h)
\text{repeats every } \tfrac{2L}{nc} \text{ seconds} @@ a swing with n inside repeats every 2π/n (24h)
\htmlClass{new}{f_n = \tfrac{nc}{2L} = n f_1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the frequency is one over the time to repeat

Every tied answer is a sum of these, with A_n from the start’s shape and B_n from its speed, by 32a’s projections, as in 33a3. One example first. The high string of a guitar is 0.648 m long between its ties and is tuned to 329.6 swings a second. So c = 2 × 0.648 × 329.6 ≈ 427 m/s, and the string can also sound 659, 989, 1318 swings a second, and so on up. A plucked string sounds all of them at once, in amounts set by where it was plucked. Their accepted name is the harmonics.

33c4Nothing Fades

Let a tied string have continuous second slopes. Its energy at time t is the total of its motion and its stretch:

\(E(t) = \tfrac12\int_0^L \big(\rho\,y_t^2 + T\,y_x^2\big)\,dx.\)

Kept-energy technique: the energy of a string tied at both ends stays the same at every time.

E^{\prime}(t) = \textstyle\int_0^L \big(\rho\,y_t y_{tt} + T\,y_x y_{xt}\big)\,dx @@ the slope under the total, as on 33a1; the chain rule on each square
\rho\,y_{tt} = T\,y_{xx} @@ the string-rule technique
E^{\prime}(t) = T\textstyle\int_0^L \big(y_t y_{xx} + y_x y_{xt}\big)\,dx = T\int_0^L \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(y_t y_x\big)\,dx @@ the product rule along x, and y_xt = y_tx (28d)
E^{\prime}(t) = T\big(y_t(L, t)\,y_x(L, t) - y_t(0, t)\,y_x(0, t)\big) = 0 @@ 16c; the ends are tied, so y_t = 0 there
\htmlClass{new}{E(t) = E(0) \text{ for every } t} \quad \blacksquare @@ zero slope means flat (14d)

So the string keeps what the rod forgets. Run the tied series backward and it settles just as well as forward: every term only swings. Two answers with the same start and speed have a difference of energy 0 at the start, so 0 always, so the difference has y_t = y_x = 0 and is the 0 it started as. One start, one future, and here one past as well. A real string does fall silent, through the air and through its ties. The rule is the string with those left out.

33c.4.4seamIn the middle of the eighteenth century three positions were taken on the swinging string. The first: the string’s motion is F(x − ct) + G(x + ct), and F and G must be given by single smooth formulas, or the rule means nothing at a corner. The second: any shape a hand can draw is a start, corners and all, and the same formula moves it. The third, from listening: every motion is a sum of the standing sine tones, and that is the whole story. The first two replied that a sum of smooth sines could never make a corner.
Who was right about the pluck of 33c.4.1? Use the two-waves technique, the string-tones technique and 32c. Was each side right about something?

The accepted names

y_tt = c²y_xx is the one-dimensional wave equation; c is the wave speed. The two-waves technique is d’Alembert’s solution, and the interval [x − ct, x + ct] is the domain of dependence. The tied tones are the normal modes of the string; sin(nπx/L) for n ≥ 2 is the nth harmonic, and the points where it is 0 are its nodes. The kept-energy technique is conservation of energy for the wave equation. The law of motion is Newton’s second law.

Who said it first

Brook Taylor found the string’s lowest tone from its tension in 1713. Jean le Rond d’Alembert wrote the wave equation and its two-waves solution in 1747. Leonhard Euler replied in 1748 that any shape a hand can draw is a start. Daniel Bernoulli claimed in 1753 that every motion is a sum of sine tones, from what musicians hear. The argument went on into the 1760s, with Joseph-Louis Lagrange joining in 1759, and it was left unsettled until Joseph Fourier’s series and the work after him on when they return the function. Marin Mersenne had measured how a string’s pitch goes with its length, tension and weight in 1636.

A proof that is not allowed

“The plucked string has a corner, and every sine is smooth. A sum of smooth functions is smooth. So the plucked string is not a sum of sines.” Not allowed: a finite sum of smooth functions is smooth, but an endless sum need not be, and 32c says when the endless sum returns the function, corner and all.
