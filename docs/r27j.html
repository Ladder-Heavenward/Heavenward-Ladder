27j · technique

When Small Errors Grow Large

A machine solving Ax = b works with numbers rounded to sixteen places, and data measured to three. Usually that is plenty. But some systems turn a tiny change in b into a huge change in x: their rows point almost the same way, and the solution is where two nearly parallel lines cross. This step measures how much a matrix can magnify errors, by the most and least it stretches, proves the fence on the error, and shows a system where four digits of data give no digits of answer.

The stretch of A is the most it lengthens any vector, ‖A‖. If Ax = b and A(x + δx) = b + δb, then ‖δx‖/‖x‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖A⁻¹‖ · ‖δb‖/‖b‖. The number ‖A‖‖A⁻¹‖ is the magnifier: near 1 is safe, 10ᵏ loses k digits.

27j1Two Nearly Parallel Lines

x + y = 2,\ \ x + 1.001y = 2.001:\ x = 1,\ y = 1 @@ two lines that nearly coincide
\text{change } 2.001 \text{ to } 2.002 @@ a change of one part in 2000
x = 0,\ \ y = 2 @@ the crossing slides far
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a } 0.05\% \text{ change in } b \text{ gives a } 100\% \text{ change in } x} @@ a magnification of about 2000

From here on, the stretch of a matrix, ‖A‖, is the largest value of ‖Av‖/‖v‖ over nonzero v, measured with 27h’s length.

27j2The Magnifier

Magnifier technique: if A is undoable, Ax = b with b ≠ 0, and A(x + δx) = b + δb, then ‖δx‖/‖x‖ ≤ ‖A‖ ‖A⁻¹‖ ‖δb‖/‖b‖.

A\,\delta x = \delta b \Rightarrow \|\delta x\| = \|A^{-1}\delta b\| \le \|A^{-1}\|\,\|\delta b\| @@ subtract; the stretch of A⁻¹
\|b\| = \|Ax\| \le \|A\|\,\|x\| \Rightarrow \dfrac{1}{\|x\|} \le \dfrac{\|A\|}{\|b\|} @@ the stretch of A
\dfrac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|} \le \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|\,\dfrac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|} @@ multiply
\htmlClass{new}{\kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\| \ge 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 = ‖I‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖A⁻¹‖

κ(A) is at least 1, and for the nearly parallel lines it is about 4000. A system with magnifier 10ᵏ can lose k of the digits in its data. It is a fact about the problem, not the method: no cleverness of solving can recover digits the problem destroys.

27j3Good Methods and Bad Problems

\text{a bad problem: large } \kappa @@ any method loses digits
\text{a bad method: elimination without swapping rows} @@ can lose digits on a good problem
10^{-20}x + y = 1,\ x + y = 2:\ \text{divide by } 10^{-20} \text{ and the 2 is rounded away} @@ a tiny pivot
\htmlClass{new}{\text{swap the rows first: the answer } x \approx 1,\ y \approx 1 \text{ is found}} @@ the largest pivot first (27l’s elimination by hand)

A problem with a small magnifier can still be ruined by a method that divides by a tiny number; taking the largest entry of each column as the pivot avoids that. Good methods lose no more than the problem’s own magnifier.

Accepted names

The magnifier is the condition number; a problem with large κ is ill-conditioned; the stretch is the operator norm; choosing the largest pivot is partial pivoting, and a method losing no more than the problem is backward stable.

Who said it first

Alan Turing named the condition number in 1948; John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine studied rounding in elimination in 1947, and James Wilkinson built the theory of backward error in the 1960s.
