27j · technique

Solving Systems by Elimination

Many samenesses in many unknowns, all at once, are one grid times one unknown vector equal to a known vector. The oldest way to solve them is still the best by hand and by machine. Use one sameness to clear one unknown out of all the others. Then do it again with the next unknown, and the next, until the grid is a staircase. Three moves on the rows are all it takes, and none of them changes the answers. Carried all the way, the moves leave the grid in lowest terms, and like a fraction it has only one lowest form. From that form the answers can be read straight off: one answer, none, or a whole family of them, with exactly as many free choices as the grid loses directions. The same moves undo a grid. And the work can be counted. It grows like the cube of the number of unknowns, which is why a laptop solves a thousand samenesses in a moment.

Three moves on the rows of a system never change its answers. Used to clear one unknown at a time, they bring any grid to one lowest form, and the answers are read off from it. The same moves undo a grid, and the work grows like the cube of the number of unknowns.

27j1Three Moves That Keep the Answers

m samenesses in n unknowns, aᵢ₁x₁ + ⋯ + aᵢₙxₙ = bᵢ, are one sameness of vectors, Ax = b, with A the grid of the numbers in front (27d1). Write the rows of A with bᵢ at the end of each; that grid is [A | b]. Three row moves change it: swap two rows; stretch a row by a number that is not 0; add a stretch of one row to another.

Row-move technique: each row move is multiplying on the left by an undoable grid E; so a row move on [A | b] keeps exactly the same answers x, and each row move is undone by another row move.

E = I \text{ with the same move done to its rows} @@ swap: rows i, k of I swapped; stretch: c in place i, i; add: t in place i, k
\text{row } i \text{ of } EA = \textstyle\sum_l e_{il}\,(\text{row } l \text{ of } A) @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d2)
EA = A \text{ with the move done} @@ each kind of E picks out the rows the move asks for
\text{undo: swap again; stretch by } 1/c;\ \text{add } -t \text{ times the row} @@ each undo is a row move of the same kind, so E is undoable (27d3)
Ax = b \Rightarrow EAx = Eb;\ \ EAx = Eb \Rightarrow Ax = E^{-1}EAx = E^{-1}Eb = b @@ multiply on the left by E, then by E⁻¹
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same answers before and after}} \quad \blacksquare
  
x + y + z = 6,\ \ 2x + y - z = 1,\ \ x - y + 2z = 5 @@ given
-y - 3z = -11,\ \ -2y + z = -1 @@ add −2 times row 1 to row 2, and −1 times row 1 to row 3: x is cleared
7z = 21 @@ add −2 times the new row 2 to the new row 3: y is cleared
z = 3,\ \ y = 11 - 3z = 2,\ \ x = 6 - y - z = 1 @@ back up the stairs, one unknown at a time
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y, z) = (1, 2, 3)} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, 2 + 2 − 3 = 1, 1 − 2 + 6 = 5

27j2One Lowest Form

In each row that is not all 0, the first entry that is not 0 is its lead. A grid is in lowest terms when each lead is 1, each lead sits to the right of the lead in the row above, rows of zeros are at the bottom, and each lead is the only entry that is not 0 in its column. Elimination gets there column by column: find a row at or below the current one with a number that is not 0 in this column, swap it up, stretch it to make its lead 1, and clear the rest of the column by adding stretches of it to the other rows.

Grid-lowest-terms technique: row moves bring every grid to a grid in lowest terms, and to only one. As each fraction has one form in lowest terms (5h), each grid has one.

\text{elimination ends in lowest terms} @@ each column is finished once, and later moves leave it as it is
R,\ R' \text{ lowest forms of } A \Rightarrow Rx = 0 \text{ and } R'x = 0 \text{ have the same answers} @@ both have the answers of Ax = 0 (the row-move technique, 27j1)
\text{column } j \text{ is a lead column} \iff \text{it is not a mix of the columns before it} @@ a lead’s 1 sits where every column before it has 0; any other column is a mix of the lead columns before it
\text{a mix } c_1(\text{col } 1) + \cdots = \text{col } j \iff x = (c_1, \ldots, -1, 0, \ldots, 0) \text{ is an answer} @@ Ax is the mix of the columns with the numbers of x (27d1)
R \text{ and } R' \text{ have the same lead columns, and the same mixes for the others} @@ the same answers; the lead columns are free, so each mix is the only one
\htmlClass{new}{R = R'} \quad \blacksquare @@ a lead column is e₁, e₂, … in order; every other column is fixed by its mix

Bring [A | b] to lowest terms and read the answers. If a row says 0 = 1, there are none. Otherwise each unknown at a lead is its right side less the other unknowns in its row, and the unknowns with no lead are free: any numbers will do. The free unknowns are as many as the columns less the leads, and that is the dimension of what A loses, by the lost-plus-reached technique (27c2): the lead columns are a basis of what A reaches.

x + 2y + z = 4,\ \ 2x + 4y + 3z = 9,\ \ 3x + 6y + 4z = 13 @@ given
\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&3\\0&0&1&1\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix} @@ lowest terms of [A | b]: −2 row 1 to row 2, −3 row 1 to row 3, −1 row 2 to row 3, −1 row 2 to row 1
x = 3 - 2y,\ \ z = 1,\ \ y \text{ free} @@ leads in the x and z columns; no lead for y
\htmlClass{new}{(x, y, z) = (3, 0, 1) + y\,(-2, 1, 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ one free choice: A loses a line, of dimension 1

27j3Undoing a Grid, and What It Costs

Undo-by-rows technique: a square grid A can be undone exactly when row moves bring it to I; and the same row moves, done to I, give A⁻¹. So write [A | I], and bring it to lowest terms.

E_k\cdots E_1A = I @@ the row moves that bring A to I, as grids (27j1)
A = (E_k\cdots E_1)^{-1},\ \ A^{-1} = E_k\cdots E_1 = E_k\cdots E_1\,I @@ each Eᵢ is undoable, so their product is (27d3); multiply on the left by its undo
A \text{ undoable} \Rightarrow R = E_k\cdots E_1A \text{ undoable} @@ products of undoable grids are undoable (27d3)
R \text{ has no row of zeros} \Rightarrow n \text{ leads in } n \text{ columns} @@ a row of zeros in R stays one in RB, so RB ≠ I
\htmlClass{new}{R = I} \quad \blacksquare @@ the only square grid in lowest terms with n leads

Elimination-cost technique: for n samenesses in n unknowns, when no lead is 0 on the way, clearing down the stairs and solving back up takes n³/3 + n² − n/3 multiplications and divisions. So the work is ~ n³/3, and doubling n multiplies it by about 8. Swaps cost no multiplying.

\text{column } k:\ n - k \text{ rows to clear} @@ the rows below the lead, k = 1, …, n − 1
\text{each: one division, then } n - k + 1 \text{ multiplications} @@ the multiplier, then the rest of the row and its right side
\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)(n-k+2) = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(j^2 + 2j) = \tfrac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6} + (n-1)n @@ j = n − k; sum of squares and doubled sum (15g)
\text{back up: row } k \text{ takes } n - k \text{ multiplications and one division} @@ put in the unknowns already found
\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(n-k+1) = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} @@ doubled sum (15g)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{n^3}{3} + n^2 - \tfrac{n}{3} \sim \tfrac{n^3}{3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply out and add; the n³ term wins (22k)

At n = 3 that is 17. At n = 1000 it is 334 333 000, under a second on a laptop. Weather forecasts, the strength of a bridge, the pages of a search engine: each is a system of samenesses, often of millions of unknowns, and each grid is mostly zeros, which careful elimination never has to touch.

27j4The Lowest Form From the Answers

One grid has one lowest form (27j2). Now take two grids A and B with the same number of columns, and suppose Ax = 0 and Bx = 0 have exactly the same answers. The grids may even have different numbers of rows.

Same-lowest-form technique: Ax = 0 and Bx = 0 have the same answers exactly when A and B have the same lowest form, rows of zeros aside. And then each row of B is a mix of the rows of A, and each row of A is a mix of the rows of B.

Rx = 0,\ R'x = 0 \text{ have the same answers} @@ R, R′ are the lowest forms of A, B; the row-move technique (27j1), for each
\text{same lead columns, same mixes} @@ both are read from the answers alone: the proof of 27j2
R = R' \text{, rows of zeros aside} @@ a lead column is e₁, e₂, …, and every other column is its mix of them
R = EA,\ \ B = E'^{-1}R' @@ E, E′ are the products of the row-move grids, each undoable (27j1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each row of } B \text{ is a mix of rows of } A} \quad \blacksquare @@ a row of a product is a mix of the rows of its right-hand grid (27j1), twice

The other way round is the row-move technique itself: grids with one lowest form have the answers of that form. Take A with rows (1, 1, 1), (1, 2, 3) and B with rows (2, 3, 4), (0, 1, 2), (1, 0, −1). Both come down to the rows (1, 0, −1), (0, 1, 2), with a row of zeros left over in B. So both have the answers z · (1, −2, 1), and (2, 3, 4) is 3 times the first row of A less the second.

With right sides that are not 0, an answer x of Ax = b is an answer (x, −1) of the grid [A | b] with right sides 0. So two systems that each have an answer have the same answers exactly when [A | b] and [B | c] have the same lowest form.

The three row moves are more than are needed. A swap is four of the other two: add row 2 to row 1, take the new row 1 from row 2, add the new row 2 to row 1, and stretch row 2 by −1. The rows (r₁, r₂) become (r₁ + r₂, r₂), then (r₁ + r₂, −r₁), then (r₂, −r₁), then (r₂, r₁).

27j5What the Rows Build, and What Can Be Reached

The rows of a grid are vectors too, and their mixes are a space. Row moves change the rows. Do they change what the rows build?

Row-space technique: row moves keep what the rows build, and the rows of the lowest form that are not 0 are a basis of it. So the rows of a grid build a space of the same dimension as its columns do: the number of leads.

\text{row } i \text{ of } EA = \textstyle\sum_l e_{il}\,(\text{row } l \text{ of } A) @@ the second line of 27j1: each new row is a mix of the old rows
A = E^{-1}(EA) @@ and each old row is a mix of the new: the same space
c_1(\text{lead row } 1) + \cdots + c_r(\text{lead row } r) = 0 @@ suppose a mix of the lead rows of R is 0
\text{in the column of lead } i \text{ the mix has } c_i @@ a lead is 1 and the only entry of its column that is not 0
c_1 = \cdots = c_r = 0 @@ so the lead rows are free
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the lead rows are a basis; dimension } r} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lead columns of A are a basis of what A reaches (27j2): r again

The readings gave the same count by another road (27h3). Here it comes with a basis in hand, and that answers a question about spaces by row moves: is (1, 4, 7) a mix of (1, 1, 1) and (1, 2, 3)? The lowest form of those two rows is (1, 0, −1), (0, 1, 2), and (1, 4, 7) = (1, 0, −1) + 4 · (0, 1, 2). It is.

Zero-rows technique: bring [A | b] to [R | c] by the row moves that bring A to its lowest form R. Then Ax = b has an answer exactly when c is 0 in every row where R is all 0; that is, when [A | b] has no more leads than A.

[A \mid b] \to [R \mid c],\ \ c = Eb @@ the same moves done to b; the answers are kept (27j1)
\text{a row of zeros in } R \text{ with } c_i \neq 0 @@ that row says 0 = cᵢ: no answer
\text{every such } c_i = 0 @@ give each free unknown 0 and read the lead unknowns from c (27j2): an answer
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an answer} \iff [A \mid b] \text{ gains no lead}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first entry not 0 of a row 0 … 0 | cᵢ is cᵢ itself

With letters for the right sides the technique says which right sides can be reached. Take A with rows (1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3). Take row 1 from row 2, twice row 1 from row 3, then the new row 2 from the new row 3. The last row reads 0 0 | b₃ − b₁ − b₂. So Ax = b has an answer exactly when b₃ = b₁ + b₂, and what A reaches is that plane.

The same work cuts a system to fit a space. To find samenesses whose answers are exactly the mixes of (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1), put the two vectors down as rows and solve for what they lose: z · (−1, −1, 1). The one sameness −x − y + z = 0 has both vectors as answers, and its answers have dimension 3 − 1 = 2 (27c2). So its answers are their mixes and nothing else.

27j6Row and Column Moves Together

A column move changes what the unknowns are (27j7), so it is no help in solving. It still asks a fair question: how simple can a grid be made when both kinds of move are allowed? Write Dᵣ for the grid, of the same shape as A, with 1 in places 1, 1 to r, r and 0 everywhere else.

Row-and-column technique: row and column moves bring every grid A to Dᵣ, where r is the dimension of what A reaches. So PAQ = Dᵣ for an undoable P and an undoable Q. Two grids of one shape come to each other by such moves exactly when they reach the same dimension.

\text{column } j \text{ of } AE = A\,(\text{column } j \text{ of } E) @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d2)
AE = A \text{ with the column move done} @@ E is I with that move done to its columns; undoable, as in 27j1
A \to R \text{ in lowest terms, } r \text{ leads} @@ row moves (27j2)
R \to \begin{pmatrix}I & C\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} @@ column swaps bring the r lead columns to the front
\to \begin{pmatrix}I & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} = D_r @@ take stretches of the first r columns from the later ones: each clears one entry of C
\htmlClass{new}{PAQ = D_r} \quad \blacksquare @@ P is the product of the row-move grids, Q of the column-move grids

The number r cannot come out two ways. An undoable Q reaches every list, so AQ reaches what A reaches. An undoable P loses nothing, so it matches what AQ reaches with what PAQ reaches, and matched spaces have one dimension (27d5). And Dᵣ reaches a space of dimension r.

So if A and B both reach dimension r, then P₁AQ₁ = Dᵣ = P₂BQ₂, and B = P₂⁻¹P₁AQ₁Q₂⁻¹. The grid with rows (1, 2, 3), (2, 4, 6) and the grid with rows (0, 0, 5), (0, 0, 0) each reach a line. Both come to the grid with rows (1, 0, 0), (0, 0, 0). Seen this way a grid has only one number worth keeping. The answers of Ax = b are not kept: the unknowns were changed to x = Qx′ on the way.

\text{add a stretch of one column to another}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> columns hold the unknowns; mixing them changes what the unknowns are, and the answers
n \text{ samenesses in } n \text{ unknowns have exactly one answer}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> only when A can be undone: x + y = 1, 2x + 2y = 2 has a whole line of answers
\text{more unknowns than samenesses} \Rightarrow \text{many answers}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> there may be none: x + y + z = 1, x + y + z = 2

The accepted names

Solving by clearing one unknown at a time is Gaussian elimination; carried to lowest terms it is Gauss–Jordan elimination. Row moves are elementary row operations, and their grids E are elementary matrices. The staircase is row echelon form, and lowest terms is reduced row echelon form, with the leads as pivots. A system with no answer is inconsistent. The number of leads is the rank of A, and the unknowns with no lead are free variables. Swapping the largest entry up to be the lead, as 27j.4.2 suggests, is partial pivoting. Grids that are mostly zeros are sparse. Two systems with the same answers are equivalent systems, and their grids are row-equivalent. What the rows build is the row space, and the number of leads is the rank. A system with an answer is consistent. Grids with B = PAQ for undoable P and Q are equivalent, and Dᵣ is the rank normal form.

Who said it first

The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, compiled in China by about the first century, solves systems of up to six samenesses by this method, with counting rods on a board, column by column. Carl Friedrich Gauss used it around 1809 to find the orbit of the asteroid Pallas from many observations, and Wilhelm Jordan described the full reduction in 1888 for surveying. John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine, and Alan Turing, studied its rounding errors on the first computers in 1947 and 1948.
