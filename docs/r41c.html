41c · proof

Several clocks at once

A number of soldiers is lined up in threes, and two are left over. In fives, three are left over. In sevens, two. How many soldiers? An old book of puzzles asked this, and answered twenty three. The answer is one of many, since adding one hundred and five changes none of the leftovers, but between one and one hundred and five it is the only one. That is the claim of this step. Reading a number on several clocks at once loses nothing, as long as no two of the clocks share a divisor, and it keeps adding and multiplying. So a question on a big clock can be split into questions on small clocks, answered there, and put back together. The undoes of the last step do the putting back. When two clocks do share a divisor, the readings must agree on the shared part, and that condition is all that goes wrong.

If m and n share nothing, an hour of the clock of mn is the same as a pair of hours, one on each, and the pairing keeps plus and times. The pair is put back together with undoes. Clocks that share a divisor can be read together exactly when the readings agree on it.

41c1Two clocks that share nothing

Fix m and n with gcd(m, n) = 1. Read each hour of the clock of mn on both small clocks: [x] goes to the pair ([x] on the clock of m, [x] on the clock of n). That is well defined, since mn ∣ x − y gives m ∣ x − y and n ∣ x − y.

Remainder technique: if m and n share no divisor above 1, reading an hour of the clock of mn on the clocks of m and n is a matching of ℤ/mn with the pairs ℤ/m × ℤ/n.

x \equiv y \pmod m,\ \ x \equiv y \pmod n @@ suppose two hours give one pair
m \mid x - y,\ \ n \mid x - y = nk @@ ﹀ ≡ twice
m \mid nk \Rightarrow m \mid k @@ factor-that-shares-nothing (5h), since gcd(m, n) = 1
\htmlClass{new}{mn \mid x - y} @@ x − y = n(mj): one-to-one
|\mathbb Z/mn| = mn = |\mathbb Z/m \times \mathbb Z/n| @@ clock-hours (41a2); pairs counted by the product rule (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the reading is onto}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a one-to-one map between finite sets of one size misses nothing: else pigeonhole (8·cb5) on the map back

So the soldiers have one count between 0 and 104 for each list of leftovers. The proof says the answer exists. It does not say how to find it, short of trying all 105. The next footing finds it.

41c2Putting the pair back

Each small clock gets a number that is 1 on it and 0 on all the others. On the clock of m, N/m times its undo there is 1; on every other clock it is a multiple of that clock, so 0. Add them, each times its leftover.

N = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105 @@ the clocks share nothing
35 \equiv 2 \pmod 3,\ [2]^{-1} = [2];\ \ 21 \equiv 1 \pmod 5;\ \ 15 \equiv 1 \pmod 7 @@ N/3, N/5, N/7 on their own clocks; undoes by 41b
e_3 = 35 \cdot 2 = 70,\ \ e_5 = 21,\ \ e_7 = 15 @@ each is 1 on its clock and 0 on the other two
x = 2 \cdot 70 + 3 \cdot 21 + 2 \cdot 15 = 233 @@ the leftovers 2, 3 and 2
\htmlClass{new}{x \equiv 233 - 210 = 23 \pmod{105}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 23 = 3 · 7 + 2 = 5 · 4 + 3 = 7 · 3 + 2

The three numbers 70, 21 and 15 serve every list of leftovers on these clocks. Any count of soldiers is 70a + 21b + 15c on the clock of 105, where a, b and c are the leftovers in threes, fives and sevens. The old book gave exactly these three numbers as a rhyme.

41c3The pairing keeps plus and times

Two-clock technique: if m and n share nothing, the reading of the remainder technique sends sums to sums and products to products, so an hour of the clock of mn is a unit exactly when both its readings are units.

[x + y] \mapsto ([x + y]_m, [x + y]_n) = ([x]_m + [y]_m, [x]_n + [y]_n) @@ carry-to-the-clock (41a2) on each clock
[xy] \mapsto ([x]_m[y]_m, [x]_n[y]_n) @@ the same, for times
[x][y] = [1] \Rightarrow [x]_m[y]_m = [1]_m,\ [x]_n[y]_n = [1]_n @@ a unit reads as two units
[x]_m[a]_m = [1]_m,\ [x]_n[b]_n = [1]_n \Rightarrow [x][y] = [1] @@ y the hour reading as (a, b): onto (41c1), then one-to-one
\htmlClass{new}{|(\mathbb Z/mn)^\times| = |(\mathbb Z/m)^\times| \cdot |(\mathbb Z/n)^\times|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the units match the pairs of units; the product rule (4k)

So the clock of 12 has 2 · 2 = 4 units, two from the clock of 3 and two from the clock of 4. And an equation can be solved clock by clock. x² ≡ 1 on the clock of 15 reads as x ≡ ±1 on the clock of 3 and x ≡ ±1 on the clock of 5: four pairs, so four answers, 1, 4, 11 and 14. On a prime clock x² ≡ 1 has two. 41j turns the extra answers into a test for primes.

41c4Clocks that share a divisor

Two clocks of 4 and 6 hours share 2. A reading of 3 on the clock of 4 and 1 on the clock of 6 is odd on both, and 7 gives it. A reading of 1 and 2 is odd on one and even on the other, and no integer gives it.

Shared-divisor technique: x ≡ a (mod m) and x ≡ b (mod n) together have an answer exactly when a ≡ b (mod gcd(m, n)), and then all the answers are one hour on the clock of the least common multiple of m and n.

g = \gcd(m, n),\ \ x = a + mj = b + nk @@ suppose an answer
g \mid m,\ g \mid n \Rightarrow g \mid a - b @@ a − b = nk − mj (divides-a-sum)
a - b = gt,\ \ mu + nv = g @@ the other way: given; gcd-as-a-sum (41b1)
x = a - mut @@ try it
x - b = (a - b) - mut = gt - mut = nvt @@ mu = g − nv
\htmlClass{new}{x \equiv a \pmod m,\ \ x \equiv b \pmod n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uniqueness on the clock of the common multiple is the remainder technique’s one-to-one line, with gcd in place of 1, a standing question

Calendars are clocks that share divisors, and the condition is what makes some dates impossible. A rhythm of 260 days and a year of 365 share 5, so a pair of readings that disagree on the clock of 5 never comes; the pairs that can come repeat every 18 980 days, the least common multiple, about 52 years.

The accepted names

The remainder technique is the Chinese remainder theorem, and the two-clock technique the ring isomorphism ℤ/mnℤ ≅ ℤ/mℤ × ℤ/nℤ for coprime m and n, with its consequence φ(mn) = φ(m)φ(n) for Euler’s φ (41d). The shared-divisor technique is the general Chinese remainder theorem. The 260-day count and the 365-day year together are the Calendar Round of the Maya, 52 years long.

Who said it first

The soldiers’ puzzle is in the Sunzi Suanjing, a Chinese book of the third to fifth century. Qin Jiushao gave a general method in 1247. Leonhard Euler and Carl Friedrich Gauss proved it in modern form, Gauss in the Disquisitiones (1801). The rhyme with 70, 21 and 15 is from the Ming dynasty.

A proof that is not allowed

“The clock of 4 has 4 hours and the pairs on the clocks of 2 and 2 are 4 in number. A set of 4 matches a set of 4, so the remainder technique applies.” Not allowed: any two sets of 4 match, but the technique is about the reading, which must also keep plus and times. The reading [x] ↦ ([x], [x]) on the clocks of 2 is not one-to-one: [0] and [2] both read as ([0], [0]).
