Running Change Backwards

Rung 13 built the slope at a point, as a limit of chord slopes. Rung 15 built the area under a graph, as the one number squeezed between every estimate. The two were made from different parts: one from a single point and the points near it, the other from a whole interval cut into pieces. Nothing in either construction mentions the other. Rung 14 left a question open: given the slope at every input, what was the function? Rung 15 left one too: the squeeze says an area exists, but finding it took a page of sums for each function. This rung shows that the two questions are one question. Let the right-hand end of an area move, and the area becomes a function of where that end is. Its slope, at each point, is the height of the graph there. So finding an area comes down to finding a function whose slope is the height, and every continuous function has one.

Here two climbs meet. You built slope and area separately, from different parts, and this rung shows they were one thing all along. Nobody can hand you that. You have to have climbed both sides to see it.

Fix the left end of an area and slide the right end: the area becomes a function. Its slope at each point is the height of the graph there. So any function whose slope is the height differs from the area function by a constant, and the area between two ends is that function’s value at the right end less its value at the left.

The class · read it through, then go to 16a

What it means to say it

Take a function f that has an area on every stretch of an interval, and fix one point s of that interval. For each x in the interval there is a number: the integral of f from s to x. That gives a new function, the area function of f from s. Its input is where the right-hand end sits; its output is the area so far, counted negative where f is below the axis, as rung 15 agreed.

The claim of this rung is about the slope of that function. Move the end from x to a little past x. The area gains a thin strip, about as wide as the move and about as tall as f(x). So the gain divided by the move is about f(x), and the closer the two ends, the closer it gets, provided f does not jump at x. The slope of the area function is the height.

Read the other way, it says that slopes can be undone. Rung 14 proved that two functions with the same slope on an interval differ by a constant. So let G be any function whose slope is f. Then G and the area function differ by a constant. And the area from p to q is the change in G from p to q. An area is found without any sum at all.

Where it comes from

In the 1640s and in 1668, two mathematicians each saw in figures that a tangent problem and an area problem were linked. A teacher at Cambridge gave the link as a technique of geometry in his lectures of 1670, without using it to compute anything. One of the two inventors of the calculus, from 1665, treated an area as a quantity that flows as its edge moves. He found areas by running his techniques for rates of flow backwards. The other, in 1675 and 1676, wrote the area as a sum, ∫, and the slope as a ratio, d over d. He saw that the two signs undo each other.

Their techniques became a method for two centuries before anyone could prove them in the sense of rung 0. In 1823 the technique was proved for continuous functions, from a definition of the integral. The integral of 1854, and the sums of 1875, made it true for every function with an area, at the points where the function does not jump. In 1881 someone built a function whose slope exists everywhere and never passes a certain bound. Yet it has no area under it by rung 15's definition. So the undoing has a gap at its edge, and closing that gap took a new integral.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, each from the one before.

First, the area function. For f with an area on every stretch of an interval, and a fixed s there, F(x) is the integral from s to x. Rung 15's techniques for splitting and bounding areas make F well behaved: if f stays within a bound, F changes by at most that bound times the move, so F never jumps, whatever f does (16a3).

Second, its slope is the height. Where f does not jump, the area gained over a short move, divided by the move, is squeezed between the least and greatest heights of f on that stretch, and both close in on f(x) (16b1). In particular, every function that is continuous on an interval has a function whose slope it is: its own area function.

Third, evaluating. If G has slope f on [p, q], then G and the area function differ by a constant, so the integral from p to q is G(q) − G(p) (16c1). The same is true even if f jumps, so long as f has an area and G's slope is f at every point: rung 14's mean value technique does it piece by piece (16c2).

Fourth, substitution. The chain technique says the slope of a function of a function is the outside's slope at the inside times the inside's slope. Read backwards, it turns an integral of that product into an integral of the outside alone, with the ends moved to where the inside sends them (16d1).

Fifth, parts. The product technique says the slope of a product is a sum of two products. Read backwards, it trades the integral of one of those products for the other, plus the change in the product from end to end (16e1).

What follows

Every technique of slopes read backwards is a technique of areas. The table of slopes from rung 13 gives a table of functions with a given slope. The power technique, backwards, gives the area under every power but one. Areas between two graphs, average values and areas under graphs that cross the axis all come out by subtracting values at the ends. Some regions that run off forever, or under a graph that climbs without bound, still have a finite area, found as a limit of areas. And in the sciences, distance is the area under speed. Work is the area under force against distance. Impulse is the area under force against time. And any total is the area under its rate.

What you can do because you know it

You can find the area under a graph by finding a function with the right slope, instead of by sums. You can write down a function whose slope is any continuous function you are given, and say how sure you are that it is one. You can run the chain technique and the product technique backwards to find areas that the table does not list. You can decide whether an endless region has a finite area. You can find totals from rates: how far from how fast, how much work from how hard a push, how much water from how fast it flows.

That is a start. Totals from rates are how most of physics is done, and physicists still count themselves beginners at it.

And 16·bio asks how much of a dose the body took up and how much is still in it, reading the answer as the clearance times the area under the blood level. It carries a box on the ledger, closed at the end. And 16·logi asks how much water a town's tank holds at any hour, when it is lowest, and how full it must start so that it never runs dry. It carries a box on the ledger, kept by the hour.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down for numbers or for area; 16j lays down three for volume, rung 15’s three axioms one dimension up, and proves the cross-sections technique from them. The rung spends rung 15's definition of the integral and its techniques, and rung 13's chain and product techniques. It spends rung 14's mean value technique, and its technique that same slopes mean the same shape lifted. And it spends rung 12's highest-and-lowest technique, through rung 15. The first form of the technique needs f not to jump at the point. The second needs the given function to have an area, and the example of 1881 shows that need is real. And one gap is left open on purpose: the power technique, read backwards, has no function to offer for the power −1. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that area and slope undo each other, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

Evangelista Torricelli, in the 1640s, and James Gregory, in 1668, each saw in figures that a tangent problem and an area problem were linked. Isaac Barrow, Newton’s teacher at Cambridge, gave the link as a technique of geometry in his lectures of 1670. Isaac Newton, from 1665, treated an area as a quantity that flows as its edge moves. Gottfried Leibniz, in 1675 and 1676, wrote the area as a sum, ∫, and the slope as a ratio, d over d.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy, in 1823, proved the technique for continuous functions from his definition of the integral. Bernhard Riemann’s integral of 1854, and Gaston Darboux’s sums of 1875, made it true for every function with an area, at the points where the function does not jump. Vito Volterra, in 1881, built the function whose slope exists everywhere, never passes a certain bound, and yet has no area under it by Riemann’s definition.

Accepted names

The two forms of the area-and-slope technique together are the fundamental theorem of calculus: 16b’s is its first part, 16c’s its second.
