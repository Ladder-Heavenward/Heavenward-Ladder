32l · technique

Where Oscillating Integrals Live

Total a turning that spins fast, and almost everything cancels: each swing up is matched by a swing down a moment later. That is why the coefficients of 32b fade, and why the transform of a smooth signal is small at high frequencies. But the cancelling is not even. Where the spin slows down, for a moment the turning lingers in one direction, and there the swings fail to cancel. So the total of a fast-spinning turning lives almost entirely near the places where its spin pauses. This step makes that precise. Where the spin never pauses, the total shrinks at least as fast as one over the speed. Where it pauses at one point, the total is read off from that one point, with a size that shrinks only like one over the root of the speed. To get there it needs one total done exactly: the bell with a turn inside, found with a loop from rung 26. And it ends with the ringing of a drum, read off from two pauses.

If the phase has no level point, a by-parts step puts one over the speed in front, so the total shrinks like one over the speed. The total of e to the i times the speed times x squared, over the whole line, is the root of π over the speed, turned by an eighth of a turn, found by sliding the bell’s total round a wedge. Near a single level point the phase is a square, so the total is that turned bell, scaled by the function there. The drum’s function at a large speed is the sum of two such pieces.

The accepted names

The no-level-point technique is the non-stationary phase estimate; the level-point technique is the method of stationary phase, and a level point of the phase a stationary point. The turning bell is the Fresnel integral, ∫cos(x²) and ∫sin(x²) the Fresnel integrals, and the drum’s function the Bessel function J₀, whose large-λ reading √(2/(πλ)) cos(λ − π/4) is its asymptotic form. The wedge is a Jordan-type contour. The method extends to the saddle-point method, or method of steepest descent, for totals with a complex phase; 23k’s totals dominated by their peak are its real cousin, Laplace’s method.

Who said it first

George Stokes used stationary phase in 1856 for the rainbow, and William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, set it out in 1887 for ship waves. Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Siméon Denis Poisson had used such totals for water waves in 1815–16. Augustin Fresnel computed his totals for light at an edge in 1818. In 32l.5.5, Poisson, on the judging panel for Fresnel’s entry to the Paris Academy’s prize of 1818, argued that Fresnel’s theory forced a bright spot behind a disc, as a reductio; François Arago looked and found it, and the spot carries both their names.

A proof that is not allowed

“Near x₀ replace φ by its square and g by g(x₀), and away from x₀ drop everything.” As a reading, fine; as a proof, not allowed. Each replacement leaves an error, and each must be shown smaller than 1/√λ: the dropped pieces by 32l1, the replaced phase by a by-parts step on the remainder, which needs two more slopes than this page asks for.
