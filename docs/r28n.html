28n · technique

Along Curves and Across Surfaces

The last proof step of this rung totalled a field along a path and round the edge of a rectangle. This step takes those totals out into the open. A wire bent into a coil has a weight that is a total along a curve. The work a wind does on a kite, or gravity on a hiker, is a total of the push along the way. The first thing to check is that these totals belong to the curve and not to how fast it was walked. Next, a field that is uphill from something has a total along a path that depends only on the ends, and two cross slopes tell, on a rectangle, whether a field is of that kind and how to find what it is uphill from. Last, a surface in space is traced by two measures, as a curve is traced by one, and the cross of its two edge vectors gives both its area and the flow through it.

A total along a curve does not care how fast the curve is traced; walking it backwards only turns the sign of a work total. On a rectangle, a field is uphill from something exactly when its two cross slopes agree, and then the work depends only on the ends. A surface is traced by two measures; the cross of its edge vectors gives its area and the flow through it.

28n1Totals Along a Curve

Let r(t), for α ≤ t ≤ β, trace a curve C in the plane or in space, with r′ continuous and never 0. 28j1 defined the circulation of a field F along r; in space it is the same total with three places: ∫ from α to β of F(r(t)) · r′(t) dt. When F is a force, this total is the work it does. A second total weighs a function f along the curve by distance, as 18i measured length: ∫ from α to β of f(r(t)) |r′(t)| dt, written ∫C f ds. With f the weight per metre of a wire, it is the wire’s weight.

Retrace technique: let u(s), for γ ≤ s ≤ δ, have a continuous slope that is never 0, and run from α to β or from β to α. Tracing the curve again as q(s) = r(u(s)) keeps ∫C f ds. It keeps the work total when u runs forwards, and turns its sign when u runs backwards.

q'(s) = u'(s)\,r'(u(s)) @@ the chain technique for slopes (13e), place by place
\textstyle\int_\gamma^\delta F(q(s))\cdot q'(s)\,ds = \int_\gamma^\delta F(r(u(s)))\cdot r'(u(s))\,u'(s)\,ds @@ put in q′
= \textstyle\int_{u(\gamma)}^{u(\delta)} F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)\,dt @@ the substitution technique for areas (16d), t = u(s)
\text{backwards: } u(\gamma) = \beta,\ u(\delta) = \alpha, \text{ and the total turns sign} @@ swapping the ends of a total turns its sign
\textstyle\int_\gamma^\delta f(q)\,|q'|\,ds = \int_\gamma^\delta f(r(u))\,|r'(u)|\,|u'|\,ds @@ |q′| = |u′| |r′(u)|
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_C f\,ds \text{ is kept either way}} \quad \blacksquare @@ backwards |u′| = −u′, and that minus cancels the swap of the ends
  
r(t) = (3\cos t,\ 3\sin t,\ 4t),\ \ 0 \le t \le 2\pi,\ \ |r'(t)| = 5 @@ 27i4’s spiral staircase, one turn
f = z = 4t \ \text{grams per unit of length} @@ the wire thickens as it climbs
\textstyle\int_C f\,ds = \int_0^{2\pi} 4t\cdot 5\,dt = 40\pi^2 \approx 395\ \text{g} @@ the weight
F = (-y,\ x,\ 0):\ \ F\cdot r' = 9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t = 9 @@ a field that turns round the upright axis
\htmlClass{new}{\text{work } 18\pi \text{ climbing};\ \ -18\pi \text{ coming down}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the retrace technique with u(s) = 2π − s

A total along a curve belongs to the curve and its direction, not to the walk. So a curve can be traced by whatever measure makes the totals easiest: by x itself when the curve is a graph, by the angle on a circle, by the height on a coil.

28n2Fields That Are Uphill From Something

By the uphill-total technique of 28j1, if F = ∇φ, the work along every path is φ at the end minus φ at the start, and round every loop it is 0. A field of that kind is uphill from φ, and φ is its potential. Two questions remain for the hand: how to tell such a field, and how to find φ.

Cross-slopes technique: let P and Q be smoothly sloped on a rectangle R. Then F = (P, Q) is uphill from some φ on R exactly when Py = Qx on R.

F = \nabla\varphi \Rightarrow P_y = \varphi_{xy} = \varphi_{yx} = Q_x @@ one way: the swap-knobs technique (28d2); φ’s second slopes are P and Q’s slopes, continuous
\varphi(x,y) = \textstyle\int_{x_0}^x P(t, y_0)\,dt + \int_{y_0}^y Q(x, t)\,dt @@ the other way: (x₀, y₀) the lower left corner of R; across, then up
\psi(x,y) = \textstyle\int_{y_0}^y Q(x_0, t)\,dt + \int_{x_0}^x P(t, y)\,dt @@ up, then across
\varphi - \psi = \text{circulation round the edge of } [x_0, x]\times[y_0, y] @@ along the bottom, up the right side, back along the top, down the left: inside on the left
= \textstyle\iint (Q_x - P_y) = 0 @@ the swirl technique (28j2); the cross slopes agree
\varphi_y = Q(x, y),\ \ \psi_x = P(x, y) @@ the area-slope technique (16b), on the last piece of each
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi = \psi, \text{ so } \nabla\varphi = (P, Q)} \quad \blacksquare @@ φₓ = ψₓ = P
  
F = (2xy + 3,\ x^2 + 4y):\ \ P_y = 2x = Q_x @@ the cross-slopes technique: F is uphill from something
\varphi = \textstyle\int P\,dx = x^2y + 3x + c(y) @@ hold y still; the constant may hold y
\varphi_y = x^2 + c'(y) = x^2 + 4y,\ \ c(y) = 2y^2 @@ match Q
\varphi = x^2y + 3x + 2y^2 @@ check: ∇φ = F
\htmlClass{new}{\text{work from } (0, 0) \text{ to } (1, 2) = \varphi(1, 2) - \varphi(0, 0) = 13} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uphill-total technique (28j1), along every path

The rectangle matters. The field (−y, x)/(x² + y²) of 28j4 has Py = Qx at every point but 0, and works 2π round the circle about 0, so it is uphill from nothing on the plane with 0 taken out. On any rectangle that misses 0, the cross-slopes technique still hands it a potential. The trouble is the hole, not the field.

28n3Totals Across a Surface

A patch is a map r(u, v) from a region S of the uv-plane into space, smoothly sloped and one-to-one, with ru × rv ≠ 0 inside S. Its edge vectors ru and rv are the partial slopes, place by place. A small box of sides Δu, Δv goes to nearly the parallelogram on ruΔu and rvΔv, of area |ru × rv|ΔuΔv by the cross technique. So the area of the patch is ∬S |ru × rv|, and the flux of a field F through it, toward the side ru × rv points to, is ∬S F(r) · (ru × rv). For a face of 28j3’s box, r(u, v) = (b, u, v), and ru × rv = (1, 0, 0): the flux is the face total of P that 28j3 took.

Patch technique: if the patch is traced again as q(s, t) = r(T(s, t)), with T smoothly sloped and one-to-one from S′ onto S, then qs × qt = det DT · (ru × rv). So the area is kept, and the flux is kept when det DT > 0 and turns sign when det DT < 0.

q_s = u_s\,r_u + v_s\,r_v,\ \ q_t = u_t\,r_u + v_t\,r_v @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c2): a 3 × 2 grid times the 2 × 2 grid DT
q_s\times q_t = u_sv_t\,(r_u\times r_v) + v_su_t\,(r_v\times r_u) @@ the cross is straight in each slot (27e2); r_u × r_u = r_v × r_v = 0 (27i3)
= (u_sv_t - v_su_t)\,(r_u\times r_v) = \det DT\,(r_u\times r_v) @@ v × u = −(u × v) (27i3)
\textstyle\iint_{S'} F(q)\cdot(q_s\times q_t) = \iint_{S'} \big[F(r)\cdot(r_u\times r_v)\big](T)\,\det DT @@ put in
\htmlClass{new}{= \pm\textstyle\iint_S F(r)\cdot(r_u\times r_v)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the change-of-variables technique (28i3) takes |det DT|: + when det DT > 0; for the area the sizes are taken and no sign appears
  
r(\varphi, \theta) = a(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi),\ \ 0 \le \varphi \le \pi,\ 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi @@ ball measures (28m3) with ρ = a
r_\varphi\times r_\theta = a^2\sin\varphi\,(\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi) @@ the cross technique (27i3): a² sin φ times the outward vector of length 1
\text{area} = \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 4\pi a^2 @@ 21o’s skin technique, again
F = (x, y, z):\ \ F\cdot(r_\varphi\times r_\theta) = a\cdot a^2\sin\varphi @@ F is a times the outward vector
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flux out} = 4\pi a^3} \quad \blacksquare @@ and the spread of F is 3, with 3 · (4/3)πa³ = 4πa³

28j3 proved the trade of flux for spread on boxes. The sphere is not a box, and still the flux out matches the total of the spread inside. This step does not prove the trade for every solid; it only shows that the patch total and the spread agree here, as they should if the trade is as wide as it looks.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A total along a curve is a line integral: ∫C f ds is the line integral with respect to arc length, and ∫C F · dr the line integral of a vector field, which is the work when F is a force. The retrace technique is the independence of line integrals from the parametrization, up to orientation. A field uphill from φ is a conservative (or gradient) field, and φ its (scalar) potential; physicists often use −φ, so that a ball rolls down the potential. The cross-slopes technique is the test for exactness, Py = Qx; a field that passes it is closed, and one with a potential is exact. That a closed field is exact on a region with no holes is the Poincaré lemma. A patch is a parametrized surface, ru × rv its normal vector, and the two totals the surface area and the flux (or surface) integral ∬ F · dS.

Who said it first

Alexis Clairaut gave the condition Py = Qx for a total differential in 1739 and 1740. Joseph-Louis Lagrange used a potential for gravity in 1773, and George Green named it the potential in his pamphlet of 1828. Carl Friedrich Gauss took totals across closed surfaces in his work on the pull of ellipsoids in 1813, and Mikhail Ostrogradsky in his work on heat.
