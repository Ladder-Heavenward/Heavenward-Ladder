Does light take the shortest path?

Does light take the shortest path? A ray of sunlight off a still pond bounces at a point you can find with a ruler, and a straw in a glass of water looks broken at the surface. Two thousand years ago it was noticed that a bounce off a flat mirror takes the shortest path that touches the mirror. Then came the broken straw. A bent path is not the shortest, so the old rule seemed to fail. What makes it hard is that light travels at different speeds in air and in water, and the shortest path and the quickest path are no longer the same. This page shows, with 20’s flips and 14’s slopes, that the bounce is the shortest path, that the bend is the quickest, and that one rule gives both.

Flip the target across the mirror, and the shortest bouncing path becomes a straight line: the bounce makes equal angles. Two mirrors at right angles make a half turn, and send every ray straight back. Where light changes speed, the quickest path bends so that the run over the length, divided by the speed, is the same on both sides.

20phys1A flip finds the shortest bounce

A ray leaves A, touches a flat mirror at P, and reaches B, with A and B on the same side. Which P makes the path shortest? Flip B across the mirror’s line, to B′. Every point P of the line is kept by the flip, so P is as far from B′ as from B.

Flip-the-target technique: the shortest path from A to the mirror to B touches the mirror where the segment AB′ crosses it.

d(P, B) = d(P, B') @@ the flip keeps distances (20d) and keeps P
d(A, P) + d(P, B) = d(A, P) + d(P, B') \ge d(A, B') @@ the triangle technique (18b)
\text{equal exactly when } P \text{ is on the segment } AB' @@ equality-on-the-segment (18b)
\htmlClass{new}{P = AB' \cap \text{mirror}} \quad \blacksquare @@ A and B′ are on opposite sides, so the segment crosses the line

At that P, the ray in and the ray out make equal angles with the mirror, since the flip carries the ray out onto the straight continuation of the ray in. That is how light is seen to bounce. The bounce that light makes is the shortest path that touches the mirror.

20phys2Two mirrors make a half turn

Put two mirrors at right angles, along the x-axis and the y-axis, and send a ray at the corner. It bounces off one mirror, then the other. Each bounce flips the ray’s direction across its mirror.

(a, b) \mapsto (a, -b) @@ the bounce off the x-axis flips the direction
(a, -b) \mapsto (-a, -b) @@ the bounce off the y-axis
\htmlClass{new}{(a, b) \mapsto (-a, -b)} \quad \blacksquare @@ two flips across lines at right angles: a half turn (20e)

Corner technique: two mirrors at right angles send every ray back the way it came, whatever its direction. The reflectors on a bicycle and at the edge of a road are made of tiny corners, in three mirrors, so that a car’s headlight comes back to the driver.

20phys3The quickest path bends

Light runs at speed v₁ in air and a slower v₂ in water. Put the surface along the x-axis, A = (0, a) above it and B = (d, −b) below, and let the ray cross at (x, 0). Write ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ for the two lengths of the path.

T(x) = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}}{v_1} + \frac{\sqrt{b^2 + (d - x)^2}}{v_2} @@ time is length over speed, on each side
T'(x) = \frac{x}{v_1 \ell_1} - \frac{d - x}{v_2 \ell_2} @@ the chain rule
T' \text{ rises from below } 0 \text{ to above } 0 \text{ on } [0, d] @@ each fraction x/ℓ₁ rises with x, and (d − x)/ℓ₂ falls
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{x / \ell_1}{v_1} = \frac{(d - x)/\ell_2}{v_2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one input with T′ = 0 is the least (14a, 14d)

x/ℓ₁ is the ray’s run along the surface over its length, above; (d − x)/ℓ₂ the same below. Bend technique: the quickest path bends so that the run over the length, divided by the speed, is the same on both sides. When the speeds are equal, the ratios are equal, and the path is straight: quickest and shortest agree.

20phys4A broken straw, and light that cannot leave

Light runs about 1.33 times faster in air than in water. So the run over the length in air is 1.33 times that in water.

\text{in air } 0.8 \Rightarrow \text{in water } 0.8 / 1.33 \approx 0.60 @@ the bend: the ray steepens going in
\text{in water } r \Rightarrow \text{in air } 1.33\,r @@ the same rule, from below
1.33\,r \le 1 \Rightarrow r \le 0.75 @@ a run is never longer than its length
\htmlClass{new}{r \gt 0.75:\ \text{no path out}} @@ the light bounces back into the water

Past a certain slant, light inside water has no quickest way out, so it stays in. A glass thread carries light round a bend this way, bouncing off its own walls, and so do the fibres that carry phone calls under the sea.

The accepted names

Equal angles at a mirror is the law of reflection. The bend technique is the law of refraction, Snell’s law, usually written with sines (rung 21) and the refractive index n = c/v: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Light trapped inside is total internal reflection, beyond the critical angle. A corner of mirrors is a corner reflector, or retroreflector. That light takes the quickest path is Fermat’s principle of least time.

Who said it first

Hero of Alexandria (first century) showed that the bounce is the shortest path, by this page’s flip. Ibn Sahl (984) stated the law of refraction; Willebrord Snell (1621) and René Descartes (1637) found it again. Pierre de Fermat (1662) derived it from least time. In 20·phys.6.5, Claude Clerselier, defending Descartes, answered him that light cannot choose, having no knowledge of where it is going. Three centuries later, Richard Feynman’s sum over paths gave one answer: light, in a sense, tries every path. Corner reflectors left on the Moon in 1969 still return laser pulses fired from Earth.
