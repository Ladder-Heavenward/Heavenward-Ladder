Where Should a Solar Panel Work?

Where should a solar panel work? A panel in full sun can give its full power, or almost none, depending on what it is connected to. Short it with a wire and current flows but the voltage is zero; leave it open and the voltage is high but no current flows. In between lies one best point, and a small box beside every solar array spends its life hunting for it. This page finds which light a cell can use from 36b’s band and its gap; draws the cell’s current against voltage as a bending curve with 17’s exp; finds power as a hump with 14h; finds how a controller works at the top; and watches a cloud move the top.

Light past the cell’s gap makes a fixed current I_L; the cell’s own diode leaks back I₀(e^(V/V_T) − 1), which is tiny until V nears its open voltage and then soars. Power V·I is 0 at both ends and a hump between. Its top is where the slope of power is 0. Less light lowers the curve and moves the top, so the controller must keep climbing to it.

36eng1Only Light Past the Gap Counts

Jean installs solar arrays. A silicon cell turns light into current only when a bit of light carries more energy than the cell’s gap, 1.12 electronvolts for silicon. Energy and wavelength are tied: each bit of light carries 1240/λ electronvolts, with λ in nanometres.

\dfrac{1240}{\lambda} \ge 1.12:\ \ \lambda \le 1\,107 \text{ nm} @@ visible light and the near infrared (36b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{longer waves pass through unused; shorter ones give up their excess as heat}} @@

From here on, on this page, I_L is the current the light makes, 9 A for one of Jean’s cells in full sun, and V the voltage across the cell.

36eng2Current Against Voltage: a Bending Curve

Inside, the cell is also a one-way valve for current, which leaks current back as the voltage rises, by an exponential (17).

I = I_L - I_0\left(e^{V/V_T} - 1\right) @@ V_T = 0.0257 V at room heat; I₀ = 10⁻¹⁰ A
\text{open, } I = 0:\ \ V_{\text{oc}} = V_T \ln\!\left(\dfrac{I_L}{I_0} + 1\right) \approx 0.0257 \times 25.2 \approx 0.65 \text{ V} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flat near } I_L \text{ for most voltages, then falling sharply to 0 near } 0.65 \text{ V}} @@ a bending curve

36eng3Power Is a Hump

Power is V times I: 0 at V = 0, 0 at V = V_oc, and positive between. Find its top (14h).

P(V) = V\left(I_L - I_0 (e^{V/V_T} - 1)\right) @@ 
P\prime(V) = 0:\ \ e^{v}(1 + v) = \dfrac{I_L + I_0}{I_0} \approx 9 \times 10^{10},\ \ v = V/V_T @@ 
v \approx 22.1:\ \ V_{\text{mp}} \approx 0.568 \text{ V},\ \ I_{\text{mp}} \approx 9 - 0.39 = 8.61 \text{ A} @@ found by halving (12e)
\htmlClass{new}{P_{\max} \approx 4.89 \text{ W},\ \text{about } 84\% \text{ of } V_{\text{oc}} \times I_L} @@

Best-point technique: a solar cell gives most power at the voltage where the slope of V·I is 0, a little below its open voltage, where the current has only begun to fall. In a test lab under standard light, a cell’s traced curve and its measured top sit where this count puts them.

36eng4Working at the Top

What the panel is connected to sets its voltage. A battery or the grid would hold it at the wrong voltage most of the time. So a controller sits between: it nudges the panel’s voltage up a little, measures the power, keeps the nudge if power rose and reverses it if it fell.

\text{nudge } V \text{ by } \delta:\ \text{if } P \text{ rose, keep going; if it fell, turn back} @@ the slope’s sign says which way (14)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the voltage hovers about } V_{\text{mp}}, \text{ stepping across it and back}} @@

Jean watches the controller’s log on a clear day: the panel’s working voltage wanders in tiny steps just around the top, and the power stays within a fraction of a percent of the most the curve allows.

36eng5Clouds Move the Top

A cloud cuts the light, and with it I_L. The curve drops, and its top moves.

V_{\text{oc}} \approx 0.0257 \ln(3 \times 10^{10}) \approx 0.62 \text{ V} @@ only a little lower: a log
e^{v}(1 + v) \approx 3 \times 10^{10}:\ \ v \approx 21.0,\ V_{\text{mp}} \approx 0.54 \text{ V} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{P_{\max} \approx 0.54 \times 2.86 \approx 1.55 \text{ W}} @@ a third of the light, a third of the power

Light sets the current almost in step; voltage changes only as its log; so the best voltage drifts a little as clouds pass, and the controller must follow it. Heat also lowers the voltage, so panels give less on a hot day than a cold bright one. An installer sizes the array and its controller from these two curves, for the site’s sun and heat.

36eng7Hot Panels Give Less

A panel’s rating is measured with its cells at 25 °C. In full summer sun the cells run far hotter than the air around them, and a hot cell gives less: its voltage falls as it warms while its current hardly rises, so the best point (36eng4) slides down.

P = P_{25}\,\big(1 - c\,(T - 25)\big) @@ c: the share lost for each degree above 25 °C
c \approx 0.0024 \text{ to } 0.0034 \text{ per } {}^\circ\text{C} @@ from makers’ sheets
\text{story: } 400 \text{ W panel},\ c = 0.003,\ T = 65:\ \ 400\,(1 - 0.12) = 352 \text{ W} @@ a hot roof in summer
\htmlClass{new}{T < 25:\ \ P > P_{25}} @@ a cold bright day beats the rating
\text{story: } T = 5:\ \ 400\,(1 + 0.06) = 424 \text{ W} @@ snow on the ground, sun in a clear sky

So the best hours for a panel are bright and cool, and a panel mounted with air moving behind it gives more than one laid flat on a hot roof. A desert has the most sun but also the hottest cells; its gain is in hours, not in watts per hour.

Hot-panel technique: a panel loses a share c of its rated power for each degree its cells run above 25 °C, so P = P₂₅(1 − c(T − 25)), and gains the same share for each degree below.

Accepted names

The cell is a photovoltaic cell; its curve is the diode equation with a light current; the top is the maximum power point and the controller a maximum power point tracker, here by perturb and observe. The ratio 84% is the fill factor. The most any one-gap cell can turn into power is the Shockley-Queisser limit, about 33%. The share c is the temperature coefficient of power, and 25 °C with full sun the standard test conditions.

Who said it first

William Shockley set out the diode equation in 1949; Shockley and Hans Queisser found the one-gap limit in 1961 (not yet revisited for the dates).
