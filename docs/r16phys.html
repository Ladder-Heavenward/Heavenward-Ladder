How Far Did It Go, If You Only Know How Fast?

How far did it go, if you only know how fast? 13·phys read velocity as the slope of position, and acceleration as the slope of velocity. 14·phys read force as the slope of momentum. Each of those is a slope, so 16c runs each one backwards: position from velocity, velocity from acceleration, momentum from force. A force can be added up two ways, over the time it acts or over the distance it pushes through, and the two totals are different things. Added over time, it gives the change in momentum. Added over distance, it gives the change in 14·phys's kinetic energy. This page proves both, follows a spring through a whole swing, and finds how fast a stone must leave the Earth never to come back.

The change in place is the area under the speed, and the change in speed the area under the push. A force added over time, the impulse, is the change in mass times speed. A force added over distance, the work, is the change in half the mass times the square of the speed, by substitution for areas. A spring stores energy that grows with the square of its stretch. Escaping the Earth takes a fixed, finite amount of work.

16phys2Force Over Time: Impulse

Impulse technique: if the total force F(t) on a body is continuous on [t₁, t₂] and F = p′, then the change in momentum p(t₂) − p(t₁) is ∫_{t₁}^{t₂} F(t) dt. It is area-is-a-change with p as the antiderivative of F. 14·phys defined the impulse as the change in momentum; impulse says it is the area under the force.

16phys3Force Over Distance: Work

From here on, the work done by a force F(x) on a body moving along a line from x₁ to x₂ is W = ∫_{x₁}^{x₂} F(x) dx, in joules when F is in newtons and x in metres. For a constant force it is force times displacement. Its accepted name is work.

Work-energy technique: let a body of mass m move along a line with position x(t), with x′ = v and v′ continuous. Let the total force on it depend only on position, F(x), continuous, with F(x(t)) = m v′(t). Then ∫_{x(t₁)}^{x(t₂)} F(x) dx = ½ m v(t₂)² − ½ m v(t₁)².

\textstyle\int_{x(t_1)}^{x(t_2)} F(x)\,dx = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} F(x(t))\,x'(t)\,dt @@ substitution for areas (16d), read right to left, with the inside r = x(t): x′ = v is continuous since v has a slope (slope-means-no-gap)
= \textstyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2} m\,v'(t)\,v(t)\,dt @@ F(x(t)) = m v′(t); x′ = v
\big(\tfrac12 m v^2\big)' = m v v' @@ chain for slopes with power for slopes
= \tfrac12 m v(t_2)^2 - \tfrac12 m v(t_1)^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ area-is-a-change

Its accepted name is the work–energy theorem. So adding a force up over time gives Δ(mv), and adding the same force up over distance gives Δ(½mv²). Neither replaces the other. 14·phys's crash kept the first and lost some of the second.

A writer of 1740 argued, against the followers of an older school, that the force of a moving body is measured by its mass times the square of its speed. Her evidence was a set of experiments with soft clay. A ball dropped from four times the height arrives twice as fast, and sinks four times as deep.

\text{work by the spring from } x_1 \text{ to } x_2:\ \ \textstyle\int_{x_1}^{x_2} -kx\,dx = \tfrac12 k x_1^2 - \tfrac12 k x_2^2 @@ the table; area-is-a-change

From A to 0 the spring does work ½kA², and the mass arrives at its top speed A√(k/m). From 0 to −A the spring does work −½kA², and the mass stops. Then the same backwards. 14·phys's ½ks² is the work it takes to squeeze the spring by s. That closes the premise 14·phys held in trust. Its accepted name is the spring's potential energy.

Power is the rate at which work is done, P = W′(t); its accepted unit is the watt, 1 W = 1 J/s. For a force F on a body at speed v, P = Fv, since W′ = F x′ by chain for slopes on ∫ F dx. That answers the question 13·eng left for rung 16.

A helmet or a crumple zone does not change the impulse of a crash, which is fixed by the change in momentum (impulse). It spreads it over a longer time, and the average force, impulse over time, falls in proportion.

Who said it first

Du Châtelet argued, against the followers of Descartes and from ’s Gravesande’s experiments, that the force of a moving body is measured by its mass times the square of its speed, since a ball dropped from four times the height, arriving twice as fast, sinks four times as deep into soft clay.

Émilie du Châtelet, Institutions de physique (1740), chapter XXI; paraphrase

In 16·phys.8.4, the mv² school is Leibniz’s and the mv school Descartes’s; the mathematician of 1743 is Jean le Rond d’Alembert. The spring law is Robert Hooke’s, the law of gravitation Isaac Newton’s, and the law of charges Charles-Augustin de Coulomb’s.
