16g · technique

Definite Integrals and Areas

A definite integral counts the region below the axis as negative, so it is not always the area people mean. The region between two graphs is not the region under either one. And a long record of readings is often summed up by one number, its average. This step sorts these out. It proves, by rung 15's boxes, that the region between two graphs has as its area the integral of the top minus the bottom. It shows how to get the total area, with nothing counted negative, by cutting at the points where the graph crosses the axis. And it works out averages with 16c's antiderivatives instead of 15f's existence proof.

The area between two graphs is the integral of the top less the bottom. For the total area under a graph, find where it crosses zero, cut there, and add the sizes of the pieces. The average height is the integral divided by the width of the interval.

16g1The Region Between Two Graphs

Area-between technique: let f and g be integrable on [p, q], with f(x) ≥ g(x) there. Let the set of points (x, y) with p ≤ x ≤ q and g(x) ≤ y ≤ f(x) have an area A obeying the three area axioms. Then A = ∫ₚ^q (f − g).

\begin{aligned}&\text{on the } i\text{-th piece of } P:\ \text{the box from }\\ &\quad  M_i(g) \text{ up to } m_i(f), \text{ if } m_i(f) \gt M_i(g)\end{aligned} @@ it fits inside the region: below f and above g on the whole piece
\textstyle\sum \max(0,\ m_i(f) - M_i(g))\,\Delta x_i \le A @@ inside-no-more for the boxes inside, side-by-side to add them (they meet only on lines x = xᵢ)
L(f, P) - U(g, P) \le A @@ each term is at least mᵢ(f)Δxᵢ − Mᵢ(g)Δxᵢ
\begin{aligned}&\text{the boxes from } m_i(g) \text{ up to } M_i(f) \text{ cover}\\ &\quad\text{the region:\ } A \le U(f, P) - L(g, P)\end{aligned} @@ inside-no-more, side-by-side the same way
\text{fix } \varepsilon:\ \text{one } P \text{ has both gaps } U - L \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ gap-closes for f and for g, then a common refinement (15b's refining-tightens)
|A - (\textstyle\int f - \int g)| \lt \varepsilon \text{ for every } \varepsilon,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{A = \int_p^q (f - g)} \quad \blacksquare @@ both numbers lie in [L(f) − U(g), U(f) − L(g)], an interval of width under ε; three-way on ℝ; areas-add

No height of zero was needed. The graphs can sit above the axis, below it, or across it; only f ≥ g matters. Where the graphs cross, f ≥ g fails, so the interval is cut at the crossings and the top and bottom swapped on each piece.

2x = x^2 \iff x = 0 \text{ or } x = 2 @@ x(2 − x) = 0
2x \ge x^2 \text{ on } [0, 2] @@ x(2 − x) ≥ 0 there: both factors ≥ 0
A = \textstyle\int_0^2 (2x - x^2)\,dx = \big(x^2 - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\big)\Big|_0^2 = 4 - \tfrac83 = \tfrac43 @@ area-between; the table (16f); area-is-a-change
  
x^3 = x \iff x(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0 @@ crossings at −1, 0 and 1
x^3 \ge x \text{ on } [-1, 0],\ \ x \ge x^3 \text{ on } [0, 1] @@ the sign of x(x − 1)(x + 1) on each piece
A = \textstyle\int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x)\,dx + \int_0^1 (x - x^3)\,dx = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 @@ area-between on each piece; interval-cut-in-two; odd-and-even-areas (16d) would see the two pieces are equal

16g2Slicing the Other Way

Read the graphs x = y² and x = y + 2 as functions of y. The region between them has a left edge and a right edge for each height y from −1 to 2. Cut it into boxes lying on their sides, one for each piece of [−1, 2] on the y-axis. Then the squeeze of area-between runs the same way, with right minus left in place of top minus bottom. That uses the side-by-side axiom for boxes that meet along lines y = k; the cellar says why it is there to use. With it, area-between is true with x and y swapped.

y^2 = y + 2 \iff y = -1 \text{ or } y = 2 @@ (y − 2)(y + 1) = 0
A = \textstyle\int_{-1}^{2} (y + 2 - y^2)\,dy = \big(\tfrac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \tfrac{y^3}{3}\big)\Big|_{-1}^{2} = \tfrac{10}{3} + \tfrac76 = \tfrac92 @@ area-between on its side; area-is-a-change
\text{in } x:\ \ \textstyle\int_0^1 2\sqrt{x}\,dx + \int_1^4 (\sqrt{x} - x + 2)\,dx = \tfrac43 + \tfrac{19}{6} = \tfrac92 @@ the top edge is √x; the bottom is −√x up to x = 1, then the line y = x − 2

The same number both ways, and the sideways road needed one integral where the upright one needed two.

16g3Reading an Area Function

F' = f @@ area-slope (16b)
f \gt 0 \text{ on a stretch} \Rightarrow F \text{ increasing there};\ f \lt 0 \Rightarrow F \text{ decreasing} @@ slope-sign-order (14d)
f \text{ changes from } + \text{ to } - \text{ at } c \Rightarrow F \text{ has a crest at } c @@ first-test (14f)
f \text{ increasing} \Rightarrow F \text{ bends up};\ \ f \text{ decreasing} \Rightarrow F \text{ bends down} @@ bends-up (14g), where f has a slope

So the area function of x² − 1 from 0 falls until x = 1. There f crosses from negative to positive, and F has a trough. Then F climbs. It bends up wherever x > 0, since there x² − 1 is increasing.

16g4Net and Total

From here on, ∫ₚ^q f is the net area of f on [p, q], and ∫ₚ^q |f| its total area. Their accepted names are signed area and total area; for a velocity, displacement and distance travelled.

x^2 - 1 = 0 \text{ at } x = 1 \text{ in } [0, 2] @@ the one crossing; f < 0 on [0, 1), f > 0 on (1, 2]
\text{net: } \textstyle\int_0^2 (x^2 - 1)\,dx = \big(\tfrac{x^3}{3} - x\big)\Big|_0^2 = \tfrac23 @@ area-is-a-change
\textstyle\int_0^1 (x^2 - 1)\,dx = -\tfrac23,\ \ \int_1^2 (x^2 - 1)\,dx = \tfrac43 @@ interval-cut-in-two (15e): −2/3 + 4/3 = 2/3
\text{total: } \textstyle\int_0^2 |x^2 - 1|\,dx = \tfrac23 + \tfrac43 = 2 @@ |f| = −f on [0, 1], f on [1, 2] (unpack-the-bars); interval-cut-in-two, areas-scale

Between two crossings f keeps one sign, by 12a's in-between-value, general form: a continuous function that changed sign there would have to cross. So the recipe always works for a continuous f with finitely many crossings: find them, integrate on each piece, and add the sizes.

16g5Averages

\text{avg of } x^2 \text{ on } [0, 3] = \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac{x^3}{3}\Big|_0^3 = 3 @@ 15f's line; area-is-a-change
x^2 = 3 \text{ at } c = \sqrt3 \in [0, 3] @@ the c that average-height promises
\text{avg of } 6t - t^2 \text{ on } [0, 6] = \tfrac16\big(3t^2 - \tfrac{t^3}{3}\big)\Big|_0^6 = \tfrac16(108 - 72) = 6 @@ a speed 6t − t² for 6 hours: average speed 6 km an hour

An average height times the width gives the area. The box on [p, q] of height avg(f) has the same area as the region under f. 15f proved such a height is reached somewhere. 16c finds its value.

16g6The Middle Speed

A teacher in Paris, around 1350, drew the speeds of a body as heights standing on a line of times. If the speed grows evenly from nothing, the figure is cut off by a straight line. The whole distance, he said, is the same as if the body had moved all the time at the speed it had at the middle instant.

16g.4.4seamScholars in Paris and at a college in Oxford had, by 1350, the rule that a body speeding up evenly goes as far as it would at its middle speed. In 1638 a famous scientist gave the same rule as his own, with experiments on balls rolling down slopes. In 1913 a historian argued that he owed it to the medieval schools. Another answered that a rule about imagined qualities is not yet a law about falling bodies.

Side by side, both ways

15a laid the side-by-side axiom down for sets that meet along a line x = k. Nothing in it cares which of the two coordinates the line keeps fixed. Turn the roles of x and y about and it reads the same for a line y = k: the boxes and their areas are the same boxes and areas, named the other way round. So the ladder had earned it both ways without saying so, and 16g is the first page to use the second way. Oh, it works both ways.

Who said it first

Oresme drew the speeds of a body as heights standing on a line of times. If the speed grows evenly from nothing, the figure is cut off by a straight line, and the whole distance, he said, is the same as if the body had moved all the time at the speed it had at the middle instant.

Nicole Oresme, Tractatus de configurationibus qualitatum et motuum (about 1350), part III; paraphrase

In 16g.4.4, the scholars are Nicole Oresme in Paris and the calculators of Merton College, Oxford; the scientist of 1638 is Galileo; the historian of 1913 is Pierre Duhem, and the one who answered him is Alexandre Koyré. The middle-speed rule’s accepted name is the mean speed theorem, or the Merton rule.
