14·bio · biology

Why Are Cells so Small?

Why are cells so small? A drop of food colouring in still water spreads, with no stirring, because its molecules are knocked about at random by the water's. Oxygen gets into a living cell the same way. Over the width of a cell this is fast: a few hundredths of a second. Over the length of an arm it takes years. So anything that lives on oxygen arriving this way alone must keep every part of itself close to its skin. This page finds how close, by asking where the oxygen inside a living sheet is least, and when that least value reaches 0.

The time to spread grows with the square of the distance. Inside a sheet that uses oxygen, the oxygen is least at the middle, a bottom where its slope is 0. The sheet can be no thicker than the distance at which that bottom reaches 0.

How far a random walk gets

A single oxygen molecule in water is struck many billions of times a second and goes nowhere in particular. Watch a great many of them leave one place together, and the typical distance they have spread grows with time, but not in proportion to it. The rule was found in 1905 and checked with grains seen under a microscope. It is a physical premise on this page, held in trust:

x² = 2Dt: after time t, the typical distance x along one line satisfies x² = 2Dt, where D is a constant for the molecule and the liquid. Its accepted name is the diffusion coefficient.

For oxygen in water near 20 °C, D ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s. Turned around, the time to spread a distance x is t = x²/(2D).

Walking at a steady pace, every metre costs the same. Spreading, every micrometre costs more than the last. There is no speed of diffusion, only a time for each distance.

Oxygen inside a living sheet

Take a flat sheet of living tissue, thickness 2L. It is bathed on both faces in water holding oxygen at c₀ moles per cubic metre (about 0.27 mol/m³ in water at 20 °C that is open to the air). Measure x from the middle of the sheet, so the faces are at x = −L and x = L. Every part of the tissue uses oxygen at the same rate, q moles per cubic metre each second. Once the oxygen has settled, its concentration at x is, as a second physical premise, held in trust,

c(x) = c₀ − (q/(2D)) (L² − x²), for −L ≤ x ≤ L.

At both faces, x² = L², and c = c₀: the sheet matches the water outside. Where is the oxygen least?

c'(x) = \tfrac{q}{D}\,x @@ power for slopes, constant-factor for slopes: c₀ and L² are constants
c'(x) = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; x = 0 @@ flat-top: the only input worth testing inside
c' \lt 0 \text{ on } [-L, 0),\ \ c' \gt 0 \text{ on } (0, L] @@ positive-but-finitely (14d): c falls to the middle and rises after
c''(x) = \tfrac{q}{D} \gt 0 @@ second-test (14f): a bottom, and the curve bends up everywhere
c(0) = \htmlClass{new}{c_0 - \tfrac{qL^2}{2D}} @@ the least oxygen in the sheet, at its middle

The middle is the worst place in the sheet, and it gets worse as the sheet gets thicker: c(0) falls as L grows. The sheet stays supplied all through only while c(0) ≥ 0.

c_0 - \tfrac{qL^2}{2D} \ge 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; L^2 \le \tfrac{2Dc_0}{q} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree (8d): q and D are positive
\htmlClass{new}{L_{\max} = \sqrt{2Dc_0/q}} @@ squares-keep-order on ℚ (8a): squares keep order from 0 up; the greatest half-thickness
L_{\max} = \sqrt{\tfrac{2 \cdot 2 \times 10^{-9} \cdot 0.27}{0.01}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} @@ q = 0.01 mol/(m³·s), a modest demand

About a third of a millimetre each side of the middle: a sheet well under a millimetre thick. Flatworms that live on oxygen soaking in from the water are this thin, however long and wide they grow. A blade of bull kelp off the Mendocino coast is thin for the same reason.

The time to arrive, the time to run out

Put L_max back into the first premise. The time for oxygen to spread from a face to the middle of the thickest sheet is

t = L_max²/(2D) = (2Dc₀/q)/(2D) = c₀/q.

And c₀/q is the time the tissue would take to use up the oxygen it holds, if the supply stopped. The thickest sheet is the one where the time for oxygen to arrive equals the time for it to be used. D has dropped out.

One biologist argued that for every kind of animal there is a most convenient size, and that a change of size forces a change of form. A large animal must grow lungs, gills or folded guts that a small one does without. The small one is mostly near its surface.

A ball-shaped cell

Ball, centre and radius are geometry words, and the ladder builds them at rung 18. Here they mean only this. Take a cell that is the same in every direction from one middle point, its centre. Let r be the distance from the centre, and R the distance from the centre to the skin, its radius. For such a cell the settled oxygen is, as a physical premise of the same kind,

c(r) = c₀ − (q/(6D)) (R² − r²), for 0 ≤ r ≤ R,

the sheet's curve with a 6 where the sheet had a 2. The same techs give the least value at the centre and the greatest radius R_max = √(6Dc₀/q). A bacterium uses oxygen fast, near 10 mol/(m³·s): R_max is then about 18 µm, and the bacterium itself is about 1 µm. A frog's egg, which uses oxygen slowly, can be about a millimetre across.

Where the two premises are earned

Both premises on this page come from the lab. The spreading rule, x² = 2Dt, is derived on 30n from many molecules each moving at random, and that closes its flag. The curve inside the sheet is derived much higher up, from the oxygen’s own motion, and that closes the other. This page needs only what the two premises say.

Who said it first

Haldane argued that for every kind of animal there is a most convenient size, and that a change of size forces a change of form: a large animal must grow lungs, gills or folded guts that a small one does without, because the small one is mostly near its surface.

Haldane, “On being the right size” (1926); paraphrase

The rule x² = 2Dt is Albert Einstein’s (1905), and Jean Perrin checked it with grains seen under a microscope. In the closer, the naturalist of 1917 is D’Arcy Thompson, in On Growth and Form, and the theory of evolution is Charles Darwin’s.
