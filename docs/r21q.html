21q · technique

Angles standing on arcs

Pick two points on a circle and a third anywhere else on its rim, and join the third to the other two. The angle it makes never changes as it moves along the rim, so long as it stays on the same side. This step proves that, and finds the angle: half the angle the same two points make at the centre. From it come the right angle in every half circle, the rule for four points on one circle, and the one circle that fits snugly inside any triangle.

An angle at the rim is half the angle at the centre on the same arc, so every angle on that arc is the same. An angle in a half circle is right. Four corners on one circle have opposite angles adding to a half turn. Every triangle holds one circle that touches all three sides.

21q1Rim and centre

Let A and B be on a circle with centre O, and P on the rim, on the far side of the line AB from the short arc AB. Rim-half-centre technique: the angle APB at the rim is half the angle AOB at the centre.

OA = OB = OP @@ radii
\angle OPA = \angle OAP = a,\ \ \angle OPB = \angle OBP = b @@ equal-sides-equal-angles (18f), twice
\angle AOP = \pi - 2a,\ \ \angle BOP = \pi - 2b @@ the angle sum (18f, with 180° = π by 21j2)
\angle AOB = 2\pi - (\pi - 2a) - (\pi - 2b) = 2(a + b) @@ the three angles at O make a whole turn
\htmlClass{new}{\angle AOB = 2\,\angle APB} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∠APB = a + b; with O outside, subtract instead of add

21q2One arc, one angle; the half circle

Same-arc technique: all rim angles standing on one arc, from the same side, are equal; and an angle standing on a half circle is right.

\angle APB = \tfrac12\angle AOB = \angle AQB @@ rim-half-centre, for P and Q on the same side
AB \text{ a diameter} \Rightarrow \angle AOB = \pi @@ A, O, B in a line
\htmlClass{new}{\angle APB = \tfrac{\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ half of π: the angle in a half circle is right

21q3Four corners on one circle

Four-on-a-circle technique: if A, B, C, D lie in that order round a circle, then the angles at A and C add to π, and so do the angles at B and D.

\angle DAB = \tfrac12 \cdot \text{centre angle on arc } BCD @@ rim-half-centre
\angle BCD = \tfrac12 \cdot \text{centre angle on arc } DAB @@ the same, from the other side
\text{the two centre angles add to } 2\pi @@ the two arcs make the whole circle
\htmlClass{new}{\angle A + \angle C = \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ half of 2π; likewise B and D

21q4The circle inside

Incircle technique: the three lines halving a triangle’s angles meet in one point I, and the circle about I that touches one side touches all three; its radius r makes the area r times half the perimeter.

X \text{ on the line halving angle } A \iff X \text{ is equally far from } AB \text{ and } AC @@ two right triangles with a shared side and equal angles are congruent (18f)
I = \text{where the halving lines at } A \text{ and } B \text{ meet} @@ then I is equally far from all three sides
I \text{ is on the halving line at } C @@ equally far from CA and CB
\text{the circle about } I \text{ of radius } r \text{ touches each side at the foot from } I @@ tangent-meets-radius (18·cb13)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = \tfrac12 ar + \tfrac12 br + \tfrac12 cr = r \cdot \tfrac{a + b + c}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ three triangles with apex I, heights r

From here on, this is the incircle, and I its centre.

The accepted names

The rim angle is the inscribed angle and the centre angle the central angle; rim-half-centre is the inscribed angle theorem. The angle in a half circle is right by Thales’ theorem. Four points on one circle make a cyclic quadrilateral. The point I is the incentre.

Who said it first

In 21q.4.5, the merchant is Thales of Miletus (sixth century BC), as told by Diogenes Laertius, who credits the story to Pamphila. The rim-half-centre technique is Euclid, Elements III.20 and III.21; four on a circle is III.22.
