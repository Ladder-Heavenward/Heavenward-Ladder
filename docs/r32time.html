32·G · research front

Sometimes Time Has Holes, and Apparently That's Ok (II)

Sometimes time has holes, and apparently that’s ok. 29·G rebuilt mechanics with time in steps, a body at one place each tick and nowhere between, and found that the stepped statement keeps a momentum exactly and keeps the area of every patch of states, at any size of step. It found something odd too: the energy was not kept, and yet it never wandered far. Two years later a dissertation carried the stepped mechanics further, and asked what keeping area buys and what it costs. This rung gives a new way to look. A stepped spring, like a flowing one, has solutions that are turnings, but stepped ones: each tick turns them by a fixed angle. Keeping area turns out to be exactly what makes the stepped turnings stay on the circle instead of spiralling out, as the plain way of stepping does. The cost is a small error in the pace: the stepped spring turns a little faster than the real one, by an amount the turnings let us compute exactly. This page builds those three facts, and then says what the dissertation proved beyond them and what is still open.

Try a stepped turning, the kth power of a number z, in the stepped spring: z must solve z squared minus c z plus one equals zero, so the two roots multiply to one. When the step is small enough they are a turning and its flip, and the solutions go round without growing. Any straight step that keeps area has eigenvalues that multiply to one, so if they are not real they sit on the circle; the plain step has eigenvalues of size more than one and spirals out. The stepped turning goes round a little faster than the flowing one, by about one part in twenty-four times the square of the step times the speed.

32time1The Stepped Spring Turns

29·G’s stepped statement for a spring, V = ½mω²x², with ticks of length h, is x_(k+1) = c x_k − x_(k−1), with c = 2 − ω²h². Try a stepped turning: xₖ = zᵏ for a fixed complex number z.

Stepped-turning technique: if ωh < 2, every solution of the stepped spring is a sum of the stepped turnings e^(iθk) and e^(−iθk), with cos θ = 1 − ω²h²/2; they neither grow nor shrink.

z^{k+1} = c\,z^k - z^{k-1} \iff z^2 - cz + 1 = 0 @@ divide by z^(k−1); z ≠ 0
z_1z_2 = 1,\ \ z_1 + z_2 = c @@ the roots of a quadratic (8i)
|c| \lt 2 \iff \omega h \lt 2 @@ c = 2 − ω²h² lies in (−2, 2)
z = \tfrac{c}{2} \pm i\sqrt{1 - \tfrac{c^2}{4}} = e^{\pm i\theta},\ \ \cos\theta = \tfrac c2 @@ the quadratic formula; a point of the circle of size 1 (21·cb20)
\htmlClass{new}{x_k = A\,e^{i\theta k} + B\,e^{-i\theta k},\ \ \cos\theta = 1 - \tfrac{\omega^2h^2}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two starts fix A and B, and two solutions of the step make every one (27a)

With ωh > 2 the roots are real, one of them below −1, and the stepped places flip sign and grow every tick: the step is too long for the spring, as 29·G’s moves to watch out for warned.

A machine with several springs must keep ωh below 2 for its stiffest one. So the fastest tremble sets the tick, even when only the slow swing is wanted and the tremble has long died away. The stepping then creeps through a motion that is nearly still. Such a system is called stiff.

32time2Keeping Area Keeps the Circle

29·G.3 wrote the stepped statement as a map of pairs of places, (x_(k−1), x_k) ↦ (x_k, x_(k+1)), with determinant 1. For the spring the map is straight, with grid (0, 1; −1, c).

Unit-product technique: a straight step map of the plane that keeps area has two eigenvalues whose product is 1; so if they are not real, both have size 1, and every solution turns without growing.

\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det = 1 @@ the eigenvalues multiply to the determinant (27f, 27e); keeping area means |det| = 1 (28i)
\lambda_2 = \overline{\lambda_1} \text{ if they are not real} @@ the grid is real, so its roots come in flipped pairs (25e)
|\lambda_1|^2 = \lambda_1\overline{\lambda_1} = 1 @@ z z̄ = |z|²
\text{plain step: grid } \begin{pmatrix} 1 & h\\ -\omega^2h & 1\end{pmatrix},\ \ \lambda = 1 \pm i\omega h @@ 29·G.3’s plain step, its own characteristic polynomial (27f)
\htmlClass{new}{|1 \pm i\omega h| = \sqrt{1 + \omega^2h^2} \gt 1:\ \text{the plain step spirals out}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its determinant is 1 + ω²h², not 1

Follow 29·G’s Moon as a spring about the Earth with one-day ticks: ω = 2π/27.32 per day, so ωh = 0.230. The plain step grows the swing by √(1 + 0.230²) = 1.026 a day, which doubles it in a month. The stepped statement keeps it, for ever. The difference is the determinant, and nothing else.

32time3The Stepped Spring Is a Little Fast

The flowing spring turns by ωh in a tick. The stepped one turns by θ.

Stepped-pace technique: the stepped spring turns by θ = 2 arcsin(ωh/2) each tick, a little more than ωh: about ωh(1 + ω²h²/24).

\cos\theta = 1 - \tfrac{\omega^2h^2}{2} @@ the stepped-turning technique (32·G.1)
1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2 @@ the half-turn formula (21e)
\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac{\omega h}{2},\ \ \theta = 2\arcsin\tfrac{\omega h}{2} @@ θ in (0, π)
\arcsin u = u + \tfrac{u^3}{6} + \cdots @@ its series (23h)
\htmlClass{new}{\theta = \omega h\big(1 + \tfrac{\omega^2h^2}{24} + \cdots\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ u = ωh/2

The stepped Moon, with ωh = 0.230, goes round in 2π/θ = 27.26 ticks against 27.32 days: two tenths of a per cent fast. The error does not grow with time as a size; it grows as a lag, a fixed amount each turn. A stepped orbit stays an orbit, and its clock runs a little fast. Halve the tick, and the clock error falls to a quarter.

32time4What the Dissertation Adds, and What Is Open

Proved, 1996. A dissertation set the stepped mechanics of 29·G on a general footing: any stepped action, for any number of bodies and any stored energy, gives a stepped statement; every symmetry of the stepped action keeps a stepped quantity; and the stepped statement always keeps the area form of the states, the general kind of area that 29·G kept for one body. It also built stepped statements from the transforms that join one set of states to another.

Proved, in the years after. A stepped statement that keeps area keeps a nearby energy for a number of ticks that grows like e to a constant over h, and its errors are errors of pace, as in 32·G.3, not of size. Stepped statements are now the way the solar system is followed for billions of years, and the way molecules are followed in a computer.

Open. Whether a stepped statement can keep area, momentum and energy all at once, exactly, for a general body: it is proved that with a fixed tick it cannot, unless it is the exact flow, and a tick that changes from step to step is one way out, still being worked on. Open, as on 29·G: whether time in nature comes in steps.

The accepted names

The stepped turnings are the discrete normal modes of the leapfrog (Störmer–Verlet) method, and θ its numerical frequency; the stepped-pace technique gives its phase error, of order h². The unit-product technique is the eigenvalue property of symplectic matrices (for the plane, determinant 1), which keeps linear symplectic methods on the unit circle; ωh < 2 is the leapfrog stability limit. The plain step is explicit Euler. The general area kept is the symplectic form, and stepped statements built from generating functions are symplectic integrators by generating functions. The open problem is the Ge–Marsden theorem: a fixed-step symplectic integrator that also conserves energy exactly is, for a general system, the exact flow up to a reparametrisation of time.

Who said it first

The dissertation this page is named for is James W. Gilliam, Lagrangian and Symplectic Techniques in Discrete Mechanics, University of California, Riverside (1996), written under John Baez, after their paper of 1994 (29·G). Zhong Ge and Jerrold Marsden proved in 1988 that a fixed-step method cannot keep area and energy at once for a general system. Ernst Hairer, Christian Lubich and Gerhard Wanner gathered the theory in Geometric Numerical Integration (2002). Jack Wisdom and Matthew Holman’s stepped map of 1991 made runs of a hundred million years cheap, and with it Gerald Sussman and Wisdom confirmed in 1992 that the inner solar system is chaotic, as Jacques Laskar had found in 1989.

A proof that is not allowed

“The stepped statement keeps area, so it keeps energy, since for a spring energy is area.” Not allowed: a patch of states keeps its area while it is turned and sheared, and energy is the size of one state, not a patch. The stepped spring keeps patches and moves each state at a slightly wrong pace, so the energy wobbles, as 29·G found.
