How Does a Phone Pack Bits Into a Wave?

How does a phone pack bits into a wave? A radio wave is a steady swing millions of times a second. To carry a voice or a film, something about the swing must change: its size, its timing, or both, in ways the receiver can read back. This page rides a tone on a carrier by 21’s sum formulas, sees the sidebands it makes in the spectrum (32), places the swing’s size and timing as a point in the plane with 26’s turning arrows, finds how noise’s bell curve (30j) makes errors, and weighs more points against 30·comp’s capacity.

A carrier swing cos(ωt) multiplied by a slower signal splits into two nearby frequencies, the sidebands. Any swing at ω is Re(s e^(iωt)) for a complex number s, so a symbol is a point in the plane. Noise blurs each point by a bell curve; an error is a blur past half the gap to a neighbour. More points carry more bits per symbol but sit closer, so they err more, up to the line’s capacity.

35comp1A Tone Riding a Carrier, by the Sum Formulas

Joy designs radios. The simplest way to carry a tone of frequency ω_m on a carrier of frequency ω_c is to let the tone swell and shrink the carrier’s size.

(1 + m\cos\omega_m t)\cos\omega_c t @@ m the depth, at most 1
= \cos\omega_c t + \tfrac{m}{2}\cos(\omega_c + \omega_m)t + \tfrac{m}{2}\cos(\omega_c - \omega_m)t @@ the sum formulas (21d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the carrier plus two new frequencies, just above and below it}} @@

From here on, on this page, a carrier is the steady swing, and riding on it means changing its size or timing with the message.

Breaking the result into frequencies (32e) shows the carrier and two sidebands. A voice holds many tones up to about 4,000 a second, so each sideband is a copy of the voice’s whole spectrum, one flipped.

35comp3Points on a Circle: the Constellation

Any swing at the carrier frequency, whatever its size and timing, is the real part of s e^(iω_c t) for one complex number s (26·eng). Its size is |s| and its timing the turn of s. So each symbol sent, held for a short time, is a point s in the plane.

patternpointsbits a symbol
4 points on a circle42
8 points on a circle83
16 points in a 4 by 4 square164
64 points in an 8 by 8 square646

Constellation technique: send each group of bits as one point s in the plane, by setting the carrier’s size and timing; M points carry log₂ M bits a symbol. The receiver measures s and picks the nearest point.

35comp4Noise and the Bell Curve’s Tail Give the Bit Error Rate

Noise adds a small random arrow to each received point, with each part spread as a bell curve of spread σ (30j). An error happens when noise carries the point past halfway to a neighbour.

\text{neighbours } \sqrt 2 \text{ apart; halfway } = 0.71 @@ 
\text{chance noise passes it, one way: tail beyond } 0.71/0.2 = 3.5 \text{ spreads} \approx 2 \times 10^{-4} @@ the bell curve’s tail (30j)
\htmlClass{new}{16 \text{ points, same power: halfway} \approx 0.32,\ \text{tail beyond } 1.6 \text{ spreads} \approx 0.06} @@ hundreds of times worse

35comp5More Points Carry More Bits and More Errors

More points carry more bits but crowd closer. 30·comp’s capacity says the most any pattern can carry without error, with enough clever coding.

\text{signal to noise} = \dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2} = 12.5 @@ power 1, noise 2σ² in the two parts
\log_2(1 + 12.5) \approx 3.75 \text{ bits a symbol} @@ capacity (30·comp)
\htmlClass{new}{4 \text{ points } (2 \text{ bits}) \text{ is safe here; } 16 (4 \text{ bits}) \text{ asks for more than the line allows}} @@

Each doubling of the points adds one bit a symbol but pushes the points closer, so the pattern must be matched to the noise; the capacity is the ceiling no pattern can pass. A phone measures its signal’s noise many times a second and switches between 4, 16, 64 and more points to stay just under the ceiling: close to a mast it uses many points, at the edge of coverage few. A test set that adds measured noise shows the error rate rising exactly along the bell curve’s tail as the noise grows.

Accepted names

Changing the size is amplitude modulation, AM; points in the plane are a constellation diagram; 4 points on a circle are QPSK, and square patterns QAM. The error chance is the bit error rate; switching patterns is adaptive modulation; capacity is the Shannon-Hartley limit; many narrow carriers are OFDM.

Who said it first

Claude Shannon set out the capacity of a noisy line in 1948.
