18d · proof

Circles

A circle is the set of points at one distance from a centre, so its equation is the distance formula squared. This step proves what the definition gives. The points as far from one point as from another make a line, so three points not on one line lie on exactly one circle. A line meets a circle in at most two points, and when it meets it once it is perpendicular to the radius there. Two circles meet exactly when their radii and the distance between their centres could be the sides of a triangle, which proves that such a triangle exists.

A circle is every point whose squared horizontal and vertical gaps from the centre add to the squared radius. The points equally far from two points make the line through their midpoint, perpendicular to the segment between them. A line meets a circle twice, once or never as its distance from the centre is less than, equal to or more than the radius. Two circles meet when the distance between their centres is no more than the sum of the radii and no less than their difference.

18d1The Definition, and Its Equation

From here on, for a point O = (p, q) and a real r > 0, the circle with centre O and radius r is {P : d(P, O) = r}, the set of (x, y) with (x − p)² + (y − q)² = r². A segment from O to a point of the circle is also called a radius; a segment joining two points of the circle is a chord, and a chord through O is a diameter, of length 2r.

The equation is the definition squared: both sides are not negative, so squaring loses nothing (powers-keep-order). Expanded, it is x² + y² − 2px − 2qy + (p² + q² − r²) = 0. Circle-equation technique: x² + y² + Dx + Ey + F = 0 is a circle exactly when D² + E² − 4F > 0, with centre (−D/2, −E/2) and radius √(D² + E² − 4F)/2. When D² + E² − 4F = 0 it is one point, and when it is negative it holds no point. It is completing the square twice (8i): (x + D/2)² + (y + E/2)² = (D² + E² − 4F)/4.

18d2Equally Far From Two Points

Perpendicular-bisector technique: for points A ≠ B, the set {X : d(X, A) = d(X, B)} is the line 2(B − A) · X = |B|² − |A|². It passes through the midpoint M of AB and is perpendicular to AB. Its accepted name is the perpendicular bisector of AB.

d(X, A) = d(X, B) \iff |X - A|^2 = |X - B|^2 @@ both not negative (powers-keep-order)
|X|^2 - 2A \cdot X + |A|^2 = |X|^2 - 2B \cdot X + |B|^2 @@ right-triangle with u = X, v = −A; likewise B
\iff 2(B - A) \cdot X = |B|^2 - |A|^2 @@ adding-keeps-answers: the |X|² cancels, and what is left is of the first degree
B - A \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{a line with normal } B - A @@ 18c; so perpendicular to AB
2(B - A) \cdot \tfrac{A + B}{2} = |B|^2 - |A|^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ the dot technique: M is on it

Circle-through-three technique: three points A, B, C that are not collinear lie on exactly one circle. Its centre is where the perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC meet. Three collinear points lie on no circle. Its accepted name is the circumscribed circle of the triangle ABC, or circumcircle, with centre the circumcentre.

\text{bisectors of } AB, BC:\ \text{normals } B - A,\ C - B @@ perpendicular-bisector
C - B = k(B - A) \Rightarrow A, B, C \text{ on one line} @@ the line through B with direction B − A (two-descriptions-agree); three points of a line are collinear (part-of-the-way)
\text{not collinear} \Rightarrow D \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{one common point } O @@ two-lines-meet
d(O, A) = d(O, B) = d(O, C) = r @@ perpendicular-bisector twice; so all three are on the circle with centre O
\begin{aligned}&\text{any circle through } A, B,\\ &\quad C \text{ has its centre on both bisectors}\end{aligned} @@ perpendicular-bisector: its centre is equally far from each
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so its centre is } O \text{ and its radius } r} \quad \blacksquare @@ two-lines-meet: only one common point; collinear: D = 0 and the bisectors are parallel

18d3A Line and a Circle

Line-meets-circle technique: let a line ℓ be at distance δ from the centre O of a circle of radius r, with F the foot of the perpendicular from O. If δ < r, ℓ meets the circle in exactly two points, F ± √(r² − δ²) e, for a direction e of ℓ with |e| = 1. If δ = r, only at F. If δ > r, nowhere. When it meets only at F, ℓ is perpendicular to the radius OF. This e is not 17e's number e.

X \in \ell:\ \ X = F + se,\ \ |e| = 1 @@ two-descriptions-agree, with the direction scaled to length 1 (distance-laws)
d(O, X)^2 = \delta^2 + s^2 @@ right-triangle: O − F ⊥ e (nearest-point)
d(O, X) = r \iff s^2 = r^2 - \delta^2 @@ ﹀ d(O, X), squared
\delta \lt r:\ s = \pm\sqrt{r^2 - \delta^2};\ \ \delta = r:\ s = 0;\ \ \delta \gt r:\ \text{none} @@ square-root; a square is never negative (square-never-negative)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one point} \Rightarrow \text{it is } F,\ \text{and } OF \perp \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ nearest-point

A line meeting a circle twice has the accepted name a secant, and one meeting it once a tangent line, which touches the circle at F. The chord a secant cuts has length 2√(r² − δ²).

18d4Two Circles, and Which Triangles Exist

Two-circles-meet technique: let circles have centres O₁ ≠ O₂, radii r₁ and r₂, and d = d(O₁, O₂). They meet in exactly two points when |r₁ − r₂| < d < r₁ + r₂, in exactly one when d = r₁ + r₂ or d = |r₁ − r₂|, and nowhere otherwise.

|X - O_1|^2 - |X - O_2|^2 = r_1^2 - r_2^2 @@ a common point satisfies both; subtract
2(O_2 - O_1) \cdot X = r_1^2 - r_2^2 + |O_2|^2 - |O_1|^2 @@ as in perpendicular-bisector: a line ℓ, with normal O₂ − O₁
\text{common points} = \ell \cap \text{circle } 1 @@ each step runs back
\delta = \tfrac{|d^2 + r_1^2 - r_2^2|}{2d} @@ nearest-point from O₁ to ℓ: |O₂|² − 2O₁ · O₂ + |O₁|² = d², so the top is |d² + r₁² − r₂²| and the normal has length 2d
r_1^2 - \delta^2 = \tfrac{(r_1 + r_2 - d)(d + r_1 - r_2)(d - r_1 + r_2)(d + r_1 + r_2)}{4d^2} @@ the difference of squares (difference-of-squares), twice
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{two points} \iff \text{each of } d, r_1, r_2 \text{ is less }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{than the sum of the other two}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ line-meets-circle; the last factor is positive; at most one of the other three can be ≤ 0 (triangle in the plane)

So the converse of the triangle inequality is true. Which-triangles technique: three positive lengths are the sides of a triangle exactly when each is less than the sum of the other two. Put two corners one of the lengths apart, and about them take circles whose radii are the other two: by two-circles-meet they meet in two points, either of which is the third corner, and it is not on the line through the other two, since then equality-on-the-segment would make one side the sum of the others. A Greek geometer, about 300 BCE, did this in the twenty-second proposition of his Elements, with a compass.

(0, 0), (4, 0), (0, 2):\ \ x = 2,\ y = 1 @@ circle-through-three: the bisectors of the two legs; radius √5

18d5A Circle Inside a Triangle

For a triangle ABC, let e₁ = (B − A)/|B − A| and e₂ = (C − A)/|C − A|. The line through A with direction e₁ + e₂ has the accepted name the angle bisector at A. Inscribed-circle technique: every point of the bisector at A is equally far from the lines AB and AC. The bisectors at A and at B meet in one point I, equally far, say ρ, from all three side lines, and the circle with centre I and radius ρ touches each of them once.

X = A + t(e_1 + e_2):\ \ \text{distance to } AB = |t|\,|e_2 - (e_1 \cdot e_2)e_1| @@ nearest-point: take away the part of X − A along e₁
|e_2 - (e_1 \cdot e_2)e_1|^2 = 1 - (e_1 \cdot e_2)^2 @@ right-triangle, with |e₁| = |e₂| = 1
\text{distance to } AC = |t|\sqrt{1 - (e_1 \cdot e_2)^2} @@ the same, with e₁ and e₂ exchanged: equal
\text{bisectors at } A, B \text{ are not parallel} @@ else the triangle's sides AB, AC, BC would fall on one line; two-lines-meet gives I
\htmlClass{new}{I \text{ is } \rho \text{ from all three lines}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first lines at A and at B; line-meets-circle: each side line touches the circle once

The circle has the accepted name the inscribed circle, or incircle, and I the incentre. Four points that lie on one circle make a cyclic quadrilateral; by circle-through-three they do exactly when the perpendicular bisectors of its four sides all pass through one point.

Who said it first

The Greek geometer of 18d4 is Euclid, whose Elements (about 300 BCE) builds the triangle from three given lengths in Book I, proposition 22, and the circles of 18d5 in Books I and IV.

Euclid defines a circle as a plane figure bounded by one line, such that all the straight lines falling on it from one point inside the figure are equal to one another; and that point is called the centre.

Euclid, Elements, Book I, definitions 15 and 16 (about 300 BCE); paraphrase

In 18d.4.5, the philosopher who reports the refutation is Aristotle, the thinker who refuted the geometers is Protagoras, and the philosopher who answered is Plato.
