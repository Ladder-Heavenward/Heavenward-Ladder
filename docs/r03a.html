3a · proof

Earning outputs

Rows let you work out what the givens already said, and cases let you follow each way things could be. Neither lets you try out a way that nothing handed you. This step does: you may suppose. A supposition is a row you set in for yourself, a thing tried and not claimed, and the rows worked under it make a box. When the box closes, its rows go with it and it leaves one output behind: a promise, from what was tried to what it reached, or a denial, when what was tried reached the totally false. For the first time an argument can earn a promise or a denial instead of being handed one. Boxes open inside boxes, and the step earns the techniques of rung 1’s words that a single line could never reach, among them “neither”, “not both” and the turned-about promise. It shows the one step that still cannot be taken. It ends with what boxes do to rung 2’s sets, and with the supposition this rung is about: suppose a collection goes on forever.

To earn a promise, suppose its condition, reach its result, and close the box. To earn a denial, suppose the thing, reach the totally false, and close the box. Boxes close latest first, and nothing inside a closed box is in reach.

3a1Supposing

A case was handed to you: an or-row offered its options, and each got rows of its own. Sometimes no or-row offers the way you want to look at. You want to try a thing out, see where it goes, and come back with a report, without ever claiming the thing was so. People do this whenever they say “suppose…”.

A supposition is a set-in row you choose for yourself: a statement tried, and not claimed. It is written with the word “suppose”. You may suppose anything you can say, about any name you like. Supposing “u is a unicorn” invents no unicorn: u is only a name, as in 0a, and the supposition asks what would follow if u were one.

Under a supposition, the rows are worked as a case’s rows were (2a5): they may lean on the supposition and on any row above it at the margin. Each row under it is earned, but only as far as the supposition. It is claimed only as reached from it.

3a2A box

A supposition and the rows worked under it make a box. The box opens with the supposition and closes when you stop working under it. A box cannot stay open: an argument ends with nothing left only supposed.

When a box closes, every row inside it goes with it. A row outside may never lean on a row inside a closed box, because that row leaned on something only tried. What a closed box leaves behind is its output, and the next steps say what that may be.

Rows inside an open box may lean on any row above them that is still in reach: the givens, the axioms that stand behind every argument, any technique already earned, and any row above them inside a box still open. The cases of 2a were a kind of box all along: a case is a box that an or-row opens for you. Saying “case” says that the thing in hand is a member of one of the ways the or-row offers: R is a member of S, as when 2b set in t ∈ A. A supposition opens the same kind of box with no or-row handed to you. Saying “suppose S” is really saying “S or not S”, and working only its first case. The box never comes back to the margin from both cases, so it never needs “S or not S” to be earned. Rung 2 kept that detail out of the way.

3a3Prove an if

Prove an if: open a box by supposing P; reach Q inside it; close it; the output is P ⇒ Q. This makes an “if”. It is the box’s report: what the trying showed, said as reached from the supposition, so it leans on nothing that was only tried.

Given: if it rains, the street is wet. Suppose it rains. Carry the if through: the street is wet. Add an option: the street is wet or the gutter overflows. Close the box. The output is an if: if it rains, then the street is wet or the gutter overflows.

Once the box is closed, you do not have “it rains”, and you do not have “the street is wet or the gutter overflows”. You have only the if that joins them. A promise is a statement about what follows, not a claim that anything has happened. On rung 1 a promise had to be given; here, for the first time, a promise is earned.

3a4Refute

On rung 1 a line that held a statement and its denial equalled ⊥, and stopped there: which statement to give up was an output the line could not offer. A box can offer it. Refute: open a box by supposing P; reach ⊥ inside it; close it; the output is ¬P. The supposition is what the box tried, so the blame for ⊥ falls on it.

Mia is in class: that is given. If Mia is out sick, Mia is not in class: given too. Suppose Mia is out sick. Carry the if through: Mia is not in class. That clashes with the first given, so ⊥. Close the box: Mia is not out sick.

Refute ends only at a statement that begins with ¬. It denies the box’s first row and nothing else: the givens were never tried, so they keep standing.

3a5What an empty row can reach

An argument may start on an empty row, with no givens at all. Its first row is a supposition, and nothing stands at the margin until that box is closed. Nothing is given, but the argument is not working from nothing: the axioms stand behind it, and every move uses a tech the ladder allows.

What a box reaches with nothing given is so however things are. “If Rex is a puppy, Rex is a dog” is one: suppose Rex is a puppy, unpack, close. Because an argument reached it with nothing given, you may write it in any argument at all, with nothing above it. Writing it there uses a tech that needs nothing, and the ladder calls that tech a technique from nothing. 1e’s tables check such a statement row by row; an argument with nothing given earns it without checking a single row.

3a6Boxes in boxes

A box may be opened while another is still open. The inner box leans on both: its rows may lean on anything the outer box’s rows could, and on the outer box’s rows above it. Boxes close latest first, each with its own output. The output is written inside the box around it, and that box carries on with the output among its rows. However many boxes are opened, the argument ends with all of them closed.

Look at a deep argument from outside. At the margin stand only the givens and the foot. Everything else is machinery, inside boxes. Givens and foot outside, machinery inside: that is the shape of nearly every argument on the ladder from here on.

The order matters when something clashes. Suppose Ben is a bachelor; inside that, suppose Ben is married. A bachelor is an unmarried man, so the two clash. The blame falls on the inner box, “Ben is married”, and it is refuted. The bachelor box carries on with that denial among its rows. If something totally false is reached again later, the blame falls on the bachelor box, now the latest still open.

3a7Promises chain

On rung 1 a line holding P, P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R ran along to R. A box makes the promise itself. Chain technique for If: from P ⇒ Q and Q ⇒ R, write P ⇒ R. Suppose P. Carry the first if through: Q. Carry the second: R. Close the box: P ⇒ R.

A long chain is the technique made again on its own result, as 0b7 chained samenesses. If it rains the street is wet; if the street is wet Ann wears boots; so if it rains, Ann wears boots.

3a8Not, as an if

“Ann is not at home” and “if Ann is at home, then totally false” say the same thing. Not-as-if technique: ¬P and P ⇒ ⊥ each give the other.

One way. Start from ¬P. Suppose P: it clashes with ¬P, so ⊥. Close the box: P ⇒ ⊥. The other way. Start from P ⇒ ⊥. Suppose P. Carry the if through: ⊥. Close the box by refute: ¬P.

So refute is nothing but prove-an-if aimed at ⊥, and clash is carry-through aimed at ⊥. A denial is a promise of the totally false.

3a9Free ifs

Free-if technique: from ¬P you may write P ⇒ Q, for any Q; from Q you may write P ⇒ Q, for any P. You already use the first kind when you say “if pigs can fly, I’ll eat my hat”. The if is safe because its condition is false.

From a denied condition: start from ¬P. Suppose P. It clashes with ¬P: totally false. Everything is permitted, so Q. Close the box: P ⇒ Q. The Q itself never leaves the box; only the if does, and that if is empty, since carrying it through would need P. From a result you have: start from Q. Suppose P. Q is still in reach. Close the box: P ⇒ Q.

A promise is only ever broken by its condition being met and its promise failing. If the condition cannot be met, or the promise is already kept, nothing can break it. 1e’s if machine already said so in its rows; the box now earns it.

3a10Making an exactly-when

An exactly-when comes out of two boxes, one for each direction, joined after both are closed. Take (P ∧ Q) ⇔ (Q ∧ P). Suppose P ∧ Q; keep both parts; join them the other way about; close: (P ∧ Q) ⇒ (Q ∧ P). Suppose Q ∧ P; keep, join; close: (Q ∧ P) ⇒ (P ∧ Q). Join the two ifs, and pack ⇔.

The second box cannot use the rows of the first: when the first closed, its rows went with it, and only its if remains. So each direction is earned on its own, and the join waits until both ifs stand at the margin.

3a11Not-not in

Not-not-in technique: from P, you may write ¬¬P. If Ann is at home, then it is not so that Ann is not at home, and nobody would argue.

3a12The step you cannot take

Now try the other way, from ¬¬P to P. Refute ends only at a statement that begins with ¬. Clash needs a statement and its denial, and you have only ¬¬P. Everything permitted needs ⊥. So no tech yet takes ¬¬P to P. Rung 7 closes the gap. Until then, an argument that reaches ¬¬P stops there and says so.

Hear ¬P as “P leads to something totally false”, as 3a8 made exact. Then ¬¬P says that “P leads to something totally false” itself leads to something totally false. Nobody has seen Ann; all that is known is that “Ann is not at home” cannot stand. That is plainly weaker than seeing her.

3a13Neither

Ann is neither at the bakery nor at the library. Neither technique: from ¬(P ∨ Q), write ¬P ∧ ¬Q; and from ¬P ∧ ¬Q, write ¬(P ∨ Q).

From ¬(P ∨ Q): suppose P; add an option, P ∨ Q; clash; refute: ¬P. The same for Q, then join: ¬P ∧ ¬Q.

Back again, from ¬P ∧ ¬Q: keep both parts. Suppose P ∨ Q, and set in its two cases inside the box. The case P clashes with ¬P and the case Q with ¬Q, so both reach ⊥, and ⊥ comes back to the box’s margin. Refute: ¬(P ∨ Q). Cases inside a box: 2a’s rows and this step’s boxes, working together. 1f’s rulebook named this rewrite; here it is earned both ways.

3a14Not both

From ¬P ∨ ¬Q you can get ¬(P ∧ Q). Suppose P ∧ Q, and keep both parts. Set in the cases of the or-row: each clashes, so ⊥. Refute: ¬(P ∧ Q).

But from ¬(P ∧ Q), “not both”, no tech yet writes ¬P ∨ ¬Q, “P fails or Q fails”. The missing direction is a cousin of the step you cannot take (3a12), and it is settled in the same place, rung 7. Ann and Ben cannot both be guilty: that is “not both”. It does not hand you an innocent suspect. “Ann is innocent or Ben is innocent” points to one of them, and “not both” never pointed.

3a15Turn and deny

Turn-and-deny technique for If: from P ⇒ Q, write ¬Q ⇒ ¬P. From “if it rains, the street is wet”, write “if the street is not wet, it did not rain”.

Its argument is a box inside a box. Suppose the street is not wet. Inside that, suppose it rains. Carry the if through: the street is wet. That clashes with “the street is not wet”: ⊥. Close the inner box by refute: it did not rain. Close the outer one: if the street is not wet, it did not rain. Nothing used what rain or streets are, so it holds for any P and Q.

On rung 1 the same three rows sat in one line and equalled ⊥, with nothing to say which to give up. The boxes say it: the inner supposition takes the blame.

3a16Where the turn back stops

Try to turn it back: from ¬Q ⇒ ¬P, get P ⇒ Q. Suppose P. Inside, suppose ¬Q. Carry the if through: ¬P. That clashes with P: ⊥. Close the inner box by refute: ¬¬Q. And there it stops, at the step you cannot take.

3a17Boxes and sets

Rung 2 earned “every member of A is in B” by a case: a fresh name t, set in as t ∈ A and worked to t ∈ B. A box lets the same work be said as a promise. Suppose t ∈ A; reach t ∈ B; close the box: t ∈ A ⇒ t ∈ B. Since t was fresh, that if holds of every thing: ∀x (x ∈ A ⇒ x ∈ B). So with boxes, a part of a set is a promise about every thing, and every principle of rung 2 can be read as one. Boxes also finish 2b’s work on the not: rows showed that ∀x ¬P(x) cannot stand beside ∃x P(x), and refute now writes the rewrite, ¬∃x P(x) and ∀x ¬P(x) each giving the other; the counterexample gives ¬∀x P(x) from ∃x ¬P(x).

Boxes also finish 2d14’s outsider set. Rows hold r, with r ∈ r ⇔ r ∉ r, and the case r ∈ r closed. Now open a box on r ∈ r instead: rightward, r ∉ r; clash; close the box and refute: r ∉ r. Carry the leftward direction through on that: r ∈ r. It clashes with r ∉ r, and the ⊥ does not mention r, so it comes out of r’s case. So no set has as its members exactly the things that are not members of themselves: ¬∃r ∀x (x ∈ r ⇔ x ∉ x). Inside a set u you have, the same box gives rᵤ ∉ rᵤ, and a second box on rᵤ ∈ u closes too: rᵤ ∉ u, as 2d15 said.

A box can also suppose what rows could never be handed. Suppose a set goes on forever, each member followed by another. No given hands you such a set, and no case of an or offers it. A box can try it, see what follows, and report. That is the work of this rung.

The basement · there if you want it

Accepted names

A box has the accepted name subproof; opening one is making an assumption, and closing it discharges the assumption. Prove an if is conditional proof, or ⇒-introduction; refute is proof by contradiction of a denial, or ¬-introduction. The chain technique for If is the hypothetical syllogism; turn and deny is the contrapositive. Neither and not both are two of De Morgan’s laws. Free ifs are the paradoxes of material implication. A statement reached with nothing given is a theorem of logic, or a tautology when it is checked by tables.

Who said it first

Arguing from assumptions that are made and then discharged is natural deduction, published by Stanisław Jaśkowski and Gerhard Gentzen in 1934. Augustus De Morgan stated his laws in 1847, though medieval logicians, William of Ockham among them, already knew them in words. The missing step from ¬¬P to P is where L. E. J. Brouwer’s intuitionists part company with classical logic.

The basement · foundation · there if you want it

Foundation

Every set this rung has built was built in stages. It started from the empty set, by gathering and merging and sifting and taking subsets, each new set made from sets already in hand. Nothing built that way is a member of itself, or a member of a member of itself, or the start of a chain of memberships that runs down forever. But nothing so far forbids such things. The axioms laid down would all be kept in a world that contained a set whose only member was itself. This step lays down an axiom that is false in such worlds, in words and in marks. Its form never splits a statement into “so or not so”: a condition that passes from the members of a set to the set itself is so of every set. From it the step proves the techniques the ladder needs. No set is a member of itself, no set is its own gathering, membership never loops, and there is no set of all sets. The last of these, no set of all sets, it proves again by another route. The earlier proof, the outsider sift, needs no foundation at all, and the difference between the proofs is worth seeing. Last, it says why the axiom's more familiar form, which speaks of a member at the bottom, has to wait.

A condition that climbs from the members of a set to the set is so of every set. To forbid a loop, name the condition “in no such loop”, show that it climbs, and carry foundation through: it is so of every set.

3a·f1 · The question the tools leave open

Could a set be a member of itself? Write u for it: u ∈ u. The tools never make one. Gathering builds {u, b} from u and b you already have, so {u, b} is newer than its members. Merge and sift and power set likewise take sets in hand and give a set back. Everything the rung has built sits in a stage above the stages of its members, with ∅ at the bottom.

But “the tools never make one” is not a proof that there is none. The axioms so far say what sets exist and when they are the same. They do not say that every set was made by the tools.

3a·f2 · The looped world

Picture a world containing an extra set, call it Ω, whose only member is Ω itself. Such a world would keep every axiom laid down so far. Ω = {Ω} is a perfectly good listing, the same-members axiom is content, and sifting and gathering inside such a world go on as before.

Exactly such a world was built late in the century before this one, and shown to be as consistent as the ordinary one. Call it the looped world. So nothing laid down so far can rule Ω out. If we want membership never to loop, we must say so, as an axiom.

From here on, Ω (the Greek letter omega, in its capital form) names that set of the looped world, whose only member is itself, wherever that world is spoken of. It is a name like any other letter.

Why want such an axiom? Because the ladder's objects, from rung 3 onward, are built in stages, and proofs about them climb the stages: what is so of the members is so of the set made from them. They need to know that the climbing reaches every set. No set may be left over, perched on a loop or on a chain that never stops going down. The claim that the climbing reaches every set is the axiom of foundation.

3a·f3 · Passing up

If a condition passes to a set whenever it is so of every member of that set, it is so of every set.

Take a condition φ, a statement with a gap in 2d's sense.

Say that φ passes up when, for any set u, if every member of u meets φ, then u meets φ too.

Example: for φ “x ∉ x”, passing up means: if no member of u is in itself, then u is not in itself.

Foundation says: a condition that passes up is so of every set, with none left over.

3a·f4 · Watching the climb

Why lay it down? Here is the picture behind it, as motivation and not as proof: the axiom is laid down, never proved. Watch a condition that passes up climb. The empty set has no members, so “every member of ∅ meets φ” is true vacuously (2i's word), and since φ passes up, φ(∅) is so. Now {∅}: its only member meets φ, so {∅} does. Then {∅, {∅}}, and every set built from those, stage by stage. Each set the tools make is made from sets already reached, so it is reached too. A set the climb never reached would have to have a member never reached, and that member another, and so on down without stopping. That is a loop, or a chain that runs down forever. Foundation says there is no such set. Every set is reached.

A cat, which is not a set, has no members either, so in the climb it stands where ∅ stands.

Notice what the axiom never says. It never says of a statement that it is so or not so, and it names no member of any set. It is a statement about every set, and the proofs below are all of the same shape: name a condition, show that it passes up, and carry the axiom through.

3a·f5 · The axiom, in marks

From here on, “φ passes up” means:

Read it “whenever every member of u meets φ, u meets φ”.

Read it: if φ passes up, then φ is so of every set. Like the sift axiom, it is a pattern, an axiom for each condition. The condition may mention other things you have, and may have its own every or some inside it.

3a·f6 · Using it: the hypothesis about the members

To use it, begin by naming the condition. Then prove that it passes up, with 3a’s boxes.

Write “let u be anything” and “suppose ∀x (x ∈ u ⇒ φ(x))”, and work inside until you reach φ(u). Then prove an if, and speak of everything.

Call the supposed line the hypothesis about the members. It speaks of the members of u, never of u itself.

Then carry the foundation axiom through, and use the every on whatever set you like.

There is no separate start. At ∅ the hypothesis about the members says nothing, so the passing up must prove φ(∅) with nothing to lean on. A proof that passing up is true at every u covers ∅ along with the rest.

3a·f7 · No set is a member of itself

No-self-membership technique: no set is a member of itself.

The sketch. The condition is x ∉ x. If u were in u, u would be one of its own members, so the hypothesis about the members would say u ∉ u.

In order. Name the condition: x ∉ x. Let u be anything, and suppose every member of u is not a member of itself. Suppose, aiming to refute, that u ∈ u. Then u is one of u’s members, so by the hypothesis about the members, u ∉ u. Clash.

So drop the supposition: u is not a member of itself. That is true for any set u: if every member of u is not a member of itself, then u is not either. So the condition passes up. Carry the foundation axiom through: no set is a member of itself.

3a·f8 · What no-self-membership settles

Look at where the hypothesis about the members was used: with u itself, which is a member of u only inside the supposition being refuted. The passing up is pure box work, from 3a; only the last line leans on the axiom. Nothing about u was used, so no set is a member of itself. 3a·f9 · A set is not its own gathering

Rung 3 will need this one by name. Not-its-own-gathering technique: no set is its own gathering. In marks, u ≠ {u}. If u were {u}, then since u ∈ {u}, u would be a member of itself.

In order: u is in {u}, by the listing. Suppose u = {u}, until we drop it. Write u where {u} stood: u is in u. That clashes with the no-self-membership technique. Drop the supposition: u is not {u}.

For the empty set the not-its-own-gathering technique needed no foundation: ∅ ≠ {∅} by the same-members axiom backwards, since {∅} has a member and ∅ has none. For a set with members, the proof needs foundation. The looped world's Ω = {Ω} is exactly the counterexample that world allows.

3a·f10 · Membership does not loop

No-loop technique: no two sets are each a member of the other. The condition to climb is “x is in no such loop, with any partner”: ∀y ¬(x ∈ y ∧ y ∈ x). Suppose every member of u meets it, and suppose a loop u ∈ b ∈ u. Then b is a member of u, so b is in no loop with any partner, and in particular not with u. But b ∈ u and u ∈ b is exactly such a loop.

Here it is in order. Name the condition: x is in no loop with any partner. Let u be anything, and suppose every member of u meets the condition. Let b be anything. Suppose u ∈ b and b ∈ u, until we drop it.

b is in u, so b meets the condition: b is in no loop with any partner. Take u as the partner: not both b ∈ u and u ∈ b. But that is what we supposed, turned about. Clash. Drop the supposition: u and b are not in a loop.

b was anything, so u meets the condition. So the condition passes up. Carry the foundation axiom through: every set meets it. Use that every on u: u is in no loop with any partner. Use the every inside on b: not both u ∈ b and b ∈ u.

3a·f11 · Why every partner

Why the every over y inside the condition? Because the hypothesis about the members is used with b. What the hypothesis must say of b is that b is in no loop with u. But u is the set the condition was used on, not a fixed partner, so the partner must be free. The partner changes when the hypothesis is used, so the condition must speak of every partner.

A loop through more links is refuted the same way. Take u ∈ b ∈ c ∈ u. The condition is “x is in no loop of that shape”, with an every for each of the other links: ∀y ∀z ¬(x ∈ y ∧ y ∈ z ∧ z ∈ x). Use the hypothesis about the members with c, the link just inside u. That turns the loop round to start at c, and c ∈ u ∈ b ∈ c clashes with it.

A chain running down forever, … ∈ c ∈ b ∈ u, is refuted the same way once such a chain can be written as an object: the condition is “no chain runs down forever from x”. That waits for 4d, which writes a chain as an object, and 7·cb4 closes it. What proofs need now is the axiom as written, and the techniques no-self-membership, not-its-own-gathering and no-loop.

A set from which no chain of membership runs down forever has the accepted name well-founded.

3a·f12 · No set of all sets, by the outsider sift

No-set-of-all-sets technique: no set has everything as a member. Suppose a set v with everything in it: ∀x (x ∈ v). 2d set up and 3a17 finished the outsider argument without foundation; run inside v, it refutes v; foundation refutes v in two lines, on 3a·f13, and the earlier proof is the deeper one. Here it is in words. 2e held “there is no set of everything” in trust, and with this argument that flag is closed.

Suppose some set has everything as a member, until we drop it. Call it v. Sift v by the outsider condition: r is the set of members of v that are not members of themselves. The sift is legal, since v is a set in hand.

Suppose r ∈ r, until we drop it. Then r passes the sift, so r ∉ r. Clash. Drop it: r ∉ r. But r is in v, since everything is. So r is in v and not in itself, and it passes the sift: r ∈ r. Clash again.

That clash does not mention v, so the some closes. Drop the first supposition: no set has everything as a member.

Only the word everything holds everything. No set holds all sets: sift it for sets not members of themselves, and it breaks. And “everything is everything” is true, but leaves no room to make guesses in.

3a·f13 · No set of all sets, by foundation, and what that shows

And by foundation. Suppose some set has everything as a member. Call it v. v is a thing, so v is in v. The no-self-membership technique says v is not in v. Clash. So no set has everything as a member.

Now set them side by side. The proof by foundation is shorter and leans on an axiom. The proof by the outsider sift leans only on the sift axiom and the boxes of 3a. So “no set of all sets” does not need foundation.

Does “no set of all sets” follow from foundation? Yes, at once, as the second argument shows. Does foundation follow from it? No, and here is how to show that: build a world where “no set of all sets” is true and foundation is false.

The looped world is such a world. It has Ω = {Ω}, so foundation fails there: the condition x ∉ x passes up, by the techs alone, as no-self-membership's argument showed, and Ω breaks it. And it still has no set of all sets, because the outsider sift works there just as here.

By foundation no set is self-membered; “no set of all sets” speaks only of the universal one. Foundation is the stronger, and they come apart.

A consequence to carry: there is no “everything”. The collection of all sets is a way of talking, never a set, so it may not be sifted from and has no complement. Whenever a later page says “the set of all x such that…”, it will name the set it sifts from, or you will be able to supply it.

3a·f14 · The form with a bottom, and why it waits

Most books state foundation another way, a way later given the name “Fundierung”, grounding: every set that has a member has a member that shares no member with it. Such a member is a bottom of the set: look inside it and you find nothing of the set.

Example: in {∅, {∅}}, ∅ is a bottom: nothing of the set is inside it. {∅} is not a bottom: ∅ is in {∅} and in the set.

Once every statement is true or false, the forms say the same thing. On this ladder that is not yet so. Rung 1 left out the tech from ¬¬P to P on purpose. The form with a bottom, laid down as an axiom, would bring it back by the side door. From it, for any statement P whatever, a short argument proves P ∨ ¬P, which rung 1's techs never give.

A bottom is a member picked out of a set, and picking it out is where the “so or not so” hides. So the ladder lays down the climbing form, from which everything this rung and the next ones need can be proved. The form with a bottom waits for rung 7.

For a world you can list, nothing has to wait. In the picture the memberships among the listed sets are given, line by line, so each one is settled in 1f's word. A bottom of a listed set can be found by looking. Start at any member, and if some member of the set is inside it, step to that one. With no loops, the steps run out at a bottom. Set theorists who keep rung 1's gap, among them the builder of the looped world, take foundation in the climbing form this step uses.

3a·f16 · Running it backwards

Wanting u ∉ u, you cite the no-self-membership technique. Wanting u ≠ {u}, the not-its-own-gathering technique. Wanting ¬(u ∈ b ∧ b ∈ u), the no-loop technique.

Wanting something new of the same kind, say that no u has u ∈ b ∈ c ∈ u, the skeleton is fixed. Name the condition: x is in no such loop, with an every for each other link. Let u be anything, and suppose the hypothesis about the members. Suppose the loop, and use the hypothesis with the link just inside u. Clash, refute, close, and carry the foundation axiom through.

The art is the condition. Choose it so that what the hypothesis says of a member is exactly what the refutation needs. When the hypothesis is used with a different set from the one the condition was named for, the condition needs an every over the part that changes.

Wanting to show that some principle does not follow from others, build a world where the others are true and it is false. That is a shape of argument, not a run of lines, and the ladder will ask it of you again. Found upward; written downward.

Foundation: who said it first

The looped world is Peter Aczel’s (1988), built on his anti-foundation axiom. The form of foundation with a bottom is John von Neumann’s, later taken up by Ernst Zermelo under the name “Fundierung”. Among the set theorists who keep rung 1’s gap and take foundation in the climbing form are John Myhill and Aczel. The outsider sift is Bertrand Russell’s. The first to sort sets by whether their chains of membership end was Dmitry Mirimanoff:

Mirimanoff, in 1917, sorted sets into two kinds. In an ordinary set every chain of “is a member of”, followed downward, comes to an end. A set with a chain that never ends he called extraordinary, and he asked whether set theory should allow it.

Mirimanoff, “Les antinomies de Russell et de Burali-Forti et le problème fondamental de la théorie des ensembles” (1917); paraphrase

Foundation: accepted names

The axiom of foundation is also called the axiom of regularity.

A set from which no chain of membership runs down forever is called well-founded.
