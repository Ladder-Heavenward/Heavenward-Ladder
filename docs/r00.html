Rung 0 · theory

Thinking logically

The Ladder is not alien to you. It is what makes your language work. The only thing you need to climb it is the promise that everything you can say or sense has a home on the ladder. So the only real hurdle is finding out how the writing system turns everyday speech and observation into its funny little symbols. One foot in front of the other, one step at a time.
This is the floor the whole ladder stands on. There is nothing beneath it: no earlier rung, no school, no book you are expected to have read. What is here is the English language, the letters of the alphabet, and a very small claim that nobody can seriously deny: a thing is the same as itself. Add the plain fact that what is true of a thing is true of what is the same as it. From those two, every rung above this one will be built, with nothing smuggled in. This page says in words what the claim means and what it lets you do. The steps do the work by hand.

A thing is the same as itself. Whatever is so of a thing is so of anything that is the same as it. So a fact may be carried from a name to another name of the same thing, and from nothing else.

What this ladder is

A rule of honesty governs every page: nothing appears before it has been built. No mark is written until a line has said, in words, what the mark means. No word is used in a special sense until the page that gives it that sense. No fact is leaned on because “everybody knows it”. This is slower than the usual way of teaching, and it is the whole value of the thing. At every rung you will know exactly what you are standing on, because you put it there.

The raw material

If nothing may appear before it is built, what is there to build with on the ground floor? The English language, and the letters of the alphabet. English does a great deal of the work on every rung. It supplies “thing”, “is”, “same” and “so”. It supplies the grammar that lets us say that a cat is asleep or a town is by the sea. The ladder trusts your English completely, and it will never redefine a plain word behind your back. When it gives a word a special meaning, it says so out loud on the page that does it.

Stare too long at any part of the language and it starts to melt. For that reason the ladder says “so” a lot. It is a gesture: a vague waggling of the hands toward something that exists. “Not so” is a stiff shrug in the face of something that isn't seen to exist. The ladder does not try to say what lies behind the gesture; it builds on the gesture, and keeps every line to what it can earn.

The letters are used in a particular way: as names. In ordinary writing a letter is a piece of a word. On this ladder a letter standing alone is a name for a thing, the way “Tibbles” is a name for a cat, for as long as the page is talking about that cat; 0a2 gives the cat her letter.

What a statement is

A statement is a sentence that says something is so. What makes it a statement is that it can be judged: things either are as it says, or they are not. Commands and questions each carry a statement inside them, and 0a3 asks them to say it before they are used. The ladder's lessons are built entirely out of statements, because statements are the only sentences that can be true, and truth is what a proof delivers.

A statement can take more than one shape. It can say what a thing is, what it has, or what it does. It can also build a claim into a name, as “winged Mike” does. 0a names the four shapes and lets a claim move between them. And a statement may speak of two things at once, as “Ann lives next door to Ben” does. The sameness this rung is built on is a statement of exactly that kind.

What a question is

The lessons are built from statements. The annexes, the pages that carry the ladder out into the world, are built from another kind of sentence: the question. “Is Tibbles asleep?” claims nothing, so no argument can prove it. But it can be answered, well or badly. You look, you measure, and you make a careful guess. The annexes ask questions of that kind.

A problem is a kind of question. So is a challenge, a difficulty, or a hurdle. The history of mathematics and logic is a history of fights over questions like these. People came at the same problem from different directions. Each direction asked a different question, and they argued over which question was the right one. The ladder sets many of those fights going again, with you in the middle of them.

What “true” means here

In ordinary life a statement is true when what it says is so, and you find out by looking. The ladder cannot look. On the ladder a statement is treated as true when it stands in an argument with a reason beside it, and nothing feels obvious enough to skip that. Every truth on the ladder can be traced back, line by line, to the ground floor. 1a6 builds this in full.

What an axiom is

Trace any statement back, reason by reason, and the tracing must stop: reasons cannot go back for ever. An axiom is a statement you start from because there is nothing before it. It is laid down, and earned by showing what breaks without it, and everything else is proved from it and its fellows. The honest thing is to lay down as few as possible, choose ones that nobody who understands the words could seriously deny, and say plainly which statements are axioms and which are proved. This ladder names each axiom when it lays it down, so that an argument can cite it by name. An axiom is so in every case, whether or not any technique uses it. 0a1 lays the first one down. A challenge may put an axiom off limits; that forbids citing it, not having it.

The oldest book of geometry that has come down whole begins with a short list of things to be granted before anything is proved. Its list opens with sameness. Its word was “equal”, and it had sizes in mind; ours will be “the same”, and we have anything at all in mind. But the gesture is the one this rung makes: before proving, say what you are granting, and grant very little. The basement gives the book and its words.

What an argument is

An argument starts from guesses, statements that might be so, and ends by ruling out the ones that do not fit. In form it is statements in a row, one to a line, each with its reason, and a mark at the end that says it is complete. Laid out that way it can be checked by anyone, line by line, without trusting the writer. 1a1 says what a line is, and 4a builds the form of a proof; this page keeps to words.

The ground-floor principles

This rung lays down its claims in words. The step 0b gives each claim its mark and its name, and argues from them in words.

The claim in the title: a thing is the same as itself. Whatever thing you name, by whatever name, it is the same thing as the thing you named. Tibbles is the same as Tibbles. The cat on the mat is the same as the cat on the mat. This is so plainly true that it feels like nothing. It is laid down as an axiom: there is nothing beneath it to prove it from, and what breaks without it earns it. To ask why a thing is itself is to ask nothing, and the answer is the same for every thing whatever.

The other principle: whatever is so of a thing is so of anything that is the same as it. If the cat on the mat is the same cat as Tibbles, and Tibbles is grey, then the cat on the mat is grey. Of course it is: it is the same cat, and the cat is grey. The principle only says this plainly and in general. Any statement that is so of a thing is so of the thing under any of its names, because changing the name changes nothing about the thing. Turned the other way about, it is the test mathematics still uses: things are the same when either can be put in the place of the other and nothing so becomes not so.

Notice what the principle does not say. It does not say that things which look alike share their facts: a grey cat is not Tibbles because Tibbles is grey. It does not say that things of the same kind share their facts: every cat is a cat, and the cats differ. It speaks only of the same thing under different names. And “the same” is always the same as something; 0b3 says as what. Keeping the difference between “the same thing” and “alike” is the earliest discipline of the ladder, and 0b makes a gallery of the ways to get it wrong.

What you can do because you know them

You can carry a fact from a name to another name of the same thing. That is the whole power of the ground floor, and it is a great deal. Suppose a later rung tells you that a thing with a long description is the same as a thing with a short name. Then every fact about the long description becomes, at once, a fact about the short name. And every fact travels back the other way. That is how a later rung will be allowed to replace a piece of a long working with something that means the same. Not because “it is obviously fine”, but because the thing on the left is the same as the thing on the right.

The step 0b argues, from the ground-floor principles alone, that sameness can be turned about: if this is the same as that, then that is the same as this. It argues that sameness chains: if this is the same as that, and that is the same as the other, then this is the same as the other. Neither is an axiom. Each is a technique, and 4a proves each, one move to a line, each with its reason. The rung's conclusion shows them in that shape ahead of time, held in trust until 4a. They are the earliest techniques on the ladder and the pattern for every one after them: said in words, then in marks, then argued sound, then the end mark.

You can also refuse. Knowing exactly which swaps the principle covers, you can see when a tempting swap is not licensed. The things may merely look alike. They may only be of the same kind. Or the sentence may really be about a name rather than about the thing it names. Refusing a bad move is as much a skill as making a good one.

Rung 0 is the floor, and you are standing on it. Everything above is still ahead of you, and the ladder has no top.

The steps, and what each does

0a Names and statements builds the grammar: letters as names, and how names can overlap; what a statement is, and the sentences that do not look like statements; the way of writing a claim about a thing, with the name in a bracket, for a gap in one place or in several; the four shapes a claim can take, and how to rewrite a sentence so its claims come out one at a time; a whole statement named by a single capital, with no gap at all; and using a name against mentioning it. It ends with the ladder's rule for bringing in any new mark: a line that says “from here on, this mark means…”. Its picture lets you name things, fill gaps, and judge what you have written.

0b The same as itself builds the ground floor proper: the mark for sameness, a statement with two gaps, equal always as something; what an axiom is; the axiom that a thing is the same as itself, the axiom that a fact may be carried across a sameness, either way, and, argued in plain words, why sameness turns about and why it chains. It separates a sameness from a claim, and using a name from mentioning it, and it ends with the illegal swaps. Its picture is labelled tokens that you can declare the same and watch a fact carry across.

The form every argument takes, statements in a row, one to a line with a reason beside it, is built next, on 1a, the first step of rung 1.

Who said it first

Euclid, writing in Alexandria long ago, began his geometry with a short list of what he called common notions: things to be granted before anything is proved. His list opens with sameness, in Heath's translation:

“Things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another.”

Euclid, Elements, book I, common notion 1, trans. T. L. Heath (Cambridge, 1908)

Aristotle, who thought harder about sameness than anyone before him, said that to ask why a thing is itself is to ask nothing.

To ask why a thing is itself is to ask no real question: that a thing is itself is the answer to all such questions, and it is the same answer for every thing. Things are called the same in the strictest sense when there is a thing under several names, as a cloak and a garment.

Aristotle, Metaphysics VII.17 (1041a) and Topics I.7 (103a)

Leibniz put the test the other way about, and it is the test mathematics still uses.

Things are the same when either can be put in place of the other without any truth turning into a falsehood.

Leibniz, Non inelegans specimen demonstrandi in abstractis (c. 1687)

Accepted names: the self-sameness principle is the law of identity; the carrying principle is the indiscernibility of identicals, or Leibniz's law.
