22c · proof

Settling Without Knowing Where

To prove a list settles, it seems you must first know where. You name the limit, then show the terms come within any distance of it. But often the limit is exactly what you do not know. This step finds a way round. Look only at the terms themselves: if, far enough along, every two of them sit as close together as anyone demands, the list settles. You never have to name the place. The catch is the one from the eighth rung again. On the line with no holes this works. On the fractions alone, terms can crowd together around a hole and settle nowhere.

If, far enough along, every two terms are as close as anyone demands, the list settles. You can prove it without knowing the limit. It works only on a line with no holes, which is why the line had to be built first.

22c1Crowding

From here on, a sequence s crowds when for every ε > 0 there is N such that every m, n ≥ N have |sₘ − sₙ| < ε.

Compare the two sentences. "Settles" says each term far out is near some L. "Crowds" says every two terms far out are near each other. No L appears in it. A challenge ε is answered by a place N, and past N the terms huddle within ε of one another.

Settle-crowd technique: a sequence that settles crowds. If sₙ → L, take N with |sₙ − L| < ε/2 for n ≥ N. Then for m, n ≥ N, the triangle technique gives |sₘ − sₙ| ≤ |sₘ − L| + |L − sₙ| < ε.

22c2Crowding Is Enough

Crowd technique: on the line with no holes, a sequence that crowds settles. The proof never names the limit in advance. It borrows one from 22b and then shows the whole sequence follows.

\varepsilon = 1:\ |s_n - s_N| < 1 \text{ for } n \ge N @@ crowding, with the challenge 1
|s_n| \le \max(|s_1|, \dots, |s_{N-1}|, |s_N| + 1) @@ so s has a floor and a ceiling
s_{k(j)} \to c \text{ for some thread} @@ 22b2
|s_n - c| \le |s_n - s_{k(j)}| + |s_{k(j)} - c| @@ the triangle technique (8h), for any j
\text{far out, each part} < \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ crowding for the first; the thread settles for the second; k(j) ≥ j
\htmlClass{new}{s_n \to c} \quad \blacksquare @@ so the whole sequence settles where its thread does

Put with 22c1: on the reals, a sequence settles exactly when it crowds. One sentence is about a place; the other is about the terms alone. That second sentence is the one you can check when the place is unknown.

22c3Where the Line Has Holes

The crowd technique borrowed a gathering point from 22b, and 22b borrowed the climb technique, which borrowed the least upper bound. Remove the least upper bound and the chain breaks. On the fractions alone, crowding is not enough.

t_n = \text{the decimal of } \sqrt2 \text{ cut after } n \text{ places} @@ fractions
m, n \ge N:\ |t_m - t_n| < 10^{-N} @@ they agree in the first N places
(t_n) \text{ crowds} @@ 10⁻ᴺ < ε far enough out (8, counting passes any real)
t_n \to \sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q} @@ 22a3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{in } \mathbb{Q},\ \text{crowding does not mean settling}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the limit is a hole
The = dividendCrowding is about the arrangement of the terms among themselves. Settling is about the series heading somewhere. On the line with no holes, the = between "crowds" and "settles" holds. On the fractions it fails, and the failure is exactly a hole.

The accepted names

A crowding sequence is a Cauchy sequence, and the crowd technique is the Cauchy criterion (or the statement that ℝ is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges). A line on which every Cauchy sequence converges is called complete; rung 15c met the word. One way to build the reals is to take crowding sequences of fractions as the numbers themselves.

Who said it first

Bernard Bolzano stated the criterion in 1817, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy used it in his Cours d’analyse (1821), though neither could prove crowding is enough without a built line of reals. Georg Cantor and Charles Méray (1872) built the reals from crowding sequences of fractions, the same year Dedekind built them from cuts (rung 8). The two constructions give the same line. In 22c.4.5, the older colleague is Leopold Kronecker: “God made the whole numbers; all else is the work of man” (reported by Heinrich Weber from an 1886 lecture).
