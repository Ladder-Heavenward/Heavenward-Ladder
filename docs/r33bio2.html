How Does a Cell Get Things Where They Need to Go?

How does a cell get things where they need to go? Inside a cell there are no pipes and no pumps for most things: molecules simply jostle, and by jostling they spread. Over a bacterium that is fast enough. Over a nerve cell a metre long it would take centuries. This page finds how far jostling carries a molecule in a given time, by rung 30’s random walks and rung 33’s heat equation, works out times across cells of different sizes, finds the motors that carry cargo where jostling cannot, sees why cells are small, and finds the surprising answer to how much of its surface a cell must cover to catch what it needs.

A jostling molecule spreads a typical distance √(2Dt) in time t, so crossing a length L takes about L²/(2D): ten times the distance, a hundred times the time. Short trips use jostling; long ones need motors walking along tracks at a steady speed. A ball of radius a absorbing from far away catches molecules at 4πDac, growing with a, not with its area.

33bio21Spreading by Jostling

A protein in a cell is knocked about by water molecules, each knock a random step. From here on, on this page, its spreading constant D measures how fast it spreads; for a typical protein inside a cell, D is about 10 square micrometres a second.

\text{a walk of } n \text{ random steps of size } s:\ \text{spread}^2 = n s^2 @@ spreads² add (30n)
n \propto t \Rightarrow \text{spread}^2 = 2Dt @@ along one line
\text{spread} = \sqrt{2Dt} @@ the heat equation’s spreading, √t (33b)
\htmlClass{new}{t \approx L^2 / (2D)} @@ the time to cross a length L

Jostling technique: a molecule spreads a typical distance √(2Dt) in time t, so crossing a length L takes about L²/(2D). Twice the distance takes four times as long.

33bio22Times Across Cells

Put in sizes, with D = 10 μm²/s for a protein.

\text{a bacterium, } 1\ \mu\text{m}:\ 1/20 = 0.05 \text{ s} @@ fast
\text{a human cell, } 20\ \mu\text{m}:\ 400/20 = 20 \text{ s} @@ slow but workable
\text{a nerve fibre, } 1 \text{ m} = 10^6\ \mu\text{m}:\ 10^{12}/20 = 5 \times 10^{10} \text{ s} @@ a million micrometres
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 1600 \text{ years}} @@ from the spinal cord to the foot

Jostling is quick over a micrometre and hopeless over a metre, because its time grows as the square of the distance. A nerve cell makes its proteins near its centre and must deliver them to the far tip of a fibre that can be a metre long.

33bio23Motors on Tracks

Cells lay down long protein tracks, and small motor proteins walk along them, two-footed, carrying cargo, each step paid for with one molecule of fuel.

\text{walking: time} = L / v @@ grows only as the distance
\text{fast transport in nerves: up to about } 400 \text{ mm a day} @@ measured
1 \text{ m}:\ 1000/400 = 2.5 \text{ days} @@ against 1600 years
\htmlClass{new}{L/v < L^2/(2D) \iff L > 2D/v} @@ past that length, walking wins

Motor technique: past a length of about 2D/v, walking at speed v beats jostling, and the advantage grows with the distance. For a motor at about 1 μm/s and a protein with D = 10, that length is about 20 μm: the size of a cell. Small cells jostle; large ones need roads.

33bio24Why Cells Are Small

A round cell of radius r burns food and oxygen through its whole volume, but takes them in through its surface.

\text{need} \propto \tfrac43 \pi r^3 @@ every part uses
\text{supply through the surface} \propto 4\pi r^2 @@ what can cross the skin
\text{supply / need} \propto 3/r @@ falls as the cell grows
\htmlClass{new}{\text{past some radius, the surface cannot feed the inside}} @@ and jostling is too slow to fill it

Need grows as the cube of a cell’s size and supply as the square, and jostling slows as the square of the distance: so cells stay small, or build roads and folds. Large creatures are made of many small cells, not a few large ones.

33bio25How Much Surface Must Catch?

A cell catches a nutrient with catchers on its surface. How much does the catch depend on covering the surface? First, a ball of radius a that catches every molecule touching it, in a fluid of concentration c far away. Steady jostling makes the concentration an average of its neighbours, as in 33d, so in space it goes as 1/r.

c(r) = c\,(1 - a/r) @@ 0 at the ball, c far off: steady (33d)
\text{flow in} = 4\pi r^2 \cdot D\,c\prime(r) = 4\pi r^2 \cdot D c a / r^2 @@ flux through any sphere (28j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{catch} = 4\pi D a c} @@ grows as the radius, not the area
\text{a few thousand small catchers: nearly the whole catch} @@ each catcher is a small ball of its own

Catch technique: a ball catches from its surroundings at 4πDac, in proportion to its radius; so a few thousand small catchers spread over a cell catch nearly as much as a fully covered surface, leaving room for everything else. Jostling is wasteful over distance but generous to small targets.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Spreading by jostling is diffusion, the jostling itself Brownian motion (30·then), and D the diffusion coefficient. The tracks are microtubules and the motors kinesin and dynein; carriage along a nerve fibre is axonal transport. Supply against need is the surface-to-volume ratio, and 4πDac the diffusion-limited rate, or Smoluchowski rate.

Who said it first

Albert Einstein linked jostling to spreading, √(2Dt), in 1905. Marian Smoluchowski found the catch of a ball in 1917. Howard Berg and Edward Purcell showed in 1977 that a few thousand small catchers nearly match a covered cell. Kinesin was found in 1985.
