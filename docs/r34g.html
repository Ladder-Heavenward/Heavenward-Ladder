34g · proof

Loops count holes

Rung 26 walked once round a hole in the plane and found a total that never came out zero, however the walk was bent, as long as it stayed off the hole. Rung 29 read holes in a region by the same kind of total. Both needed slopes. This step counts holes with loops alone. Fix a point, and take every loop that starts and ends there. Two loops are the same when one can be slid into the other without leaving the space. Loops can be run one after another, and run backwards, and their sliding classes make a group. In the plane every loop slides down to its starting point, so the group has one member. On the circle a loop that goes round three times cannot be slid into one that goes round once, and the group is the whole numbers. The proof unrolls the circle onto the line, the way a tape measure unrolls from its case, and follows each loop up into the line, where it can be measured. Spaces that unroll like that onto others are called coverings, and they count loops on the torus and the cross-cap too.

A loop at a point is a path that starts and ends there, and two loops are the same when a continuous family of loops slides one into the other. Run one loop after another and the sliding classes form a group, the loop group, and a continuous map carries it along. In a space of arrows every loop slides to a point. Wrapping the line round the circle unrolls every path on the circle into exactly one path on the line from a chosen start, and the distance a loop climbs on the line is a whole number that slides cannot change. So the loop group of the circle is the whole numbers, and a covering, which unrolls the same way over every small open set, makes its loop group a part of the loop group below.

34g1Loops, and sliding them

Fix a point x₀ of a space X. A loop at x₀ is a path γ with γ(0) = γ(1) = x₀. Two loops γ and δ at x₀ slide into each other when some continuous map H from the square [0, 1] × [0, 1] to X has H(t, 0) = γ(t), H(t, 1) = δ(t), and H(0, s) = H(1, s) = x₀ for every s. Each s gives a loop, and as s runs from 0 to 1 the loops slide from γ to δ, with the ends held at x₀.

Sliding is a sameness relation (2g). H(t, s) = γ(t) slides γ to itself. H(t, 1 − s) slides back. To chain two slides, run the first for s from 0 to ½ and the second for s from ½ to 1; the two pieces agree where they meet, so the glued map is continuous, by 11e’s glued pieces carried to closed parts of the square.

Straight-slide technique: in a space of arrows, every loop at x₀ slides to the loop that stays at x₀; and so in any part of one that holds the straight segment from each of its points to x₀.

H(t, s) = (1 - s)\,\gamma(t) + s\,x_0 @@ the point part of the way from γ(t) to x₀
H(t, 0) = \gamma(t),\ \ H(t, 1) = x_0,\ \ H(0, s) = H(1, s) = x_0 @@ γ(0) = γ(1) = x₀
H \text{ is continuous} @@ sums and products of continuous maps, in each entry (11b, 34b3)
\htmlClass{new}{\gamma \text{ slides to the loop that stays at } x_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ H stays in the part, which holds each segment

So in the plane, in a disc, in a ball, every loop slides to a point. The plane with a hole is not covered: a segment from a point of a loop round the hole to x₀ may run through the hole.

34g2Loops form a group

For loops γ and δ at x₀, γ · δ runs γ at double speed for t in [0, ½], then δ at double speed for t in [½, 1]. γ⁻¹ runs γ backwards: γ⁻¹(t) = γ(1 − t). Write [γ] for the sliding class of γ. A path γ ∘ φ, with φ a continuous map of [0, 1] to itself with φ(0) = 0 and φ(1) = 1, is γ run on a new timetable. It slides to γ by H(t, s) = γ((1 − s)φ(t) + st).

Loop-group technique: the sliding classes of loops at x₀, with [γ][δ] = [γ · δ], form a group, whose member that does nothing is the class of the loop that stays at x₀, and in which [γ] is undone by [γ⁻¹].

\gamma \text{ slides to } \gamma' \text{ by } H,\ \ \delta \text{ to } \delta' \text{ by } K \Rightarrow \gamma\cdot\delta \text{ slides to } \gamma'\cdot\delta' @@ run H on the left half of the square, K on the right; they agree at t = ½: so the product is well named
(\gamma\cdot\delta)\cdot\varepsilon \text{ is } \gamma\cdot(\delta\cdot\varepsilon) \text{ on a new timetable} @@ the same three loops, run at speeds 4, 4, 2 and 2, 4, 4: brackets do not matter
c\cdot\gamma \text{ and } \gamma\cdot c \text{ are } \gamma \text{ on new timetables} @@ c stays at x₀; a pause, then γ at double speed
H(t, s) = \gamma(2t(1 - s)) \text{ for } t \le \tfrac12,\ \ \gamma((2 - 2t)(1 - s)) \text{ for } t \ge \tfrac12 @@ slides γ · γ⁻¹ to c: go out less far each time, and come back
\htmlClass{new}{[\gamma][\gamma^{-1}] = [c]} \quad \blacksquare @@ and likewise [γ⁻¹][γ] = [c]: the rules of a group (19a)

This group is the loop group of X at x₀, written π₁(X, x₀). The mark π here is a name, with a small 1 beside it, and it has nothing to do with the π of 21a, which keeps its meaning everywhere else. Its accepted name is the fundamental group. A continuous map f from X to Y with f(x₀) = y₀ sends loops to loops, and slides to slides, so it gives a map f∗[γ] = [f ∘ γ] from π₁(X, x₀) to π₁(Y, y₀) that keeps products: f∗([γ][δ]) = f∗[γ] f∗[δ]. A same-shape map gives a matching of the two groups that keeps products both ways. So the loop group is a shape-property.

34g3Unrolling the circle

Let C be the circle, and p(t) = (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) for t on the line: 21b’s point at t whole turns. p wraps the line round C endlessly, and p(t) = p(t′) exactly when t − t′ is a whole number. Over an open arc of C shorter than the whole circle, p⁻¹ of the arc is endlessly many separate open stretches, one in each whole turn, and p matches each of them with the arc by a same-shape map. Its backwards map reads off an angle on that stretch, as one of 26c’s logarithm values does.

A path γ̃ on the line with p ∘ γ̃ = γ is a lift of γ: γ unrolled.

Path-lift technique: for every path γ on C and every t₀ with p(t₀) = γ(0), exactly one path γ̃ on the line has p ∘ γ̃ = γ and γ̃(0) = t₀.

A_1 = C \setminus \{(1, 0)\},\ \ A_2 = C \setminus \{(-1, 0)\} @@ two open arcs, each shorter than the circle, that cover it
\gamma^{-1}(A_1),\ \gamma^{-1}(A_2) \text{ cover } [0, 1] @@ open sets, by continuity
0 < \tfrac1N < \tfrac2N < \cdots < 1,\ \ \gamma([\tfrac kN, \tfrac{k+1}N]) \text{ in one arc} @@ the cover-reach technique (34d4), with 1/N below the reach
\text{on each piece: the stretch over that arc holding the lift so far, and its backwards map} @@ start at t₀; each piece starts where the last one ended
\tilde\gamma \text{ is continuous} @@ finitely many pieces, agreeing at their joins (11e)
\tilde\gamma - \tilde\gamma' \text{ is a whole number at each } t,\ \text{and continuous} @@ two lifts of the same path; p(t) = p(t′) only for whole gaps
\htmlClass{new}{\tilde\gamma - \tilde\gamma' = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the whole numbers are scattered, so its image is one point (34c2); it is 0 at t = 0

The same lines, run on the square [0, 1] × [0, 1], which is finitely covered (34d5), lift a slide of loops to a slide of paths on the line. Slide-lift: if two loops on C slide into each other, their lifts from the same start end at the same point. The end of the lifted slide moves continuously through numbers that all sit over the same point of C, so it does not move.

34g4The circle’s loops are the whole numbers

Take x₀ = (1, 0) = p(0). For a loop γ at x₀, lift it from 0. Its end γ̃(1) sits over x₀, so it is a whole number: the winding number w(γ), the number of times γ goes round, anticlockwise counted as positive.

Circle-loops technique: γ ↦ w(γ) matches the loop group of the circle with the whole numbers, and turns the product of loops into the sum of their winding numbers.

\gamma \text{ slides to } \delta \Rightarrow w(\gamma) = w(\delta) @@ slide-lift (34g3): w is a function of the class
\widetilde{\gamma\cdot\delta} = \tilde\gamma \text{ then } (\tilde\delta + w(\gamma)) @@ the lift of δ from w(γ) is the lift from 0, slid up by w(γ); one lift (34g3)
w(\gamma\cdot\delta) = w(\gamma) + w(\delta) @@ read off the end
\gamma_n(t) = p(nt):\ \ \tilde\gamma_n(t) = nt,\ \ w(\gamma_n) = n @@ every whole number is reached
w(\gamma) = 0 \Rightarrow \tilde\gamma \text{ is a loop at } 0 \text{ on the line} @@ it starts and ends at 0
\tilde\gamma \text{ slides to } 0 \text{ by } H,\ \ \text{so } \gamma \text{ slides to } x_0 \text{ by } p \circ H @@ the straight-slide technique on the line (34g1)
\htmlClass{new}{\pi_1(C, x_0) \text{ matches } \mathbb Z} \quad \blacksquare @@ one-to-one: w(γ) = w(δ) gives w(γ · δ⁻¹) = 0

So the circle is not the same shape as a disc: their loop groups differ. The plane with its centre taken out slides onto the circle by x ↦ x/|x|, keeping the circle in place, and its loop group is the whole numbers too. That is the shape of 26f’s hole technique: 1/z totals 2πi round the circle because the circle goes once round a hole, and the total is 2πi times the winding number. The plane with a point out and space with a point out, which 34e could not tell apart, now part company: every loop in space with a point taken out slides to a point, which is proved much higher up the ladder.

34g5Coverings

A continuous map p from Y onto X is a covering when every point of X has an open set U about it whose pull-back is a union of separate open sets, each matched with U by p, as a same-shape map. Each of those open sets is a sheet over U. The line over the circle is one, with a sheet for each whole number. The circle over itself by z ↦ z³ (26a’s powers) is one with three sheets. The plane over the torus, by (t₁, t₂) ↦ (p(t₁), p(t₂)), is one. The sphere over the cross-cap, sending x and −x to the same point, is one with two sheets.

Covering-lift technique: for every covering, every path below has exactly one lift from each start above it, and slides lift to slides; so p∗ is one-to-one on loop groups.

\text{open sets } U \text{ with sheets cover } X @@ the definition
\text{the lines of 34g3 with } U \text{ for the arcs} @@ the cover-reach technique cuts a path, and then a slide, into pieces each in one U
p\circ\tilde\gamma = \gamma,\ \ \tilde\gamma(0) = y_0,\ \text{one lift} @@ the pieces glue; two lifts agree where they start and stay together sheet by sheet
p_*[\tilde\delta] = [c] \Rightarrow p\circ\tilde\delta \text{ slides to } c \text{ by } H @@ suppose a loop above goes to the class of the loop that stays put
\htmlClass{new}{\tilde H \text{ slides } \tilde\delta \text{ to the loop that stays at } y_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ lift H from y₀; its sides stay over x₀ and are lifted to the constant path

Lift each loop on the torus to the plane, from 0, and it ends at a pair (m, n) of whole numbers: how often it goes round each way. The same lines as 34g4 match the loop group of the torus with pairs of whole numbers, added entry by entry. A loop on the cross-cap lifts to a path on the sphere from x to x or from x to −x. Every loop on the sphere slides to a point, which is proved much higher up the ladder, and with it the loop group of the cross-cap has exactly two members.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Sliding is homotopy relative to the ends; a slide is a homotopy. The loop group is the fundamental group, π₁(X, x₀), and f∗ is the induced homomorphism, a product-keeping map, or homomorphism, of groups. A space whose loops all slide to a point is simply connected. A lift is a lift, and the path-lift technique and slide-lift are the path lifting and homotopy lifting properties. A covering is a covering map, and the line over the circle is the universal cover of the circle. The winding number is the degree.

Who said it first

Henri Poincaré introduced the fundamental group in Analysis Situs in 1895, with coverings, and used it to tell three-dimensional shapes apart. Bernhard Riemann had used cuts and coverings of the plane in the 1850s to study the many values of functions like the complex logarithm. The question of whether holes are things was argued by David and Stephanie Lewis in 1970, and by Roberto Casati and Achille Varzi in 1994.

A proof that is not allowed

“The loop round the circle slides to a point: shrink it toward the centre.” Not allowed: the shrinking loops leave the circle. A slide must stay in the space. In the disc this slide is allowed, and the loop does slide to a point there; on the circle it is not, and the winding number shows no other slide can do it.
