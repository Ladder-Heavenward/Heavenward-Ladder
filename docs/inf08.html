Can a Proof Use a Fact Only Once?

Can a proof use a fact only once? Rung 6 named the free moves, and rung 38’s trees showed a given copied once for each use. Take two free moves away, copying a given and throwing one away, and a proof becomes an account of resources: each given is spent exactly once. Then “and” splits in two, both at once and your choice of one, and “if” becomes “spend this to get that”. A mark that gives back free copying lets the old logic live inside the new. In this logic the both-at-once is rung 47’s tensor product, and the fact that a quantum state cannot be copied is proved. The logic was published in 1987. Since 2010 conversations between programs have been typed as its proofs, and a systems language released in 2015 stops a value from being used after it has been handed on.

Without copying and throwing away, each given is spent once: in every proof built from ⊗ (both at once) and ⊸ (spend this to get that), each letter’s signed count is 0, so A ⊬ A ⊗ A and A, B ⊬ A. A & B (your choice) gives A; A ⊗ B does not. A, A ⊸ B ⊢ B spends both. !A may be copied and thrown away, and A ⇒ B is !A ⊸ B. v ↦ v ⊗ v is not straight, so no linear map copies.

inf8.1Two Free Moves Taken Away

38n1 noticed that a supposition used twice appears twice in a tree: the tree copies it. 6a3’s first free if, from x write y ⇒ x, throws y away unused. Remove both moves: no given may be copied, and none may be dropped. Write the givens as a list, Γ ⊢ A, and every proof spends each given in Γ exactly once.

Keep two connectives for now: A ⊗ B, both A and B at once, and A ⊸ B, spend A to get B. Count each letter’s appearances with a sign: +1 to the right of ⊢, −1 to the left, and flipped once more inside the left side of a ⊸.

Balance technique: in a proof with only ⊗ and ⊸ and no copying or throwing away, every letter’s signed count is 0.

A \vdash A:\ -1 + 1 = 0 @@ the starting move
\Gamma \vdash A,\ \Delta \vdash B \ \text{ give } \ \Gamma, \Delta \vdash A \otimes B @@ counts add: 0 + 0
\Gamma, A \vdash B \ \text{ gives } \ \Gamma \vdash A \multimap B @@ A moves right and into a ⊸’s left: two flips, same sign
\Gamma \vdash A,\ \Delta, B \vdash C \ \text{ give } \ \Gamma, \Delta, A \multimap B \vdash C @@ A goes left and inside: +1 kept; B stays left: −1 kept
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every letter’s signed count is } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ each move keeps it, and copying or dropping would change it

So A ⊢ A ⊗ A has count −1 + 2 = 1 for A, and cannot be proved: no copying. A, B ⊢ A has count −1 for B: no throwing away.

inf8.2Two Ands

In ordinary logic “A and B” can be used for A, for B, or for both. Spent once, it splits. A ⊗ B is both at once: a coffee and a pastry, both paid for, both received. A & B is your choice of one: a coffee or a pastry, as you like, but one. To reach A & B you must be able to reach A and, separately, to reach B, from the same givens; you then get only the one you choose.

Two-ands technique: A & B ⊢ A and A & B ⊢ B, but A ⊗ B ⊬ A.

A \vdash A @@ the starting move
A \mathbin{\&} B \vdash A @@ choose the left side of a choice
A \otimes B \vdash A:\ \ B \text{ counts } -1 @@ the balance technique
\htmlClass{new}{A \mathbin{\&} B \vdash A,\quad A \otimes B \nvdash A} \quad \blacksquare @@ from both at once, B cannot be left unspent

Ordinary “and” is both at once with free throwing away; the choice is what is left when throwing away is gone and copying too.

inf8.3Spend This to Get That

A ⊸ B is a machine that takes one A and gives one B, and is used up. A coffee machine: coin ⊸ coffee. Two coins and one machine give one coffee and leave one coin; they do not give two coffees.

Spend-to-get technique: A, A ⊸ B ⊢ B spends both givens; and coin ⊗ coin, coin ⊸ coffee ⊢ coffee ⊗ coin.

\mathrm{coin} \vdash \mathrm{coin},\ \ \mathrm{coffee} \vdash \mathrm{coffee} @@ starting moves
\mathrm{coin},\ \mathrm{coin} \multimap \mathrm{coffee} \vdash \mathrm{coffee} @@ spend one coin and the machine
\mathrm{coin},\ \mathrm{coin},\ \mathrm{coin} \multimap \mathrm{coffee} \vdash \mathrm{coffee} \otimes \mathrm{coin} @@ both at once, with the other coin
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{coin} \otimes \mathrm{coin},\ \mathrm{coin} \multimap \mathrm{coffee} \vdash \mathrm{coffee} \otimes \mathrm{coin}} \quad \blacksquare @@ unpack the pair of coins

coffee ⊗ coffee has count +2 for coffee against −1 from the one machine: it cannot be reached. Ordinary logic, which may copy “coin ⇒ coffee”, would happily give two.

inf8.4A Mark That Gives Copying Back

Add a mark !: !A is an A that may be copied and thrown away, as many times as wanted. Then ordinary logic sits inside: A ⇒ B is read as !A ⊸ B, “given as many A as needed, one B”.

Bang-copy technique: !A ⊢ !A ⊗ !A and !A, B ⊢ B: only marked givens may be copied or dropped.

!A \vdash !A @@ the starting move
!A,\ !A \vdash !A \otimes !A @@ both at once, from two copies
\htmlClass{new}{!A \vdash !A \otimes !A} @@ copy the marked given
B \vdash B \ \Rightarrow\ \htmlClass{new}{!A,\ B \vdash B} \quad \blacksquare @@ drop the marked given

By 47j2 proofs are terms, and the terms of this logic use each input once unless it is marked. 47j1’s λf. λx. f (f x) uses f twice and x once, so its type here is !(A ⊸ A) ⊸ (A ⊸ A): the function needs the mark, the input does not.

inf8.5Copying Is Not Straight

Read A ⊗ B as 47h1’s tensor product of spaces, and proofs as straight maps. Then a copy rule, A ⊢ A ⊗ A for every A, would be a straight map V → V ⊗ V sending each v to v ⊗ v.

No-straight-copy technique: for a space V other than {0}, the function v ↦ v ⊗ v is not straight.

v \ne 0 \Rightarrow v \otimes v \ne 0 @@ in the grid-space basis, v ⊗ v has entry vᵢ² ≠ 0 at eᵢ ⊗ eᵢ for some i (47h1)
(2v) \otimes (2v) = 4\,(v \otimes v) @@ ⊗ is straight in each slot
\text{straight would give } 2\,(v \otimes v) @@ a straight map takes 2v to twice the image of v
\htmlClass{new}{4\,(v \otimes v) \ne 2\,(v \otimes v)} \quad \blacksquare @@ since v ⊗ v ≠ 0

A qubit’s gates are straight maps, so no gate copies every state. The logic without copying is the logic of the tensor product; its missing rule is the missing copier. ∞28 proves the same for gates with lengths kept.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞8.oq.1When are two proofs the same proof? Trees that differ only in the order of independent moves should count as one, and drawings that forget the order were proposed in 1987; for the whole logic, with both ands and the mark, finding drawings that capture sameness exactly is still open. And how hard is it to decide what can be proved? For the whole logic no machine decides it, shown in 1990; for smaller parts the cost is known for some and open for others.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1987 a logician published a logic in which copying and throwing away are allowed only on marked givens. It came from the study of what proofs mean as maps, where copying turned out to be a separate step that could be isolated. The logic gave the old one back through the mark, and split “and” and “or” each in two.

Computer scientists took it up as a logic of resources. In 1990 it was shown that no machine decides provability in the whole logic.

In 2010 conversations between programs were typed as its proofs, so a well-typed program follows its protocol to the end. In 2015 a systems language was released whose compiler tracks ownership: a value handed on cannot be used again by the giver, which is the logic without copying, with throwing away still allowed.

The accepted names

The logic is linear logic; copying and throwing away are the structural rules of contraction and weakening. ⊗ is tensor (multiplicative conjunction), & is with (additive conjunction), ⊸ is linear implication (“lollipop”), ! is the exponential “of course”. A logic with weakening but no contraction is affine. Drawings that forget the order of moves are proof nets. Conversations typed as proofs are session types. The no-straight-copy technique is the simplest form of the no-cloning theorem.
