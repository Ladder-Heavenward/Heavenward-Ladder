16·∎ · conclusion

Area and slope undo each other

Let the right end of an area move, and the area so far has the height of the graph as its slope. Turned round, the area under a slope is the change in the function from end to end.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 16e parts is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 16 promised that rung 14's open question and rung 15's are one. Given a slope, what was the function? And how is an area found without a page of sums? 16a defined the area function F(x) = ∫ₛˣ f(t) dt, with t as a stand-in letter, and proved that it never jumps. 16b proved area-slope: where f is continuous, F′ = f. So every continuous function has an antiderivative.

16c proved area-is-a-change: an area is the change, end to end, of any function with the right slope. It proved it twice, once from 16b and once from the boxes with average-slope. 16d ran the chain rule backwards as substitution, and 16e ran the product rule backwards as parts. This page states both directions of the undoing in one claim, and proves it from those steps.

The claim, in full

Take the area to a moving end and then its slope, and you get the height back; take a slope and then its area, and you get the change from end to end.

Let f be continuous on an interval I, let s be in I, and let F(x) = ∫ₛˣ f(t) dt.

Then F has a slope at every x in I, from one side at an end of I, and F′(x) = f(x).

Let f be continuous on [p, q], and let G′ = f at every input of [p, q]. Then ∫ₚ^q f(x) dx = G(q) − G(p).

The second part is true also when f is only integrable on [p, q], if G is continuous on [p, q] and G′ = f inside it.

The proof

The first part is 16b's area-slope, at a point c inside I. f is integrable on every closed interval inside I by continuous-is-integrable (15f), so F is defined.

\text{call } \delta:\ |t - c| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(t) - f(c)| \lt \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} @@ fix ε > 0; f is continuous at c (11a); shrink δ so the stretch lies in I
0 \lt |h| \lt \delta:\ \ F(c + h) - F(c) = \textstyle\int_c^{c+h} f(t)\,dt @@ split-in-any-order (16a)
\tfrac{F(c+h) - F(c)}{h} - f(c) = \tfrac1h \textstyle\int_c^{c+h} \big(f(t) - f(c)\big)\,dt @@ the integral of a constant (15c); areas-add and areas-scale (15e)
\Big|\tfrac{F(c+h) - F(c)}{h} - f(c)\Big| \le \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \lt \varepsilon @@ area-bound (16a) on the stretch, where |t − c| < δ; divide by |h|
\htmlClass{new}{F'(c) = f(c)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 13a's line for the slope: area-slope (16b). At an end, h of one sign only (13f)
  
F \text{ is an antiderivative of } f \text{ on } [p, q] @@ the first part: antiderivative-exists (16b)
F = G + C \text{ for some real } C @@ all-antiderivatives (14e): same slopes on an interval, same shape lifted
0 = F(p) = G(p) + C @@ ∫ₐᵃ f = 0 (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_p^q f = F(q) = G(q) - G(p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same line at q, with C = −G(p): area-is-p-change (16c)

The second part asks less than continuity. 16c's second road reaches it from the boxes, and never uses the first part. Take any partition P of [p, q].

G(x_i) - G(x_{i-1}) = f(t_i)\,\Delta x_i,\ \ t_i \in (x_{i-1}, x_i) @@ average-slope (14c) on each piece; G′ = f inside
m_i\,\Delta x_i \le G(x_i) - G(x_{i-1}) \le M_i\,\Delta x_i @@ mᵢ ≤ f(tᵢ) ≤ Mᵢ (15a); Δxᵢ > 0
L(f, P) \le G(q) - G(p) \le U(f, P) @@ add the n lines; collapsing-sum (15g)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_p^q f = G(q) - G(p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ P was any partition; exactly-one-between (15c): change-for-any-integrable

Read through the second part, every rule for slopes becomes a rule for areas. 16d did it for the chain rule. Here is the product rule, as 16e did it, for f and g whose slopes are continuous on [p, q].

(fg)' = f'g + fg' \text{ on } [p, q] @@ product for slopes (13c)
\textstyle\int_p^q f'g + \int_p^q fg' = fg\Big|_p^q @@ the second part with G = fg; areas-add (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_p^q fg' = fg\Big|_p^q - \int_p^q f'g} \quad \blacksquare @@ take ∫ f′g from both sides: parts for areas (16e)

Neither part is free at its edge. At a jump of f the first part can fail: the step u's area function from 0 has a joint there and no slope. And the second part needs f to have an area. 16c2's function of 1881 has a slope everywhere, never past a fixed bound, and still no area under its slope.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 16f the table run backwards: It turns rung 13's table of slopes round into a table of antiderivatives, checks each entry by taking the slope of the answer, and marks the one entry no power fills: 1/x.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16g definite integrals and areas: It finds the area between two graphs as the integral of the top less the bottom, the total area by cutting where the graph crosses the axis, and averages by antiderivatives.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16h substitution and parts by hand: It practises choosing between substitution and parts, and ends with a tech that expresses an integral through a smaller one of its kind, so a whole list of areas comes from one line.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16i improper integrals: It lets an end run out to forever, or in to a point where the graph climbs without bound, and asks whether the areas up to a cut settle on a number.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16j volumes by cross sections: It takes a volume as the integral of the areas of its slices. Equal slices give equal volumes, and a mass comes from a density that changes from slice to slice.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·phys how far did it go, if you only know how fast?: It runs velocity, acceleration and force backwards. A force added up over time is the change in momentum, and added up over distance the change in kinetic energy.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·cb12 floating and sinking: It adds up the push of water with depth to get the pressure under a column, and proves the buoyancy technique: the push up is the weight of the water pushed aside.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·then how much has piled up, if you only know the rate?: It reads a total change as the area under its rate, keeps a lake's books from what flows in and out, and estimates totals from readings taken once an hour.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·eng where will a loaded beam crack?: A load can be run backwards twice, shear as the area under the load and bending as the area under the shear, so area-and-slope finds where a beam bends most and why joists stand on edge.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·econ who gains from a trade, and how much?: The gain from trade is the area between two curves, and area-slope shows that trading the market's quantity makes it greatest and that a tax destroys exactly the area over the trades it stops.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·nav where am I now?: A place is the area under a speed, and area-bound proves why reckoning drifts: a doubt in speed grows like the time, and a doubt in push like its square.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·bio how much of a dose is still in the blood?: It reads the amount of a dose the body took up as the clearance times the area under the blood level, and shows that two pills with one area can differ in how high and how long.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 16·logi how much is in the tank right now?: It reads a tank's level as its start plus the area under inflow less outflow, and proves the tank never runs dry exactly when it starts with its deepest dip.its line appears when you finish it.

Slope and area were built from different parts, and they turned out to undo each other. You have only just started to use that.
