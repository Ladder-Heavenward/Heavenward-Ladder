23a · proof

Polynomials That Match Every Slope

A tangent line is the straight line that agrees with a curve at one point in two ways: it has the same height there, and the same slope. A straight line can do no more. But a curve that bends has a slope that changes, and the rate of that change is another number at the point, and so on without end. This step asks for a polynomial that agrees with the curve in all of those ways at once, up to some number of them. There is exactly one, and its recipe is short: each slope at the point, divided by a factorial, sits in front of a power. Near the point, the match hugs the curve far more tightly than the tangent did.

At one point a curve has a height, a slope, a second slope and more. For each count of them there is exactly one polynomial that agrees with all of them there. Each slope, over a factorial, is the number in front of a power.

23a1Slopes of Every Order

The slope of f is a function f′. Its slope is the second slope f″. The slope of that is the third slope, and so on, while each one exists.

From here on, f⁽ⁿ⁾ is the nth slope of f: f⁽⁰⁾ = f, and f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾ is the slope of f⁽ⁿ⁾. A function is smooth on an open interval when it has slopes of every order there. Accepted name: infinitely differentiable.

Polynomials, eˣ, sin and cos are smooth everywhere. ln is smooth on (0, ∞). |x| is not smooth at 0: it has no slope there at all.

\big((x-w)^k\big)' = k(x-w)^{k-1} @@ the power rule (13d) and the chain rule with slope 1
\big((x-w)^k\big)^{(j)} = k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)\,(x-w)^{k-j} \ (j \le k) @@ j times
\big((x-w)^k\big)^{(j)}\big|_{x=w} = \begin{cases} k! & j = k \\ 0 & j \ne k \end{cases} @@ at x = w every leftover power of (x − w) is 0; past k the slopes are 0
\htmlClass{new}{p(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} u_k (x-w)^k \Rightarrow p^{(k)}(w) = k!\,u_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ each slope at w picks out one term

23a2The One Polynomial That Matches

Match technique: if f has n slopes at w, exactly one polynomial of degree at most n agrees with f and its first n slopes at w: Tₙ(x) = f(w) + f′(w)(x − w) + f″(w)(x − w)²/2! + ⋯ + f⁽ⁿ⁾(w)(x − w)ⁿ/n!.

p(x) = \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} u_k (x-w)^k @@ any polynomial of degree at most n, written in powers of (x − w)
p^{(k)}(w) = f^{(k)}(w) \text{ for } k = 0, \ldots, n @@ what matching asks
k!\,u_k = f^{(k)}(w) @@ 23a1
\htmlClass{new}{u_k = \tfrac{f^{(k)}(w)}{k!},\ \text{so } p = T_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ each coefficient is forced: there is one, and only one

From here on, Tₙ is the matching polynomial of degree n for f at w. When w is 0 it is the matching polynomial at 0.

T₁ is the tangent line of rung 13: same height, same slope. T₂ adds a bend. Each new term fixes one more slope and leaves the earlier ones alone, because its power of (x − w) has every lower slope 0 at w.

23a3Four Matches to Know

e^x:\ f^{(k)}(0) = 1 \Rightarrow T_n = 1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots + \tfrac{x^n}{n!} @@ exp is its own slope (17f)
\sin:\ 0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, \ldots \Rightarrow T_{2m+1} = x - \tfrac{x^3}{3!} + \tfrac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots \pm \tfrac{x^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!} @@ the slopes of sin cycle in four (21g)
\cos:\ 1, 0, -1, 0, \ldots \Rightarrow T_{2m} = 1 - \tfrac{x^2}{2!} + \tfrac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots \pm \tfrac{x^{2m}}{(2m)!} @@ the same cycle, one step on
\htmlClass{new}{\ln(1+x):\ T_n = x - \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \tfrac{x^3}{3} - \cdots \pm \tfrac{x^n}{n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ f^{(k)}(0) = (−1)^{k+1}(k − 1)! for k ≥ 1, from 1/(1 + x)

The first of these is 22j's sum for e^λ, cut off. Sin keeps only odd powers and cos only even ones, as 21e's flips said they must: sin(−x) = −sin x, cos(−x) = cos x. Whether a matching polynomial heads to its function as n grows is a separate question. 23b answers it. Here λ (little lambda) means any real number.

The accepted names

A smooth function is infinitely differentiable (class C^∞). The matching polynomial is the Taylor polynomial; at 0, the Maclaurin polynomial. The nth slope is the nth derivative.

Who said it first

James Gregory had the idea in 1671 and Isaac Newton used it in his early work; Brook Taylor published the general recipe in 1715, and Colin Maclaurin made the case w = 0 famous in 1742. Madhava of Sangamagrama, in Kerala, found the endless sums for sine and cosine around 1400, three centuries earlier. In 23a.4.5, the scientist is Robert Hooke (anagram 1676, solved 1678).
