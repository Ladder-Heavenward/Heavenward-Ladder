Can a Group Choose the Way a Person Does?

Can a group choose the way a person does? A town has money for one new thing: a park, a library or a pool. Each person on the council can rank the three, best to worst, and choose by their ranking. It seems the council can do the same: vote on each pair, and rank by the votes. This page shows three councillors whose pairwise votes go round in a circle, so the group has no ranking to choose by. Then whoever sets the order of the votes decides the winner, and a points system can be overturned by an option that loses. A theorem from 1951 says no fair rule escapes all of this.

Write each voter’s ranking. Compare two options by majority: count the voters who put one above the other. Check whether the group’s comparisons are transitive. If they go round, any order of votes picks a winner, and different orders pick different winners. Points for places give a ranking, but check what happens when an option that loses is taken away.

6econ1A Ranking

Sarah, Mike and Evita sit on the council. Each ranks the park, the library and the pool. Sarah ranks the park first, then the library, then the pool.

firstsecondthird
Sarahparklibrarypool
Mikelibrarypoolpark
Evitapoolparklibrary

From here on, on this page, x ≻ y for a voter means that voter ranks x above y. A ranking is a relation ≻ on the options in which any two different options compare one way or the other, and which is transitive, as in 2i: if x ≻ y and y ≻ z, then x ≻ z. A ranking is a chain in 6e’s sense, and 6b’s least-chooses can pick its top from any set of options.

Each councillor, alone, has a ranking, and so a way to choose from any set of the three. The question is whether the council has one too.

6econ2Majority Between Two

Compare two options by a show of hands: the group puts x above y when more voters rank x above y than y above x. With three voters and no ties, one side always has 2 or 3.

\text{Sarah: park} \succ \text{library} @@ her ranking
\text{Evita: park} \succ \text{library} @@ hers
\text{Mike: library} \succ \text{park} @@ his
\htmlClass{new}{\text{park} \succ_G \text{library},\ 2 \text{ to } 1} @@ majority

Between any two options this gives an answer, and the answer is one way or the other. So ≻_G passes the first test of a ranking. The second test is transitivity.

6econ3The Majority Goes Round

Circle technique: with these three rankings, the group puts the park above the library, the library above the pool, and the pool above the park. So ≻_G is not transitive, and it is not a ranking.

\text{park} \succ_G \text{library} @@ Sarah and Evita (6econ2)
\text{library} \succ_G \text{pool} @@ Sarah and Mike
\text{pool} \succ_G \text{park} @@ Mike and Evita
\text{suppose } \succ_G \text{ transitive} @@ for contradiction
\text{park} \succ_G \text{pool} @@ from the first two lines
\text{pool} \succ_G \text{park} @@ the third line: the two clash, since one side has the majority
\htmlClass{new}{\succ_G \text{ is not transitive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition fails

Every councillor is reasonable alone: each ranking is transitive. The group made from them is not. Rankings that are each transitive can give a majority that goes round. Least-chooses has nothing to work with: every option has another above it.

6econ4Whoever Sets the Order Picks the Winner

The council still has to decide, so the chair proposes: vote on two of the options, then put the winner against the third. There are three ways to pick the first pair.

\text{park vs library} \to \text{park};\ \text{park vs pool} \to \text{pool} @@ the pool wins
\text{library vs pool} \to \text{library};\ \text{library vs park} \to \text{park} @@ the park wins
\text{park vs pool} \to \text{pool};\ \text{pool vs library} \to \text{library} @@ the library wins
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each option wins under some order}} @@ the circle, entered at three places

Every vote was a fair majority, and the outcome was decided by the order the chair chose. When the majority goes round, the order of the votes decides the winner. The option left out of the first vote always wins.

6econ5Points for Places

Another way: each voter gives 2 points to their first choice, 1 to their second and 0 to their last, and the most points wins. Points are numbers, so 4h’s any-two-compare orders them, and unless two options tie, points give the group a ranking. Here is a different council, of five: three rank A over B over C, and two rank B over C over A.

A:\ 3 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 = 6 @@ first for three, last for two
B:\ 3 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 7 @@ second for three, first for two
C:\ 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 = 2 @@ B wins on points
\text{take away } C:\ A \text{ over } B \text{ for three, } B \text{ over } A \text{ for two} @@ now only A and B
\htmlClass{new}{A \text{ wins}} @@ C came last, and removing it changed the winner

No voter changed their mind about A against B. Yet whether A or B wins depends on whether C is on the list. With points for places, an option that loses can decide which of two others wins.

6econ6No Rule Escapes Everything

Majority by pairs can fail to be transitive. Points can let a losing option change the result. Kenneth Arrow asked whether any rule can avoid both, and in 1951 he proved that none can, except one kind.

Arrow’s technique, stated here: with three or more options, a rule that turns every list of voters’ rankings into a ranking for the group, puts x above y whenever every voter does, and decides x against y from the voters’ rankings of x against y alone, always copies one voter. That voter decides every pair for the group, whatever the others think.

This page does not prove it. The proof takes longer than the page and uses nothing beyond counting and rankings. The circle of 6econ3 and the points of 6econ5 are the two ways a rule can fail, seen up close.

Accepted names

A majority that goes round is the Condorcet paradox, or a Condorcet cycle; an option that beats every other by majority is a Condorcet winner. Voting on pairs in a set order is agenda voting, and the chair’s power is agenda control. Points for places is the Borda count. Arrow’s asks are unrestricted domain and a transitive social ranking, the Pareto condition, and independence of irrelevant alternatives; the copied voter is a dictator, and the result is Arrow’s impossibility theorem.

Who said it first

The Marquis de Condorcet described the circle in 1785. Jean-Charles de Borda proposed points for places to the French Academy of Sciences in 1770. Kenneth Arrow proved his theorem in Social Choice and Individual Values, 1951, and shared the 1972 Nobel memorial prize in economics.
