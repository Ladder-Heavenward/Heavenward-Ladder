How Can Headphones Cancel Noise?

How can headphones cancel noise? On a flight from Seattle to San Jose the cabin is loud with a deep, steady roar from the engines and the air rushing past. Put on a pair of noise-cancelling headphones and switch them on, and the roar drops away, while a voice beside you still comes through. The headphones do not block the sound. They add more sound: a small microphone listens to the roar, and the speaker plays its exact opposite, so the two add up to almost nothing at the ear. What makes the question hard is the word exact. The opposite has to arrive at the same moment as the noise, and nothing is instant: the microphone, the chip and the speaker all take time, and the sound takes time to cross the ear-cup. This page splits the noise into turnings and asks, speed by speed, how much survives a small delay. Low notes come through the arithmetic almost untouched by the delay, and are cancelled well. High notes are not, and that is why a voice gets through. The last footing shows how a roar that repeats can be cancelled whatever the delay.

A turning plus the same turning half a repeat later is nothing, so a sound plus its negative is silence. If the negative comes a time τ late, what is left of each turning of frequency f has size two times the sine of π f τ. That is small when f τ is small: low notes cancel well, and high notes do not, and above one over two τ the anti-noise makes things worse. A roar that repeats can be cancelled whatever the delay, because its next repeat is known from its last one.

32eng1A Turning Half a Turn Late

A sound at the ear is a pressure p(t), a sum of turnings, or a total of them by 32e. The speaker can add a second pressure. If it adds −p(t), the sum is 0.

Anti-turning technique: a turning of frequency f added to the same turning slid by half its repeat, 1/(2f), gives 0; so a sound added to its negative is silence, every frequency at once.

e^{2\pi if(t - 1/(2f))} = e^{2\pi ift}\,e^{-i\pi} @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
e^{-i\pi} = -1 @@ the turn-growth technique (26a)
\htmlClass{new}{e^{2\pi ift} + e^{2\pi if(t - 1/(2f))} = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the turning and its negative

For one frequency, a slide by half a repeat is the same as a minus sign. For a sound of many frequencies, no single slide works: half a repeat of 100 Hz is 5 ms, and half a repeat of 1000 Hz is 0.5 ms. Only the minus sign itself cancels everything at once. The speaker must play the negative, not a delayed copy.

32eng2The Delay That Spoils It

The headphones hear the noise at the microphone and play the negative a time τ later. In a small ear-cup, with the chip quick, τ is about 50 microseconds: the time sound takes to cross 1.7 cm of air at 343 m/s.

Delay-residue technique: if the negative arrives τ late, each turning of frequency f survives with its size multiplied by 2|sin(πfτ)|.

p(t) - p(t - \tau) @@ the noise plus the late negative
e^{2\pi ift} - e^{2\pi if(t - \tau)} = e^{2\pi ift}\big(1 - e^{-2\pi if\tau}\big) @@ a slide multiplies each turning by a turn (32h2)
1 - e^{-2\pi if\tau} = e^{-i\pi f\tau}\big(e^{i\pi f\tau} - e^{-i\pi f\tau}\big) = 2i\,e^{-i\pi f\tau}\sin(\pi f\tau) @@ the power-to-sum technique (26i)
\htmlClass{new}{|1 - e^{-2\pi if\tau}| = 2|\sin(\pi f\tau)|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gain-per-turning technique (32i2): a gain for each frequency
  
f = 100 \text{ Hz}:\ \ 2\sin(\pi \cdot 0.005) \approx 0.031 @@ the engine’s drone: cut to 3%, a drop of 30 dB
f = 318 \text{ Hz}:\ \ \approx 0.10 @@ cut to a tenth, 20 dB
f = 1000 \text{ Hz}:\ \ \approx 0.31 @@ a voice’s middle: cut by only 10 dB
f = 3000 \text{ Hz}:\ \ \approx 0.91 @@ hardly cut at all
\htmlClass{new}{f = 10\,000 \text{ Hz}:\ \ 2\sin(\pi/2) = 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the anti-noise doubles it

So the cancelling is good below a few hundred hertz and useless above a few thousand, and above 1/(6τ), about 3300 Hz here, where 2 sin(πfτ) passes 1, it makes things worse. The headphones filter their anti-noise to keep it out of the high frequencies, and the padded cups block the high ones by plain bulk, which works best exactly where the arithmetic fails.

32eng3A Roar That Repeats

An engine that turns at a steady rate makes a roar that very nearly repeats, with a repeat P set by the turning of its blades. The delay hurts only because the headphones must guess the noise before they have heard it.

Repeat-ahead technique: if the noise repeats every P, and P is longer than the delay τ, then playing the negative of what was heard one repeat earlier cancels it exactly.

p(t) = p(t - P) \text{ for every } t @@ the roar repeats
\text{at time } t - \tau, \text{ the value } p(t - P) \text{ has been heard} @@ t − P is earlier than t − τ, since P > τ
\text{play } -p(t - P) \text{ so that it arrives at } t @@ sent at t − τ, it arrives τ later
\htmlClass{new}{p(t) - p(t - P) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the repeat

Real roars repeat only nearly: their coefficients drift as the plane climbs or the engine speeds up. So the headphones keep a running estimate of the drone’s coefficients, updated from what they hear, the running filter of 32i in another dress, and play its negative ahead. What cannot be predicted, the hiss of air and the voice beside you, is left to the delay-residue technique, and so to the low frequencies.

The accepted names

Cancelling sound by its negative is active noise control (ANC), or active noise cancellation, and the negative is anti-noise. The delay-residue factor is the comb-filter response of a delayed subtraction. A microphone outside the cup that listens ahead is feedforward control; one inside the cup, in a loop with the speaker, is feedback control. The running estimate of the drone’s coefficients is an adaptive filter, typically by the least-mean-squares method. The blocking by the cups is passive isolation.

Who said it first

Paul Lueg patented cancelling sound with its negative in 1936. Harry Olson and Everett May built an electronic sound absorber in 1953. Amar Bose began designing cancelling headsets in 1978 after a flight on which the engine noise made his airline headphones useless; pilots’ headsets came first, in the 1980s. Bernard Widrow and Ted Hoff gave the least-mean-squares filter in 1960.

A proof that is not allowed

“The anti-noise is the negative of the noise, so the sum is 0 everywhere in the room.” Not allowed: the negative arrives with the right timing only at one place, the ear. A metre away the two paths differ, and the delay-residue technique applies with the difference of travel times as τ. That is why cancelling works in a small cup and not in a hall.
