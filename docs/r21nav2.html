What Is the Cheapest Way to Mars?

What is the cheapest way to Mars? A probe cannot steer like a ship. It fires its engine for a few minutes and then coasts for months on an ellipse that the sun decides. Fuel is the whole cost, and every change of speed spends it. This page finds how fast a planet goes round on its circle, builds the ellipse that just touches the earth’s circle and Mars’s, finds the two pushes it takes, and works out how long the trip lasts and when to leave. Then it shows why, to catch something ahead of you on your own orbit, you slow down.

On a circle, the sun’s pull GM/r² supplies the pull toward the centre v²/r, so v = √(GM/r). The transfer ellipse touches both circles at its ends; there, equal areas make r·v the same, and the energy ½v² − GM/r is the same. Solve for the end speeds, and the pushes are the differences from the circle speeds. Kepler’s third law gives the time; leave when the target leads by what it moves in that time, less half a turn.

21nav21The Speed of a Circle

Terry plans a probe to Mars. Take both planets’ orbits as circles about the sun, at r = 1 and r = 1.524 in units of the earth’s distance. The sun pulls a body at distance r toward it with GM/r² per kilogram, Newton’s law, with GM fixed for the sun. Going round a circle at speed v needs a pull of v²/r toward the centre, as in 21·phys.

\dfrac{GM}{r^2} = \dfrac{v^2}{r} @@ the pull is what the circle needs (21·phys)
v = \sqrt{GM/r} @@ a farther circle is slower
\text{earth: } 29.8 \text{ km/s} @@ measured
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Mars: } 29.8/\sqrt{1.524} \approx 24.1 \text{ km/s}} @@ the speed scales as 1/√r

From here on, on this page, a push is a short firing of the engine that changes the speed by some amount, written Δv. Fuel goes by the pushes added up.

21nav22An Ellipse That Touches Both Circles

Coasting, the probe travels on an ellipse with the sun at a focus (18·phys). The cheapest route for two pushes is the ellipse whose nearest end touches the earth’s circle and whose farthest end touches Mars’s: by 18h’s orbit ends, its long axis is 1 + 1.524. At each end the probe moves square to the line to the sun. Two rules hold all along an orbit.

r_1 v_1 = r_2 v_2 @@ Kepler’s equal areas: at an end, the area swept per second is ½ r v
\tfrac12 v_1^2 - \tfrac{GM}{r_1} = \tfrac12 v_2^2 - \tfrac{GM}{r_2} @@ the energy is the same all along
\tfrac12 v_1^2\left(1 - \tfrac{r_1^2}{r_2^2}\right) = GM\,\tfrac{r_2 - r_1}{r_1 r_2} @@ put v₂ = v₁r₁/r₂ in (5g)
\htmlClass{new}{v_1^2 = \dfrac{GM}{r_1} \cdot \dfrac{2 r_2}{r_1 + r_2}} @@ cancel r₂ − r₁ (5h)

Ends technique: at the near end, the transfer ellipse is faster than the near circle by the factor √(2r₂/(r₁ + r₂)), and at the far end its speed is r₁/r₂ of that.

21nav23The Two Pushes

Put in the numbers. The first push speeds the probe up from the earth’s circle onto the ellipse; the second, at Mars’s distance, speeds it up again from the ellipse onto Mars’s circle.

v_1 = 29.8\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.524 / 2.524} \approx 32.7 @@ the near end
\Delta v_1 \approx 32.7 - 29.8 = 2.9 @@ the first push, km/s
v_2 = 32.7 / 1.524 \approx 21.5 @@ equal areas
\Delta v_2 \approx 24.1 - 21.5 = 2.6 @@ the second push, km/s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 5.6 \text{ km/s in all, from the earth’s path to Mars’s}} @@ leaving the earth’s own pull costs more on top

At the far end the probe is slower than Mars, though it was faster than the earth when it left: coasting outward, the sun slowed it. Both pushes are forward.

21nav24How Long, and When to Leave

Kepler’s third law, read off the planets: a period in years is a3/2, where a is half the long axis in the earth’s distances. The trip is half the transfer ellipse.

a = (1 + 1.524)/2 = 1.262 @@ half the long axis
\tfrac12 \cdot 1.262^{3/2} \approx 0.709 \text{ years} \approx 259 \text{ days} @@ half a period, by 17g’s powers
\text{Mars moves } 360° \cdot 0.709 / 1.881 \approx 136° @@ its period is 1.524^{3/2} ≈ 1.881 years
\htmlClass{new}{\text{leave when Mars leads by } 180° - 136° \approx 44°} @@ so that it arrives where the probe does

Window technique: leave when the target leads by half a turn less the angle it moves during the trip. The earth gains a turn on Mars every 1/(1 − 1/1.881) ≈ 2.1 years, so the window opens about every 26 months.

21nav25Slow Down to Catch Up

A supply craft is on the same circle as a station, a little behind it. Pushing forward raises the craft onto a wider ellipse, and a wider orbit takes longer: the craft falls further behind. The cure runs the other way.

\text{push backward: the far end becomes the near end} @@ the craft drops inside its circle
\text{smaller } a \Rightarrow \text{shorter period} @@ Kepler’s third law
\text{one lap later: back at the same place, sooner} @@ it has gained on the station
\htmlClass{new}{\text{push forward there to rejoin the circle}} @@ beside the station

To gain on something ahead in your own orbit, slow down: a lower orbit is shorter in period, though the craft speeds up as it drops. A craft on a smaller circle is faster everywhere, by v = √(GM/r).

Accepted names

The transfer ellipse is a Hohmann transfer orbit, and the window is a launch window; the earth’s turn gained on Mars is the synodic period. The energy rule at the ends is the vis-viva equation, v² = GM(2/r − 1/a). Equal areas is conservation of angular momentum. Catching up from behind on one orbit is orbital rendezvous, and a near pass that changes speed is a gravity assist.

Who said it first

Johannes Kepler published equal areas in 1609 and the third law in 1619. Walter Hohmann worked out the cheapest two-push transfer in Die Erreichbarkeit der Himmelskörper, 1925.
