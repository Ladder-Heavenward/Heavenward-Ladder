What makes a violin sound like a violin?

What makes a violin sound like a violin? Play the A above middle C on a violin, a flute and a guitar in a hall in San Francisco. All three are the same note: the air at your ear repeats 440 times a second. A tuner cannot tell them apart. Your ear can, at once, with its eyes shut. 6·phys found why no scale is perfectly in tune, by treating each note as one frequency. A real note is not one frequency. It repeats, so it is a sum of turnings at 440, 880, 1320 and every whole multiple, and the instrument decides how much of each goes in. What makes the question hard is that the same violin, played twice, makes waves that look different on a screen, and still sounds the same. This page takes the recipe apart: what the string makes, what the body of the instrument does to it, why two strings slightly out of tune throb, and why a ripple of many frequencies does not travel at the speed of any of its crests.

A string fixed at both ends repeats at one speed and its whole multiples. Plucked at a point, its recipe falls like one over the square of the speed and skips the speeds that are still at the pluck; bowed, it falls like one over the speed. The body of the violin filters that recipe, speed by speed, and the shares it leaves are what the ear hears as the violin. Two turnings close in speed add to a swell and fade at their difference, which is how a string is tuned. And a bunch of turnings travels at the slope of speed against wave number, which for waves on deep water is half the speed of the crests. Many slits add as a geometric sum of turnings, and their bright lines sharpen as one over their number.

32phys1A string makes whole multiples

A string of length L, held at both ends, with waves on it travelling at speed v, can hold the shapes sin(nπx/L) for n = 1, 2, 3, …, each swinging at n f₁, with f₁ = v/(2L). Any shape it is given is a sum of these (32a3, for the odd wave on [−L, L] that the shape makes). A violin’s A string is 32.5 cm long and sounds 440 Hz, so v = 2 × 0.325 × 440 = 286 m/s.

Pluck-point technique: a string plucked to height h at a point d along it has, in its nth shape, the amount 2hL² sin(nπd/L) / (π²n²d(L − d)); so the amounts fall like 1/n², and every shape that is still at the pluck point is missing.

y(x) = \tfrac{h}{d}x \text{ on } [0, d],\ \ \tfrac{h}{L - d}(L - x) \text{ on } [d, L] @@ the pulled string: two straight pieces, a corner at d
b_n = \tfrac2L\int_0^L y(x)\sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx @@ the sine coefficient for a repeat of length 2L (32a3, 32a.5.2)
y'' = 0 \text{ off } d,\ \ y' \text{ jumps by } -\tfrac{hL}{d(L - d)} \text{ at } d @@ the corner
b_n = \tfrac2L\cdot\tfrac{L^2}{n^2\pi^2}\cdot\tfrac{hL}{d(L - d)}\sin\tfrac{n\pi d}{L} @@ parts twice (16), as in the corner-and-jump technique (32h3)
\htmlClass{new}{b_n = \tfrac{2hL^2}{\pi^2n^2\,d(L - d)}\sin\tfrac{n\pi d}{L}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0 whenever nd/L is a whole number

Pluck a guitar a fifth of the way along, and the 5th, 10th, 15th shapes are missing; pluck it near the bridge and the high shapes are strong, and the note is bright. A bow does something else. It drags the string along, lets it slip back, drags it again, and the string’s push on the bridge is close to a sawtooth (32h2), with amounts falling like 1/n, not 1/n². A bowed note is richer in high shapes than a plucked one.

32phys2The body filters the string

A string alone is nearly silent: it is too thin to push much air. It pushes the bridge, the bridge shakes the wooden body, and the body pushes the air. The body is a blend, in the sense of 32i: what goes in at the bridge comes out at the ear with each speed scaled by the body’s own gain at that speed.

Body-filter technique: the recipe of a played note at the ear is the string’s recipe multiplied, speed by speed, by the gain of the body.

\text{push on the bridge} = \textstyle\sum_n F_n\,e^{2\pi i n f_1 t} @@ a repeating push: the string’s recipe
\text{each turning in, a multiple of it out} @@ the gain-per-turning technique (32i2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{sound at the ear} = \textstyle\sum_n H(nf_1)\,F_n\,e^{2\pi i n f_1 t}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add up the turnings

A violin’s body has gains with peaks: one near 280 Hz from the air in its box, several between about 400 and 600 Hz from its plates, and a broad hill near 2500 Hz from the bridge. The same body shapes every note, so its peaks stay at the same frequencies while the notes move: that fixed shaping is much of what the ear calls the violin. It also explains the screen. The turns of the H(nf₁) shift each turning, so two bowings look different, while their shares |H(nf₁)F_n|², which the ear mostly hears, stay put.

32phys3Two strings that throb

6·phys tuned a bass by the slow wah, wah of two notes that should agree. Here is the wah, in turnings.

Beat technique: two equal turnings of frequencies f₁ and f₂ add to a turning of the middle frequency (f₁ + f₂)/2 whose size swells and fades |f₁ − f₂| times a second.

e^{2\pi if_1t} + e^{2\pi if_2t} = e^{\pi i(f_1 + f_2)t}\big(e^{\pi i(f_1 - f_2)t} + e^{-\pi i(f_1 - f_2)t}\big) @@ the plane-adding technique (26b)
= 2\cos\big(\pi(f_1 - f_2)t\big)\,e^{\pi i(f_1 + f_2)t} @@ the power-to-sum technique (26i)
\text{size } 2|\cos(\pi(f_1 - f_2)t)| \text{ peaks every } \tfrac{1}{|f_1 - f_2|} @@ |cos| repeats every π
\htmlClass{new}{\text{beats at } |f_1 - f_2| \text{ a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the loudness swells and fades

A violin’s A at 442 Hz against a tuning fork at 440 Hz beats twice a second; tuned to within a beat every ten seconds, it is within 0.1 Hz. The beats are the same throb that 6·phys heard between a pure fifth and a tempered one: their higher shapes, the 3rd of the lower note and the 2nd of the upper, nearly meet.

32phys4A bunch of waves, and its own speed

A wave along a line with wave number k (turns of 2π per unit length) and frequency ω(k) (turns of 2π per unit time) is e^(i(kx − ω(k)t)). Its crests move at ω/k, the crest speed. A short burst is a bunch of such waves, ∫ A(k)e^(i(kx − ω(k)t)) dk, with A gathered near k₀.

Group-speed technique: a bunch of waves gathered near wave number k₀ travels at the slope ω′(k₀), which differs from the crest speed ω(k₀)/k₀ unless ω is in proportion to k.

\omega(k) \approx \omega(k_0) + \omega'(k_0)(k - k_0) @@ the line that matches at k₀ (23a), since A is gathered there
kx - \omega(k)t \approx k_0x - \omega(k_0)t + (k - k_0)\big(x - \omega'(k_0)t\big) @@ multiply out
\textstyle\int A(k)e^{i(kx - \omega t)}dk \approx e^{i(k_0x - \omega(k_0)t)}\int A(k)e^{i(k - k_0)(x - \omega'(k_0)t)}dk @@ the crests times an envelope
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the envelope depends on } x - \omega'(k_0)t:\ \text{it moves at } \omega'(k_0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the level-point technique (32l3) reads the same: the bulk sits where the phase is level in k

On a violin string ω = vk, so crests and bunches move together and a note keeps its shape. Waves on deep water have ω = √(gk). Their crest speed is √(g/k) and their bunches move at half that. A swell with a period of 12 s has crests moving at gT/(2π) ≈ 18.7 m/s, and its energy arrives at 9.4 m/s: a storm 3000 km off the coast reaches the surf at Santa Cruz about 3.7 days later. A piano’s stiff strings have ω² = v²k² + βk⁴, and their high shapes are a little sharp of whole multiples, part of what makes a piano a piano.

32phys6Many slits make sharp lines

21·then.4’s two slits are brightest where d sin θ = mλ, and the bright places are broad: halfway between two of them it is dark, and the light swells and fades evenly between. Cut N slits, each d from the next, in a sheet lit straight on. Light leaving at the angle θ travels a path longer by d sin θ from each slit than from the one before, a turn φ = 2πd sin θ/λ, and at a far screen the N waves add as turnings.

Sharp-lines technique: N slits d apart send their light at the angle θ with size |sin(Nφ/2)/sin(φ/2)|, φ = 2πd sin θ/λ: it is N at each bright line d sin θ = mλ and already 0 at d sin θ = mλ + λ/N, so the lines narrow as 1/N; and two wavelengths stand apart in the line m once their gap Δλ is at least λ/(mN).

\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} e^{ij\varphi} = \tfrac{e^{iN\varphi} - 1}{e^{i\varphi} - 1} @@ a geometric sum (15i), ratio e^(iφ), not 1
= \tfrac{e^{iN\varphi/2}\,(e^{iN\varphi/2} - e^{-iN\varphi/2})}{e^{i\varphi/2}\,(e^{i\varphi/2} - e^{-i\varphi/2})} = e^{i(N-1)\varphi/2}\,\tfrac{\sin(N\varphi/2)}{\sin(\varphi/2)} @@ half-turns out of top and bottom; the part-reading technique (26i1)
\varphi \to 2\pi m:\ \ \text{size} \to N @@ every turning is 1: all N in step
\varphi = 2\pi m + \tfrac{2\pi}{N}:\ \ \sin\tfrac{N\varphi}{2} = \sin(\pi mN + \pi) = 0 @@ the first dark place beside the line m
\htmlClass{new}{\text{width} \propto \tfrac1N,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \ \tfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = mN} \quad \blacksquare @@ one wavelength’s line on the other’s first dark place: mΔλ = λ/N

A grating ruled with 600 lines in a millimetre has d ≈ 1667 nm, and sends the yellow light of a sodium lamp, 589.3 nm, off at sin θ = 0.354, about 20.7°, in its first line. That yellow is two wavelengths, 589.0 and 589.6 nm, and the sharp-lines technique needs N ≥ 589.3/0.6 ≈ 982 slits lit to split them in the first line. A centimetre of this grating lights 6000, and splits lines 0.1 nm apart. The size at a line is N, so its brightness is N², while the N slits pass only N times the light of one: the light is not made at the lines, it is gathered there from the dark places between.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The whole multiples are the harmonics (or partials, or overtones), and f₁ the fundamental. A note’s recipe of shares is its spectrum, and what the ear makes of it timbre. The bowed string’s slip and drag is Helmholtz motion. The body’s peaks are its resonances, or formants; the air peak is the A0 mode and the hill near 2.5 kHz the bridge hill. The beat technique gives the beat frequency. The crest speed is the phase velocity, the bunch’s speed the group velocity, ω(k) the dispersion relation, and waves whose speed depends on k are dispersive; the piano’s sharp partials are inharmonicity. The claim that the ear ignores turns is Ohm’s acoustic law. Many slits make a diffraction grating; its lines are orders, m the order, and λ/Δλ = mN its resolving power; the small bright places between the lines are subsidiary maxima.

Who said it first

Marin Mersenne measured the frequencies of strings in 1636, and Joseph Sauveur named the harmonics around 1701. Thomas Young found in 1800 that a string plucked at a point lacks the harmonics that have a node there. Hermann von Helmholtz described the bowed string’s motion and set out the theory of timbre in On the Sensations of Tone (1863), building on Georg Ohm’s acoustic law of 1843; August Seebeck had argued against Ohm, and later experiments found the ear does hear some phase. William Rowan Hamilton described group velocity in 1839, and Lord Rayleigh gave the theory in The Theory of Sound (1877). Walter Munk tracked swells from storms near Antarctica to Alaska in 1963. David Rittenhouse made a grating of hairs strung between two screws in 1785, and Joseph von Fraunhofer ruled gratings from 1821 and measured wavelengths of the sun’s light with them. Henry Rowland ruled gratings of great precision in the 1880s.
