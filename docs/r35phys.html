Is an electron a dot or a flow?

Is an electron a dot or a flow? Rung 17 asked the same of light, and found that light knocks electrons out of a metal in lumps, one lump to one electron. Rung 29 found that light bounces off an electron like one ball off another, carrying a push of its own. So light, the flow, behaves like dots. In the nineteen twenties the question was turned round. If a wave can behave like a stream of dots, can a stream of dots behave like a wave? A beam of electrons was fired at a crystal of nickel, and it came off in bright and dark bands, as light does from a grating. The electron makes a pattern, as a wave does, and it lands at one spot, as a dot does. What makes the question hard is that both answers are measured, every time. This page follows the guess that settled it in practice. An electron is described by a wave whose size, squared, gives the chance of finding it. The wave obeys one rule of rates in space and time, and the rule’s shapes, held in place by walls or pulls, come with energies that can take only certain values. From that one rule come an electron in a box, an electron that passes through a wall it cannot climb, a spring whose energies stand in an even ladder, and the energies of hydrogen, measured to many places in the colours of its light.

An electron with a given push behaves as a wave of a matching wavelength. The wave obeys a rule of rates, its size squared gives the chance of finding the electron, and the shapes of the rule, held by walls or pulls, have only certain energies: squares of whole numbers in a box, an even ladder on a spring, and one over the square of a whole number in hydrogen.

35phys1Lumps of light, waves of matter

17·phys’s photoelectric technique gave each lump of light the energy E = hf, and 29·phys’s scattered-light technique showed that it carries a push, a momentum p = h/λ, which it shares with an electron in a collision. In 1924 the guess was turned round: a body with momentum p should behave as a wave with wavelength h/p.

Matter-wave law: a body with momentum p spreads, bends and interferes as a wave of wavelength h/p. It is a law: it was guessed, and then measured.

E = 54 \text{ eV},\ \ p = \sqrt{2m_eE} @@ the energy of motion is p²/(2m)
\lambda = \tfrac{h}{p} = \tfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(54 \times 1.602\times10^{-19})}} = 1.67\times10^{-10}\ \text{m} @@ the matter-wave law
d = 2.15\times10^{-10}\ \text{m} @@ the spacing of the rows of atoms on the face of the crystal
d\sin\theta = \lambda:\ \ \sin\theta = 0.777 @@ the first bright band: waves from neighbouring rows differ in path by one wavelength (21’s sines)
\htmlClass{new}{\theta \approx 51^\circ;\ \text{measured, } 50^\circ} \quad \blacksquare @@ the electrons came off strongest there

A baseball thrown at 40 m/s, 0.145 kg, has a wavelength of 1.1 × 10⁻³⁴ m, far below the width of anything it could pass through. Its wave never shows. An electron of a few volts has a wavelength the size of an atom, and in an atom its wave is all there is.

35phys2A rule for the wave

Write ħ = h/(2π). A wave e^(i(kx − ωt)) has wavelength 2π/k and frequency ω/(2π), so the matter-wave law and E = hf give p = ħk and E = ħω. For a free body E = p²/(2m). The rule of rates that this wave obeys, and that a pull with energy V(x) changes, was guessed in 1926 and is the law below.

\psi = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}:\ \ i\hbar\,\psi_t = \hbar\omega\,\psi,\ \ -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} = \tfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\psi @@ slopes of e to a complex power (26)
\hbar\omega = \tfrac{(\hbar k)^2}{2m} \iff E = \tfrac{p^2}{2m} @@ the two sides agree exactly when energy is energy of motion
\htmlClass{new}{i\hbar\,\psi_t = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} + V\psi} @@ the law, with a pull V added
\psi = \varphi(x)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} \Rightarrow -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\varphi^{\prime\prime} + V\varphi = E\varphi @@ put it in: the time part cancels (33a’s separation)
|\psi|^2 = |\varphi|^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ |e^(−iEt/ħ)| = 1: the standing wave does not change in size

Standing-wave technique: a wave φ(x)e^(−iEt/ħ) obeys the law exactly when φ is a shape of the rule −(ħ²/2m)φ″ + Vφ = Eφ with number E, and then its size never changes. So the energies an electron can hold are the numbers of 33f’s shapes. The law says nothing yet about what the wave is. The rest of the guess, laid down beside it, is that |ψ|² is a density (30m): the chance of finding the electron between x and x + dx is |ψ(x)|²dx, and the total is 1.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

Fire electrons one at a time at the crystal. Each lands at one spot, and no one can say which. After thousands, the spots make the bands, as |ψ|² says, to the accuracy of the count.

The wave law says exactly how often an electron lands in each place, and nothing about where the next one lands.

The law is exact about the crowd and silent about the one. That is 30f’s chance, read in the world: a law stated with how often each outcome turns up.

A detector counts 10 000 electrons, and |ψ|² gives a chance of 0.02 for one narrow band. How many would you expect there, and how far from that would a count of 10 000 tries be likely to wander (30h)?

35phys3An electron in a box

Hold an electron between walls at 0 and L: V = 0 inside, and the wave is 0 at the walls. That is 33c’s string tied at both ends.

Box technique: an electron held between walls a distance L apart has the shapes √(2/L) sin(nπx/L) and the energies n²h²/(8mL²), for n = 1, 2, 3, …, and no others.

-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\varphi^{\prime\prime} = E\varphi,\ \ \varphi(0) = \varphi(L) = 0 @@ the standing-wave technique with V = 0
\varphi_n = \sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L},\ \ \varphi_n^{\prime\prime} = -(\tfrac{n\pi}{L})^2\varphi_n @@ the string-tones technique (33c): the only shapes held at both ends
E_n = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\tfrac{n\pi}{L})^2 = \tfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2} @@ ħ = h/(2π)
\textstyle\int_0^L \sin^2\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \tfrac L2 @@ so √(2/L) makes the total chance 1
\htmlClass{new}{L = 1\ \text{nm}:\ \ E_1 = 0.376\ \text{eV},\ \ E_2 - E_1 = 1.13\ \text{eV}} \quad \blacksquare @@ h²/(8m_e) = 6.03 × 10⁻³⁸ J m²; 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

There is no n = 0: the shape would be 0 everywhere, with no electron to find. So a held electron can never be at rest, and its least energy is set by the box. In a box 1 nm wide the rungs are far apart: at room temperature kT is 0.025 eV, so the electron sits on the lowest rung. In a box 1 cm wide the rungs are 10⁻¹⁴ times as far apart, and they run together into what looks like a flow of energies.

35phys4Through a wall

A wall of height V, higher than the electron’s energy E, stops a ball dead. In the wave law, inside the wall the rule reads φ″ = κ²φ, with κ = √(2m(V − E))/ħ. Its shapes are e^(κx) and e^(−κx). The first grows without bound, which no chance density can do; so the wave dies away as e^(−κx), and does not stop at the wall’s face.

Thin-wall technique: across a wall of width d higher than the electron’s energy, the chance of getting through falls about as e^(−2κd). The size squared of e^(−κx) at x = d is e^(−2κd).

V - E = 4.5\ \text{eV} @@ the work function of a tungsten tip (17·phys’s φ)
\kappa = \tfrac{\sqrt{2m_e(4.5)(1.602\times10^{-19})}}{1.055\times10^{-34}} = 1.09\times10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-1} @@ the thin-wall technique’s κ
\text{widen the gap by } 0.1\ \text{nm}:\ \ e^{-2\kappa(10^{-10})} = e^{-2.17} @@ one more tenth of a nanometre of empty space
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the current falls by a factor of about } 9} \quad \blacksquare @@ the electrons that cross are the ones that tunnel

A sharp tip moved over a surface, held so that this current stays fixed, rises and falls with the atoms below it. Heights a hundredth of a nanometre are read from the current. The same falling-off lets a nucleus throw out an alpha particle it could never climb out of, on its own timetable.

35phys5A spring and its ladder

A spring pulls with energy V = ½mω²x². Measure x in units of √(ħ/(mω)) and energy in units of ħω, and the rule becomes Hφ = Eφ with Hφ = ½(−φ″ + x²φ). Write Rφ = xφ − φ′, the raising move.

Ladder technique: the spring’s energies are (n + ½)ħω for n = 0, 1, 2, …: an even ladder whose lowest rung is the bell e^(−x²/2).

\varphi_0 = e^{-x^2/2}:\ \ \varphi_0^{\prime\prime} = (x^2 - 1)\varphi_0 @@ two slopes of e to a power (17)
H\varphi_0 = \tfrac12\big(-(x^2 - 1) + x^2\big)\varphi_0 = \tfrac12\varphi_0 @@ the lowest rung has energy ½
(R\varphi)^{\prime\prime} = x\varphi^{\prime\prime} + 2\varphi^{\prime} - \varphi^{\prime\prime\prime} @@ two slopes of xφ − φ′
H(R\varphi) - R(H\varphi) = R\varphi @@ write both out and cancel: the x²φ′ and xφ″ terms meet their partners
H\varphi = E\varphi \Rightarrow H(R\varphi) = (E + 1)\,R\varphi @@ each raising climbs one rung
\htmlClass{new}{E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega,\ \ \varphi_n = R^n\varphi_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ back in units; 33f’s perpendicular shapes leave no rungs in between (35·phys.8.3 asks why)

The rungs are hf apart, with f = ω/(2π): 17·phys’s lumps and 30·phys’s frozen shake, now from the wave law. The lowest rung is not 0 but ½hf. A spring can never be still, and its lowest shape is 32f’s tightest bell, as narrow in place and in push together as anything can be.

35phys6Hydrogen and its orbitals

An electron round a proton feels V = −ke²/r. For a wave that depends only on r, 33g’s round-ball technique gives Δφ = (1/r)(rφ)″, and the rule is −(ħ²/2m)(1/r)(rφ)″ − (ke²/r)φ = Eφ.

Ground-orbital technique: φ = e^(−r/r₀) is a shape of hydrogen’s rule exactly when r₀ = ħ²/(mke²), about 0.0529 nm, and its energy is −13.6 eV.

(r e^{-r/r_0})^{\prime\prime} = \big(\tfrac{r}{r_0^2} - \tfrac{2}{r_0}\big)e^{-r/r_0} @@ two slopes of a product (13, 17)
-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\big(\tfrac{1}{r_0^2} - \tfrac{2}{r_0 r}\big) - \tfrac{ke^2}{r} = E @@ divide the rule by φ
\tfrac{\hbar^2}{m r_0} = ke^2 @@ the 1/r terms must cancel, for E is one number
r_0 = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{mke^2} = 5.29\times10^{-11}\ \text{m} @@ the size of the atom
\htmlClass{new}{E = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2mr_0^2} = -13.6\ \text{eV}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the energy to pull the electron away, as measured

The other shapes take more work and come out with energies −13.6 eV/n². Falling from n = 3 to n = 2 gives off 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.89 eV, light of 656 nm: the red line of hydrogen, seen in the glow of every nebula. From n = 4 to n = 2, 486 nm, blue-green. Level n holds n² shapes, with names from the old spectra, s, p, d and f, and with the electron’s own spin each holds two electrons. The counts 2, 8 and 18 turn up as the lengths of the rows of the chemists’ table.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The matter-wave law is de Broglie’s relation, λ = h/p. The law of 35·phys.2 is the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, and the shape rule is the time-independent Schrödinger equation; ψ is the wave function, ħ the reduced Planck constant, and reading |ψ|² as a density of chance is the Born rule. The box is the particle in a box, or infinite square well. The thin-wall technique describes quantum tunnelling, and the microscope is the scanning tunnelling microscope. The spring is the quantum harmonic oscillator, R the raising (creation) operator, and ½ħω the zero-point energy. r₀ is the Bohr radius, 13.6 eV the Rydberg energy, the 656 nm line Hα of the Balmer series, and the shapes of hydrogen its orbitals.

Who said it first

Louis de Broglie proposed matter waves in his thesis of 1924. Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer measured the electrons off nickel in 1927, and George Paget Thomson saw the same bands through thin foils. Erwin Schrödinger wrote the wave law in 1926 and solved hydrogen with it; Max Born read |ψ|² as a chance the same year. George Gamow explained alpha decay by tunnelling in 1928. Paul Dirac set out the raising and lowering moves in 1930. Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer built the scanning tunnelling microscope in Zurich in 1981. Niels Bohr had found hydrogen’s energies in 1913 from orbits that fit; the reroute of 35·phys.8.4 is his picture with de Broglie’s reason. Albert Einstein objected that God does not play dice; the question of 35·phys.8.5 was sharpened by John Bell in 1964 and tested by experiments from the 1970s on.
