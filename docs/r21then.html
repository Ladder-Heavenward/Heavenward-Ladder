Why Is Summer Warmer Than Winter?

Why is summer warmer than winter? The obvious guess is that the earth is nearer the sun in summer. It is not: the earth is nearest the sun in early January, in the middle of a northern winter. What makes it hard is that the sun gives the same light all year, so the difference must be in how the light lands. This page shows, with rung 21’s cosine and sine, that a slanted beam spreads its light thin by the cosine of its slant, and then follows the same factor into two places light was never expected to obey a rule about turns: two filters, and two slits.

A beam that lands slanted from straight overhead spreads over more ground, so each patch gets only the cosine of the slant’s share of the light. Light through a turned filter keeps the square of the cosine of the turn’s share of its strength. Light through two slits is brightest where the gap between the slits times the sine of the angle is a whole number of wavelengths.

21then1A Slanted Beam Spreads Thin

Sunlight arrives in parallel rays. Take a beam of width w, landing on flat ground at angle θ from straight overhead. The patch it lights is the longest side of a right triangle whose side next to θ is w.

w = L \cos\theta @@ next-and-facing (21b): the side next to θ is L cos θ
L = \tfrac{w}{\cos\theta} @@ the patch is longer than the beam by 1/cos θ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{light per patch} = \cos\theta \times \text{light overhead}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same light, over 1/cos θ times the ground

Slant technique: light landing at angle θ from overhead gives each patch of ground cos θ of what it would give from straight above.

21then2The Seasons, Counted

The earth’s axis leans 23.4° from upright against its path round the sun. So the sun at noon stands overhead at a latitude δ that runs from 23.4° north in June to 23.4° south in December. At latitude φ, the noon sun is φ − δ from overhead.

\text{June}:\ 38.6 - 23.4 = 15.2^\circ,\ \ \cos 15.2^\circ \approx 0.965 @@ the noon sun nearly overhead
\text{December}:\ 38.6 + 23.4 = 62.0^\circ,\ \ \cos 62.0^\circ \approx 0.469 @@ low in the south
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{0.965}{0.469} \approx 2.1} @@ twice the light per patch at noon in June
  
\text{January}:\ 0.983,\ \ \text{July}:\ 1.017 @@ the earth’s distance from the sun, in average distances
\left(\tfrac{1.017}{0.983}\right)^2 \approx 1.07 @@ light falls off as one over the distance squared
\htmlClass{new}{7\% \text{ more light in January}} @@ the wrong way round for the north, and too small

The slant doubles the light; the distance changes it by 7%, the wrong way. The distance guess is killed by its own count. Longer days in June add to the slant, since the sun is up for more hours.

21then3Two Filters

Some sunglasses pass only light that shakes along one line, their filter line. Light that shakes along a line turned θ from it has a part along the filter line and a part across it, as a point at angle θ has a cos and a sin. The filter keeps the part along, s cos θ for shaking of size s. A measured law says that the strength of light goes as the square of the size of its shaking.

s \mapsto (s\cos\theta,\ s\sin\theta) @@ along the filter line, and across it (21b)
\text{kept}: s\cos\theta @@ the filter keeps the part along its line
\htmlClass{new}{\text{strength kept} = \cos^2\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ squares; the part lost is sin² θ (21c)

Two-filter technique: light through a filter turned θ from the last one keeps cos² θ of its strength. Two filters crossed at 90° pass nothing. Now slip a third between them, at 45°.

0^\circ \to 90^\circ:\ \ \cos^2 90^\circ = 0 @@ crossed: dark
0^\circ \to 45^\circ \to 90^\circ:\ \ \cos^2 45^\circ \cdot \cos^2 45^\circ = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 @@ each filter keeps cos² of its turn
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac14 \text{ gets through}} @@ adding a filter lets more light through

21then4Two Slits

Shine one colour of light at two thin slits a distance d apart, onto a far wall. Each slit sends light to every point of the wall. A measured law says light acts as a wave with a fixed wavelength λ: two waves arriving together add where their crests meet, and cancel where a crest meets a trough.

\text{extra length} = d\sin\theta @@ for a far wall the two rays are almost parallel; next-and-facing (21b) on the small triangle at the slits
d\sin\theta = m\lambda,\ \ m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots @@ crests meet crests: bright
\htmlClass{new}{\text{bright bands at } \sin\theta = \tfrac{m\lambda}{d}} \quad \blacksquare @@ between them, crest on trough: dark

Two-slit technique: light through two slits is brightest where the two paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths. For small angles, sin θ is nearly θ (21f), and a wall at distance D shows bands about Dλ/d apart.

\lambda = 532 \text{ nm},\ \ d = 0.1 \text{ mm},\ \ D = 2 \text{ m} @@ a green laser pointer and a slide with two scratches
\sin\theta_1 = \tfrac{532 \times 10^{-9}}{10^{-4}} = 0.00532 @@ the first bright band
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spacing} \approx 2 \cdot 0.00532 \text{ m} \approx 10.6 \text{ mm}} @@ small angle: sin θ ≈ θ ≈ tan θ

The accepted names

Light per patch of ground is insolation, and the slant technique is Lambert’s cosine law. The latitude where the noon sun is overhead is the sun’s declination; its limits are the tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. The filter line is the polarisation axis, and the two-filter technique is Malus’s law. The bright and dark bands are interference fringes, and d sin θ = mλ is the double-slit condition.

Who said it first

Johann Heinrich Lambert gave the cosine law in Photometria (1760). Étienne-Louis Malus found the cos² law in 1809, looking at sunlight off the windows of the Luxembourg Palace through a crystal. Thomas Young showed two-slit bands to the Royal Society in 1803 and argued that light is a wave, against Isaac Newton’s particles; in 21·then.6.5, that dispute was reopened by Albert Einstein’s light quanta (1905), and single photons sent one at a time through two slits still build up the bands.
