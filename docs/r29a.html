29a · proof

The fifth postulate on trial

Rung 18 built the plane out of pairs of numbers, and 18c proved that through a point off a line there is exactly one line that never meets it. The first great book of geometry, written in Alexandria about 2300 years ago, had no pairs of numbers. It had a short list of axioms about points and lines, and the fifth, about lines that never meet, was longer and stranger than the rest. For two thousand years people tried to prove it from the others and failed. This step takes the pairs of numbers away, keeps the other axioms, and puts the fifth on trial. It finds how much can be proved without it, and it follows the first person who tried to refute its denial, to see what he found.

Without the parallel axiom, a triangle’s angles still add to no more than a straight angle, and a figure with two equal upright sides has equal top angles. Whether those angles must be right is the question the axiom answers.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 18c (the parallel-through-a-point technique), 18f (angles and the angle sum) and 20f (side-angle-side and side-side-side, earned from the moves). 29b finds a surface where the axiom fails.

29a1Geometry with the numbers taken away

Stands on 18c, 18f, 20f.

On this ladder the parallel statement is a technique about ℝ² (18c). It was proved from coordinates, and coordinates carry a great deal. So ask a narrower question. Take away the coordinates. Keep only these axioms, the ones that old book used besides the fifth, in plain words:

  • Two points lie on exactly one line.
  • Of three points on a line, exactly one is between the other two, and a line cuts the plane into two sides.
  • Segments and angles have sizes, and a size can be laid off from any point, along any ray, on either side.
  • Two triangles with two sides and the angle between them equal are equal in every part (side-angle-side).
  • A length added to itself often enough passes any other length, and so does an angle that fits.

Call these the other axioms. Every move of 20f obeys them, and side-side-side follows from them as it did there. The parallel axiom adds one more: through a point off a line passes at most one line that never meets it. The question of this step is whether the other axioms already force it.

Outside-angle technique: an outside angle of a triangle is larger than each of the two inside angles away from it.

\triangle ABC,\ D \text{ on ray } BC \text{ past } C;\ \ M \text{ the midpoint of } AC @@ the setting: the outside angle is ∠ACD
E \text{ on ray } BM \text{ past } M,\ \ ME = BM @@ lay off a length (the other axioms)
AM = CM,\ \ BM = EM,\ \ \angle AMB = \angle CME @@ midpoint, construction, opposite angles at M are equal
\triangle AMB \text{ and } \triangle CME \text{ are equal in every part} @@ side-angle-side
\angle MCE = \angle MAB = \angle BAC @@ matching parts
E \text{ lies inside } \angle ACD @@ E is on the far side of AC from B, and on the same side of BC as A
\htmlClass{new}{\angle ACD > \angle ACE = \angle BAC} \quad \blacksquare @@ a part is smaller than the whole angle; the angle at B the same way, using the midpoint of BC

No line in this proof asks whether two lines meet. The outside-angle technique holds in every geometry that obeys the other axioms.

29a2At least one line that never meets

Stands on 29a1.

Two lines at right angles to one line: could they meet? If they met at P, the triangle they make with the first line would have two right angles. The outside angle at one of them is also a right angle, and it would have to be larger than the right angle across from it.

Never-meet technique: two lines at right angles to the same line never meet.

m \perp \ell \text{ at } A,\ \ n \perp \ell \text{ at } B,\ \ A \ne B @@ the setting
\text{suppose } m \text{ and } n \text{ meet at } P @@ to be refuted
\angle PAB = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \ \text{the outside angle at } B \text{ is } \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ both lines are at right angles to ℓ
\tfrac{\pi}{2} > \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ outside-angle (29a1)
\htmlClass{new}{m \text{ and } n \text{ never meet}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition gave a false line

So through a point P off a line ℓ there is always at least one line that never meets ℓ: drop the line from P at right angles to ℓ, then take the line through P at right angles to that one. The other axioms give at least one. The parallel axiom asks for at most one. That half is the whole question.

29a3The upright figure

Stands on 29a1, 20f.

Take a segment AB as a base. At A and at B raise sides AD and BC at right angles to it, of equal length and on the same side. Join D to C. This four-sided figure is the upright figure, and its angles at D and at C are its top angles. In ℝ² the top angles are right and the figure is a rectangle. Without the parallel axiom, that is the open question. One thing can be settled now.

Top-angles technique: the two top angles of an upright figure are equal.

AD = BC,\ \ \angle DAB = \angle CBA = \tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \ AB = BA @@ the upright figure
\triangle DAB \text{ and } \triangle CBA \text{ are equal in every part} @@ side-angle-side
DB = CA @@ matching parts: the two diagonals
AD = BC,\ \ DC = CD,\ \ AC = BD @@ the line above, and the shared top
\triangle ADC \text{ and } \triangle BCD \text{ are equal in every part} @@ side-side-side (20f)
\htmlClass{new}{\angle ADC = \angle BCD} \quad \blacksquare @@ matching parts: the top angles

Equal, then. But equal to what? There are three cases. The top angles are right, or both blunt (larger than right), or both sharp (smaller than right). Accepted names: obtuse and acute.

29a4No more than a straight angle

Stands on 29a1, the other axioms.

In ℝ² a triangle’s angles add to π, the straight angle (18f). That proof used parallel-through-a-point. Without it, half the answer can still be proved.

No-more-than-straight technique: the angles of every triangle add to at most π.

\triangle ABC,\ \ S = \angle A + \angle B + \angle C @@ the setting
M \text{ the midpoint of } BC,\ \ E \text{ on ray } AM,\ \ ME = AM @@ lay off a length
\triangle AMB \text{ and } \triangle EMC \text{ are equal in every part} @@ side-angle-side, opposite angles at M
\angle MEC = \angle BAM,\ \ \angle ECM = \angle ABC @@ matching parts
\text{angles of } \triangle AEC:\ \angle CAM,\ \ \angle BAM,\ \ \angle ACB + \angle ABC @@ the angle at E is ∠MEC; the angle at C is ∠ACB plus ∠ECM
\text{their sum is } \angle A + \angle B + \angle C = S @@ ∠CAM + ∠BAM = ∠A
\htmlClass{new}{\triangle AEC \text{ has sum } S \text{ and an angle at most } \tfrac12 \angle A} \quad \blacksquare @@ ∠CAM + ∠BAM = ∠A, so one of them is at most half
  
\text{suppose } S = \pi + \varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon > 0 @@ to be refuted
\text{a triangle with sum } S \text{ and an angle at most } \angle A / 2^n @@ the halving move, n times
\angle A / 2^n < \varepsilon \text{ for large } n @@ the last of the other axioms: ε doubled often enough passes ∠A
\text{its other two angles add to more than } \pi @@ S minus an angle smaller than ε
\text{two angles of a triangle add to less than } \pi @@ outside-angle: the outside angle at one is π minus it, and is larger than the other
\htmlClass{new}{S \le \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the supposition gave a false line

29a5The trial of 1733

Stands on 29a3, 29a4.

Back to the upright figure. Its diagonal cuts it into two triangles. Each has angles adding to at most π, so the whole figure has angles adding to at most 2π. Two of its angles are right, and the other two are the equal top angles t.

No-blunt-top technique: the top angles of an upright figure are right or sharp, never blunt.

\tfrac{\pi}{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{2} + t + t \le 2\pi @@ the two triangles, no-more-than-straight; their angles fill the figure’s
\htmlClass{new}{t \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract π, halve

With the parallel axiom, the top angles are right, as in ℝ² (18f’s angle sum used only the parallel statement and the moves). So the axiom is proved exactly when the sharp case is refuted. In 1733 a teacher of mathematics set out to do just that. He assumed sharp top angles and worked forward, looking for a false line. He found a great deal: triangles whose angles add to less than π, and lines that come nearer and nearer to one another without meeting. None of it was false. At the end he declared the sharp case repugnant to the nature of the straight line, and so refuted.

His last line cited no axiom. Two lines that come nearer forever and never meet are strange, but strange is not a false line. The trial was not over. 29c finishes it.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The upright figure is the Saccheri quadrilateral, with its base, legs and summit; its top angles are the summit angles. The outside-angle technique is the exterior angle theorem (Euclid’s Elements, Book I, proposition 16). No-more-than-straight is the Saccheri–Legendre theorem. The three cases are the hypotheses of the right, obtuse and acute angle. The other axioms, set out in full, are the axioms of neutral (or absolute) geometry; the at-most-one form of the parallel axiom is Playfair’s axiom.

Who said it first

Euclid’s fifth postulate (about 300 BCE) is longer than the other four, and many tried to prove it from them: Ptolemy, Proclus in the fifth century, Ibn al-Haytham, and Omar Khayyam in the eleventh century, who studied the same four-sided figure. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi took it up in the thirteenth century. Girolamo Saccheri, a Jesuit who taught mathematics at Pavia, published Euclid Freed of Every Flaw in 1733, the year he died. He refuted the obtuse case and believed he had refuted the acute one. Johann Heinrich Lambert went further in 1766 and saw that the acute case looked like geometry on a sphere of imaginary radius. Adrien-Marie Legendre gave the halving proof of the angle-sum bound in the many editions of his Elements of Geometry from 1794 on, and kept trying, and failing, to prove the rest.

Worked wrong proofs, labelled “not allowed”

Not allowed: “Lines that never meet are everywhere the same distance apart, so the top side of an upright figure is straight across and the top angles are right.” The first clause is the parallel statement in disguise. Proclus’s proof of the fifth postulate in the fifth century leaned on a claim of this kind.
