13·phys · physics

How fast is a falling stone going right now?

How fast is a falling stone going right now? 9·phys wrote motion as a function of time and found average velocities by dividing a displacement by a time. One thing it took as understood: how fast a falling stone is going at the third second, when at a single instant no distance is covered and no time passes. Rung 13 is the answer. The velocity at an instant is the slope of the position, the limit of the average velocities. The acceleration is the slope of the velocity. With those two lines the old falling numbers come back as slopes, and a ball thrown upward can be followed to the top and down. And the second law of motion says what a force does: it sets the second slope.

Position is a function of time. Its slope at an instant is the velocity, in metres per second. The slope of the velocity is the acceleration, in metres per second per second. Near the ground, for a body falling freely, that last slope is the same at every instant, about 9.8.

What a velocity at an instant is

9·phys's position x(t) is a function: each instant t, in seconds, has one position, in metres from a mark. It is positive one way along the track and negative the other. Between the instants t and t + h the body's displacement is x(t + h) − x(t). 9·phys's average velocity over that stretch is the displacement divided by the time taken: exactly 13a's chord slope of x at t with change h. Let h head to 0, and the chord slopes head to the slope.

From here on, take a motion with position x(t). The velocity at the instant t, written v(t), is the slope of the position, x′(t). It is the limit of the average velocities (x(t + h) − x(t))/h as h → 0. In d-marks, read as one mark (13e), v = dx/dt. Its unit is the position's unit per the time's unit: a slope's unit is always the top's unit over the bottom's, 5j's per, so metres per second, m/s. The speed at the instant t is |v(t)|, velocity without its sign. 9·phys's v for a steady motion, and its v(t) = kt for the falling motion, were names for this before it was built. Below they are shown to agree with it. The accepted name for v(t) is the instantaneous velocity: the velocity at one instant.

From here on, the acceleration at the instant t, written a(t), is the slope of the velocity, v′(t) = x″(t), 13d's second slope. Its unit is metres per second, per second: m/s², 5j's acceleration, “how much a speed grows each second”, now said at an instant. In this page's working the letter a names an acceleration; 9·phys's k in v = kt was the same quantity, kept fixed, and the prose below keeps k for it.

The sign of the velocity says which way the body is going, by 13k's slope-sign-near. If v(t) > 0, then just after t the body is further along the track than at t, and just before it was less far. Acceleration's sign says which way the velocity is changing, not which way the body moves: a ball rising toward its top has positive velocity and negative acceleration.

An old answer called the velocity's ratio the last ratio of vanishing quantities. It is not the ratio before they vanish, nor after, but the ratio with which they vanish. Such ratios, it warned, are limits that the ratios approach as near as you please, and never pass. That “ratio with which they vanish” is the slope. 10a's game made “as near as you please” provable, and the velocity at an instant is that ratio for a displacement and a time.

The falling numbers, as slopes

9·phys proved the falling-distance technique: a body starting from rest, whose speed grows by equal amounts in equal times, covers x(t) = ½kt² in time t. It had to assume what “speed at an instant” meant. Now take the slopes of falling-distance's answer.

x(t) = \tfrac12 k\,t^2 @@ falling-distance (9·phys): from rest at the mark, k kept fixed, in m/s²
v(t) = x'(t) = \tfrac12 k \cdot 2t = \htmlClass{new}{k\,t} @@ the velocity line; power for slopes and constant-factor for slopes (13d)
a(t) = v'(t) = \htmlClass{new}{k} @@ slope-of-a-line: the slope of a linear function is its step

The velocity at an instant, now exactly defined, comes out as kt: 9·phys's definition, equal speed gained in equal times. The two agree, so 9·phys's informal v(t) = kt and this page's line name the same thing. Likewise 9·phys's steady motion, x = x₀ + vt (steady-motion), has slope v at every instant by slope-of-a-line: its v was the velocity all along. And the acceleration is k at every instant: a constant second slope is what “uniformly accelerated” means. Near the ground, ignoring the air, k is g. By international agreement in 1901 the standard value is exactly 9.80665 m/s². Measured values run from about 9.78 at the equator to 9.83 at the poles, so g ≈ 9.8 m/s². On the Moon it is about 1.62 m/s², on Mars about 3.71. 9·phys held the speed at an instant in trust, through one plain premise. This page says exactly what it is, and that flag is closed.

Thrown upward: the same second slope, a different start

Throw a ball straight up from the height y₀ with velocity v₀, and measure its height y upward, in metres. (It is y, not h: on this page h is the change of time in the velocity line.) The Earth pulls down, so the acceleration is −g. The height that does this is y(t) = y₀ + v₀t − ½gt². Check it by taking slopes: y′(t) = v₀ − gt, which is v₀ at t = 0, as it should be; and y″(t) = −g at every instant. That this is the only height with these starting values and this acceleration is rung 14's question; here we check that it fits.

y(t) = 19.6\,t - 4.9\,t^2 @@ thrown up at 19.6 m/s from the ground, g = 9.8 m/s²
v(t) = 19.6 - 9.8\,t @@ polynomial-slope: term by term
\htmlClass{new}{19.6 - 9.8\,t = 0} \;\Rightarrow\; t = 2 \ \text{s} @@ at the top the velocity is 0: a condition on t, true at one instant (adding-keeps-answers, multiplying-keeps-answers)
y(2) = 39.2 - 19.6 = \htmlClass{new}{19.6} \ \text{m} @@ the height then
a(2) = \htmlClass{keep}{-9.8} \ \text{m/s}^2 @@ not 0: the velocity is still being changed at the top
y(4) = 0,\ \ v(4) = -19.6 \ \text{m/s} @@ it lands at 4 s, at the speed it left with, going down

The fifth line is where intuition misleads. At the top the ball is still for an instant, and it is tempting to say nothing is happening to it. But acceleration is the slope of the velocity, and the velocity v = 19.6 − 9.8t is passing through 0 with slope −9.8, not resting at 0. If the acceleration were 0 at every instant from the top on, the velocity would stay 0 and the ball would hang there. That a slope of 0 everywhere means no change at all is held in trust here, until 14d proves it. Zero velocity and zero acceleration are different things: one is a value of the velocity, the other its slope.

Average and instant

The average velocity over a stretch is a chord slope; the velocity at an instant is a slope. They answer different questions, and the commonest slip in mechanics is to answer one with the other. For a stone dropped from rest, 44.1/3 = 14.7 m/s is its average velocity over the first 3 s; its velocity at 3 s is 29.4 m/s. For the falling motion the two meet at the halfway instant. For x = t³ (metres, seconds) over [0, 2] they do not. The chord slope is 8/2 = 4 m/s, and the velocity at the halfway instant 1 s is 3 m/s. The plain average of the end velocities, 0 and 12, is 6 m/s. Three different numbers. Some instant does have velocity exactly 4: 3t² = 4 at t = 2/√3 ≈ 1.15 s. That every such motion has such an instant is held in trust here, until 14c proves it.

The second law of motion, named

Why should physics care about the second slope rather than the first or the third? Because force sets it. 12·phys gave the newton its line: the force that, acting alone, changes the speed of 1 kg by 1 m/s each second. With acceleration now a slope, that says it for every instant and every mass.

The second law was first stated in words: the change of motion is proportional to the motive force impressed, and takes place along the straight line in which that force is impressed. Its “motion” is the mass times the velocity, and its change per second is the mass times the acceleration. So, in words: a force F, acting alone on a body of mass m, gives it at every instant the acceleration F/m. So F is m times the acceleration, in newtons, when m is in kilograms and the acceleration is in m/s². The units check by 5j's rules: kg · m/s² is 12·phys's newton. Two readings follow.

  • 12·phys's weight is m · g, so a body falling freely has acceleration (m · g)/m = g, whatever its mass. In empty space a feather and a hammer fall alike; on the Moon in 1971 an astronaut dropped the two together, and they landed together.
  • A 1200 kg car braking with a force of 6000 N has acceleration −5 m/s². From 25 m/s (90 km/h) its velocity is v = 25 − 5t, 0 at t = 5 s. Its position x = 25t − 2.5t² is 62.5 m then: v is x's slope (power for slopes, polynomial-slope), as it must be.

A constant force gives a constant second slope, which is why free fall fits falling-distance's ½gt². That it is the only fit is rung 14's question. The times-squared law was first found by experiment; the second slope later explained it.

Where the clean story stops

“Falling freely” means the air is ignored. A real falling body is slowed by the air, more the faster it goes. So the force on it, and with it the acceleration, shrinks as it speeds up. The velocity levels off at a terminal velocity: about 50 to 55 m/s for a skydiver lying flat. On the position picture the graph straightens; on the velocity picture it levels off; its slope, the acceleration, heads to 0. You can read all of that from slopes without solving anything, which is the lesson of the page: once velocity and acceleration are slopes, every motion's pictures tell its story.

Running it backwards: from a velocity to a position

The page took slopes: position to velocity, velocity to acceleration. A speedometer hands you the velocity, and you want the position. Run 13d's rules back: find a function whose slope is the velocity, then fix the one thing a slope cannot see, where the body started.

Every position that fits differs from this one by a constant. That is held in trust here, until 14e proves it: for a velocity given at every instant of an interval, a constant is the only freedom.

Who said it first

The falling numbers, the falling-distance technique and the tilted board are Galileo Galilei’s, in Two New Sciences (1638). The last ratio of vanishing quantities and the second law of motion are Isaac Newton’s, in the Principia (1687); the second law is Newton’s second law. The d-mark is Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s. The arrow puzzle is Zeno of Elea’s.

Newton, in the Principia, calls the last ratio of vanishing quantities not the ratio before they vanish, nor after, but the ratio with which they vanish; and he warns that such ratios are limits that the ratios of the quantities approach as near as you please, and never pass.

Newton, Principia (1687), Book I, Section I, Scholium to Lemma XI; after Motte (1729)

Galileo states that a body starting from rest and uniformly accelerated covers a distance in the same time as it would at a steady speed equal to half its final speed.

Galileo, Two New Sciences (1638), Third Day, Theorem I; after Crew and de Salvio (1914)

Newton's second law: the change of motion is proportional to the motive force impressed, and takes place along the straight line in which that force is impressed.

Newton, Principia (1687), Axioms, or Laws of Motion, Law II; after Motte (1729)

Accepted names: the velocity at an instant, x′(t), is the instantaneous velocity.
