28o · technique

Gradient descent

To find the bottom of a valley in fog, take a step in the steepest way down, look again, and take another. With a formula in place of a valley, the steepest way down is minus the uphill arrow, and the walk is a few multiplications per step, even with a million inputs where no one could solve for the flat point. This step proves that a small enough step always goes down. Then it asks how big a step can be. Too small and the walk crawls; too big and it bounces from wall to wall and climbs out. In a long narrow valley the walls are steep and the floor is gentle, and the walk is slow however the step is chosen. Last, it finds the valleys where the walk cannot be fooled: those that bend up along every line have one bottom, and any flat point is it.

Step against the uphill arrow: a small enough step always goes down. In a bowl, the step must be shorter than two over the steepest bend, and the best step balances the steepest bend against the gentlest. A valley that bends up along every line has exactly one bottom.

28o1A step against the uphill arrow

Stands on 28g1 (the uphill technique), 28c3 (the roads-add technique) and 13k (the slope-sign-near technique).

By 28g1, the steepest way down from p is −∇f(p). A downhill step of size η > 0 goes from p to p − η∇f(p). Repeated, it is the walk xk+1 = xk − η∇f(xk). Accepted name: gradient descent.

Downhill-step technique: let f be smoothly sloped near p, with ∇f(p) ≠ 0. Then some η₀ > 0 makes f(p − η∇f(p)) < f(p) for every η with 0 < η < η₀.

g'(0) = \nabla f(p)\cdot\big(-\nabla f(p)\big) = -|\nabla f(p)|^2 @@ the roads-add technique (28c3), with the moving point p − η∇f(p)
g'(0) < 0 @@ ∇f(p) ≠ 0, so its length is not 0 (18a)
\htmlClass{new}{g(\eta) < g(0) \text{ for } 0 < \eta < \eta_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope-sign-near technique (13k), for −g: where the slope is negative, lower just to the right
  
f = (x - 3)^2 + 2(y + 1)^2,\ \ \nabla f = \big(2(x - 3),\ 4(y + 1)\big),\ \ \eta = 0.1 @@ a bowl with its bottom at (3, −1)
(0, 0):\ \ f = 11,\ \ \nabla f = (-6,\ 4) @@ the start
(0.6,\ -0.4):\ \ f = 6.48,\ \ \nabla f = (-4.8,\ 2.4) @@ one step: (0, 0) − 0.1(−6, 4)
\htmlClass{new}{(1.08,\ -0.64):\ \ f = 3.9456} \quad \blacksquare @@ two steps: down from 11, toward 0 at the bottom

The technique promises some step that goes down, not which one. A walk that uses one fixed η can still go wrong, and the next footing finds when. It also promises nothing at a flat point: there the uphill arrow is 0, the walk stops, and whether it has stopped at a bottom is a question for 28l.

28o2How big a step

Stands on 28o1, 28a2 (the one-knob technique), 22h2 (the standard-sequences technique), 4c (induction) and 24i (the stiff-step technique).

A bowl is f(x) = ½(λ₁x₁² + ⋯ + λₙxₙ²) with 0 < λ₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ λₙ: its bottom is 0, and λᵢ is how sharply it bends along the i-th knob. Near a bottom, 28l’s hug makes every smooth valley look like a bowl, turned to some directions.

Step-size technique: on a bowl, the walk with step η heads to the bottom from every start exactly when 0 < η < 2/λₙ. Each step shrinks the distance to the bottom by a factor of at most the largest |1 − ηλᵢ|. That factor is least at η = 2/(λ₁ + λₙ), where it is (λₙ − λ₁)/(λₙ + λ₁).

\nabla f(x) = (\lambda_1x_1, \ldots, \lambda_nx_n) @@ the one-knob technique (28a2)
x_i \mapsto x_i - \eta \lambda_ix_i = (1 - \eta \lambda_i)\,x_i @@ one step, place by place: the places do not mix
x_i \text{ after } k \text{ steps} = (1 - \eta \lambda_i)^k\,x_i @@ induction (4c)
\to 0 \text{ for every start} \iff |1 - \eta \lambda_i| < 1 \text{ for every } i \iff 0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_n @@ the standard-sequences technique (22h2): rᵏ → 0 exactly when |r| < 1; λₙ is the largest
|x \text{ after one step}| \le \max_i|1 - \eta \lambda_i|\;|x| @@ each place shrinks by at least that factor
\htmlClass{new}{\text{least at } 1 - \eta \lambda_1 = \eta \lambda_n - 1:\ \ \eta = \tfrac{2}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_n},\ \ \text{factor } \tfrac{\lambda_n - \lambda_1}{\lambda_n + \lambda_1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the largest is |1 − ηλ₁| or |1 − ηλₙ|; one falls and the other rises as η grows
  
f = \tfrac12(x^2 + 10y^2):\ \ \lambda_1 = 1,\ \lambda_2 = 10 @@ ten times steeper across than along
\eta < 0.2 @@ the step-size technique; at η = 0.2 the y place flips sign forever
\eta = \tfrac{2}{11} \approx 0.18:\ \ \text{factor } \tfrac{9}{11} \approx 0.82 @@ the best step
\htmlClass{new}{(9/11)^k \le 0.001 \text{ for } k \ge 35} \quad \blacksquare @@ 35 steps to cut the distance a thousandfold: ln 1000/ln(11/9) ≈ 34.4

Each downhill step on a bowl is a forward step of 24i on the rate rule x′ = −∇f(x), place by place xᵢ′ = −λᵢxᵢ, with η as the step length. The stiff-step technique of 24i asked for hk < 2, and here it is again as ηλᵢ < 2. A narrow valley is a stiff rule: the steep walls force a short step, and the gentle floor then takes many of them.

28o3Valleys with one bottom

Stands on 28l1 (the surface-match technique), 28l2 (the grid-test technique), 28c3, 14d (the slope-sign-order technique) and 14g (the bends-up technique).

14g said f bends up on an interval when, between any two inputs, f lies below the chord. A function of two inputs bends up along every line when for every p and every arrow h ≠ 0 the curve t ↦ f(p + th) bends up.

One-bottom technique: let f have continuous second partial slopes on the whole plane, with fxx > 0 and D = fxxfyy − fxy² > 0 at every point. Then f bends up along every line. And if ∇f(p) = 0, then f(q) > f(p) for every q ≠ p: p is the one bottom, and the lowest point of all.

g(t) = f(p + th),\ \ h = q - p \ne 0 @@ f along the line through p and q
g''(t) = h^{\mathsf T}H(p + th)\,h > 0 @@ as in 28l1; A > 0 and D > 0 make the form positive off 0, by the square completed in 28l2
g' \text{ is increasing, so } g \text{ bends up} @@ the slope-sign-order technique (14d) on g′; the bends-up technique (14g)
g(1) > g(0) + g'(0)\cdot 1 @@ the bends-up technique (14g): g lies above its tangent line away from 0
g'(0) = \nabla f(p)\cdot h = 0 @@ the roads-add technique (28c3); p is flat
\htmlClass{new}{f(q) = g(1) > g(0) = f(p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every q ≠ p
  
f = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x:\ \ f_{xx} = 2,\ f_{xy} = 1,\ f_{yy} = 2,\ D = 3 @@ the same at every point: f bends up along every line
\nabla f = (2x + y - 3,\ x + 2y) = 0:\ \ (x, y) = (2, -1),\ \ f = -3 @@ two samenesses; the one bottom
\text{descent with } \eta = 0.5 \text{ from } (0, 0):\ (1.5, 0),\ (1.5, -0.75),\ (1.875, -0.75),\ \ldots @@ each step cuts the distance of one place to the bottom by four
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the walk heads to } (2, -1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one-bottom technique: a flat point it reaches can only be the bottom

If a walk with a fixed step settles at a point x*, then xk+1 − xk = −η∇f(xk) heads to 0, so ∇f(x*) = 0 by continuity: walks only settle at flat points. On a valley that bends up along every line there is one flat point, so a walk that settles has found the bottom, whatever its start. Elsewhere a walk can settle in a shallow dip and never learn of a deeper one.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The walk is gradient descent (or steepest descent), and η the step size, or in machine learning the learning rate. A bowl is a quadratic function; λₙ/λ₁ is the condition number of its Hessian, and the factor (λₙ − λ₁)/(λₙ + λ₁) is the convergence rate of gradient descent with the best fixed step. A function that bends up along every line is strictly convex; the one-bottom technique says a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer, and any critical point is the global minimum. The tangent step on the uphill arrow is Newton’s method for minimization.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proposed the method of steepest descent in 1847, to solve the equations of the orbits of stars. Johan Jensen defined convex functions in 1906. Haskell Curry studied the method’s convergence in 1944. Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro introduced the walk with noisy slopes in 1951, the ancestor of how machines are trained today.
