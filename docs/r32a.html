32a · proof

Coefficients as Projections

A tide gauge at the foot of a pier, a heartbeat on a monitor, the hum of a fridge: each one repeats. After some fixed stretch of time it is back where it started, and it goes round again. Rung 26 found the simplest thing that repeats. Feed the growth function a turn instead of a number, and it runs round the circle of size one, once for each length of the circle, for ever. Run it twice as fast and it goes round twice in the same stretch. Three times as fast, three times. These are the turnings, one for every whole number, forwards and backwards. Rung 31 gave functions lengths and angles, so that two functions can be at right angles just as two vectors can, and it found the closest point to a function inside a space built from a list of functions at right angles. This step puts the two together. The turnings turn out to be at right angles to each other, every one to every other, and each has length one. So the share of a repeating function that lies along a turning is its shadow on that turning, found by one total, and the shadows do not move when more turnings join the list.

Any two different turnings are at right angles, and each has size one. So the closest sum of turnings to a repeating function is the sum of its shadows on them, each shadow found by one total and fixed once found. For a real function the turnings pair into a cosine and a sine of each speed, and an even function needs cosines alone.

32a1The Turnings Are at Right Angles

A function f on the line repeats every 2π when f(x + 2π) = f(x) for every x. Its accepted name is periodic, with period 2π. Such a function is known from its values on [−π, π], so the product of 31a1 can be taken there. On this rung it carries the weight 1/2π, so that the constant function 1 has size 1:

For each whole number n, positive, zero or negative, the nth turning is eₙ(x) = e^(inx). By the turn-growth technique (26a3) it is cos nx + i sin nx: as x runs once along [−π, π], it runs n times round the circle of size 1, backwards when n is negative. By the whole-turn technique (26b) it repeats every 2π.

Right-angled-turnings technique: any two different turnings are at right angles, and every turning has size 1; so the turnings make a right-angled list.

e_m(x)\,\overline{e_n(x)} = e^{imx}e^{-inx} = e^{i(m - n)x} @@ the flip of e^(iθ) is e^(−iθ) (26a); the plane-adding technique (26b)
k = m - n \ne 0:\ \ \tfrac{d}{dx}\,\tfrac{e^{ikx}}{ik} = e^{ikx} @@ the complex-rate technique (26·cb24), with rate ik
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{ikx}\,dx = \tfrac{e^{ik\pi} - e^{-ik\pi}}{ik} = 0 @@ area as a change (16), floor and upright parts each; e^(ikπ) = e^(−ikπ), since they differ by a whole number of 2πi (26b)
m = n:\ \ \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1\,dx = 1 @@ e^(i0x) = 1
\htmlClass{new}{\langle e_m, e_n\rangle = 1 \text{ if } m = n,\ \ 0 \text{ if } m \ne n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the product, with its weight

Being at right angles here is a claim about totals, not about where graphs cross. cos x and sin x meet at π/4, and still ⟨cos, sin⟩ = 0: over a whole turn their product is above the floor exactly as much as below it.

32a2A Coefficient Is a Shadow

Let f repeat every 2π, be sized, and have finite size ‖f‖ (31g). Its nth coefficient is its shadow on the nth turning, cₙ = ⟨f, eₙ⟩. Its Nth partial sum is S_N f = Σ cₙeₙ, over n from −N to N. The accepted names are the Fourier coefficient and the Fourier partial sum.

Turning-shadow technique: among all sums of the turnings from −N to N, the one closest to f is the sum of f’s shadows on them; and a shadow, once found, does not change when more turnings are added.

c_n = \langle f, e_n\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\,e^{-inx}\,dx @@ the product, with the flip of e^(inx)
e_{-N}, \ldots, e_N \text{ is a right-angled list} @@ the right-angled-turnings technique (32a1)
\|f - \textstyle\sum b_ne_n\| \text{ is least exactly when } b_n = c_n @@ the shadow-sum technique (31b2): the closest point is the sum of the shadows
\langle f - S_Nf,\ e_k\rangle = c_k - c_k = 0 \text{ for } |k| \le N @@ the miss is at right angles to every turning used
\|f\|^2 = \textstyle\sum_{|n| \le N}|c_n|^2 + \|f - S_Nf\|^2 @@ sizes at right angles add as squares (31a)
\htmlClass{new}{c_n \text{ depends on } f \text{ and } n \text{ alone, not on } N} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first line names no N

That last line is what right angles buy. Fit a sum of the powers 1, x, x² to a curve, and adding x³ changes every number found so far. Fit a sum of turnings, and each coefficient is found once, by its own total, and stays.

32a3Cosines and Sines

A real function has real values, so its coefficients come in flipped pairs, and each pair adds to a real wave.

Cosine-and-sine technique: for a real f, the turnings n and −n pair into a cosine and a sine of speed n; an even function needs cosines alone, and an odd function sines alone.

c_{-n} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int f(x)\,e^{inx}\,dx = \overline{c_n} @@ f is real, so the flip passes onto e^(−inx) (25)
c_ne^{inx} + c_{-n}e^{-inx} = 2\,\text{floor part of } c_ne^{inx} @@ z + z̄ is twice the floor part (25)
= a_n\cos nx + b_n\sin nx,\ \ a_n = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos nx\,dx,\ \ b_n = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin nx\,dx @@ the turn-growth technique (26a), multiplied out
f \text{ even: } f(x)\sin nx \text{ is odd, so } b_n = 0 @@ an odd function totals 0 over [−π, π] (9e: the halves cancel)
\htmlClass{new}{S_Nf = \tfrac{a_0}{2} + \textstyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n\cos nx + b_n\sin nx)} \quad \blacksquare @@ for f odd, a_n = 0 the same way

Every function on [−π, π] is an even part plus an odd part, ½(f(x) + f(−x)) plus ½(f(x) − f(−x)). The cosines carry the first and the sines the second. The cosine-and-sine form is the one the sciences write; the turnings are the one that proves things, since multiplying turnings adds their speeds.

The accepted names

A function that repeats every 2π is 2π-periodic. The turnings are the complex exponentials e^(inx), the characters of the circle group. That they are at right angles is the orthogonality of the exponentials, or of the trigonometric system. The coefficients cₙ are the Fourier coefficients, the partial sums S_N f the Fourier partial sums, and the endless sum Σ cₙe^(inx) the Fourier series of f; aₙ and bₙ are the cosine and sine coefficients. A finite sum of turnings is a trigonometric polynomial. The fixed shadows are the reason the coefficient formula is often called the Euler–Fourier formula.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli proposed in 1753 that a vibrating string is a sum of sine waves. Leonhard Euler used the coefficient totals for particular sums in 1777. Joseph Fourier put the coefficients at the centre of his memoir on heat, read to the Paris Academy in 1807 and printed as The Analytical Theory of Heat in 1822, and claimed that every function has such a sum. The right-angle reading came much later, with the spaces of rung 31.

A proof that is not allowed

“Write f = Σ cₙeₙ, take the product of both sides with e_k, and swap the total with the endless sum: c_k = ⟨f, e_k⟩.” Not allowed yet: it supposes f is the sum, and that the total of an endless sum is the sum of the totals. The turning-shadow technique needs neither: it finds the closest finite sum, and 32b proves that those sums come as near as you like.
