Is the Machine Drifting?

Is the machine drifting? A lathe turns out shafts all day, each meant to be 25.00 mm across. No two come out exactly alike. When one measures 25.05, is the machine drifting, or is that just the day’s normal spread? Stopping a line costs money; not stopping it can cost more. This page finds that spreads add as squares (30), so a stack of parts is far tighter than the worst case 8·eng allowed for; draws a control chart three spreads wide with 30j; finds the chance that a point outside, or a run on one side, is luck; and ends with the cutting tool’s life as a line on log paper (17).

Independent errors add their squares, not their sizes, so n parts each of spread σ stack to σ√n, not nσ. A machine in control scatters around its mean with a steady spread; a point beyond three spreads, or seven in a row on one side, is too unlikely to be luck. The tool wears out faster as it cuts faster, as V·T^n = C, a straight line on log paper.

35stat1Spreads Add as Squares, Not as Sizes

Mike runs a machine shop. Each part he makes is a little off its drawing size, by an error that averages 0 and has a spread σ. When parts are stacked end to end, their errors add.

\text{spread}^2(e_1 + e_2) = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 @@ independent errors (30h)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread of } n \text{ equal parts} = \sigma\sqrt{n}} @@ not nσ

From here on, on this page, a part’s spread is its σ, and a machine is in control when its errors are independent with a steady mean and spread.

35stat2The Likely Stack Is Far Tighter Than the Worst Case

8·eng allowed for every part being at its worst at once. Nine parts each allowed ±0.1 mm can stack to ±0.9 mm in the worst case. But if ±0.1 mm is three spreads, each σ is 0.033 mm.

\text{worst case: } 9 \times 0.1 = 0.9 \text{ mm} @@ 8·eng
\text{spread of the stack: } 0.033 \times \sqrt 9 = 0.1 \text{ mm} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{three spreads: } \pm 0.3 \text{ mm, a third of the worst case}} @@ √n against n

Spread-stack technique: independent errors in a stack of n parts add to √n times one part’s spread, not n times; the worst case is so unlikely that designing for it wastes precision. Mike can give each part a looser, cheaper allowance and still have 997 stacks in 1,000 fit.

35stat3A Control Chart: Limits Three Spreads From the Mean

Every hour Mike measures a shaft and plots it on a chart with a centre line at the mean, 25.00 mm, and limits three spreads either side. His lathe’s spread, measured over a week in control, is 0.02 mm.

\text{centre: } 25.00 \text{ mm} @@ 
\text{limits: } 25.00 \pm 3 \times 0.02 = 24.94 \text{ and } 25.06 \text{ mm} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a machine in control stays inside } 997 \text{ times in } 1\,000} @@ the bell curve (30j)

35stat4A Point Outside, or a Run on One Side: the Chance of It by Luck

\text{one point outside the limits: chance } 0.003 \text{ by luck} @@ 
\text{seven in a row above the centre: } (1/2)^7 = 1/128 \approx 0.008 @@ each side equally likely
\htmlClass{new}{\text{either is rare enough to stop and look}} @@

A point beyond three spreads, or a long run on one side of the centre, is too rare to be luck in a machine in control, so it signals a cause: a worn tool, a warm spindle, a new batch of metal. On Tuesday Mike’s shafts read 25.01, 25.02, 25.01, 25.03, 25.02, 25.02, 25.03: all inside the limits, but seven above the centre. He checks, finds the tool worn, and replaces it, and the next readings scatter back about 25.00.

35stat5Tool Life Against Cutting Speed Is a Line on Log Paper

A cutting tool wears faster the faster it cuts. Over a wide range of speeds, its life T and the speed V fit one rule.

V\,T^{\,n} = C @@ V in metres a minute, T in minutes
\log V + n\log T = \log C @@ a straight line on log paper (17)
V = 200:\ T = (400/200)^4 = 16 \text{ min};\ \ V = 160:\ T = 2.5^4 \approx 39 \text{ min} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a fifth slower, nearly two and a half times the life}} @@

Tool life falls steeply with cutting speed, as a power, so a shop weighs faster cutting against more tool changes. Mike times tools to wear-out at three speeds; the points fall on a straight line on log paper, and he reads the best speed for each job from it.

Accepted names

Adding spreads as squares is root-sum-square tolerancing; the chart is a Shewhart control chart and the practice statistical process control; the run rules are the Western Electric rules. The tool rule is Taylor’s tool life equation.

Who said it first

Walter Shewhart drew the first control chart in 1924; Frederick Winslow Taylor published the tool life rule in 1907 (not yet revisited for the dates).
