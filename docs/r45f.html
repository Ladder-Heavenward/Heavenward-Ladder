45f · technique

Functions on a Shape

On the circle, x² + y² and 1 are the same function. The polynomial functions on a shape X are the polynomials, counted as the same when they differ by a member of I(X): the ring F[X] = F[x]/I(X). That ring knows the shape. Its points are its largest ideals, and maps between shapes are maps between their rings, run backwards. Two shapes are the same exactly when their rings are. This step builds the ring, turns maps around, and tells a parabola from a sharp curve.

F[X] = F[x₁, …, xₙ]/I(X). A polynomial map φ: X → Y gives φ*: F[Y] → F[X], g ↦ g ∘ φ, and every ring map comes from one map. So X and Y are the same shape exactly when F[X] and F[Y] are the same ring. The parabola has ring F[t], the line’s; the curve y² = x³ has F[t², t³], which is not.

45f1The Ring of a Shape

f \sim g \iff f - g \in I(X) \iff f = g \text{ on } X @@ classes are functions on X (44b)
\text{circle: } x^2 + y^2 \sim 1 @@ one function
\text{points of } X \leftrightarrow \text{largest ideals of } \mathbb C[X] @@ by 45d2, the largest ideals holding I(X)
\htmlClass{new}{X \text{ whole} \iff \mathbb C[X] \text{ has no zero products}} @@ prime ideal, cancelling ring (44b, 45e1)

Shape-ring technique: the polynomial functions on X form the ring F[X] = F[x]/I(X); over ℂ its largest ideals are the points of X, and it cancels exactly when X is whole.

45f2Maps Run Backwards

\varphi^*(g) = g \circ \varphi @@ a function on Y becomes one on X
(g + h)\circ\varphi = g\circ\varphi + h\circ\varphi,\ \ (gh)\circ\varphi = (g\circ\varphi)(h\circ\varphi) @@ φ* keeps sums and products
\psi: F[Y] \to F[X] \Rightarrow \varphi = (\psi(y_1), \ldots, \psi(y_m)) @@ every ring map comes from one map
\htmlClass{new}{X \cong Y \iff F[X] \cong F[Y]} @@ shapes and rings, with arrows reversed

Backwards technique: polynomial maps X → Y match the ring maps F[Y] → F[X] that fix F, one to one, with composition reversed; so two shapes are the same exactly when their rings are.

45f3A Parabola and a Sharp Curve

\text{parabola } y = x^2:\ F[x, y]/(y - x^2) \cong F[x] @@ y is x²: the line’s ring
\text{sharp curve } y^2 = x^3:\ t \mapsto (t^2, t^3),\ \text{ring } F[t^2, t^3] @@ all polynomials in t with no t term
F[t^2, t^3] \not\cong F[t] @@ its largest ideal at 0 needs two makers, t² and t³
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the sharp curve is not a line, though } t \mapsto (t^2, t^3) \text{ is one to one}} @@ the map cannot be undone by polynomials

The parabola is a line in disguise; the sharp curve y² = x³ is not, though it is traced once by t ↦ (t², t³), because its ring is missing t.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

F[X] is the coordinate ring; polynomial maps are morphisms; maps running backwards is the equivalence of categories between affine varieties and finitely generated reduced algebras. y² = x³ is the cuspidal cubic.

Who said it first

Richard Dedekind and Heinrich Weber treated curves through their rings of functions in 1882; Alexander Grothendieck built all of algebraic geometry on rings from 1958.
