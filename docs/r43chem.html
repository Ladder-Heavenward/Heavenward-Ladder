Why Does One Fuel Hold More Than Another?

Why does one fuel hold more than another? Randy fills a camping stove with alcohol and a lamp with paraffin, the same weight of each. The paraffin boils nearly twice as much water. Both are carbon, hydrogen and little else. This page finds where the difference is kept.

The heat of burning is the heat of the bonds made less the heat of the bonds broken, and it comes out close to a fixed amount for each molecule of oxygen taken up. A fuel that already holds oxygen has less left to give.

The three at the top are carbon and hydrogen only. Coal has less hydrogen for its carbon. The two alcohols carry an oxygen atom. The rest of the page explains the order.

43chem2Bonds Broken, Bonds Made

11chem11 kept a ledger for a salt. The same ledger works for burning. Breaking a bond always takes heat in. Making one gives the same heat out. Each kind of bond has its price, nearly the same from molecule to molecule: a C-H bond costs between 377 and 438 kJ/mol across five different molecules, within 8 in 100 of the average.

\text{C-H 416, C-C 342} @@ two bond prices
\htmlClass{new}{\text{O=O 498, C=O in CO}_{2}\text{ 799, O-H 463}} @@ three more

Methane, CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O:

\text{4 }\times \text{ 416 + 2 }\times \text{ 498 = 2660} @@ broken
\text{2 }\times \text{ 799 + 4 }\times \text{ 463 = 3450} @@ made
\htmlClass{new}{\text{2660 }-\text{ 3450 = }-\text{790 kJ/mol}} @@ heat given out

The ledger shows where the heat comes from. The bonds in the fuel are not weak. The O=O bond is the cheap one to break for what it becomes: each oxygen atom ends in bonds worth far more.

Bond technique: the heat of a reaction is close to the sum of the prices of the bonds broken less the sum of the prices of the bonds made.

43chem3From the Elements Up

Bond prices are averages, so the ledger is a few parts in 100 out. An exact ledger uses one measured number for each compound: the heat of making it from its elements. 28·chem’s point is what allows it: the heat between a start and an end does not depend on the road, so any reaction can be routed through the elements.

CO₂: −393.5 kJ/mol; H₂O (liquid): −285.8; O₂: 0, being an element already; glucose: −1260.

Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O:

\text{6 }\times \text{ (}-\text{393.5) + 6 }\times \text{ (}-\text{285.8) = }-\text{4075.8} @@ products
-\text{1260} @@ reactants
\htmlClass{new}{-4075.8 - (-1260) = -2816\text{ kJ/mol}} @@ products less reactants

The bond ledger for glucose gives −2740, within 3 in 100.

Elements technique: the heat of a reaction is the heats of making the products from their elements, less the heats of making the reactants.

43chem4Already Half Burnt

Now the table of 43chem1 can be read. Take the heat for each mole of fuel and divide by the O₂ that 27chem8’s gap count says it needs.

fuelkJ per moleO₂ neededkJ per mole of O₂
petrol, per CH1.876101.47416

ethanol C₂H₆O11963399
methanol CH₄O6401.5427

The last column hardly moves. Whatever the fuel, about 400 to 430 kJ comes out for each mole of oxygen taken up.

So the order of the first table is an order of oxygen appetite. Methanol’s carbon already has one bond to oxygen. It needs 1.5 O₂ where methane needs 2, and half its weight is the oxygen it brought. Glucose brings six. A fat is nearly all CH₂. That is why a body stores fat and not sugar when weight matters (19·bio).

Oxygen technique: the heat a compound of carbon and hydrogen gives on burning is close to a fixed amount for each molecule of oxygen it takes up, so the gap count ranks fuels.

1.42 \times 2.44 = 3.47\text{ MJ} @@ kg of water times the heat to boil a kg

43chem6Shut In or Open

Jean measures a heat of burning in a sealed steel vessel sunk in water, and reads the water’s rise in temperature. The vessel cannot swell, so the gases do no pushing. In an open flame they push the air back, or are pushed. The two heats differ by that work.

One case: 4 HCl + O₂ → 2 H₂O (liquid) + 2 Cl₂ at 298 K; five moles of gas become two, Δn = −3.

-\text{195.0 kJ} @@ shut in
-3 \times 8.314 \times 298.15 = -7.4\text{ kJ} @@ Δn times R times T
\htmlClass{new}{-\text{202.4 kJ}} @@ open

Small, and it has to be carried in the ledger.

43chem8Downhill and Standing Still

All these heats are large and negative, and petrol sits in a can. 25·chem said which way a reaction runs; it did not say how fast. Glucose with oxygen is 2816 kJ/mol downhill and does nothing on a shelf. The hill of 41chem2 is why. A flame passes it by heat. A cell passes it with enzymes, and takes the same 2816 in small steps so that most of it can be caught (19·bio).

43chem9Fuel and Oxygen in One Compound

A log waits for air (41chem8). Ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, does not. It is sold by the tonne as fertiliser, and one end of it is nitrogen with hydrogen on, a fuel, and the other is nitrogen with oxygen on. Heated, it rearranges with nothing added.

\text{NH}_{4}\text{NO}_{3} \to \text{N}_{2}\text{O} + 2\,\text{H}_{2}\text{O} @@ a liquid gives two gases
\text{740 kJ for each kg} @@ heat given out
\text{one mole in, three moles of gas out} @@  
\htmlClass{new}{\text{1 kg is 12.5 mol, so 37.5 mol of gas}} @@ about 900 litres at room temperature from under a litre of solid, and far more when hot

Set that beside 43chem1. Petrol gives 44,000 kJ for each kg, sixty times as much. An explosive is a poor fuel. What it has is that nothing must arrive from outside, so all of it can react at once, and that its products are gas.

Blasting technique: a blasting compound is judged by how much gas it makes and how fast, since the work is done by the gas. Its heat for each kilogram is small beside a fuel’s.

43chem10The Reaction That Runs Away

A works made laughing gas by that same reaction, on purpose, in a stirred tank at 510 °F with 380 kg of molten ammonium nitrate in it. In 1977 it blew up.

\text{0.307 per hour} @@ rate constant at 510 °F
\text{2.91 per hour} @@ rate constant at 560 °F
\htmlClass{new}{\text{nearly ten times}} @@ for fifty degrees

That is 41chem2’s line. The tank was steady so long as cold feed kept coming in, since warming the feed took up most of the heat: of 86 MJ made each hour, the feed took 82. The operators shut the feed off. The heat then had nowhere to go, the tank warmed, the rate rose, and the model reaches the blast in about four minutes.

Runaway technique: heat made rises with temperature as e−E/RT and heat removed rises only in step with temperature, so above some temperature a reaction that gives out heat cannot be held.

43chem11Burning or Bursting

Black powder burns: each grain lights the next by heat, as a flame does, fast but slower than sound. It pushes. It throws a ball down a barrel and heaves rock apart along its cracks.

Nitroglycerin does something else. A sharp enough knock squeezes and heats a layer so that it reacts at once, and the gas made squeezes the next layer. The reaction travels as a wave of pressure faster than sound in the stuff. It shatters. A quarry uses the one that suits the rock. Most blasting today is ammonium nitrate soaked with a little fuel oil, so that the spare oxygen of 43chem9 has something to burn.

3 \times 4 + 5 = 17 @@ nitroglycerin C₃H₅N₃O₉: gaps of the fuel part, needing 8.5 O; it carries 9. Nothing is wanted from outside
\htmlClass{new}{\text{33 gaps needing 16.5 O; it carries 6}} @@ TNT C₇H₅N₃O₆. It leaves soot

The gap count of 27chem8 says whether an explosive has enough oxygen of its own.

43chem12A Metal as the Fuel

Terry watches a rail gang join two rails. A clay pot above the joint holds a grey and red powder, aluminium and iron oxide. It is lit with a flare, burns for some seconds with almost no flame, and white-hot liquid iron runs down into the gap.

\text{2 Al + Fe}_{2}\text{O}_{3}\text{ }\to \text{ Al}_{2}\text{O}_{3}\text{ + 2 Fe} @@  
\htmlClass{new}{-1675.7 - (-824.2) = -851.5\text{ kJ}} @@ by 43chem3

213.7 g of powder, so 3.98 MJ for each kg.

No gas is made, so there is no blast. All the heat stays in two products with nowhere to spread, and that is why the iron comes out molten.

33eng31 took oxygen off iron with carbon. Aluminium does the same and far harder. The oxygen technique of 43chem4 ranks them: heat for each mole of O₂ taken up.

taking up oxygenkJ for each mole of O₂
aluminium1117
iron549
carbon and hydrogen compoundsabout 400

A metal high in the table takes oxygen from the oxide of one below it. That is how chromium and manganese are won, and why aluminium powder is the fuel in a solid rocket and magnesium in a flare. Carbon sits below iron in the table and still wins iron from its ore in a furnace. 28·chem holds why: its product is a gas, and heat favours the side with the gas.

Accepted names

Heat at constant pressure is enthalpy, H; the heat of a reaction is ΔH. A bond’s price is its bond enthalpy or bond dissociation energy. The heat of making a compound from its elements is its standard enthalpy of formation; routing through the elements is Hess’s law. The two heats of burning are the higher and lower heating value. The sealed vessel is a bomb calorimeter; shut in it measures ΔU. The near-constant heat for each mole of oxygen is Thornton’s rule, and it is how oxygen-use meters measure the heat of a fire or of an animal. Burning slower than sound is deflagration; the pressure wave is detonation; the two classes are low and high explosives. Ammonium nitrate with fuel oil is ANFO. Whether a compound carries enough oxygen is its oxygen balance. A reaction that outruns its cooling is thermal runaway. Aluminium with iron oxide is thermite, and winning a metal that way is aluminothermic reduction. The table of 43chem12 is a slice of an Ellingham diagram. Things that give up oxygen are oxidisers: nitrates, chlorates, perchlorates.
