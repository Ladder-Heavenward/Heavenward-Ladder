Where do a molecule’s electrons sit?

Where do a molecule’s electrons sit? Benzene is a ring of six carbon atoms, each holding one hydrogen. Drawn with pen and paper it has three double links and three single ones, taking turns round the ring. But a chemist in a lab in Seattle who measures it finds all six links the same length, 140 picometres, between a single link’s 154 and a double link’s 134. And benzene is steadier than three double links have any right to be: adding hydrogen to it gives out 152 kJ/mol less heat than three separate double links give. A ring of four carbons, drawn the same way, is so unsteady that it lasts only frozen in a cage of other atoms. 41·chem read a molecule’s orbitals off its grid of links. This page finds the stretches of a ring’s grid exactly, by the companion grid of 43·cb27, fills them with electrons, and counts which rings come out steady: those holding 2, 6, 10, … electrons, the rings of 4n + 2.

The links of a flat ring of n carbon atoms make a grid A = S + S⁻¹, where S turns the ring by one place. S is the companion grid of xⁿ − 1, whose zeros on ℂ are the n turnings ωᵏ, all different; so S and A are diagonal in one basis, and A’s stretches are 2cos(2πk/n). Filling these levels two electrons each, from the bottom, gives benzene 8 units of sharing where three double links give 6, and leaves a ring of n electrons with no half-filled level exactly when n = 4m + 2.

42chem1Levels from the grid of links

Number the carbon atoms of a flat molecule 1 to n. Its grid of links A has a 1 in place (j, k) when atom j is linked to atom k, and 0 elsewhere, as for any dots and links (41a). Each carbon of a flat ring or chain gives one electron to a cloud spread above and below the flat of the molecule. 41·chem took the places an electron of that cloud can sit, its orbitals, to be the stretched arrows of A.

Link-level technique: an orbital that is a stretched arrow of A with stretch x has energy α + xβ, where α and β are two numbers measured once for carbon, and β is below 0. This is a model from the physics of electrons, held in trust from 35·phys: α is an electron’s energy on one atom alone, and β the change from sharing across one link. Since β is below 0, a larger stretch x means a lower energy, and electrons sit there first.

A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} @@ two atoms, one link
A(1, 1) = 1 \cdot (1, 1),\ \ A(1, -1) = -1 \cdot (1, -1) @@ the stretched arrows, with stretches 1 and −1
\text{levels } \alpha + \beta \text{ and } \alpha - \beta @@ the link-level technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two electrons in } \alpha + \beta:\ \ 2\alpha + 2\beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sharing of one double link is 2β

From here on, the sharing of a molecule is the sum of x over its electrons, so that the energy of its cloud is (number of electrons)·α + (sharing)·β. Each double link drawn on paper is worth a sharing of 2.

42chem2A ring’s levels exactly

On a ring of n atoms, let S move the number on each atom to the next atom round. Then A = S + S⁻¹: each atom is linked to the one before and the one after. S done n times is no move at all, so Sⁿ = I. In the basis of the n atoms, S is the companion grid of xⁿ − 1 (43·cb27’s companion technique).

Ring-level technique: a ring of n atoms has the stretches 2cos(2πk/n) for k = 0, 1, …, n − 1, with the stretched arrow (1, ωᵏ, ω²ᵏ, …) where ω is the turning by 2π/n. The least polynomial of S is xⁿ − 1, whose n zeros, the turnings ωᵏ of 25d, are all different. By the plain-diagonal technique S is diagonal in a basis of stretched arrows, and the arrow with stretch ωᵏ has stretch ωᵏ + ω⁻ᵏ = 2cos(2πk/n) under A.

2\cos 0 = 2,\ \ 2\cos 60^\circ = 1,\ \ 2\cos 120^\circ = -1,\ \ 2\cos 180^\circ = -2 @@ k = 0, 1, 2, 3
k = 5 \text{ gives } 1 \text{ again},\ k = 4 \text{ gives } -1 @@ cos(360° − θ) = cos θ
A(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1) = 2(1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1) @@ check: each atom has two neighbours
\htmlClass{new}{2,\ 1,\ 1,\ -1,\ -1,\ -2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the six levels of benzene, one at the bottom, then pairs

The levels with stretch 1 come in a pair: (2, 1, −1, −2, −1, 1) and (0, 1, 1, 0, −1, −1) are both stretched by 1, and multiplying out A times each checks it. On every ring the levels pair up the same way, since k and n − k give the same cosine. Only k = 0 stands alone, and k = n/2 when n is even.

42chem3Why benzene’s six share so well

Fill-from-bottom technique: a molecule’s electrons fill its levels from the largest stretch down, two to a level, and when a pair of levels of the same stretch has fewer than four electrons, they spread one to a level before any doubles up. Two to a level is a rule of electrons from physics, held in trust from 35·phys, as is the spreading out.

6 \text{ electrons}:\ \ 2 \text{ in level } 2,\ \ 4 \text{ in the pair at } 1 @@ the fill-from-bottom technique
\text{sharing} = 2 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1 = 8 @@ against three double links drawn on paper
3 \text{ double links} = 3 \cdot 2 = 6 @@ 43·chem.1
\htmlClass{new}{8 - 6 = 2:\ \ \text{benzene is } 2|\beta| \text{ lower}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the six share the whole ring

Measured: adding hydrogen to one double link, in cyclohexene, gives out 120 kJ/mol. Three would give 360 kJ/mol, and benzene gives 208. By 11chem’s rule that heats add along any route, benzene sits 152 kJ/mol lower than three separate double links, so the model fits with |β| about 76 kJ/mol. The lowest level (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1) is the same on all six atoms, and the filled pair is spread evenly across the ring too, which is why the six links come out the same length.

42chem4Why rings of 4n + 2 are steady

By the ring-level technique, every ring has one level at the bottom, stretch 2, and above it pairs: on a ring of n atoms, the pairs k and n − k for k = 1, 2, …, below n/2, and one last level alone when n is even.

Full-pairs technique: on a ring, a cloud of e electrons leaves no level half filled, among the bottom level and the pairs above it, exactly when e = 4m + 2 for a whole number m. The bottom level takes 2, and each pair after it takes 4, so the full fillings are 2, 6, 10, 14, …, the numbers 4m + 2. Any other count leaves a pair with one, two or three electrons, and by the fill-from-bottom technique two of them sit alone.

n = 4:\ \ 2\cos(90^\circ k) = 2,\ 0,\ -2,\ 0 @@ the ring-level technique
4 \text{ electrons}:\ \ 2 \text{ at } 2,\ \text{one each at the pair } 0, 0 @@ the fill-from-bottom technique
\text{sharing} = 2 \cdot 2 + 0 + 0 = 4 = 2 \cdot 2 @@ the same as two double links drawn on paper
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no gain, and two electrons alone}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 4 = 4m + 2 has no whole solution m

The count is of electrons in the cloud, not atoms. A ring of five carbons with one extra electron holds 6: its levels are 2, 0.618, 0.618, −1.618, −1.618, its sharing is 4 + 4(0.618) ≈ 6.47, and it is steady, which is why that ring readily takes the extra electron. A ring of seven with one electron taken away also holds 6, with sharing about 8.99.

42chem5Chains against rings

A chain of n carbons, with no link from the last back to the first, is not a ring, and S is gone. Its levels come from a different arrow.

Chain-level technique: a chain of n atoms has the stretches 2cos(kπ/(n + 1)) for k = 1, …, n, with stretched arrow (sin(kθ), sin(2kθ), …, sin(nkθ)) where θ = π/(n + 1). The jth atom of A times that arrow is sin((j − 1)kθ) + sin((j + 1)kθ) = 2cos(kθ)·sin(jkθ), by 21d’s sum formulas; at the two ends the missing neighbour is sin(0) = 0 and sin((n + 1)kθ) = sin(kπ) = 0.

2\cos 36^\circ \approx 1.618,\ \ 2\cos 72^\circ \approx 0.618 @@ k = 1, 2, and their negatives for k = 3, 4
4 \text{ electrons}:\ \ 2(1.618 + 0.618) = 4.472 @@ the fill-from-bottom technique
\text{two double links drawn}:\ \ 4 @@ 43·chem.1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{gain } 0.472\,|\beta| \text{ for the chain}} \quad \blacksquare @@ against 2 for benzene’s ring

A chain of six, hexatriene, has sharing 2(1.802 + 1.247 + 0.445) ≈ 6.99, close to its three drawn double links. Closing it into benzene’s ring raises the sharing to 8: the ring gains about one whole |β| from its last link, which is the steadiness a chain does not have.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The link-level technique is Hückel molecular orbital theory, with α the Coulomb integral and β the resonance integral; it is the simplest LCAO model, a linear combination of atomic orbitals, and the grid of links is the adjacency matrix of the molecule’s graph. The cloud is the π system of the molecule. The sharing gained over drawn double links is the delocalisation, or resonance, energy. The full-pairs technique is Hückel’s 4n + 2 rule; molecules that obey it are aromatic and those with 4n electrons in a flat ring are antiaromatic. The fill-from-bottom technique is the aufbau principle with the Pauli exclusion principle and Hund’s rule. The ring of four is cyclobutadiene, the ring of five with an extra electron the cyclopentadienyl anion, the ring of seven with one taken away the tropylium cation, and the ring of eight cyclooctatetraene. The grid S + S⁻¹ is a circulant matrix, and the arrows (1, ωᵏ, ω²ᵏ, …) are the columns of the discrete Fourier transform.

Who said it first

Michael Faraday isolated benzene from lamp gas in 1825. August Kekulé proposed the ring of six in 1865. Erich Hückel gave the model and the 4n + 2 count in 1931. Cyclobutadiene was first trapped in a frozen cage of argon in 1973.

A proof that is not allowed

“Benzene flips back and forth between its two drawings with three double links, and the flipping makes it steady.” Not allowed: there is no flipping. The molecule is one shape, with six equal links, and the levels of the ring-level technique describe it as it is. The two drawings are pictures, each wrong in the same way.
