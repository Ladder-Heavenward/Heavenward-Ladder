11e · proof

A joint doesn't break continuity

Many functions are given in pieces: one formula on the left of an input, another on the right. Where the formula changes, does continuity survive? This step proves that it does exactly when the two pieces arrive at the same height and the function's value is that height. It names such a place a joint. At a joint the pieces may climb at quite different rates: the size of a number falls on the left of 0 and climbs on the right, and is continuous at 0 all the same. Then the step turns to sequences: a continuous function carries every sequence of inputs heading to an input into a sequence of outputs heading to its value there. That costs nothing. The converse is also true, and the step says exactly what it costs.

Where the formula changes, look at the two pieces arriving. Same height, and the value is that height: continuous, whatever rates the pieces climb at. Different heights: no value can save it.

11e1Where the formula changes

10e glued two formulas at 1: x² on the left, 2x − 1 on the right, and the stray value 5 at 1. Both pieces head to 1 at 1, so the function heads to 1 there (two-sides-agree), and its value is 5: discontinuous at 1. Change the value at 1 to 1, and it lands where it was headed. The pieces arrived at the same height; the value had to be that height too. In general:

Glued-functions technique: let f be a function with w as an input, equal to g on some interval (w − r, w) and to h on (w, w + r). Then f is continuous at w exactly when lim_{x→w⁻} g(x) = f(w) = lim_{x→w⁺} h(x). A function given by different formulas on different pieces of its domain is called piecewise defined.

\lim_{x \to w^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to w^-} g(x) @@ agreeing-near side by side (10e): f and g agree on (w − r, w); either both limits exist and are equal, or neither exists
\lim_{x \to w^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to w^+} h(x) @@ the same, on (w, w + r)
f \text{ continuous at } w \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{from the left} \land \text{from the right} @@ continuous-both-ways (11a)
\Leftrightarrow\; \htmlClass{keep}{\lim_{x \to w^-} g(x)} = \htmlClass{new}{f(w)} = \htmlClass{keep}{\lim_{x \to w^+} h(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 11a's one-sided lines, read through the two lines above

The commonest case has continuous pieces with the value given by one of them, as “g for x ≤ w, h for x > w”. Then lim_{x→w⁻} g(x) = g(w) and lim_{x→w⁺} h(x) = h(w) (continuity, from each side), f(w) = g(w), and glued-functions says: f is continuous at w exactly when g(w) = h(w). At every other input near w, f agrees with one piece on a whole neighbourhood, so it is continuous wherever that piece is (agreeing-near). Continuous pieces, glued where their heights match, make a continuous function.

11e2Joints, and rates

A place where the formula changes and continuity survives needs a name, and so does the thing that may differ there.

From here on, let a function f be given by one formula just left of an input w and by another just right of it. Then w is a joint of f when the two pieces arrive at the same height and f lands there: lim_{x→w⁻} f(x) = f(w) = lim_{x→w⁺} f(x). At a joint the left and right pieces need not climb at the same rate.

From here on, a joint where the two pieces climb at different rates is a corner. How sharp a corner is, its angle, is measured once angles are built (rung 21).

From here on, the rate of a linear function x ↦ mx + b is its step m: how much the output changes per step of the input, in 9e's words for m. Same-ratio showed it is the same ratio (f(s) − f(t))/(s − t) between any two inputs. A piece of a function that agrees with a linear function climbs at that kind's rate.

The = dividendA rate is a sameness: the same change per step, wherever you take the step. Same-ratio's ratio between any two inputs = the ratio between any other two, and that = is the one from 0b, now running along a whole line.

This is a new job for the word. 5j's rate, an amount of one kind compared with an amount of another, and 10·bio's rate of a reaction keep their meanings. When a linear function's inputs and outputs carry units, its rate is a rate in 5j's sense too.

By glued-functions and continuous-both-ways, a joint is an input where the function is continuous. That is principle 5 in its general form: a joint doesn't break continuity. The rates of the two pieces play no part in glued-functions's column. Continuity asks how the pieces arrive, never how they climb.

11e3|x| at 0

Size-is-continuous technique: |x| is continuous at every real; at 0 it has a joint whose pieces climb at the rates −1 and 1. Three roads, each complete.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ \delta = \htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon} @@ continuity-unpacked, at any real a
\big|\,|x| - |w|\,\big| \le |x - w| @@ the reverse form of triangle (8h), and size-of-a-negative for its other side
|x - w| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; \big|\,|x| - |w|\,\big| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ at-most-is-a-partial-order on ℝ. At w = 0 this reads: |x| < ε whenever |x| < ε
  
|x| = -x \ (x \lt 0),\ \ x\ (x \ge 0) @@ 8h's line, by cases
\lim_{x\to 0^-}(-x) = 0,\ \ \lim_{x \to 0^+} x = 0,\ \ |0| = 0 @@ lines-are-continuous from each side; the value
0 \text{ is a joint: continuous at } 0 @@ glued-functions: both pieces arrive at 0, and the value is 0
\text{rates: } \htmlClass{new}{-1} \text{ on the left},\ \ \htmlClass{new}{1} \text{ on the right} @@ the steps of −x and x: each step left of 0 lowers the output by 1, each step right raises it by 1

The third road is 11c's: |x| = √(x²), by chain-at-an-edge at 0. Three proofs, and the second says the most. The pieces meet at the same height, 0. On the left the output falls by 1 for every step across; on the right it climbs by 1. None of that troubles continuity. It is the plainest corner, and the plainest example of the rung's fifth principle. What the difference in rates does cost is a different question, with a different answer, on rung 13.

11e4More joints, and a place that is not one

f(x) = 2x + 1\ (x \le 1),\ \ 4 - x\ (x \gt 1) @@ two linear functions; the value at 1 from the left piece
2 \cdot 1 + 1 = 3 = 4 - 1 @@ the pieces' values at 1 agree: glued-functions, continuous. A joint, with rates 2 and −1
g(x) = 2x + 1\ (x \le 1),\ \ x + 3\ (x \gt 1) @@ the same left piece, a different right one
3 \ne \htmlClass{new}{4} @@ the right piece arrives at 4: glued-functions fails. Not a joint; 11f calls it a jump
|x - 2| + |x + 1| = -2x + 1,\ \ 3,\ \ 2x - 1 @@ on (−∞, −1), [−1, 2], (2, ∞): 8h's line on each bar, by cases
\text{at } -1:\ 3 = 3;\ \ \text{at } 2:\ 3 = 3 @@ two joints, with rates −2, 0, 2 on the three stretches. Continuous at every real (also by size-is-continuous, continuous-after-continuous, continuous-sums)

Whether a place where the formula changes is a joint depends only on the heights arriving and the value. Writing a function as one formula does not hide its joints: |x − 2| + |x + 1| is one formula with two joints. And splitting a function into pieces does not create breaks: a polynomial given as “itself on the left, itself on the right” has a joint, trivially, wherever you choose to cut it.

11e5Sequences go through

10f's line says what it means for a sequence of reals to head to a value. Continuity carries such sequences along. Sequences-go-through technique: if f is continuous at w, and x is a sequence of inputs of f with x_n → w, then f(x_n) → f(w).

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \text{call } \delta @@ continuity-unpacked for f at a
\text{call } N:\ n \ge N \Rightarrow |x_n - w| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\delta} @@ x_n → w, challenged with the gap δ (10f's sequence line)
n \ge N \;\Rightarrow\; |f(x_n) - f(w)| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ x_n is an input of f within δ of w: continuity-unpacked's promise. So f(x_n) → f(w)

It is 11c's chain again, with a sequence as the inside function: the outside answers ε with δ, and the sequence answers δ with a threshold. No either-way principle, no axiom. You have met its use without its name. The cut-offs of √2, 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ⋯, head to √2 (10f's cut-offs). Squaring is continuous (lines-are-continuous, continuous-products). So their squares, 1, 1.96, 1.9881, 1.999396, ⋯, head to (√2)² = 2.

Turned about by turn-and-deny, sequences-go-through is a refutation tool. One sequence of inputs heading to w whose outputs do not head to f(w) shows f is discontinuous at w. For 11a's step u at 0, x_n = −1/S(n) heads to 0 (10f's one-over-n and constant-factor), u(x_n) = 0 for every n, and 0 does not head to u(0) = 1. For q at any w, a sequence of fractions and a sequence of irrationals, both heading to w, have outputs 1 and 0 forever. They cannot both head to q(w). But one sequence that passes proves nothing: u(1/S(n)) = 1 heads to u(0).

11e6The test, the other way, and what it costs

Sequence-test-back technique: for f defined near w with w an input, if f(x_n) → f(w) for every sequence x of inputs of f with x_n → w, then f is continuous at w. The proof is by contradiction proper, and two lines in it lean on principles the ladder chose rather than proved.

\text{suppose } \neg(f \text{ continuous at } w) @@ a supposition for contradiction (7e)
\exists \varepsilon \gt 0\ \forall \delta \gt 0\ \exists x \in \operatorname{dom} f\ (|x - w| \lt \delta \land \varepsilon \le |f(x) - f(w)|) @@ continuity-unpacked denied by 10g's not-heading-to techs: not-every, not-some, negated-if forward, three-way-order on ℝ. Rung 7's either-way principle
\text{call such } \varepsilon @@ use a some
A_n = \{x \in \operatorname{dom} f : |x - w| \lt \tfrac{1}{S(n)} \land \varepsilon \le |f(x) - f(w)|\} @@ a sift of dom f, one for each number n; non-empty, by the line above with δ = 1/S(n)
\mathcal A = \{A_n : n \in \mathbb N\} @@ 2h's shorthand, a sift of 𝒫(ℝ): a collection of non-empty sets
\text{call } c,\ \text{a choosing function for } \mathcal A @@ the choosing axiom (6b): one bad input from each of endlessly many windows, with no rule
x_n = \htmlClass{new}{c(A_n)} @@ a sequence of inputs of f, by rule-makes-a-function (4d)
x_n \to w @@ |x_n − w| < 1/S(n); given ε′, everyday-counting-passes gives N ≠ 0 with 1/N < ε′; for n ≥ N, 1/S(n) < 1/N (positive-reciprocal on ℚ)
f(x_n) \to f(w) @@ the hypothesis, used with this sequence
|f(x_{N'}) - f(w)| \lt \varepsilon \text{ for some } N' @@ the line above, at the gap ε
\varepsilon \le |f(x_{N'}) - f(w)|;\ \ \bot @@ x_N′ ∈ A_N′; clash (three-way-order on ℝ: ε ≤ p is ¬(p < ε))
f \text{ continuous at } w \quad \blacksquare @@ refute: ¬¬(continuous); not-not-out, rung 7's either-way principle

Say which, with care. Three lines are marked. The second line and the last are rung 7's either-way principle. Turning “not continuous” into the challenger's strategy needs not-every (10g used the principle there too), and the proof ends with not-not out. The line that calls c is a move cited to the choosing axiom, made on a collection of endlessly many sets with no rule to pick from them. On this ladder the either-way principle itself came from Choice, through 7c's choice-gives-either-way, so every marked line rests on Choice in the end. But the two kinds of use are not alike, and the difference matters.

The choosing is the essential cost. Set theorists have built worlds in which every statement is true or false, so rung 7's either-way principle is true, but Choice is false. In some of them there is a function on the reals that passes every sequence test at a point and is still discontinuous there. In those worlds the line sequence-test-back would write is false for that function, so no proof from the either-way principle alone can exist. What sequence-test-back needs is less than the full axiom. It needs only choosing from a collection in bijection with ℕ, of sets of reals: the listed choosing that 6c named. Set theorists have shown that, given the rest, sequence-test-back for every function on the reals is exactly as strong as that much choice. That a proof of this technique leans on the choosing axiom was noticed early in the century before this one. A survey of 1918 listed it among the axiom's uses in analysis.

A modern survey of what fails without the choosing axiom gathers this case among many. Given the other axioms, “sequentially continuous at every point” and “continuous” agree for all functions on the reals exactly when listed choosing is true for sets of reals.

And when a rule can do the picking, no axiom is needed. For a particular function you can usually name the bad inputs: for u at 0 the sequence −1/S(n) was given by a formula, and the refutation of continuity used no choice at all. The axiom is spent only on the technique about every function, where nobody can say in advance where the bad inputs lie.

11e8Running it backwards

Handed a function in pieces, find where the formula changes, compute the heights arriving from each side and the value, and decide by glued-functions: a joint, or not. Handed a joint, read off the rates if the pieces are linear functions, and expect them to differ. Handed a claim of continuity to refute, look for one sequence of inputs heading to the point whose outputs go elsewhere. Handed a proof that uses every sequence, ask where it picks, and whether a rule could pick instead. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The choice axiom is Ernst Zermelo’s. The survey of 1918 is Wacław Sierpiński’s; the modern survey is Horst Herrlich’s. q is Dirichlet’s function.

Herrlich's survey of what fails without the axiom of choice gathers this case among many: given the other axioms, “sequentially continuous at every point” and “continuous” agree for all functions on the reals exactly when countable choice is true for sets of reals.

Herrlich, Axiom of Choice, Lecture Notes in Mathematics 1876 (2006), ch. 4

Accepted names: sequences-go-through and sequence-test-back together are the sequential criterion for continuity; a function given by different formulas on different pieces of its domain is piecewise defined.
