32·∎ · conclusion

A Periodic Function Is the Sum of Its Shadows

The turnings are at right angles and leave no direction out, so a repeating function is the sum of its shadows on them and keeps its energy. The sum returns f where f has a slope, the halfway value at a jump, and averaged sums settle everywhere. The transform breaks any signal into frequencies, keeps energy, costs a width, and needs only samples at twice the top frequency.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 32g samples suffice is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 32 asked whether every repeating function is a sum of turnings. The turnings are a right-angled list that leaves no direction out, so every periodic function with finite energy is the sum of its shadows on them, and its energy is the sum of the squares of their sizes. At a single point the sum is subtler: it returns f where f has a slope, the halfway value at a jump, and an overshoot near the jump that never dies.

32a showed the turnings are at right angles and each coefficient is a shadow. 32b showed the turnings leave no direction out, so energy is kept. 32c found where the series returns the function, and 32d repaired the rest by blending and averaging. 32e let the repeat grow without end and built the transform, 32f proved short in time means wide in frequency, and 32g showed samples suffice. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

On [−π, π], with ⟨f, g⟩ = (1/2π)∫ f ḡ, the turnings en(x) = einx are a right-angled list, and cn = ⟨f, en⟩ is the shadow of f on en. For every periodic f with finite energy, cn → 0, the partial sums SNf come as close as wanted to f in energy, and ‖f‖² = Σ|cn|².

Where f has a slope at x, SNf(x) → f(x); at a jump, SNf(x) → (f(x⁻) + f(x⁺))/2; and near a jump of size 2 the sums rise to about 1.179, for every N. The blend f * g has coefficients cn(f)cn(g) and the smoothness of the smoother part, and the averages σNf of the partial sums settle at every continuous f everywhere at once.

For f with a finite total on the line, f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x)e−2πixξ dx is bounded and continuous, the bell e−πx² is its own transform, f is rebuilt as ∫ f̂(ξ)e2πixξ dξ, and ∫|f̂|² = ∫|f|². A slope becomes the multiple 2πiξ, so Δx Δξ ≥ 1/(4π), with equality only for bells. A signal with no frequency above B is fixed by its samples f(k/(2B)) and is Σ f(k/(2B)) sinc(2Bx − k); frequencies ξ and ξ + r give the same samples at rate r.

So the rung’s claim holds: every periodic function with finite energy is the sum of its shadows on the turnings, its energy is kept, and letting the repeat grow without end gives the transform with its two prices. This also pays what 31h held in trust: the turnings, and so the sines on [0, 1], leave no direction out.
