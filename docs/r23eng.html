23·eng · engineering

What makes an engine powerful?

What makes an engine powerful? Every engine is sold with two numbers, one in horsepower and one in pound-feet, and each comes with the speed of turning at which it was measured. People argue over which number matters. Truck drivers on the long grades of the interstate praise an engine’s pull at low speed. Racing drivers rev their engines high. What makes it hard is that the two numbers measure different things, and both change with the speed of the engine. One is a turning push. The other is how fast work gets done. This page shows, with rung twelve’s lever, rung thirteen’s power, rung twenty-one’s turning and rung twenty-three’s polynomials that hug a curve, how the two are tied together, why the number five thousand two hundred and fifty-two turns up whenever they meet, why gears can trade one for the other but never make more of the second, and why the most pull and the most power come at different speeds.

A turning push is a push times the length of the arm it acts on. Every turn, a steady turning push does a fixed amount of work. So the power, the work done each second, is the turning push times how fast the shaft turns. Gears can make the turning push bigger by making the turning slower, but the power through them stays the same. At a given road speed, power alone decides how hard the wheels can push. On a test stand, the power still climbs at the speed where the pull is greatest, so the most power always comes at a higher speed.

23eng1A turning push

Stands on 12·phys (the lever; work as force times distance), 21b (cos wnd sin ws coordinates) and 21j (the arc-on-any-circle technique).

12·phys balanced a lever by weight times distance from the pivot. The same product measures a turning push. Push on a wrench, square to its handle, at a distance r from the nut.

From here on, a push F square to an arm of length r makes a turning push τ = F r about the pivot, in newton metres (N·m) or pound-feet (lb-ft). One lb-ft is about 1.356 N·m. A push at a slant φ to the arm turns only by its part square to the arm, F sin φ (21b), so τ = F r sin φ. Accepted name: torque.

\text{the push’s point runs once round a circle of radius } r:\ 2\pi r @@ arc on any circle (21j), a whole turn is 2π
\text{the push stays along that path}, \text{ so the work in one turn is } F\cdot 2\pi r @@ work is force times distance along the motion (12·phys)
\htmlClass{new}{W_{\text{turn}} = 2\pi\tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ τ = F r

Turn-work technique: a steady turning push τ does 2πτ of work in every turn, whatever the length of the arm. A wheel nut on a family car is tightened to about 100 lb-ft, 136 N·m. On a breaker bar 1.5 ft long that is a push of 100/1.5 ≈ 67 lb, square to the bar. A bar twice as long halves the push. It does not change the work done in each turn.

23eng2Power is turning push times turning speed

Stands on 23·eng.1, 13·eng (the force-times-speed technique; the horsepower) and 21j (radians).

A shaft turning ω radians each second carries a point at radius r round at speed v = rω (21j). 13·eng found that a push along the motion delivers power equal to the push times the speed.

v = r\omega @@ arc on any circle (21j): rω of path each second
P = F v @@ the force-times-speed technique (13·eng)
= F r\,\omega @@ put v in
\htmlClass{new}{P = \tau\,\omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ τ = F r

Turn-power technique: an engine’s power is its turning push times how fast it turns, in radians a second. Engines are timed in revolutions a minute, rpm. N rpm is ω = 2πN/60 radians a second. In watts, P = τ · 2πN/60 = τN/9549, with τ in N·m.

13·eng told how the horsepower was set: 33 000 foot-pounds a minute, which is 550 foot-pounds a second.

P = \tau\cdot\tfrac{2\pi N}{60}\ \text{ft·lb/s} @@ τ in lb-ft, N in rpm
P_{\text{hp}} = \tfrac{2\pi\,\tau N}{60 \cdot 550} = \tfrac{\tau N}{33\,000/(2\pi)} @@ divide by 550
\tfrac{33\,000}{2\pi} \approx 5252.1 @@ 2π ≈ 6.2832
\htmlClass{new}{P_{\text{hp}} = \tfrac{\tau_{\text{lb-ft}}\,N}{5252}} \quad \blacksquare @@ so at N = 5252 rpm the two numbers agree

5252 technique: horsepower is pound-feet times rpm over 5252, so on any chart drawn in those units the two curves cross at 5252 rpm. The 5252 belongs to the units, not to the engine. With N·m and kilowatts the curves cross at 9549 rpm instead.

23eng3Gears trade turning push for turning speed

Stands on 23·eng.2, 5·eng (the meshing technique) and 13·eng (the climbing-power technique).

Gears neither make work nor store it, so the power that goes in comes out, less a few percent lost to rubbing. Take that as a measured law, and leave the loss aside. By the meshing technique (5·eng), a gear pair turns its output shaft k times as fast as its input, for a fixed ratio k.

\omega_{\text{out}} = k\,\omega_{\text{in}} @@ the meshing technique (5·eng)
\tau_{\text{out}}\,\omega_{\text{out}} = \tau_{\text{in}}\,\omega_{\text{in}} @@ power in is power out; the turn-power technique
\htmlClass{new}{\tau_{\text{out}} = \tfrac{\tau_{\text{in}}}{k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by k ω_in

A low gear has k well below 1: the wheels turn slowly and push hard. Follow the power to the road. A wheel of radius r pushes the road with F = τ/r, and the car moves at v = rω.

F\,v = \tfrac{\tau}{r}\cdot r\omega = \tau\,\omega @@ at the wheels
\tau\,\omega = P @@ the gear-trade technique: the engine’s power reaches the wheels
\htmlClass{new}{F = \tfrac{P}{v}} \quad \blacksquare @@ whatever the gears

Road-push technique: at road speed v the wheels can push no harder than the engine’s power over v, whatever gear is chosen. So at a given speed, power decides how a car climbs. 13·eng found that climbing a grade r at speed v takes mgrv. A loaded truck of 36 000 kg climbing a 6% grade at 50 km/h needs 36 000 × 9.8 × 0.06 × 13.9 ≈ 294 kW for the climb alone, about 395 hp.

23eng4Reading a dynamometer chart

Stands on 23·eng.2, 13c (the product rule), 14a (the flat-top technique) and 23a (the match technique).

A dynamometer holds an engine at each speed N, with the throttle wide open, and measures its turning push. The result is the torque curve T(N), in lb-ft. The power curve is P(N) = T(N) N / 5252, in hp. Take a torque curve that rises to one peak, at N₀, and then falls.

P'(N) = \tfrac{T(N) + N\,T'(N)}{5252} @@ the product rule (13c)
T'(N_0) = 0 \Rightarrow P'(N_0) = \tfrac{T(N_0)}{5252} \gt 0 @@ the flat-top technique (14a) at the torque peak
P'(N_1) = 0 \Rightarrow T'(N_1) = -\tfrac{T(N_1)}{N_1} \lt 0 @@ the flat-top technique at the power peak; T(N₁) > 0
\htmlClass{new}{N_1 \gt N_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ T falls only past its one peak

Two-peaks technique: the most power comes at higher revs than the most torque, because at the torque peak the power is still climbing. Past the torque peak, each extra rpm loses a little push and gains a little speed. The power keeps rising until the push falls by a share as big as the share the speed gains.

Where is N₁? Near its top, the torque curve is hugged by its matching polynomial of degree two (23a), a bend: T(N) ≈ T₀ − c(N − N₀)², with c = −T″(N₀)/2. The middle term is missing because T′(N₀) = 0. Treat that bend as the curve, and find its power peak.

T(N) = T_0 - c\,u^2,\ \ u = N - N_0 @@ the match technique (23a), degree two, at N₀
\tfrac{d}{dN}\big(N(T_0 - c\,u^2)\big) = T_0 - c\,u^2 - 2cNu @@ the product rule (13c)
T_0 - c\,u^2 - 2c(N_0 + u)u = 0 \iff 3c\,u^2 + 2cN_0\,u - T_0 = 0 @@ the flat-top technique (14a)
\htmlClass{new}{N_1 = N_0 + \tfrac{N_0}{3}\Big(\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{3T_0}{cN_0^2}} - 1\Big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one root with u > 0

Power-peak technique: for a torque curve that bends as T₀ − c(N − N₀)², the most power comes at N₀ + (N₀/3)(√(1 + 3T₀/(cN₀²)) − 1). A small sports car’s engine gives about 150 lb-ft at 4500 rpm and about 130 lb-ft at 7000. Then c = 20/2500² = 3.2 × 10⁻⁶, and 3T₀/(cN₀²) ≈ 6.94. The formula puts the power peak near 7230 rpm, past the 7000 rpm where the engine is cut off. So its power still rises all the way to the cut-off: about 173 hp there, against 129 hp at the torque peak. That is why such a car is driven fast by revving it high.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A turning push is a torque, or a moment of force; the turn-work technique is W = 2πτ per revolution, and the turn-power technique is P = τω. A dynamometer, or dyno, measures torque, and the torque curve and power curve are read from it. The gear-trade technique is the torque–speed trade of a gearbox, and the ratio k is the gear ratio (its inverse is often quoted). An engine’s cut-off speed is its redline. That the two curves cross at 5252 rpm is a fact of the units, lb-ft and hp.

Who said it first

Archimedes stated the law of the lever. James Watt set the horsepower in the 1780s at 33 000 foot-pounds a minute, from the work of mill horses, partly to sell his engines against them; 23·eng.6.5 asks what that number measures. Gaspard de Prony built the first brake dynamometer, the Prony brake, in 1821. R. D. Stevenson and R. J. Wassersug (1993) estimated from records of horse-pulling contests that a horse can briefly give about 15 hp, and a fit person a little over 1.
