How Wide Should a Claim Be When Many Are Made?

How wide should a claim be when many are made? A test at the usual five in a hundred lets one false idea in twenty pass. That is a fair price for one idea. A laboratory that measures ten thousand genes at once, or a team that tries a thousand features of a web page, pays it a thousand times, and fifty false ideas pass on average even if every idea is false. The old cure was to make each test so strict that the chance of any false pass at all stays small, and it works, but it throws away most of the true findings with the false. In 1995 two statisticians in Tel Aviv proposed asking for less: not that no false idea passes, but that false ideas make up only a small share of what passes on average. Their rule sorts the surprise chances and passes as many as a rising line allows, and when the tests are independent it keeps that share below the level chosen, with a short proof by counting. By its first author’s account the paper took five years and three journals to be accepted; the genes made it famous. A second question is about one claim at a time: how wide should a forecast be to cover the truth nineteen times in twenty? A method of 2005 answers with no model of the world at all, only by ranking past errors, and its proof is a count of equally likely orders. Both rules cost something, and what the cost is when tests depend on each other, or when the future does not look like the past, is open.

m false ideas tested at α give mα false passes on average; tested at α/m, any false pass has chance at most α. Sort the surprise chances p₍₁₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₘ₎ and pass the first k, for the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ qk/m: for independent tests the false share averages m₀q/m ≤ q. Rank n past errors with a new one; the new one is any of n + 1 ranks with equal chance, so a width set at the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest past error covers with chance at least 1 − α.

sky52.1Fifty False Passes

30now2 counted the forks of one analysis and found that more forks give more passes. Make it exact. Test m ideas, each at the level α of 30s1, so each false one passes with chance α. Let V be the number of false ideas that pass.

Split-the-level technique: if m₀ of the m ideas are false, the false passes average m₀α; and if each idea is tested at α/m instead, the chance that any false idea passes is at most α, however the tests depend on each other.

V = \textstyle\sum_{i \text{ false}} \mathbf 1[\text{test } i \text{ passes}] @@ a count is a sum of ones
\mathrm E\,V = m_0\,\alpha @@ the average-adds technique (30g1), each one averaging α
P(A_1 \text{ or } \cdots \text{ or } A_{m_0}) \le P(A_1) + \cdots + P(A_{m_0}) @@ the chance-rules technique (30f), dropping the overlap each time
\htmlClass{new}{\le m_0 \cdot \tfrac{\alpha}{m} \le \alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ no independence used
  
1000 \times 0.05 = 50 @@ false passes, on average
1 - 0.95^{1000} \approx 1 - 5 \times 10^{-23} @@ at least one, if independent (30l)
0.05/1000 = 0.000\,05 \to z \ge 3.89 @@ the strict level, one-sided
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a true effect of 3 widths: passes } 91\% \text{ at } 0.05,\ 19\% \text{ at } 0.000\,05} \quad \blacksquare @@ the price of strictness

The strict rule works and is ruinous. An idea that is true, with an effect three widths across, passes the single test nine times in ten and the strict one fewer than one time in five. A study of ten thousand genes that asks that not one false gene be named must ask each gene for a surprise chance below five in a million, and a gene with a modest true effect will almost never give one.

sky52.2A Share, Not a Chance

Ask for less. Let R be the number of ideas that pass and V the false ones among them. The false share is V/R, counted as 0 when nothing passes. A rule controls the false share at q when the average of V/R is at most q. A list of a hundred passing genes with five false ones in it is still a useful list.

The step rule at level q: sort the m surprise chances, p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ ⋯ ≤ p₍ₘ₎. Find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ qk/m. Pass the ideas with the k smallest surprise chances, and no others. The line qk/m starts at the strict level q/m and rises to q: the first idea is held to the strict standard, and each one after is allowed a little more, because by then others have already passed.

qk/m = 0.005,\ 0.010,\ 0.015,\ 0.020,\ 0.025,\ 0.030,\ \ldots @@ the rising line
p = 0.001,\ 0.008,\ 0.012,\ 0.021,\ 0.024,\ 0.041,\ 0.074,\ \ldots @@ sorted
0.021 > 0.020,\ \ 0.024 \le 0.025,\ \ 0.041 > 0.030 @@ the 4th is above the line, the 5th is below
\htmlClass{new}{k = 5:\ \text{pass the first five}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the largest k under the line, the 4th included

Against the same list, the strict rule of sky52.1 passes one idea, the first, and the plain five-in-a-hundred passes six. The step rule passes five, and the fourth passes though it lies above its own step: what counts is the last crossing, not the first. That is the whole rule. Why it controls the false share is the next footing.

sky52.3Why the Share Is Controlled

Suppose the m tests are independent (30f2), and each false idea’s surprise chance is spread evenly on [0, 1], so P(pᵢ ≤ t) = t. For each false idea i, let Rᵢ be the number the step rule passes when pᵢ is replaced by 0. Rᵢ depends only on the other tests, so it is independent of pᵢ, and it is at least 1.

Swap-in-zero technique: if pᵢ ≤ qk/m, then the step rule passes k ideas exactly when it passes k ideas with pᵢ replaced by 0. Write N(t) for the number of surprise chances at most t; the step rule passes the largest j with N(qj/m) ≥ j. For j ≥ k, pᵢ ≤ qj/m is counted in N(qj/m) whether it is pᵢ or 0, so the counts agree for every j ≥ k, and for j < k replacing by 0 can only raise them. So the largest crossing at k or above is the same for both.

False-share technique: for independent tests, with the false surprise chances spread evenly, the step rule at level q keeps the average false share at m₀q/m, which is at most q.

\tfrac VR = \textstyle\sum_{i \text{ false}} \tfrac{\mathbf 1[i \text{ passes}]}{R} @@ V counts the false ideas that pass
\mathrm E\tfrac{\mathbf 1[i \text{ passes}]}{R} = \textstyle\sum_k \tfrac1k P\!\left(p_i \le \tfrac{qk}m,\ R = k\right) @@ i passes with R = k exactly when pᵢ ≤ qk/m
= \textstyle\sum_k \tfrac1k P\!\left(p_i \le \tfrac{qk}m\right) P(R_i = k) @@ the swap-in-zero technique, then independence
= \textstyle\sum_k \tfrac1k \cdot \tfrac{qk}m \cdot P(R_i = k) = \tfrac qm @@ the even spread; the P(Rᵢ = k) add to 1
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm E\tfrac VR = m_0\,\tfrac qm \le q} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-adds technique, over the m₀ false ideas

Every false idea contributes exactly q/m, whatever the true ideas do. So the rule is, if anything, too careful by the factor m₀/m, and later work tries to estimate m₀ and loosen the line. A computer run of twenty tests, fifteen false and five true, with q = 0.1, gives an average false share of 0.0747 over two hundred thousand repeats, against the 0.075 the technique says.

sky52.4Ten Thousand Genes

44·bio read gene data as a cloud of points. The other common question asks of each gene separately: is it more active in the sick samples than in the well? That is ten thousand tests at once, and it is where the step rule found its use.

Take m = 10 000 genes, 200 of them truly changed by 4 widths and the rest unchanged, and q = 0.05. When many pass, the step rule’s line at the last crossing is a level t with t = q · R(t)/m, where R(t) is the average number of surprise chances below t: 9800t from the unchanged genes, and 200 times the chance π(t) that a changed one passes.

\text{strict: } t = 0.05/10\,000 = 5 \times 10^{-6},\ z \ge 4.42 @@ sky52.1
\pi = P(Z > 4.42 - 4) = 0.34:\ \ 68 \text{ of the } 200 \text{ found} @@ 30s1’s bell
\text{step: } t = 0.05\,(9800t + 200\,\pi(t))/10\,000 \Rightarrow t \approx 0.000\,85,\ z \ge 3.14 @@ solved by repeating the step
\htmlClass{new}{\pi = 0.81:\ 161 \text{ found},\ \text{about } 8 \text{ false among } 169} \quad \blacksquare @@ a false share of 4.9%

The step rule passes a list more than twice as long, at a level about 170 times looser than the strict one, and pays for it with eight false genes in a list of 169. Whether that is a good trade depends on what is done next. A list sent on to a cheaper second experiment can carry eight passengers; a list of drug targets for a costly trial may not. The genes are also not independent: they act in groups, so their tests lean together, and the false-share technique as proved does not cover them.

sky52.5A Width From Ranks

30q3 built an interval that covers 95 times in 100, but only for an average and only as the sample grows, by the bell. A forecast is harder: it guesses one new value, a house price, tomorrow’s demand, from a model that may be wrong. Fit any model on some of the data. On the other n records, the calibration records, measure its errors s₁, …, sₙ, each the size of the miss. A new record will have its own error sₙ₊₁.

Suppose the n + 1 records are drawn in a way that does not depend on their order, as independent draws from one source are, and no two errors tie. Then each of the n + 1 orders of the errors is equally likely, so the new error’s rank among all n + 1 is any of 1, …, n + 1 with chance 1/(n + 1) each, by 30l1’s equal-cases technique.

Rank-cover technique: let k = ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉ ≤ n, and let w be the k-th smallest of the n calibration errors. Then the forecast plus or minus w covers the new value with chance at least 1 − α, and with no ties, less than 1 − α + 1/(n + 1).

s_{n+1} \le w \iff \text{rank of } s_{n+1} \text{ among all } n + 1 \text{ is at most } k @@ w is the k-th smallest of the others
P(\text{rank} \le k) = \tfrac{k}{n+1} @@ equal cases
\tfrac{k}{n+1} \ge 1 - \alpha \text{ and } \tfrac{k}{n+1} < 1 - \alpha + \tfrac1{n+1} @@ k is the least whole number ≥ (n + 1)(1 − α)
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{cover}) \in [\,1 - \alpha,\ 1 - \alpha + \tfrac{1}{n+1})} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every source and every model
  
3,5,6,8,9,11,12,14,15,17,18,21,23,26,30,34,41,55,80 @@ sorted
\alpha = 0.1:\ k = \lceil 20 \times 0.9 \rceil = 18,\ w = 55 @@ covers with chance exactly 18/20
\alpha = 0.05:\ k = 19,\ w = 80 @@ the largest error
\htmlClass{new}{n = 9,\ \alpha = 0.05:\ k = 10 > 9,\ \text{no finite width}} \quad \blacksquare @@ too few records for 95%

No model of the world entered: the model can be a straight line or a machine with a billion settings, and the coverage holds. The cost is in the width. A bad model gives large errors and a wide band, honestly. The band is the same width for every house, though some prices are easy to guess and some are not, and the chance of covering is an average over all houses, not a promise for this one. That cannot be fixed for free: with finitely many records, no method covers each kind of house with a guaranteed chance unless its band is endless.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞52.oq.1How should many tests be judged when they depend on each other? The false-share technique needs independence. It is known that the step rule still works when the tests lean the same way in a certain strong sense, and that for any dependence at all it works if q is divided by 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ + 1/m, about 9.8 for ten thousand tests: safe, and much stricter. Real genes, brain scans and web experiments depend in ways between these. Which rule gives the most true findings while the false share stays controlled, for the dependence that real data has, is open.
∞52.oq.2How narrow can an honest band be? The rank-cover technique is honest for any model, but its band is as wide as the model’s errors allow, the same for every case. Bands that widen where the model is unsure, and cover each kind of case nearly as often as promised, are proposed every year. Since covering each case with a guaranteed chance is impossible from finitely many records, what is the best that can be guaranteed, and how close do the proposals come?
∞52.oq.3What can be promised when the future is not like the past? The rank-cover proof needs every order of the records to be equally likely. Prices drift, hospitals change, a model is used on people unlike those it was checked on. Methods that weight past errors by how much they resemble the new case, or adjust the width as the misses come in, keep some promise under some kinds of change. Which promises can be kept, and under which changes, is argued.
∞52.oq.4What should count as a finding when the tests are made one at a time, forever? A company tries a new web page every week; a field publishes a stream of studies. The step rule needs all m surprise chances at once. Rules that spend a budget of false passes as tests arrive exist, and each loses something against the step rule. How much must be lost by having to decide now, and how to do best with a stream, is open.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The problem is older than the cure. Statisticians had long known that testing many comparisons at the five-in-a-hundred level found false differences, and the cure they gave was the strict rule of sky52.1 and its refinements: make the chance of any false pass small. It suited an experiment that compared a handful of treatments. It was hard to argue with, and it was also hard to use, since a study that obeyed it found little.

Two statisticians in Tel Aviv proposed instead that the false share be small, in a paper published in 1995 in a journal of the Royal Statistical Society. By the first author’s later account it had been submitted in 1989 and took five years and three journals: readers objected to the definition, to what the share should be when nothing passes, and to the examples, which tested only a few dozen ideas, at a time when few people tested more.

Then the measurement changed. Laboratories began to measure thousands of genes in one experiment, and the strict rule made such experiments nearly useless. The step rule gave lists that could be followed up, and by 2010 the 1995 paper had thousands of citations, most of them from the life sciences. In 2001 the same group treated tests that depend on each other, showing the rule safe when the tests lean the same way and giving a stricter version safe always. In 2024 the first author, the coauthor of the 2001 work and a later collaborator received a new prize for statistics, worth a million dollars, for this line of work.

The rule was not a cure for everything. The replication arguments of 30·now are about forks taken quietly, and no correction can count tests nobody reports. Some argue that the false share invites long lists that are mostly right but never checked one by one; others that the strict rule had pushed scientists into hiding their many tests.

The ranking idea for forecasts came from another direction, from a group who studied learning from data with no assumptions about its source, and was set out in a book in 2005. A paper of 2019 made sharp what cannot be had: a band that covers each kind of case with a guaranteed chance. The method is now widely used to put bands on the forecasts of machine learning models whose errors nobody can describe with a formula, and the argument is over whether a promise that holds only on average, and only if the future is like the past, is the promise that users think they are getting.

The accepted names

The false share averaged is the false discovery rate (FDR), E[V/max(R, 1)]; the chance of any false pass is the familywise error rate (FWER), and the split-the-level technique is the Bonferroni correction, from Boole’s inequality. The step rule is the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, a step-up procedure; the false-share technique is its FDR control for independent p-values, here proved by the leave-one-out argument, and the swap-in-zero technique is the self-consistency lemma behind it. The 1986 rule is Simes’ test. The version safe under any dependence is the Benjamini–Yekutieli procedure, dividing q by the harmonic sum, and the case where the tests lean the same way is positive regression dependence. The rival reading of the share is the q-value and local false discovery rate. The rank-cover technique is split conformal prediction, and its coverage is marginal coverage under exchangeability; covering each case is conditional coverage, shown impossible in finite samples without infinite width. Tests made one at a time are online multiple testing, and the budget rules are alpha-investing.

Who said it first

Yoav Benjamini and Yosef Hochberg, “Controlling the false discovery rate: a practical and powerful approach to multiple testing”, Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, Series B, 1995; Benjamini told its history in “Discovering the false discovery rate” (2010). Benjamini and Daniel Yekutieli, “The control of the false discovery rate in multiple testing under dependency”, Annals of Statistics, 2001. Benjamini, Yekutieli and Ruth Heller received the Rousseeuw Prize for Statistics in 2024. R. John Simes, “An improved Bonferroni procedure for multiple tests of significance”, 1986. Vladimir Vovk, Alexander Gammerman and Glenn Shafer, “Algorithmic Learning in a Random World”, 2005. Rina Foygel Barber, Emmanuel Candès, Aaditya Ramdas and Ryan Tibshirani, “The limits of distribution-free conditional predictive inference”, 2019. A proof that is not allowed

“Each false gene passes with chance at most q/m by the step rule’s first step, so the false share is at most q.” Not allowed: the line rises, and a false gene can pass at a level as loose as q. The share is controlled because a gene that passes at a looser level is divided by a larger R, and the swap-in-zero technique is what makes the two balance.
