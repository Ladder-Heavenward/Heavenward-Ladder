24·now · research front

Can chaos be proved?

Can chaos be proved? In 1963 a weather scientist cut the air down to three numbers and wrote three rules on their rates. The rules are tame, so rung twenty-four fixes one future for every start. Yet his computer showed two runs, started almost together, wandering apart until they had nothing to do with each other, and never settling, and never repeating, while staying inside one butterfly-shaped cloud. For decades nobody could prove that the cloud was really there. A picture on a screen is made of rounded numbers, and the rounding is exactly the kind of small error that this rule blows up. What makes the question hard is that the thing to be proved is a kind of wildness, and the only way to watch it is with a machine that rounds. This page shows, with rung twenty-two’s rules fed their own answers, rung twenty-three’s error ceilings and rung twenty-four’s rules on rates, how far a fixed future can be foreseen, how chaos was proved by hand for one simple rule, how a computer can be made to prove rather than guess, how the butterfly cloud was finally proved to exist in 1999, and what is still open.

Two starts that differ by a tiny amount part by a fixed factor each unit of time, so each tenfold sharper start buys only a fixed extra stretch of forecast. For one simple rule fed its own answers, a change of variable turns every step into a doubling, and the wildness can be read straight off the base-two digits of the start. A computer can step a rule forward and keep, instead of a number, a box that must hold the true value, because the error ceiling of rung twenty-three caps everything it rounds or drops. Covering a slice of the butterfly cloud with many small boxes, and following each box once round, showed that the slice is sent back into itself and stretched, which is enough to make the cloud real.

24now1A fixed future, out of sight

The weather model has three amounts, x, y and z, for how the air turns over and how its warmth is spread. Their rules on rates are below. Each right side is a polynomial, so its rates are capped on any box, and 24d fixes one future from each start. Stepping the rules forward by computer, two starts ε apart are found to part by a factor of about e^0.906 each unit of time, on average over long runs. That number is a guess by case, from runs, not a technique.

x' = 10(y - x),\ \ y' = x(28 - z) - y,\ \ z' = xy - \tfrac83 z @@ three amounts, three rules on rates (1963)
\text{one future from each start} @@ polynomial right sides are tame on every box (24d)
\text{gap after time } t \approx \varepsilon\,e^{\mu t},\ \ \mu \approx 0.906 @@ measured over long computer runs
\varepsilon\,e^{\mu t} = D \iff t = \tfrac{1}{\mu}\ln\tfrac{D}{\varepsilon} @@ ln of both sides (17)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each tenfold sharper start buys } \tfrac{\ln 10}{\mu} \approx 2.5 \text{ units of time}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ln(D/(ε/10)) = ln(D/ε) + ln 10

Parting-starts technique: when two starts part by a fixed factor each unit of time, a start a thousand times sharper buys only a fixed, short extra stretch of forecast. Run with starts 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁹ apart, the two runs of the model part by more than 1 at about 14.7 and 22.3 units: 7.6 units for a thousandfold, as the technique says. The future is fixed, and still out of sight.

24now2Chaos proved by hand, once

22h fed the logistic rule its own answers, sₙ₊₁ = r sₙ(1 − sₙ), and watched its cycle of two split into four and eight as r grew. At r = 4 a change of variable makes the wildness exact.

s = \sin^2(\pi\theta) @@ every s in [0, 1] is one, for θ in [0, ½]
4s(1 - s) = 4\sin^2(\pi\theta)\cos^2(\pi\theta) = \sin^2(2\pi\theta) @@ 1 − sin² = cos²; sin 2u = 2 sin u cos u (21d)
s_n = \sin^2(\pi\,2^n\theta_0) @@ the same, n times
\sin^2(\pi\,2^n\theta_0) = \sin^2\big(\pi\operatorname{frac}(2^n\theta_0)\big) @@ sin² repeats after π
\htmlClass{new}{s_n = \sin^2\big(\pi\operatorname{frac}(2^n\theta_0)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ doubling shifts the base-two digits of θ₀ one place left (22·now.1)

Doubling technique: at r = 4 the logistic run is a doubling in disguise: its nth step depends only on the base-two digits of θ₀ from place n + 1 on. Two starts whose θ₀ agree to thirty base-two places agree for about thirty steps, and then nothing ties them. A start with θ₀ a fraction repeats in the end. A start whose digits never repeat never does. And next to any start lies one that repeats. All of this is proved, with no computer.

24now3A step a computer cannot fake

The weather model has no disguise to strip. Its proof needs a computer, and a computer rounds. The way round is to step forward with boxes instead of numbers. Try it on y′ = y from y(0) = 1, one step of h = 0.1. First a rough box, then a sharp one.

y \in [1,\ 1.2] \text{ on } [0,\ 0.1]\,? @@ a rough box, to be checked
y \le 1.2 \Rightarrow y' \le 1.2 \Rightarrow y \le 1 + 0.1 \cdot 1.2 = 1.12 \lt 1.2 @@ the rule as an area (24b): the box keeps itself
y(0.1) = 1 + 0.1 + 0.005 + \tfrac{y^{(3)}(c)}{6}(0.1)^3,\ \ y^{(3)} = y @@ the match-error technique (23b), c in (0, 0.1)
\tfrac{y(c)}{6000} \in [0.000166,\ 0.000200] @@ y(c) in the rough box; rounded down below and up above
\htmlClass{new}{y(0.1) \in [1.105166,\ 1.105200]} \quad \blacksquare @@ e^{0.1} = 1.1051709… is inside, as it must be

Boxed-step technique: a step taken with the match-error technique, inside a rough box that keeps itself, ends in a box that must hold the true value. Every rounding inside the machine is pushed outward, so the box only grows. The next step starts from the whole box. Higher-degree polynomials and shorter steps keep the boxes narrow.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

21a said that every value of π is a fence, and 22·now read a digit only where its whole fence agreed. A boxed step is the same promise made by a machine. It never gives the value. It gives a box, and a proof that the value is in it.

Report only what holds for every point of the box.

So a computer’s run of the weather model proves nothing, while a computer’s boxes can prove a great deal. The proof works with what every point of a box does. A claim that holds for only part of a box is not proved, however narrow the box.

A boxed run of the weather model, after twenty units of time, gives x in [−3.1, 4.2]. Which of these could you report: x is positive; x is between −5 and 5; x is about 0.5?

24now4A trap that stretches

Paths of the weather model cross the flat slice z = 27 again and again. Follow a point of the slice along its path until it next crosses: that is the return rule R, a rule fed its own answers, now on a slice of the plane instead of an interval. In 1979 a hand-drawn model of the butterfly cloud was shown to have a strange attractor, if its return rule did two things. The plan was to check those two things for the real rules, by boxes.

\Sigma = \{z = 27\},\ \ R(p) = \text{the next crossing of } \Sigma \text{ by the path from } p @@ the return rule
N \subset \Sigma,\ \ R(N) \subset N @@ a trap: as 22h’s interval sent into itself
\text{cover } N \text{ by thousands of small boxes; follow each by boxed steps} @@ 24·now.3, once round
\text{every box lands inside } N,\ \text{and stretches every short line in a kept cone of directions} @@ checked for every box
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the butterfly cloud exists, and is strange}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the 1979 model’s two conditions, now proved

Trap-and-stretch plan: a slice that the return rule sends into itself, while stretching it in one direction, holds a cloud that the paths never leave and never settle in. One place broke the boxes: near x = y = z = 0, where the paths crawl and the boxes swell. There a power sum that hugs the rules near that point (23) replaced the stepping. The proof appeared as a short note in 1999 and in full in 2002.

24now5What is proved, and what is open

The butterfly cloud of the weather model is proved; many of its cousins, and the doubling constant itself, are not.

  • Proved, 1999 and 2002. The weather model with 10, 28 and 8/3 has a strange attractor, and keeps one for nearby numbers. This settled the fourteenth of eighteen problems listed in 1998 for the century ahead.
  • Proved, by hand. The logistic run at r = 4 is a doubling in disguise (24·now.2).
  • Proved, with computer help, 1982. 24k’s constant, about 4.6692, shared by the doublings of every rule of the logistic kind, really is shared.
  • Proved, 1981 and 1997. The values of r with a wild logistic run fill a set of positive total length. The values with a cycle that holds lie next to every r.
  • Open. Whether 4.6692… is a fraction. Nobody knows.
  • Open. A two-number rule of 1976, xₙ₊₁ = 1 − 1.4xₙ² + yₙ, yₙ₊₁ = 0.3xₙ, shows a cloud much like the butterfly’s on every screen. Whether it has a strange attractor at those numbers is open. Proofs exist for nearby rules, not for these numbers.
  • Not a question for proof. Whether the real air parts its starts like the model. That is a law, to be found by induction under the case with predictable variation, and forecasters measure it: today’s useful forecasts reach about a week to ten days.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The weather model is the Lorenz system, and its cloud the Lorenz attractor; the parting of nearby starts is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the butterfly effect, and μ its largest Lyapunov exponent. The logistic rule at r = 4 is conjugate to the doubling map. Boxed steps are interval arithmetic, and computing with boxes is validated, or rigorous, numerics; the rough box is an a priori enclosure. The slice is a Poincaré section and R the first-return map; the kept cone is an invariant cone field; the hand-drawn model is the geometric Lorenz attractor; the power sum near the origin is a normal form. The constant 4.6692… is Feigenbaum’s δ. The 1976 rule is the Hénon map.

Who said it first

Edward Lorenz wrote the three rules in 1963, after restarting a run in 1961 from numbers rounded to three places and watching it part from the first; his 1972 talk asked whether the flap of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil sets off a tornado in Texas. Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann used the sin² change of variable for r = 4 (1947). John Guckenheimer and Robert Williams (1979), and Valentin Afraimovich, Vladimir Bykov and Leonid Shilnikov (1977), built the geometric model. Stephen Smale listed eighteen problems in 1998; Warwick Tucker settled the fourteenth (1999 note; 2002 paper). Mitchell Feigenbaum found δ (1975–78); Oscar Lanford proved its universality with computer help (1982). Michael Jakobson (1981) proved the positive length; Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek, and Mikhail Lyubich (1997), the cycles next to every r. Michel Hénon gave his map in 1976; Michael Benedicks and Lennart Carleson (1991) proved strange attractors for nearby rules. In 24·now.7.5, John von Neumann backed the first computer forecasts (1950) and hoped for forecasts, and even control, of the weather far ahead; Lorenz argued that a horizon was built into the air.
