How Many Points Two Curves Share

A line meets a conic in at most two points, two conics in at most four, and two curves of degrees m and n in at most mn. In the ordinary real plane the count is often less. Count in the projective plane, with turning numbers, and with tangencies counted twice, and it is always exactly mn. Two circles of 18 meet in two points or none; the other two of the four are the same for every pair of circles, at infinity. This step counts a line and a conic, counts two circles, and states the count.

Curves of degrees m and n with no shared piece meet in exactly mn points of the projective plane over ℂ, each counted as often as it should be. A line and a conic: a quadratic, two roots. Two circles: two ordinary points, and [1 : i : 0] and [1 : −i : 0] on every circle.

45j1A Line and a Conic

x^2 + (x + c)^2 = 1 \Rightarrow 2x^2 + 2cx + c^2 - 1 = 0 @@ a quadratic
|c| < \sqrt2:\ \text{two real points};\ \ |c| > \sqrt2:\ \text{two turning-number points} @@ never none over ℂ (26)
|c| = \sqrt2:\ \text{one root, counted twice} @@ the line touches
\htmlClass{new}{\text{always 2, counted rightly}} @@ m n = 1 · 2

A line and a conic meet in exactly two points over ℂ, counting a touching point twice.

45j2Two Circles Share Four Points

(x^2 + y^2 - z^2) - (x^2 + y^2 - 4xz) = z(4x - z) = 0 @@ subtract: the squares cancel
4x = z:\ x = \tfrac14,\ y = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{4} @@ two ordinary points
z = 0:\ x^2 + y^2 = 0,\ [1 : i : 0],\ [1 : -i : 0] @@ two points at infinity, on every circle
\htmlClass{new}{2 + 2 = 4 = 2 \cdot 2} @@ two circles meet in at most two ordinary points because two of the four are always at infinity

Any two circles share the two circular points at infinity, so they share at most two ordinary points; this is why two conics can meet in four, but two circles in only two.

45j3The Count

\text{remove } y \text{ by a resultant: a polynomial in } x \text{ of degree at most } mn @@ held in trust
\text{at most } mn \text{ points, with no shared piece} @@ a polynomial’s roots
\text{in } \mathbb P^2 \text{ over } \mathbb C, \text{ with tangency counted: exactly } mn @@ no point escapes to infinity or into ℂ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two cubics: } 9 \text{ points}} @@ a cubic and a line: 3

Shared-points technique: two curves of degrees m and n in the projective plane over ℂ with no shared piece meet in exactly mn points, counted rightly. Held in trust: the right count at each point is the size of a ring made by the two equations there.

Accepted names

The count is Bézout’s theorem; counting rightly uses intersection multiplicity; removing y is done by the resultant. The nine points of two cubics lead to the Cayley-Bacharach theorem.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton knew the bound for curves; Étienne Bézout proved it in 1779 in Théorie générale des équations algébriques; the exact count with multiplicities was settled in the 19th and 20th centuries.
