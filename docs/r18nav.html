How Can a Small Map Be True to the Land?

How can a small map be true to the land? 9e drew pairs of numbers as points, and a graph is a shape made from numbers. A map runs the other way. The shape is already there: a coast, a road, a field. The map-maker lays a grid over it, so that every place gets a pair of numbers. Then the paper shrinks every distance by the same factor, so that the county fits on a table. This page shows that the shrinking is 18f's scaling, that every triangle of places is similar to its picture, and that a distance can be read straight off the paper. Then it shows how a surveyor fixes a far point from one measured line and two sightings, which is how maps were made in the first place.

On a grid in kilometres, the distance between places is the square root of the squared east gap plus the squared north gap. At a scale of one to some number, paper distance is ground distance divided by that number, and paper area is ground area divided by its square. Every triangle of places is similar to its picture. From a measured base line, two sighted directions fix a far point: the two sight lines meet once.

18nav1A Grid Laid Over the Ground

Start with a place, and call it the origin. Choose a direction east and a direction north, at a right angle. Choose a unit: 1 km. Now every place has a pair (e, n): how far east of the origin, and how far north. Their accepted names are easting and northing. This is 9e's plane, with one change of order. In 9e the numbers came first and the drawing after. Here the ground comes first, and the numbers are read off it.

Rung 18 works in a plane. The Earth is round, but a county is a small enough patch that a flat grid serves: across Monterey Bay, the error is far less than the width of a road. On such a grid, distance-laws give the distance between two places as √(Δe² + Δn²), with Δe and Δn the differences of their eastings and northings.

18nav2Scale Is a Ratio of Similar Triangles

A paper map shrinks the grid. At scale 1 : n, a length of 1 on the paper stands for a length of n on the ground, in the same unit. So 1 : 24 000 means 1 cm on paper for 24 000 cm, or 240 m, on the ground. The United States Geological Survey's standard maps of California are at 1 : 24 000, one sheet for each small patch: Monterey, Seaside, Marina, Moss Landing, Carmel Valley and the rest.

A reuse, said out loud. 18f called X ↦ O + k(X − O) a map: a function from the plane to itself. A paper map is that same thing. Put the origin at O, take k = 1/n, then slide the result onto the paper, which changes no distance. 18f's word keeps its job; this page only adds the paper.

Map-scale technique: on a map at one scale, every distance on paper is the same fraction of the distance on the ground. At scale 1 : n, paper distance = ground distance / n and paper area = ground area / n². Every triangle of places is similar to its picture on the paper, with scale factor 1/n.

X \mapsto O + \tfrac1n (X - O) @@ the paper map, with origin O and k = 1/n
\begin{aligned}&\text{every distance times } \tfrac1n,\\ &\quad \ \text{every area times } \tfrac{1}{n^2}\end{aligned} @@ scaling in the plane (18f)
\begin{aligned}&\text{triangle } ABC \text{ and its}\\ &\quad\text{picture } A'B'C' \text{ are similar}\end{aligned} @@ scaling in the plane carries one onto the other: the meaning of similar (18f)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{A'B'}{AB} = \tfrac{B'C'}{BC} = \tfrac{C'A'}{CA} = \tfrac1n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same factor for every pair of places

So a map can be checked. Measure any three places on the ground and on the paper. If the three ratios differ, the map is not at one scale. And a map's scale can be found from one known distance: divide the paper length by the ground length, in the same unit.

Most maps also carry a scale bar: a segment drawn on the paper, marked with the ground length it stands for. A scale bar is printed as part of the map. Enlarge the map on a copier, and the bar grows with everything else, so it stays right. The ratio printed beside it does not.

18nav3Reading Distance Off the Map

There are two ways to find a distance from a map. Read the eastings and northings, and use distance-laws. Or lay a ruler across the paper and divide by the scale. The two agree. The right triangle with legs Δe and Δn on the ground is similar to its picture on the paper, so the ruler measures 1/n of √(Δe² + Δn²).

\text{Point Pinos to Moss Landing, at } 1 : 250\,000 @@ a road map of the region
\tfrac{23.1 \text{ km}}{250\,000} = \tfrac{2\,310\,000 \text{ cm}}{250\,000} \approx 9.2 \text{ cm} @@ map-scale: the ruler reads 9.2 cm
\text{a field } 2.5 \text{ cm} \times 1.5 \text{ cm at } 1 : 24\,000 @@ a lettuce field near Salinas
600 \text{ m} \times 360 \text{ m} = 216\,000 \text{ m}^2 = 21.6 \text{ ha} @@ each side times 240 m; the area times 240² (1 ha = 10 000 m²)

A ruler gives the straight distance. A road is never shorter than that, and a winding one is much longer. Highway 1 south of Carmel follows every bend of the coast. By 18b's triangle technique in the plane, each bend can only add length, since a path made of segments is at least as long as the straight segment from its start to its end.

18nav4A Surveyor's Triangle

How was the first map made, with no map to copy? Measure one length on the ground with great care: a base line AB. From each end, sight a far point T, a hilltop or a lighthouse, and record the direction. Two directions from two known points fix T. Surveyor's-triangle technique: one measured length and two sighted directions fix a far point.

There are two ways to say it, with the tools of 18f and of 18c. With angles: measure the angles at A and at B in degrees. Draw any triangle A′B′T′ with the same two angles, on paper. By similar-triangles, it is similar to ABT. So AT = AB · A′T′/A′B′, and likewise for BT. The paper triangle is a map of the ground triangle, with its scale set by the base. With coordinates: put A at (0, 0) and B at (b, 0). Mark each sight line with a stake: from A, a stake p along the base and q out from it lines up with T. Each sight line is then a line through a known point with a known direction, and two-lines-meet gives T.

A = (0, 0),\ B = (600, 0) \text{ m} @@ a base line along the beach at Del Monte, in Monterey
\text{from } A:\ \text{stake at } (100, 150);\ \ \text{from } B:\ \text{stake at } (500, 300) @@ each lines up with a buoy offshore
y = 1.5x,\ \ y = -3(x - 600) @@ the two sight lines: through a point, with a slope (18g)
1.5x = 1800 - 3x \Rightarrow T = (400, 600) @@ two-lines-meet (18c): their slopes differ
AT = \sqrt{400^2 + 600^2} \approx 721 \text{ m},\ \ BT = \sqrt{200^2 + 600^2} \approx 632 \text{ m} @@ distance-laws
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{the stake triangle is a map of the big one, }}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{at } 1 : 4} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ parallel-cut (18f): legs 100 and 150 against 400 and 600

Once T is fixed, AT is a known length too, and it can serve as the base for the next triangle. A chain of triangles, each resting on the last, carries one careful measurement across a whole state. Its accepted name is triangulation. Only the first base is measured with a tape; every other length comes from the sightings.

18nav5Heights Drawn Flat

A graph turns numbers into a shape. A height map does the reverse. Walk over a hill and note, at each place (e, n), its height above the sea. That is a function from places to heights, and paper is flat. So draw it flat: for a chosen height, mark every place at exactly that height, and join the marks. Each such line has the accepted name contour line, and the drawing is a contour map.

from above: a contour map of a hill, heights in metres
from the side: the height along the dashed line

Read the picture. Walking along one line, you neither climb nor fall. Stepping from one line to the next, you climb or fall the same 100 m every time. Where the lines crowd together, that 100 m comes in a short walk; where they spread out, the walk is long. The second drawing is the familiar kind, a graph: the height along the dashed line, cut out of the map.

Every line in this picture closes up on itself, round the top. Could a contour line ever simply stop, in the middle of the land?

18·nav.7.6seamIn 1569 a Flemish map-maker drew the whole world on a grid that keeps the shapes of small regions, and stretches lands far from the equator: Greenland looks as big as Africa, which is about fourteen times larger. Map-makers answered that his map was neither new nor better, and squashed every shape near the poles.

Who said it first

Snellius told how he measured the Earth by a chain of triangles across Holland, resting the whole on one base line measured on the ground and carrying every other length by angles alone.

Willebrord Snellius, Eratosthenes Batavus (1617); paraphrase

In 18·nav.7.6, the map-maker of 1569 is Gerardus Mercator, and the historian of 1973 is Arno Peters. In California, George Davidson of the United States Coast Survey worked the coast from 1850, and in 1881 measured a base line near Davis, in Yolo County, from which chains of triangles crossed the state.

Accepted names: easting and northing; triangulation; the grid printed on the Geological Survey's sheets is the Universal Transverse Mercator grid, in metres.
