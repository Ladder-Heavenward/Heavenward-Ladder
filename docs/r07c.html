7c · proof

Choosing Forces Either-Way

Here the either-way principle becomes a technique. Take any statement at all. Sift the set holding 0 and 1 twice, by two conditions that mention the statement, so that the two sifts are the same set exactly when the statement is true. Then pick from each with a choosing function. A function must pick the same member from the same set. So its two picks carry news about the statement out into the open, where comparing 0 with 1, which can always be done, reads it off. Every line of the column below cites a tech or an earlier result. After it, either-way may be cited as a reason. Then the step turns to the even-sum guess, where the principle says something nobody can check.

Two sifts that coincide exactly when the statement is true; one function picking from both. If the picks differ, the statement cannot be true; if they agree, it is true. Either way, it is so or not so.

7c1The Setting: Two Sifts of {0, 1}

The set is 2 = {0, 1}, from 3b. Three facts about it are needed. First, 0 ≠ 1. 1 names S(0), and next-never-empty writes S(x) ≠ ∅, so 1 ≠ 0, and the turn-about technique for not-same turns it about. Second, its members are numbers: 2 is a number, and ℕ-is-transitive says every member of a number is a number. Third, therefore, sameness between members of 2 is decidable, by settled-order: for u and v in 2, u = v ∨ u ≠ v, with no either-way principle.

Now take any statement P whatever: “2 + 2 = 4”, “2 + 2 = 5”, 5g's “this question has an answer” said of an equation, or the even-sum guess. The column below makes each of them true or false, and for the last two it does so before anyone can check. 2d's Separation is laid down for any condition, and a condition may mention P. So, by Separation, these two sets exist:

U keeps 0 for certain, and keeps 1 only if P is true. V keeps 1 for certain, and keeps 0 only if P is true. So if P is true, both keep everything and are the same set, 2; if ¬P, U = {0} and V = {1}, two different sets. If nobody knows P, nobody can list U or V, and nobody can say whether they are the same. They are perfectly good sets all the same, by Separation.

7c2A Choosing Function for the Two

Each sift has a member: 0 ∈ U, since 0 = 0 by the self-sameness axiom, then add an option, and 0 ∈ 2. Likewise 1 ∈ V. Gather the two into the collection {U, V}. Every member of it is non-empty. A member is U or V, by the listing, and each case has its member. So, by the choosing axiom, there is a choosing function for {U, V}. Call it c: c(U) ∈ U and c(V) ∈ V.

Two cheaper roads are worth trying, and both fail, which is the point. Why not 6c's finite choice, with no axiom? Finite-choice needs a collection with a number of members. {U, V} has 1 member if U = V and 2 if not, and which it is depends on P. It is 6c's lesson about two names in a listing that may name one thing, met in its sharpest form. Why not two calls, “call u ∈ U, call v ∈ V”? Because the proof needs one line that two calls cannot give: if U = V, then the two picks are the same. A function gives it. Here it is, one move per line: the line the whole argument turns on.

U = V @@ given, inside the case below
c(U) = c(U) @@ the self-sameness axiom (0b)
\htmlClass{new}{c(U) = c(V)} @@ the swap axiom (0b): from U = V, swap V in at the right-hand place only

That is all a function is for: one input, one output, so the same input gives the same output under any name. Two separate calls have no such line between them.

7c3The Proof

Choice-gives-either-way technique: with the choosing axiom, every statement is so or not so. In marks: for any statement P, P ∨ ¬P. It rests on the choosing axiom, through the line calling c, and on Separation, same-members and the facts about 2 above.

\text{let } P \text{ be any statement} @@ speak of anything
U = \{x \in 2 : x = 0 \lor P\} @@ Separation (2d); the condition mentions P
V = \{x \in 2 : x = 1 \lor P\} @@ Separation again
0 \in U,\ \ 1 \in V @@ the self-sameness axiom; add an option; 0, 1 ∈ 2 (3b); ︿ each sift
\text{every member of } \{U, V\} \text{ is non-empty} @@ Pairing; a member is U or V (the listing); cases; the swap axiom; show an example
\text{call } c \text{ with } c(U) \in U \land c(V) \in V @@ the choosing axiom (6b); use the some; U, V ∈ {U, V}
u = c(U),\ \ v = c(V) @@ names for the two picks (4d's value line)
u \in 2 \land (u = 0 \lor P),\ \ v \in 2 \land (v = 1 \lor P) @@ ﹀ each sift at its pick
u = v \lor u \ne v @@ u, v ∈ 2, so numbers (ℕ-is-transitive); settled-order (4h): decidable, no either-way
\textbf{case } u \ne v: @@ the first case
\quad \text{assume } P @@ aiming to refute
\quad \forall x\,(x \in U \Leftrightarrow x \in V) @@ let x be anything; with P, each condition is true by add an option, so each sift's line reads x ∈ 2; ⇔ chains (1d)
\quad U = V @@ same-members (2c)
\quad c(U) = c(V) @@ the self-sameness axiom, then the swap axiom, as above
\quad u = v @@ the names: the swap axiom on both sides
\quad \bot @@ clash with the case
\quad \htmlClass{new}{\neg P},\ \text{so } P \lor \neg P @@ refute; add an option
\textbf{case } u = v: @@ the second case
\quad \text{case } P:\ P @@ cases on u = 0 ∨ P: the easy side
\quad \text{case } u = 0:\ v = 0 @@ the turn-about technique on u = v, then chain
\qquad \text{case } P:\ P @@ cases on v = 1 ∨ P
\qquad \text{case } v = 1:\ 0 = 1,\ \bot,\ P @@ the chain technique with turn-about: 0 = v = 1; clash with 0 ≠ 1; everything permitted
\quad \htmlClass{new}{P},\ \text{so } P \lor \neg P @@ reason by cases (twice); add an option
\htmlClass{new}{P \lor \neg P} \quad \blacksquare @@ reason by cases on u = v ∨ u ≠ v; the call for c closes, since the line does not mention c

Read the trick in plain words. The two sets are built so that whether they coincide depends on P. The choosing function cannot see P. But it must treat equal sets alike. So if its picks differ, the sets were not equal, and P fails. If its picks agree, they cannot be the 0 that U keeps for certain and the 1 that V keeps for certain at once. So one of the picks was kept only because P is true. The picks are two members of {0, 1}, and comparing them is a finite check. By the axiom the picks exist, and the axiom says nothing of what they are; the logic reads P off them.

From here on, either-way may be cited as a reason on any line: for any statement P, P ∨ ¬P (the choice-gives-either-way technique). The choice-gives-either-way technique leans on the choosing axiom to reach its last line. It is the first proof on the ladder that does. This closes the flag 1f and 7a kept on the principle itself: either-way, named on 1f and put off on 7a, is now proved.

7c4What the Proof Used, and What It Did Not

Notice where the endless came in: nowhere. There are two sets, each with at most two members, and a collection of at most two sets. The power came from two places. One is sifting by a condition that mentions an arbitrary statement, which may itself be about all the numbers. The other is the function that, by the axiom, exists and that nobody computes. That is why set theories that keep rung 1's gap refuse the full axiom, even when they accept some forms of choice, such as 6c's choice from finitely many sets. With the full axiom, Separation and same-members, the principle they reject would be proved.

And the road runs one way. The either-way principle does not give choice. 6d told of the 1963 world, in which every statement is true or false and every principle of rungs 2 and 3 is true, and socks like the sock world's have no choosing function. So the ladder now has both, and needed the axiom for both: Choice laid down on 6b, and either-way proved from it here.

7c5Where It Bites: The Even-Sum Guess

In 1742 a guess about writing numbers as sums of primes was sent in a letter. The reply put it in the form still open: every even number above 2 is a sum of two primes. 4 = 2 + 2, 6 = 3 + 3, 8 = 3 + 5, 100 = 3 + 97. The accepted word for an open guess like this is conjecture: a claim believed but not yet proved. Write P for the statement that it fails somewhere:

Each case is decidable. For a given n, the primes to try are at most n, so 4j's bounded-some decides “n is a sum of two primes”. 7b ran the look as far as you liked, and the world's computers have run it to 4 · 10¹⁸. Every case passes. Nobody has proved P false, and nobody has found it true. Now the choice-gives-either-way technique, used with this P:

P \lor \neg P @@ the choice-gives-either-way technique, used with the even-sum P
\begin{aligned}&\text{“some even number above 2 is}\\ &\quad\text{not a sum of two primes, or none is”}\end{aligned} @@ the same line, in words

The line is true, and nobody can say which side. It is not a claim that the answer is known or will be. It is a claim about how things stand: exactly the oldest reading of the principle, and exactly what its refusers refused. On this ladder it is a technique, because the ladder chose the choosing axiom on 6b. 7d shows what such a line lets you prove, including, from this very P, a number with a strange property and no address.

7c7Running It Backwards

Wanting P ∨ ¬P for an undecided P, cite choice-gives-either-way. Wanting to know whether a proof used choice, look for a line of the form “call c, a choosing function”. Ask whether a rule, a finite collection, or the axiom supplied it; choice-gives-either-way is one place where only the axiom will do. Handed two picks from sets whose sameness is unknown, ask whether they came from one function. If they did, comparing them says something about the sets. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

Choice-gives-either-way is Radu Diaconescu’s (1975), found again by Nicolas Goodman and John Myhill (1978). The even-sum guess was sent by Christian Goldbach to Leonhard Euler in 1742, and Euler’s reply gave it its present form. The oldest reading of the principle is Aristotle’s; its refusal is L. E. J. Brouwer’s. The 1963 world is Paul Cohen’s, and the sock world is Abraham Fraenkel’s.

Diaconescu found that, in a very general setting of worlds for sets, the axiom of choice forces every statement to be true or false: choice and complementation, in his title's words, go together.

Diaconescu, “Axiom of choice and complementation”, Proceedings of the American Mathematical Society 51 (1975)

Goodman and Myhill gave the same argument, in a few lines, for set theories that keep rung 1's gap, under the title that says it all: choice implies excluded middle.

Goodman and Myhill, “Choice implies excluded middle”, Zeitschrift für mathematische Logik und Grundlagen der Mathematik 24 (1978)

Accepted names

Choice-gives-either-way is Diaconescu’s theorem (or the Diaconescu–Goodman–Myhill theorem). The either-way principle is the law of excluded middle. The even-sum guess is Goldbach’s conjecture.
