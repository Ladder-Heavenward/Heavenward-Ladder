How Is a World Built Where the Line Is Bigger?

How is a world built where the line is bigger? Rung 35 named the result of 1963, that the axioms for sets cannot prove that the line has the first size past the counting numbers, and said its proof was far beyond that rung. This page builds it. Start from a world of sets that can be listed from outside, as rung 38 found every world of the axioms has a copy that can. Inside it, write down finite pieces of a new set of counting numbers, each piece a promise about finitely many numbers. Outside it, take one long chain of pieces, each extending the last, chosen so that it meets every list of pieces the world itself can describe in which every piece has an extension. The chain is the new set. No set of the old world is equal to it, since the world can describe the list of pieces that disagree with any of its own sets. Then build the new world from names, written in the old world, whose meaning is fixed only once the new set is known. A statement is true in the new world exactly when some piece of the chain makes it so, and from that, every axiom holds there. Add not one new set but as many as the second size past the counting numbers, and the line in the new world is at least that big, provided the sizes of the old world have not collapsed. They have not: any list of pieces that pairwise contradict each other is countable, by a counting argument with boxes. So the axioms leave the size of the line open. Since then the question has moved to which new axioms to add, and whether there is one world of sets at all, which the research front of rung 35 tells.

Let M be a countable world of the axioms, P the finite partial maps from ω₂ × ω to {0, 1}, ordered by extension. A chain G meeting every dense set of P in M exists, since M has only countably many; g_α(n) = G(α, n) gives ℵ₂ reals, each new and no two equal. M[G] = {τ^G : τ a name in M} is a world of the axioms, true statements are forced by some p ∈ G, and since every set of pairwise clashing conditions is countable, ω₁ and ω₂ of M stay ω₁ and ω₂. So 2^ℵ₀ ≥ ℵ₂ there, and the axioms do not prove 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁.

sky36.1A Countable World, Seen From Outside

38e4’s seen-from-outside technique gave a world M of the axioms whose members can be listed from outside. The 1963 method needs one more property. M is transitive when every member of a member of M is itself a member of M, and the mark ∈ in M means real membership. Then M sees the sets it holds as they are: ∅, ω, pairs and maps mean in M what they mean outside.

V_0 = \varnothing,\ V_{k+1} = \text{all parts of } V_k @@ 3f’s layers
|V_0|, \ldots, |V_4| = 0, 1, 2, 4, 16 @@ counted by machine
\text{every member of a member of } V_k \text{ is in } V_k @@ checked for k ≤ 4: each layer is transitive
\htmlClass{new}{\{\{1\}\} \text{ alone is not: } \{1\} \in \text{it, } 1 \notin \text{it}} @@ a member’s member is missing

Same-inside technique: if M is transitive and x, y are in M, then “x is a part of y” is true in M exactly when x is a part of y; and a part of ω that is in M is a real part of ω. The statement “every z in x is in y” runs, inside M, over the members of M. Since M is transitive, the members of x are all in M, so “every z in M” and “every z” say the same of them.

Two things are held in trust. That a countable world can be made transitive without changing what is true in it is a technique of 1949, a collapse that renames each member by the set of the renamed members of it. And that the axioms have a countable transitive world at all is more than they can prove, by 38j. The working proof avoids this: any proof of 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁ uses finitely many axioms, and for finitely many axioms a countable transitive world can be found. So assume one, M, and remember the finitely-many fix. Inside M, by the same-inside technique and 35f3, “2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁” means what it means anywhere; from outside, M holds only countably many parts of ω.

sky36.2Finite Pieces of a New Set

The new set will be a map g from ω to {0, 1}, a part of ω written by its yes and no. A condition is a finite piece of such a map: a map p from a finite part of ω to {0, 1}. Condition q extends p when q holds p, the same promises and maybe more. Two conditions are compatible when some condition extends both, which happens exactly when they agree where both are defined.

p = \{(0, 1), (3, 0)\},\ \ q = \{(0, 1), (5, 1)\} @@ both promise g(0) = 1
p \cup q = \{(0, 1), (3, 0), (5, 1)\} @@ a common extension: compatible
r = \{(3, 1)\}:\ \ p, r \text{ clash at } 3 @@ incompatible
\text{conditions with domain inside } \{0, 1, 2\}:\ 3^3 = 27 @@ each number: missing, 0 or 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the } 8 \text{ maps on all of } \{0, 1, 2\} \text{ pairwise clash}} @@ a clashing set of size 2ⁿ, for every n

A set D of conditions is dense when every condition has an extension in D. Dense sets are the demands the new set must meet. Two are basic. For a number k, D_k, the conditions that decide k, is dense: add (k, 0) if k is undecided. For a map x from ω to {0, 1}, D_x, the conditions that disagree with x somewhere, is dense: given p, take k past its domain and add (k, 1 − x(k)).

Dense-pair technique: D_k and D_x are dense, and if the set of conditions P and the maps k, x are in M, so are D_k and D_x. Each is sifted from P by a condition on its members that mentions only P, k and x, so the world’s own Sift builds it.

sky36.3A Chain That Meets Every Demand

A filter is a set G of conditions, any two of them with a common extension in G, and holding every condition that some member of G extends. G is generic over M when it meets every dense set that is a member of M. No world can describe every demand on a set outside it; M can describe only its own dense sets, and from outside there are only countably many of them.

Chain-meets-all technique: if M is countable, a filter generic over M exists.

D_0, D_1, D_2, \ldots \text{ lists the dense sets in } M @@ M is listed from outside (sky36.1)
p_0 = \varnothing,\ \ p_{n+1} \in D_n \text{ extends } p_n @@ D_n is dense; a rule picks the first such in a listing of conditions
G = \{p : p_n \text{ extends } p \text{ for some } n\} @@ the pieces of the chain
\text{two members lie under } p_m, p_n:\ \text{both under } p_{\max(m,n)} @@ a filter
\htmlClass{new}{p_{n+1} \in G \cap D_n \text{ for every } n} \quad \blacksquare @@ generic

New-set technique: if G is generic over M, then g = the union of G is a map from all of ω to {0, 1}, and g is not in M. G meets each D_k, so g decides every k; any two members of G agree, so g is a map. For x in M, G meets D_x, so g disagrees with x somewhere. If g were in M, it would disagree with itself.

D_0:\ \{(0, 0)\} @@ decide 0
D_{\text{zeros}}:\ \{(0, 0), (1, 1)\} @@ disagree with the all-zero map
D_{\text{ones}}:\ \text{already met} @@ g(0) = 0 disagrees with the all-one map
D_{\text{alt}}:\ \{(0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 0), (3, 0)\} @@ agreed with 0, 1, 0, 1, … so far; disagree at 3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each step settles finitely much; the whole list settles } g} @@ checked by machine

sky36.4Names for Sets Not Yet Built

M cannot hold g, but it can hold descriptions of sets that will mention g. A name is a set of pairs (σ, p), with σ a name and p a condition; the definition runs up the layers, names of names, held in trust as 6d3’s stages were. Its value, once G is known, keeps each σ whose condition p is in G:

\(\tau^G = \{\sigma^G : (\sigma, p) \in \tau \text{ for some } p \in G\}.\)

\check x = \{(\check y, \varnothing) : y \in x\}:\ \ \check x^G = x @@ ∅ is in every G; by induction on the layers
\Gamma = \{(\check p, p) : p \in P\}:\ \ \Gamma^G = G @@ p’s name survives exactly when p ∈ G
\tau = \{(\check 0, \{(5, 1)\}),\ (\check 1, \{(5, 0)\})\} @@ a name whose value waits on g(5)
\tau^G = \{0\} \text{ if } g(5) = 1,\ \ \{1\} \text{ if } g(5) = 0 @@ checked by machine
\htmlClass{new}{\{(\sigma, \varnothing), (\rho, \varnothing)\}^G = \{\sigma^G, \rho^G\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the pair-name technique

Pair-name technique: for names σ and ρ in M, the name {(σ, ∅), (ρ, ∅)} is in M and its value is {σ^G, ρ^G}; so the new world holds the pair of any two of its members. The new world is M[G], the set of values of all names in M. It holds every x in M, as the value of x̌, and it holds G, as the value of Γ, and so g. Pairing is one axiom, checked; the others are checked the same way, each by a name built in M, and some need sky36.5 (held in trust). Choice is true in M[G]: unlike 6d6’s world, cut down to finite supports, M[G] keeps every name. Here Γ (capital gamma) is the name in M whose value is G, not 26l's gamma function.

sky36.5Forcing, and One Case of the Truth Fact

Write p ⊩ φ, p forces φ, for: φ is true in M[G] for every G generic over M that holds p. Two facts are held in trust here, both proved by induction on the formula. The definability fact: for each φ, the relation p ⊩ φ, between conditions and names in M, is a set M can sift, by a definition that refers only to M. The truth fact: φ is true in M[G] exactly when some p in G forces φ. The second is proved one mark at a time; here is the case of “not”.

Dense-decides technique: if the truth fact holds for φ, it holds for ¬φ: if ¬φ is true in M[G], some p in G forces ¬φ.

D = \{p : p \Vdash \varphi \text{ or } p \Vdash \neg\varphi\},\ \ D \in M @@ the definability fact
\text{given } p \text{ with } p \not\Vdash \neg\varphi:\ \text{some generic } H \ni p \text{ makes } \varphi \text{ true} @@ the definition of forcing, read backward
q \in H,\ q \Vdash \varphi;\ \ r \in H \text{ extends } p \text{ and } q @@ the truth fact for φ; H is a filter
r \Vdash \varphi \ \text{and}\ r \text{ extends } p,\ \text{so } D \text{ is dense} @@ every generic holding r holds q
p \in G \cap D;\ \ p \Vdash \varphi \text{ would make } \varphi \text{ true in } M[G] @@ G is generic; but ¬φ is true there
\htmlClass{new}{p \Vdash \neg\varphi,\ p \in G} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other choice in D

With the truth fact for every formula, each axiom’s truth in M[G] comes down to sets M can build, as the pair-name technique did for one. Held in trust: every axiom is true in M[G], M and M[G] have the same points of the run, and M[G] is the least transitive world of the axioms holding M and G. So M[G] is a new world of the axioms, made from an old one and a chain the old one cannot see.

sky36.6Adding ℵ₂ New Reals

Now let the conditions be finite maps from pairs (α, n), with α a point of the run below ω₂ of M and n in ω, to {0, 1}. A generic G gives one new map g_α for each α: g_α(n) = 1 when G promises 1 at (α, n).

Distinct-reals technique: for α ≠ β, g_α ≠ g_β; so M[G] holds as many maps from ω to {0, 1} as there are points below ω₂ of M. The set D_αβ of conditions that give (α, n) and (β, n) different values for some n is dense: extend at an n undecided at both. It is in M, so G meets it.

p \text{ decides finitely many } (\alpha, n), (\beta, n) @@ a condition is finite
n \text{ past them all}:\ p \cup \{((\alpha, n), 0), ((\beta, n), 1)\} \in D_{\alpha\beta} @@ dense
D_{\alpha\beta} \in M @@ sifted from P, α, β
\htmlClass{new}{G \cap D_{\alpha\beta} \neq \varnothing:\ \ g_\alpha \neq g_\beta} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every pair, at once

So in M[G], 2^ℵ₀ is at least the size of ω₂ of M. That is ℵ₂ in M[G] only if ω₂ of M is still the second size past ω there, and ω₁ still the first. Making new maps might make new matchings, and a new matching of ω₁ onto ω₂ of M would collapse it. sky36.7 shows it does not happen. With that, 2^ℵ₀ ≥ ℵ₂ in M[G]; counting names shows it is exactly ℵ₂ (stated). With conditions on ω_k × ω the same lines give ℵ_k, and the 1970 result of 35f4 reaches almost every size.

That is where 1963 left the question. What came after is told on 35·now: two strong new axioms of different kinds each make 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂, and in 2021 the stronger form of one was proved to imply the other (35·now.3); a programme of a described world large enough for every large size, if it can be built, points to ℵ₁ (35·now.2); and since 2012 a school holds that the 1963 method moves between worlds none of which is the true one (35·now.2). Every one of those arguments uses the method of this page.

sky36.7Why the Old Sizes Survive

Call a set of conditions clashing when any two of its members are incompatible. sky36.2 found clashing sets of size 2ⁿ for every n. The sizes survive because no clashing set is uncountable. The proof is by 42i’s crowded-box technique, used twice in its endless form: uncountably many things in countably many boxes put uncountably many in one box (with choice, held in trust).

Common-root technique: every uncountable family of finite sets has an uncountable part in which any two sets share exactly the same set r, the root.

\text{uncountably many sets, countably many sizes:\ uncountably many of one size } n @@ the crowded box, uncountable form
n = 0 \text{ or } 1:\ \text{distinct sets of size} \le 1 \text{ share } \varnothing @@ the root is empty
\text{some } e \text{ in uncountably many}:\ \text{remove } e,\ \text{size } n - 1,\ \text{root } r;\ \text{put } e \text{ back: root } r \cup \{e\} @@ by induction on n
\text{each } e \text{ in only countably many}:\ \text{pick, one at a time, sets missing all earlier picks} @@ countably many picks touch countably many sets (held in trust: a countable union of countable sets, with choice)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an uncountable part with root } \varnothing \text{ or } r \cup \{e\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ {1, 2, 5}, {1, 2, 7}, {1, 2, 9}: root {1, 2}

Agreeing-root technique: every clashing set of conditions is countable.

\text{suppose } A \text{ clashing and uncountable} @@ aiming to refute
\text{at most } 2^n \text{ conditions share one domain of size } n @@ so A has uncountably many domains
\text{an uncountable part whose domains share a root } r @@ the common-root technique
\text{only } 2^{|r|} \text{ ways to fill } r:\ \text{two of them, } p, q, \text{ agree on } r @@ the crowded box again
p \cup q \text{ is a condition} @@ they meet only on r, and agree there
\htmlClass{new}{p, q \text{ compatible}:\ \bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute; root of size 2: 4 fillings, so of 5 conditions two agree

Size-keeping technique: in M[G], no map takes ω₁ of M onto ω₂ of M. Suppose a name τ and p in G force “τ maps ω₁ onto ω₂”. In M, for each α below ω₁, let F(α) be the β with some extension of p forcing τ(α) = β. Two different β have clashing witnesses, so by the agreeing-root technique, run inside M, F(α) is countable. By the truth fact, τ^G(α) is in F(α). So the image lies in a union of ℵ₁ countable sets, which in M has size at most ℵ₁ (35e, with choice, held in trust), less than ℵ₂: not onto. The same lines keep ω₁. So ω₂ of M is ω₂ of M[G], and the line there has at least ℵ₂ points: the axioms do not prove 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞36.oq.1How many points are on a line? The axioms leave it open; this page made it ℵ₂ in one world and 35f3’s described world makes it ℵ₁ in another. Is there one right answer, fixed by new axioms most would accept, and is it ℵ₁ or ℵ₂? 35·now sets out the two strong axioms that agree on ℵ₂ and the programme that points to ℵ₁; neither side has persuaded the other.
∞36.oq.2Is there one world of sets or many? If every world the method of this page builds is as real as any other, the continuum question has been answered as fully as it can be: true in some worlds, false in others. If there is one world, the question has an answer the axioms have not reached. What would count as evidence either way, when every candidate world is built from the axioms themselves?
∞36.oq.3Can the described world be made large enough? The programme told on 35·now.2 asks for a world built in stages from below, like the described world of 6d3, that still holds every large size proposed. If it exists, the size of the line in it is ℵ₁. Whether it can be built is open, and its construction is one of the main projects of the field.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The question was asked before there were axioms to ask it of. By 1900 it headed a famous list of problems, and in 1938 a world of the axioms where the line has the first size past the counting numbers showed it could not be refuted. The other half waited a quarter of a century.

It came in 1963, published in 1963 and 1964, and its author received the best-known medal in mathematics in 1966. Within a few years it was recast in a form with truth values drawn from a structure of conditions, which some found clearer and others found further from the sets; both forms are taught.

The method spread quickly. Almost every question about the line that the old axioms could not settle was shown, one by one, to be independent of them, and the 1970 result of 35f4 showed the line can have almost any size the 1905 bound allows. Some welcomed this as a map of possibilities. Others saw it as a sign that the axioms were too weak, and turned to large sizes and new axioms, the story 35·now tells.

Since 2000 the method has been the ground of the argument about whether there is one world of sets: the 2012 view that the worlds forcing reaches are all equally real, and the programme that seeks a single described world large enough to settle the question. The independence itself had been checked line by line in a proof assistant by 2021. What is argued now is not whether the method works, but what its many worlds mean.

The accepted names

The method is forcing; the conditions with extension are the Cohen poset Fn(ω₂ × ω, 2), ordered by reverse inclusion; a filter meeting every dense set in M is an M-generic filter, and the chain-meets-all technique is the Rasiowa–Sikorski lemma. M is a countable transitive model (ctm); the collapse of 1949 is the Mostowski collapse; the finitely-many fix is the reflection argument. Names are P-names, x̌ the check name of x, Γ the canonical name of the generic, M[G] the generic extension. The definability fact and the truth fact are the forcing theorem (definability lemma and truth lemma). The common-root technique is the Δ-system lemma; a clashing set is an antichain; the agreeing-root technique is the countable chain condition (c.c.c.) of the Cohen poset, and the size-keeping technique is preservation of cardinals by c.c.c. forcing. The described world is Gödel’s constructible universe L; the 1970 result is Easton’s theorem. The recasting with truth values is Boolean-valued models. Since 2000: Woodin’s Ω-logic and the Ultimate L programme; Asperó and Schindler, “Martin’s Maximum⁺⁺ implies Woodin’s axiom (*)”, Annals of Mathematics 193 (2021); Hamkins, “The set-theoretic multiverse”, Review of Symbolic Logic (2012).

Who said it first

Paul Cohen proved the independence of the continuum hypothesis (and of choice) in 1963, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 1963 and 1964; he received the Fields Medal in 1966. Kurt Gödel built L and proved the consistency of CH in 1938. Dana Scott and Robert Solovay recast forcing as Boolean-valued models in the mid-1960s. Andrzej Mostowski gave the collapse (1949); Helena Rasiowa and Roman Sikorski the lemma (1950s); the Δ-system lemma is usually credited to N. A. Shanin (1946). William Easton proved his theorem in 1970. The formal check of 2019 to 2021 is the Flypitch project, in the Lean proof assistant; work on forcing in Isabelle/ZF began in 2018.

A proof that is not allowed

“Add ℵ₂ new reals to the world; then the line has ℵ₂ points.” Not allowed as it stands: adding sets can add matchings too, and a new matching could make ω₂ of the old world countable or of size ℵ₁ in the new one. Without the agreeing-root technique the count proves nothing; some other ways of adding sets do collapse sizes.
