47e · proof

Limits and Colimits

A product answers one question: what is the best thing with an arrow to each of two things? The question can be asked of any picture of things and arrows, not only of two dots. Ask it of two parallel arrows, and the answer in sets is the sift of the members where the two arrows agree. Turn the arrows round, and the answer in sets is the classes of rung five’s call back: the members, with each one taken as the same as whatever the two arrows force it to be. And ask it, arrows turned round, of two things glued along a third, and the answer in spaces is rung thirty four’s gluing. The integers as classes of pairs, the circle as an interval with its ends glued, the figure eight as two circles meeting at a point: each is the best thing for a picture, and what makes each one useful, that a map out of it is a map that respects the gluing, is the property that pins it down.

A picture of things and arrows is a shape; a limit of it is the best thing with arrows to the whole picture, and a colimit the best thing with arrows from it. In sets, the limit of two parallel arrows is the sift where they agree, and the colimit is the classes they force. Gluing two spaces along a third is a colimit, and a map out of the glued space is a pair of maps that agree where they are glued.

47e1Shapes and Cones

A shape is a category J with few things, such as two dots with no arrows, or two dots with two parallel arrows u, v : 1 → 2. A picture of shape J in C is a map of categories D : J → C. A cone over D is a thing L with an arrow λ_j : L → D(j) for each thing j of J, such that D(w) ∘ λ_j = λ_k for every arrow w : j → k of J. A limit of D is a cone through which every cone runs by exactly one arrow that agrees with all of its arrows. By 47d2’s proof, word for word, any two limits of one picture are matched in exactly one way.

Over two dots with no arrows, a cone is a thing with an arrow to each, and a limit is 47d’s product. Over two parallel arrows f, g : A → B, a cone is L with λ : L → A and λ′ : L → B, with f ∘ λ = λ′ and g ∘ λ = λ′: so f ∘ λ = g ∘ λ, and λ′ is fixed by λ. A limit of that picture is a meeting place of f and g.

Sift-meets technique: in Set, the sift of A where f and g agree, with its members sent to themselves, is a meeting place of f and g.

E = \{x \in A : f(x) = g(x)\},\ \ e(x) = x @@ 2e’s sift, and its members sent into A
f \circ e = g \circ e @@ each member of E is one where f and g agree
h : X \to A,\ \ f \circ h = g \circ h \Rightarrow h(x) \in E \text{ for every } x @@ each h(x) is a member where they agree
\htmlClass{new}{\text{exactly one } u : X \to E \text{ with } e \circ u = h} \quad \blacksquare @@ 9b4’s sift-function technique

So the ladder’s oldest tool, the sift of 2e, is a limit. In Vect the meeting place of f and the 0 map is the kernel, 27c’s “what a map loses”.

47e2Classes Are a Colimit

A colimit is a limit in C^op: the best thing with arrows from the whole picture. Over two dots it is 47d3’s sum. Over two parallel arrows f, g : A → B it is a forcing place: a thing Q with q : B → Q and q ∘ f = q ∘ g, through which every such arrow runs in exactly one way.

In Set, let ∼ be the smallest sameness relation on B with f(x) ∼ g(x) for every x in A: the sift of B × B by membership in every sameness relation that contains those pairs (2h14). Its classes, B/∼, are 5·cb2’s quotient set, and q sends each member to its class.

Classes-force technique: in Set, the classes of the smallest sameness relation that puts f(x) with g(x), with each member sent to its class, are a forcing place of f and g: a function out of the classes is a function on B that is constant on each class.

q(f(x)) = [f(x)] = [g(x)] = q(g(x)) @@ f(x) ∼ g(x), and same class exactly when related (5·cb2)
h : B \to X,\ \ h \circ f = h \circ g @@ a rival arrow out of B
\{(b, b') : h(b) = h(b')\} \text{ is a sameness relation holding every } (f(x), g(x)) @@ 2h14: h’s own sameness
b \sim b' \Rightarrow h(b) = h(b') @@ ∼ is the smallest such: it sits inside h’s
u([b]) = h(b) \text{ is well named} @@ the well-defined-rule technique (5·cb2)
\htmlClass{new}{u \circ q = h,\ \text{and } u \text{ is the only one}} \quad \blacksquare @@ q is onto: every class is [b] for some b

The integers of 5b are a forcing place: take B = ℕ × ℕ and put (m, n) with (m + 1, n + 1), and the classes are the integers. A function out of ℤ is a function on pairs that gives (m, n) and (m + 1, n + 1) one value. That is how every function of 5d’s was checked, and the check was the property.

47e3Gluing Is a Colimit

The shape · ← · → ·: a thing C with arrows j : C → A and k : C → B. Its colimit is A and B glued along C: a thing G with arrows i₁ : A → G, i₂ : B → G and i₁ ∘ j = i₂ ∘ k, through which every such pair runs in exactly one way. In Set, G is A ⊔ B with each j(c) in the first copy put with k(c) in the second: 47d3’s sum, then 47e2’s classes. A colimit of any shape in Set is built the same way, a sum and then classes.

Glued-is-colimit technique: the space glued from A and B along C is the colimit of the shape A ← C → B in Top: a continuous map out of it is a pair of continuous maps that agree on C.

G = (A \sqcup B)/{\sim},\ \ j(c) \sim k(c) @@ as a set: 47d3’s sum, then 47e2’s classes
\text{its open sets: those whose parts in } A \text{ and } B \text{ are open} @@ 34b5’s gluing of spaces
s : A \to X,\ t : B \to X,\ \ s \circ j = t \circ k @@ a rival pair, agreeing on C
\text{one function } u : G \to X \text{ with } u \circ i_1 = s,\ u \circ i_2 = t @@ the classes-force and cases-map techniques, in Set
u \text{ is continuous} @@ the glued-map technique (34b5): continuous on each piece
\htmlClass{new}{G \text{ is the colimit in } \mathbf{Top}} \quad \blacksquare @@ uniqueness came already in Set

The circle is [0, 1] glued to a point along the two ends. The figure eight is two circles glued along a point. 34i3’s gluing rule, held in trust there, says the loop-group map of categories carries this colimit of spaces to the colimit of groups: the free group on two letters is the sum of ℤ and ℤ in Grp (47d3). Maps of categories that carry colimits to colimits are the subject of 47f.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A shape is an index category and a picture a diagram; a cone over it with the universal property is a limit, and the dual notion a colimit. A meeting place is an equalizer and a forcing place a coequalizer. The limit of A → C ← B is a pullback, or fibre product; the colimit of A ← C → B is a pushout. Products and equalizers together give every finite limit; in Set every small limit and colimit exists, and Set is complete and cocomplete. The gluing rule of 34i3 is the Seifert–van Kampen theorem, whose modern statement is that π₁ takes such pushouts of spaces to pushouts of groups.

Who said it first

Limits of chains of groups were in use from the 1930s (inverse and direct limits); Daniel Kan defined limits and colimits for any diagram with adjoint functors in 1958. Herbert Seifert proved the gluing rule in 1931 and Egbert van Kampen in 1933. The integers as classes of pairs are older than all of it.

A proof that is not allowed

“A colimit is a sum and then classes, in every category.” Not allowed: that is a build in Set. In Grp the sum of ℤ and ℤ is the free group on two letters, not two copies side by side, and in a category where sums do not exist the build cannot start, while a colimit may still exist or may not. The property is the definition; the build is a witness in one category.
