Can Holes Be Counted in Every Dimension?

Can holes be counted in every dimension? Rung 34 counted the holes of a surface twice: once by corners minus edges plus faces, and once by loops that cannot be pulled tight. The loops worked in the plane and on surfaces, and the count gave one number. Neither says what a hollow ball has that a solid one lacks, or what a shape of four dimensions holds. This page builds the tool that does. Cut a shape into triangles and their higher cousins, give every piece a direction, and write the edge of each piece as a sum of the pieces one size down, with signs. The edge of an edge is always nothing, and that one fact runs the whole page. The pieces whose edge is nothing, but which are not themselves the edge of anything, are the holes, and in each dimension they make a group. Rung 44 sorts such a group into endless parts and clocks: the doughnut has two endless loops, and the one-sided bottle has one endless loop and one that is undone by going round twice. The old count of corners, edges and faces turns out to be the holes counted with signs. The same groups show that a map of a solid ball of any dimension into itself keeps a point, and the proof is a walk from door to door that finds the point. Since about 2000 the tool has left topology: a cloud of data points is thickened by a growing radius, and the holes that last longest are read as the shape of the data. A small move of the points moves that reading only a little, which was proved in 2005. Whether the long-lasting holes of real data mean anything is argued now.

Pieces with directions, and the edge map ∂ taking each to its faces with alternating signs; ∂∂ = 0, so every edge is a cycle, and the holes in dimension k are the cycles over the edges, Hₖ = Zₖ/Bₖ, sorted by 44i into ℤʳ and clocks. The sphere of dimension n has one hole, in dimension n. The torus has ℤ, ℤ², ℤ; the bottle ℤ, ℤ × ℤ/2, 0. Corners minus edges plus faces is the alternating sum of the free counts. Labels on a cut simplex give an odd count of full cells, so every map of a ball keeps a point. Holes born and dying as a cloud thickens are a barcode, and it moves no more than the points do.

inf41.1Chains and Their Edges

A k-cell is a list of k + 1 corners, [v₀ v₁ … vₖ], standing for the solid triangle of dimension k they span: a corner, an edge, a triangle, a solid triangular pyramid. Swapping two corners reverses its direction, so [v₁ v₀] = −[v₀ v₁]. A shape is cut into cells as 34f2 cut a surface, with triangles of every dimension in place of faces. A k-chain is a sum of k-cells with whole-number weights: 3[v₀ v₁] − [v₁ v₂] is a 1-chain. The k-chains form a group under adding, Cₖ.

The edge map ∂ takes a k-cell to its faces, each with a sign: leave out one corner at a time, and give the face without vᵢ the sign (−1)ⁱ. Extend it to chains by adding. So ∂[v₀ v₁] = [v₁] − [v₀], the end minus the start; ∂[v₀ v₁ v₂] = [v₁ v₂] − [v₀ v₂] + [v₀ v₁], the three edges walked round in one direction.

Edge-of-edge technique: for every chain c, ∂(∂c) = 0. It is 29·cb28.3’s twice-nothing technique with the slopes taken out: there, d(dω) = 0 because two slopes taken in either order agree; here, because each face of a face is reached by two roads with opposite signs.

\partial\partial [v_0 \ldots v_k] = \sum_i \sum_{j \ne i} \pm [\ldots \widehat{v_j} \ldots \widehat{v_i} \ldots] @@ every face with two corners left out
\text{drop } v_i \text{ first, then } v_j \ (j \lt i)\text{: sign } (-1)^i (-1)^j @@ v_j comes before v_i, so its place is still j
\text{drop } v_j \text{ first, then } v_i\text{: sign } (-1)^j (-1)^{i-1} @@ with v_j gone, v_i has moved to place i − 1
\text{the two signs are opposite, and every face is reached exactly these two ways} @@ the terms cancel in pairs

Read the technique as a picture: the rim of a triangle is a loop, and a loop has no ends. The rim of a solid pyramid is a hollow surface, and a hollow surface has no rim. ∂ forgets nothing about how pieces meet, and it needs no ruler.

inf41.2Holes Make a Group

A k-chain z with ∂z = 0 is a cycle: it has no rim. A k-chain of the form ∂c, with c a (k + 1)-chain, is an edge. By the edge-of-edge technique every edge is a cycle. The cycles form a group Zₖ, the edges a part of it Bₖ, and a cycle that is not an edge is a hole: something with no rim that fills nothing. Two cycles are counted as one hole when they differ by an edge.

Hole-group technique: the holes of dimension k are the classes Hₖ = Zₖ/Bₖ, a group whose combining turns about; when the shape has finitely many cells, Hₖ is a copy of ℤʳ × ℤ/d₁ × ⋯ × ℤ/dₘ, with r and the list d₁ ∣ ⋯ ∣ dₘ fixed by the shape’s cutting. The classes multiply honestly as 44b’s did for an ideal. The rest is 44i3’s structure technique over ℤ, since Zₖ, a part of a group with finitely many free members, is finitely made. The free count r is the k-th hole count, bₖ; the clocks ℤ/d are holes that are filled once gone round d times.

\partial [01] = [1]-[0],\ \partial[12] = [2]-[1],\ \partial[02] = [2]-[0] @@ no 2-cells, so B₁ = 0
z = x[01] + y[12] + w[02]\text{: } \partial z = -(x + w)[0] + (x - y)[1] + (y + w)[2] @@ collect the corners
\partial z = 0 \iff x = y = -w @@ three equations, one free choice
Z_1 = \{\, x([01] + [12] - [02]) \,\} \cong \mathbb{Z} @@ the loop once round
B_0 = \{\, \text{chains of corners with weights adding to } 0 \,\} @@ the images of the edges
\htmlClass{new}{H_0 = \mathbb{Z},\ H_1 = \mathbb{Z}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one piece; one loop

H₀ counts pieces: two corners are joined by a path of edges exactly when their difference is an edge, so b₀ is the number of pieces. H₁ agrees with 34g4: the circle’s loops are the whole numbers. The loop group of 34g need not turn about, and H₁ always does. Held in trust: for a shape in one piece, H₁ is the loop group with every two letters allowed to change places, which is how 34i4 sorted the surfaces.

inf41.3Spheres in Every Dimension

The solid triangle of dimension n is Δⁿ, all the cells on its n + 1 corners. Its rim, the hollow one, is all the cells but the top one: for n = 2 a loop, for n = 3 the four faces of a pyramid, a hollow ball. Pushed out round, the rim of Δⁿ is the sphere of dimension n − 1, as 34f1 pushed a box round. This Δ (capital delta) is not 13a's mark for a change.

Cone-fill technique: in the solid Δⁿ, every k-cycle with k ≥ 1 is an edge, and H₀ = ℤ: a solid triangle of any dimension has no holes. The cone from corner 0 does it. For a cell σ = [v₀ … vₖ] not containing 0, let 0σ = [0 v₀ … vₖ], and let 0σ = 0 when σ contains 0. Then ∂(0σ) = σ − 0(∂σ) for k ≥ 1.

\partial(0\sigma) = \sigma - 0(\partial\sigma) @@ the face without 0, then the rest with sign moved by one place
z \in Z_k,\ k \ge 1\text{: } \partial(0z) = z - 0(\partial z) = z @@ adding over the cells of z; ∂z = 0
\sigma \ni 0\text{: } 0\sigma = 0 \text{ and } \sigma - 0(\partial\sigma) = 0 @@ only the face without 0 survives the cone, and it gives σ back
\htmlClass{new}{z = \partial(0z) \in B_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ every cycle is the edge of its cone

Hollow-simplex technique: the rim of Δⁿ⁺¹, the sphere of dimension n, has H₀ = ℤ, Hₙ = ℤ, and Hₖ = 0 for every other k. Below the top, a k-cycle of the rim with k < n is the edge of its cone, which has dimension k + 1 ≤ n and so lies in the rim. At the top, the rim has no (n + 1)-cells, so Bₙ = 0; an n-cycle of the rim is a cycle of the solid Δⁿ⁺¹, so it is m · ∂[0 1 … n+1], the edge of the only top cell, and every m gives one. So Hₙ = ℤ, made by the whole rim.

4 \text{ corners},\ 6 \text{ edges},\ 4 \text{ triangles} @@ the rim of Δ³
z = [12] + [23] - [13] \text{ (a loop)} = \partial([123]) @@ here a face fills it; the cone 0z would do as well
\partial[0123] = [123] - [023] + [013] - [012] @@ a cycle with no 3-cell to fill it
\htmlClass{new}{H_0 = \mathbb{Z},\ H_1 = 0,\ H_2 = \mathbb{Z}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a hollow ball: one piece, no loop, one cavity

For n = 3 the rim of Δ⁴ has 5 corners, 10 edges, 10 triangles and 5 pyramids, and its only hole is in dimension 3: a hollow ball of four dimensions, which nobody can draw, counted by hand. That is the answer to the page’s question at its plainest.

inf41.4The Torus and the Bottle

Cutting a torus into triangles takes many cells; the grid below uses 54. Cells with more sides are allowed, as 34f2 allowed faces with more sides, if each k-cell is sewn on along a rim already built: one corner v, two edges x and y from v to v, and one square F sewn along the word of 34f3. Gluing a cell on is 47e3’s colimit; the edge of F is its word, each letter counted with its sign.

Same-groups claim, held in trust: every cutting of a shape into cells, triangles or sewn polygons, gives the same groups Hₖ; and a same-shape map carries them across. First proved in 1915, for shapes that look like flat space near every point. The usual proof straightens a continuous map, on a fine enough cutting, into one that sends cells to cells; then 34f2’s finer-cut move, done on chains, changes no group. Both steps are proved much higher up the ladder. The check below is the claim working once.

Square-word technique: for a surface sewn from one square with one corner class, H₀ = ℤ, H₁ = ℤ²/(the word as a sum), and H₂ = ℤ when the word sums to 0 and 0 otherwise. ∂x = v − v = 0 and ∂y = 0, so Z₁ = ℤ², B₀ = 0; ∂F is the word with signs; then 44i.

\text{torus: } \partial F = x + y - x - y = 0 @@ every letter twice, opposite ways
H_0 = \mathbb{Z},\ H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^2,\ H_2 = \mathbb{Z} @@ F is a cycle and B₁ = 0
\text{bottle: } \partial F = x + y + x - y = 2x @@ x sewn the same way round
H_1 = (\mathbb{Z}x + \mathbb{Z}y)/(2x) \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2 @@ the structure technique: y free, x a clock of 2
\htmlClass{new}{H_2(\text{bottle}) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ mF has edge 2mx, which is 0 only for m = 0

The check: a torus cut as a 3 by 3 grid of squares, each split by a diagonal, has 9 corners, 27 edges and 18 triangles, and a count by the diagonal-grid technique of 44i2 gives ℤ, ℤ², ℤ again. The bottle cut the same way, 4 by 4, gives ℤ, ℤ × ℤ/2, 0. The cross-cap, word x x, has ∂F = 2x: H₁ = ℤ/2, a loop filled once gone round twice, and H₂ = 0. These match 34i4’s turn-about counts, now with a dimension above them: two sides give a top hole, one side gives none.

inf41.5The Alternating Sum Is the Count

Corners minus edges plus faces is χ, the characteristic of 34f2. In any dimension let cₖ be the number of k-cells, and form c₀ − c₁ + c₂ − ⋯. It counts cells. The hole counts bₖ count something else. They agree.

Signed-count technique: for a shape cut into finitely many cells, c₀ − c₁ + c₂ − ⋯ = b₀ − b₁ + b₂ − ⋯. So the characteristic depends only on the holes, which gives a second proof of 34f2’s claim that it does not see the cutting, once the same-groups claim is granted. The proof is 27’s dimension count, done with fractions allowed as weights, which keeps the free counts and kills the clocks.

\text{weights in } \mathbb{Q}\text{: } C_k \text{ has dimension } c_k @@ a clock ℤ/d becomes 0: dx = 0 forces x = 0
c_k = \dim Z_k + \dim B_{k-1} @@ 27c’s lost-plus-reached technique, for ∂ from Cₖ
b_k = \dim Z_k - \dim B_k @@ the free count of Hₖ, by 44i3
\sum (-1)^k c_k = \sum (-1)^k \dim Z_k + \sum (-1)^k \dim B_{k-1} @@ add with signs
\sum (-1)^k \dim B_{k-1} = -\sum (-1)^k \dim B_k @@ shift the count by one place
\htmlClass{new}{\sum (-1)^k c_k = \sum (-1)^k b_k} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two sums combine into the bₖ

The checks: the hollow pyramid, 4 − 6 + 4 = 2 = 1 − 0 + 1. The grid torus, 9 − 27 + 18 = 0 = 1 − 2 + 1. The bottle, 1 − 2 + 1 = 0 = 1 − 1 + 0, the clock not counted. The rim of Δ⁴, 5 − 10 + 10 − 5 = 0 = 1 − 0 + 0 − 1. A sphere of odd dimension has count 0; of even dimension, 2.

inf41.6A Kept Point in Every Dimension

34h2 proved that a map of the disc keeps a point, by a loop that cannot be pulled tight. In dimension n the same proof needs Hₙ₋₁ of the sphere, ℤ, against Hₙ₋₁ of the solid ball, 0, from inf41.3, and the same-groups claim held in trust to carry them along a continuous map. This footing uses a proof that holds nothing in trust: the edge-of-edge idea, counted with weights 0 and 1, on a solid triangle cut fine. A solid triangle of dimension n is the same shape as the solid ball of dimension n, by pushing out from its middle along rays (34e), so a kept point for one is a kept point for the other.

Write a point of Δⁿ as x = (x₀, …, xₙ), with every xᵢ ≥ 0 and the sum 1. Cut Δⁿ into small cells. A labelling gives each corner of the cutting a label from 0 to n, and is fair when every corner labelled i has xᵢ > 0: a corner on the face where xᵢ = 0 never carries label i. A small n-cell is full when its corners carry all n + 1 labels.

Door-count technique: under a fair labelling, the number of full cells is odd. Call a face of a small cell a door when its n corners carry exactly the labels 0, …, n − 1.

\text{a full cell has exactly one door; any other cell has } 0 \text{ or } 2 @@ n labels from 0 to n − 1 on n + 1 corners: one is repeated, and either of its two corners can be dropped
\sum_{\text{cells}} \#\text{doors} = 2 \cdot \#\text{inner doors} + \#\text{doors on the rim} @@ an inner face borders two cells, a rim face one

\htmlClass{new}{\#\text{full} \equiv \#\text{doors on the rim} \equiv 1 \pmod 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the left side, mod 2, is the number of full cells

Every-dimension kept-point technique: every continuous map f of the solid ball of dimension n into itself has a point x with f(x) = x. On Δⁿ: label each corner x by some i with f(x)ᵢ < xᵢ. Such an i exists unless f(x) = x, since both lists add to 1, and the labelling is fair because f(x)ᵢ ≥ 0. Cut finer and finer, the cells shrinking to points; each cutting has a full cell. By 34d’s covered-thread technique a thread of these cells settles at a point p. Each label i sits on a corner of every full cell, those corners settle at p, and at each of them f(·)ᵢ < (·)ᵢ; by continuity f(p)ᵢ ≤ pᵢ for every i. Both lists add to 1, so f(p) = p.

\text{the rim } x_2 = 0\text{: labels } 0, 0, 1, 1, 1 \text{ from } (1,0,0) \text{ to } (0,1,0) @@ one door, between (¾, ¼, 0) and (½, ½, 0)

The proof is also a way to find the cell. Enter by the door on the rim and walk through doors, never back: each cell has at most two doors, so the walk cannot branch, and it can end only in a full cell. Finding it may take a walk past nearly every cell. How long such walks must be is the subject of ∞74.

inf41.7Holes That Last

31o read a cloud of points by its directions of spread. A cloud on a ring has no direction of spread that shows the ring. Thicken the cloud instead: put a ball of radius r round each point and watch the holes of the union as r grows. A hole is born at some radius and filled at a larger one. Its bar is the stretch [born, filled). The list of bars is the barcode. Long bars are read as the shape; short ones as noise.

To compute, the union of balls is replaced by cells: a k-cell for every k + 1 points whose balls share a point. The union and this cell shape have the same groups; that is held in trust. As r grows cells are only added, so each group Hₖ at radius r maps into the one at any larger radius, and a bar is a hole from its birth to the radius where it becomes an edge.

r \lt \tfrac12\text{: four separate discs} @@ b₀ = 4, b₁ = 0
r = \tfrac12\text{: neighbours touch; a ring round the middle} @@ three bars of H₀ end, one H₁ bar is born
\text{the middle is covered when } r = \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2} \approx 0.707 @@ half the diagonal
\htmlClass{new}{H_1 \text{ bar } [\,0.5,\ 0.707\,)} \quad \blacksquare @@ and one H₀ bar that never ends

Moved-cloud technique: if every point of a cloud is moved by at most ε, the distance from any place to the nearest point of the cloud changes by at most ε. If y is a point within distance t of a place p, its moved copy y′ is within t + ε; so the new nearest distance is at most the old plus ε, and the same the other way. The union of balls of radius r is the set of places at distance at most r, so the moved cloud’s union at r lies inside the old one’s at r + ε, and the old one’s at r inside the new at r + ε.

Stability, held in trust: when two functions differ by at most ε everywhere, the bars of the sets where each is at most r can be matched so that every end moves by at most ε, and bars shorter than 2ε may be matched to nothing. It was proved in 2005 and published in 2007. With the moved-cloud technique it says: moving the points by ε moves every bar end by at most ε. A bar longer than 2ε cannot be made by such a move. In the square, moving the corners by 0.05 leaves a bar born between 0.45 and 0.55 and filled between 0.657 and 0.757.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞41.oq.1Do the long bars of real data mean anything? Stability says a small move of the points makes only a small change. It says nothing about which bars a cloud drawn at random from a shape will show, how many points are needed, or how long a bar must be before it is more than chance. Tests for bars, in the sense of 30·now, are still argued about: what the cloud is compared with, and how many bars were looked at before one was reported.
∞41.oq.2Can barcodes be read for two growing radii at once? With one radius every collection of groups and maps splits into bars, by 44i’s structure technique over one variable. With two, for example a radius and a density, no list of whole numbers describes them all, and which partial descriptions are worth computing is open.
∞41.oq.3How fast can the bars be computed? The cells of a cloud of N points grow like a power of N with the dimension. The known methods reduce grids by row and column moves, as 44i2 did. Whether bars can be found much faster than that, for clouds in high dimension, is open.
∞41.oq.4Which groups can be the holes of a shape? For a closed surface the list of 34f and the groups of inf41.4 settle it. In dimension 4 and above, which lists of groups arise from closed shapes that look like flat space near every point, and how far the groups go toward telling such shapes apart, is a question with many partial answers and no end in sight.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Counting holes in every dimension began in 1895, with a long paper in Paris that counted, for each dimension, the cycles that bound nothing. It gave numbers, not groups. Supplements followed from 1899 to 1904, and one of them added the clocks, the kind of hole the one-sided bottle carries.

Whether the numbers depended on the cutting was the first doubt. A proof that they did not appeared in the United States in 1915, for shapes that look like flat space near every point. Until then the counts had been used on trust, as this page uses the same-groups claim.

In 1925, at Göttingen, the counts were seen as groups, in a report of fourteen lines. A young visitor there for the year saw how useful the view was, and it spread quickly. Within a few years cycles and edges were written as a pure algebra of chains, free of any picture, and the algebra spread far beyond topology.

The kept point in every dimension was first proved around 1910, by arguments that gave no way to find the point. The door count is from 1928, and soon after it gave a short proof that counts. Much later the door walk became a way to compute kept points, and how long the walk must be became a question about computing (∞74).

Persistence as computed now begins with an algorithm with bars, presented in 2000 and published in 2002. In 2005 the bars were shown to be what 44i’s structure technique gives over one variable, and the stability result, presented in 2005 and published in 2007, said that the bars do not jump.

Since then the bars have been applied to images, where in 2008 small patches of photographs were found to lie near a one-sided bottle, and to materials, disease and markets. Supporters point to stability and to features no average sees. Critics answer that the claim that long bars are signal and short bars noise has no clear justification in general, and that it rests on choices, of radius, of cells, of what counts as long. Statisticians have built confidence sets for bars since about 2014; how often they are used, and whether they answer the question, is argued now.

The accepted names

The chains, edge map and groups Hₖ are simplicial homology; Zₖ and Bₖ are cycles and boundaries, bₖ the Betti numbers, the clocks the torsion coefficients. ∂∂ = 0 makes the chains a chain complex. The cone-fill technique is the contractibility of a simplex by the cone construction; the hollow-simplex technique is the homology of spheres. Cells sewn on along their rims make a CW complex, and their groups are cellular homology; the same-groups claim is the topological invariance of homology, proved by simplicial approximation. The signed-count technique is the Euler–Poincaré formula. The door-count technique is Sperner’s lemma, and the every-dimension kept-point technique is Brouwer’s fixed point theorem; the door walk is the path-following argument behind the class PPAD. The bars are persistent homology and its barcode; the cells of a cloud are the Čech complex, the union of balls having its homotopy type by the nerve theorem; the moved-cloud technique says the distance function is 1-Lipschitz in the points, and stability is the bottleneck stability theorem. The field is topological data analysis.

Who said it first

Henri Poincaré, Analysis Situs (1895), and its complements (1899 to 1904). James Waddell Alexander proved the invariance of the Betti numbers and torsion coefficients in 1915. Emmy Noether proposed homology groups in 1925, with Heinz Hopf among the first to use them; Leopold Vietoris and Walther Mayer developed the algebra of chains in the late 1920s. Piers Bohl proved the kept point in three dimensions in 1904; L. E. J. Brouwer and, independently, Jacques Hadamard proved it in every dimension around 1910. Emanuel Sperner’s lemma is from 1928, and Bronisław Knaster, Kazimierz Kuratowski and Stefan Mazurkiewicz used it for the fixed point theorem (their lemma, 1929). Herbert Edelsbrunner, David Letscher and Afra Zomorodian gave the algorithm (2000, 2002); Afra Zomorodian and Gunnar Carlsson the algebra (2005); David Cohen-Steiner, Herbert Edelsbrunner and John Harer stability (2005, 2007). Gunnar Carlsson, Tigran Ishkhanov, Vin de Silva and Afra Zomorodian found the Klein bottle in image patches (2008). Confidence sets for persistence diagrams appeared in the Annals of Statistics in 2014.

A proof that is not allowed

“A map of the ball with no kept point pushes the ball onto its rim, and the rim has a hole the ball lacks, so no such map exists.” Right as a plan, and the plan of 34h1. Not allowed here as it stands: the holes are counted from cells, and a continuous push is not a map of cells. Carrying the groups along it is the same-groups claim, held in trust. The door count needs no such step.
