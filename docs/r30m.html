30m · technique

Densities

A train pulls out just as you reach the platform, and the next is some minutes away. An atom of a hospital tracer sits in a vial, and nobody can say when it will break apart. A cannery in Prince Rupert fills tins of salmon, and no two tins hold quite the same weight. None of these answers is one of a few cases to be counted. Each can land anywhere along a stretch of the line, and its chances are spread over that stretch the way paint is spread along a fence. The statistics annex of rung fifteen drew such a spread as a curve, its density, and read chances as areas under it. With the integral the rung has built, that picture becomes exact, and the average and the spread of the answer become integrals too. This step proves the one rule that turns every average into an integral against the density, and then works three densities that come back again and again: the flat one for a wait with a timetable, the falling one for a wait with no memory at all, and the bell.

If an answer has a density, the average of any function of it is the integral of that function times the density. A flat density gives the middle of its stretch as the average. A falling exponential density gives a wait that forgets how long it has already lasted. The bell, shifted and stretched, gives its chances by a power series.

30m1Averages Against a Density

From here on, a chance variable X has density f when f is a sized function with f ≥ 0, ∫ f = 1 over the line, and P(X in A) = ∫ over A of f, for every sized set A (30c). 15·stat asked this only of intervals. For a continuous f on an interval, 30c’s total is 15’s area (the agrees-with-area technique, 30c4), so the two agree. The average and the spread of 30g were integrals over the chance space Ω. A density moves them onto the line.

Density-average technique: if X has density f and g is a sized function with g ≥ 0, then E g(X) = ∫ g(x) f(x) dx. The same holds for any sized g with ∫ |g(x)| f(x) dx finite.

g = 1_A:\ \ E\,1_A(X) = P(X \in A) = \textstyle\int_A f @@ the average of 1 on an event and 0 off it is its chance (30g1); f is a density
g = \textstyle\sum_i c_i 1_{A_i}:\ \ E\,g(X) = \sum_i c_i\int_{A_i} f = \int g f @@ a step function: the average-adds technique (30g1), and the step-total technique (30c1) for the integral
g_n \text{ step functions},\ \ 0 \le g_n \le g_{n+1},\ \ g_n \to g @@ the total-from-below technique (30c3) gives them, for g ≥ 0 sized
E\,g_n(X) = \textstyle\int g_n f \ \text{ for every } n @@ the line before
\htmlClass{new}{E\,g(X) = \textstyle\int g(x) f(x)\,dx} @@ the rising-limit technique (30d1), on both sides: g_n(X) rises to g(X), and g_n f to g f
g = g^+ - g^-,\ \ \textstyle\int |g| f \text{ finite} \ \Rightarrow\ E\,g(X) = \int g f \quad \blacksquare @@ the two parts, each with a finite total, subtracted

With g(x) = x and g(x) = (x − μ)² the technique gives E X = ∫ x f(x) dx, and Var X = ∫ (x − μ)² f(x) dx. The spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2) gives the form that is easiest by hand: Var X = E X² − (E X)².

f(x) = \tfrac{1}{6} \text{ on } [0, 6],\ \ 0 \text{ elsewhere} @@ trains every 6 minutes, reached at a moment unrelated to the timetable: the flat density
E X = \textstyle\int_0^6 \tfrac{x}{6}\,dx = 3 @@ the density-average technique; the area-under-a-power technique (15i5)
E X^2 = \textstyle\int_0^6 \tfrac{x^2}{6}\,dx = 12 @@ the same, with g(x) = x²
\text{Var}\,X = 12 - 3^2 = 3 @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{average wait } 3 \text{ minutes, spread width } \sqrt3 \approx 1.73 \text{ minutes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ on [u, v] in general: average (u + v)/2, spread (v − u)²/12

30m2The Wait That Forgets

Let λ > 0. The exponential density of rate λ is f(x) = λe^(−λx) for x ≥ 0, and 0 for x < 0. Its running total is F(x) = 1 − e^(−λx), so P(X > t) = e^(−λt) for t ≥ 0. It is a density: F starts at 0 and rises to 1 as x grows.

E X^k = \textstyle\int_0^\infty x^k\,\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx @@ the density-average technique (30m1)
= \tfrac{1}{\lambda^k}\textstyle\int_0^\infty y^k e^{-y}\,dy = \tfrac{k!}{\lambda^k} @@ y = λx (the straight-inside technique, 16d); Γ(k + 1) = k! (the factorial-step technique, 26l2)
E X = \tfrac{1}{\lambda},\ \ E X^2 = \tfrac{2}{\lambda^2} @@ k = 1 and k = 2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Var}\,X = \tfrac{2}{\lambda^2} - \tfrac{1}{\lambda^2} = \tfrac{1}{\lambda^2},\ \ \text{spread width } \tfrac{1}{\lambda}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2)

Forgets-the-wait technique: if P(X > t) = e^(−λt) for every t ≥ 0, then for all s, t ≥ 0, P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t). Having waited s, the rest of the wait has the same chances as the whole wait had at the start.

\{X > s + t\} \subseteq \{X > s\} @@ a wait longer than s + t is longer than s
P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = \tfrac{P(X > s + t)}{P(X > s)} @@ the chance of A given B (30f2): here A and B is the smaller event
= \tfrac{e^{-\lambda(s + t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} @@ the running total
\htmlClass{new}{= e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the adding-becomes-multiplying technique (17e)
  
\text{half-life } 8.02 \text{ days}:\ \ e^{-8.02\lambda} = \tfrac12,\ \ \lambda = \tfrac{\ln 2}{8.02} \approx 0.0864 \text{ per day} @@ iodine-131: half of the atoms are left after 8.02 days; the half-life is the m with F(m) = ½
E X = \tfrac{1}{\lambda} \approx 11.57 \text{ days} @@ the average life of one atom: longer than the half-life
P(X > 30) = e^{-30\lambda} \approx 0.0748 @@ the running total
\htmlClass{new}{P(X > 38.02 \mid X > 30) = P(X > 8.02) = \tfrac12} \quad \blacksquare @@ the forgets-the-wait technique: an atom that has lasted 30 days is as new

30m3The Bell, Shifted and Stretched

30j1 named the bell: the chance variable Z with density φ(z) = e^(−z²/2) / √(2π), whose total is 1 by the bell-total technique. 30j1 found its average 0 and its spread 1. From here on, Z is called the standard bell, and the other bells come from it by a shift and a stretch.

Stretched-density technique: if X has density f, and Y = μ + σX with σ > 0, then Y has density (1/σ) f((y − μ)/σ), average μ + σ E X and spread σ² Var X.

P(u \le Y \le v) = P\big(\tfrac{u - \mu}{\sigma} \le X \le \tfrac{v - \mu}{\sigma}\big) @@ σ > 0 keeps order
= \textstyle\int_{(u - \mu)/\sigma}^{(v - \mu)/\sigma} f(x)\,dx = \int_u^v \tfrac{1}{\sigma} f\big(\tfrac{y - \mu}{\sigma}\big)\,dy @@ x = (y − μ)/σ (the straight-inside technique, 16d)
E Y = \mu + \sigma E X @@ the average-adds technique (30g1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Var}\,Y = \sigma^2\,\text{Var}\,X} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g2): Var(aX + b) = a² Var X

So μ + σZ has the bell density of average μ and spread width σ: (1/(σ√(2π))) e^(−(y − μ)²/(2σ²)). Every chance for it comes from the standard bell, at z = (y − μ)/σ. And those come by hand from a power series.

Bell-series technique: for every x ≥ 0, P(0 ≤ Z ≤ x) = (1/√(2π)) Σ (−1)ᵏ x^(2k+1) / (2ᵏ k! (2k + 1)), the sum over k = 0, 1, 2, …

e^{-z^2/2} = \textstyle\sum_k \tfrac{(-z^2/2)^k}{k!} = \sum_k \tfrac{(-1)^k z^{2k}}{2^k\,k!} @@ the exp-as-a-sum technique (22j3), at −z²/2; its reach is every real
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^x e^{-z^2/2}\,dz = \sum_k \tfrac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2^k\,k!\,(2k + 1)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the term-by-term technique (23e3), inside the reach; then divide by √(2π)
  
P(0 \le Z \le 1) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\big(1 - \tfrac16 + \tfrac1{40} - \tfrac1{336} + \tfrac1{3456} - \cdots\big) \approx 0.3413 @@ the bell-series technique; the terms alternate and shrink, so the error is less than the first left out (the bracket technique, 22g1)
P(|Z| \le 1) \approx 0.683,\ \ P(|Z| \le 2) \approx 0.954,\ \ P(|Z| \le 3) \approx 0.997 @@ φ is even; the same series at 2 and at 3
Y = 215 + 1.5\,Z \text{ grams} @@ tins labelled 213 g, filled on average to 215 g, spread width 1.5 g: a bell is the guess, checked on a histogram of weighed tins
P(Y < 213) = P\big(Z < -\tfrac{4}{3}\big) = \tfrac12 - P\big(0 \le Z \le \tfrac43\big) @@ the stretched-density technique; φ is even
\htmlClass{new}{P(Y < 213) \approx 0.5 - 0.4088 = 0.091,\ \ \text{about 1 tin in 11 under its label}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell-series technique at 4/3

The accepted names

A density is a probability density function; a chance variable with one is (absolutely) continuous. The density-average technique is often called the law of the unconscious statistician, because people use it without noticing that it needs a proof. The flat density is the uniform distribution, the falling one the exponential distribution, and λ its rate; the forgets-the-wait technique is the memoryless property, and the exponential is the only continuous distribution that has it (30m.5.3). The half-life is the median of the exponential, and 1/λ the mean lifetime. The bell is the normal (or Gaussian) distribution, Z the standard normal, μ + σZ the normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ, and (y − μ)/σ a z-score. The running total of Z is written Φ, and is tabulated; ∫₀ˣ e^(−t²) dt, scaled, is the error function. The density of 30m.5.2 is the Cauchy distribution.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre found the bell in 1733 as the shape of the counts of heads in many tosses. Carl Friedrich Gauss derived it in 1809 as the law of errors that makes the mean the best guess, and Pierre-Simon Laplace gave it its central place in 1810 and 1812. Siméon Denis Poisson noticed in 1824 that the density of 30m.5.2 has no average, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy met it in 1853. Ernest Rutherford measured the falling activity of radioactive substances from 1900 and spoke of a half-life by 1907; Egon von Schweidler argued in 1905 that each atom breaks apart by chance, which gives the exponential. The half-life of iodine-131, about 8.02 days, and of carbon-14, about 5730 years, are measured values; Willard Libby built radiocarbon dating in the late 1940s.
