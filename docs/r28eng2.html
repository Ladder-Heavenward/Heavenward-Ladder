Why Do Two Grounds Make a Hum?

Why do two grounds make a hum? Randy plugs a guitar amplifier into one wall socket and a mixing desk into another, and joins them with a cable. A low steady hum comes out of the speakers with nothing playing. He unplugs the cable and it stops. Each box is properly grounded. The fault is in there being two grounds.

A ground wire is a real wire, with resistance and with area between it and its neighbours. Current in it makes its two ends differ, and a closed loop of it catches the changing field of the mains. Good grounding gives every signal one road to zero and no loops.

28eng21A Wire Is Not Quite Zero

7eng21 called one point zero. A drawing shows every ground symbol as that same point. On the bench they are joined by copper, and copper has resistance.

A motor and a microphone amplifier share one return wire to the supply.

\text{0.05 ohm} @@ return wire
\text{10 amperes} @@ motor current in it
\htmlClass{new}{10 \times 0.05 = 0.5\text{ volt}} @@ push between its ends

The amplifier’s “zero” is half a volt away from the supply’s zero, and moves whenever the motor’s current does. The microphone’s signal is a thousandth of a volt.

The cure is in the wiring and costs nothing: give each its own return wire, meeting at one point only. No current of the motor then flows in any wire the amplifier measures from.

One-point technique: bring every return wire separately to a single point. Two circuits that share a length of ground wire each see the other’s current as a change in their zero.

28eng22Two Roads Make a Ring

Randy’s amplifier is tied to ground through the third pin of its plug (7·eng2). So is the desk, at another socket. The cable between them has a metal sheath joined to the case at both ends.

Follow the metal: amplifier case, sheath, desk case, desk’s earth wire, the wiring in the wall, amplifier’s earth wire, amplifier case. It closes. There is a ring of conductor several metres across, and no one drew it on any diagram.

28eng23A Ring Catches a Changing Field

28·phys2: a current makes a magnetic field round its wire. The other half of that fact: a field that changes, passing through a ring of conductor, drives a push round the ring equal to the rate of change of field times area.

Every wall of the room holds mains wiring, and its current runs as 26·eng’s sine at 50 turns a second.

B₀ = 1 millionth of a tesla, A = 1 square metre, f = 50.

2\pi f = 314 @@ f = 50
\htmlClass{new}{1 \times 0.000001 \times 314 = 0.0003\text{ volt}} @@ greatest push

Three tenths of a thousandth of a volt, at 50 a second, added round the ring. Part of the ring is the sheath that the signal uses as its zero, and the signal is a thousandth of a volt. That is the hum.

The circuit rules of 7·phys assumed that no changing field passes through the loops of the circuit. The loop rule, pushes round a loop summing to zero, is true only then. A ground loop is where that assumption fails.

Ring technique: a closed ring of conductor has a push driven round it equal to its area times the rate of change of the field through it. Double the area or the frequency and the push doubles.

28eng24Opening the Ring and Keeping the Earth

The quick cure is to snap the third pin off one plug. The ring opens and the hum stops. 7eng23 says what has been given up.

The proper cures open the ring somewhere else.

Join the sheath at one end only. It still screens the wire inside and no longer closes a ring.

One point. Plug both boxes into the same socket strip. The ring is still there with almost no area.

Twist the pair. Two signal wires twisted together make a chain of small loops, each facing the opposite way to the last. Their pushes are +, −, +, − and sum to nearly zero.

Take a difference. Send the signal as the difference between two wires and subtract at the far end. Whatever is added to both alike is removed by the subtraction, as in 7eng21.

Pass the signal without metal. A pair of coils, or a lamp facing a light-sensitive switch, carries the signal across a gap. No conductor crosses, so no ring can close.

28eng25The Measuring Lead Is a Loop Too

Sarah clips an oscilloscope probe onto a working circuit and the circuit starts to misbehave. The probe’s ground lead is a hand’s length of wire ending in a clip, and the instrument’s case is tied to the third pin. The lead and the circuit make a loop. Evita’s bench notes say it bluntly: the ground lead can do a lot of nasty things to a circuit, and the shorter the better. She replaces it with a bent paper clip a centimetre long.

The same notes give two more habits. Lay a whole sheet of copper under a circuit as its ground, so that every return path is short and wide. And put a small capacitor from each supply wire to ground at the point where the wire arrives, so that sudden gulps of current are met from the capacitor and do not travel along the shared wires.

Accepted names

The push in a shared wire is common-impedance coupling. The ring is a ground loop. Push driven by a changing field is induction, by Faraday’s law; field times area is flux. One-point wiring is star grounding or single-point ground. The sheath is a shield. Two wires twisted are a twisted pair; sending a difference is differential or balanced signalling, and what is added to both alike is common-mode. Crossing without metal is isolation, by a transformer or an opto-isolator. The copper sheet is a ground plane; the small capacitor is a bypass or decoupling capacitor. The assumption of no changing field through the loops is part of the lumped-circuit assumption.
