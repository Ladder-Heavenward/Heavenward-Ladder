The Ancients and the Schools

Mathematics as a form begins here: with the first proofs, the first definitions laid down before any argument, and the first book that lets a claim stand only on what came before it. The thinkers on this page set the shape that every rung of the ladder keeps.

25 climbers, in order of birth. Each of them climbed the same ladder you have climbed, and built part of it on the way. Under each name are the techniques they advanced, credited to them, and the climbers they met: who argued with whom, over what, and how it was resolved.

624–547 BCThales

Merchant of Miletus and the first Greek we are told proved anything. The angle in a half circle is right by his technique, and the ladder earns it on the Geometry floor.

Advanced Thales’ angle-in-a-semicircle technique

570–490 BCPythagoras

The brotherhood that bore his name held that number is the stuff of the world. The right-triangle technique carries his name, though the clay tablet Plimpton 322 shows Babylon had the triples a thousand years before him.

Advanced Pythagoras’ twelve-fifths comma · Pythagoras’ right-triangle technique · Pythagoras’ square-sum technique

490–425 BCZeno

Of Elea. His race between Achilles and the tortoise set the puzzle of endless division that the ladder settles with limits and sums on the Derivation and Statistics floors.

Advanced Zeno’s time technique

427–347 BCPlato

The philosopher who gave geometry its place of honour. He is the one who answered the refuters of the geometers, and whose school is said to have scorned any tool beyond straightedge and compass.

Built into Circles · Constructions With Straightedge and Compass · Two Flips

408–355 BCEudoxus

Of Cnidus. His theory of ratios fills a book of Euclid, and his way of trapping a figure between figures inside and outside it, the method of exhaustion, is the first rigorous limit.

Built into Measuring What Is Under a Curve

384–322 BCAristotle

In Athens he was the first we know of to say that an argument is good because of its shape and not its subject. His four forms, every, no, some and some not, are the ladder's oldest lines, and his answer to Zeno still stands.

Built into Making Expressions · Not, and the Totally False · Is a Whale a Fish? · Membership and Same Members · Every and Some by Hand · Learning to Keep Going · Inductive Sets and the Axiom of Infinity · Proof Shapes · Between Any Two, Another; Yet Holes · The Either-Way Principle · Choosing Forces Either-Way · The Payoff: Double Negation and the Withheld Licences · Can These Meetings Fit in Three Rooms? · Where Will a Rolling Ball Be, and When? · Can a Runner Ever Catch a Tortoise? · Zero Slope Means Flat · Improper Integrals · How Much Has Piled up, If You Only Know the Rate? · Circles · A Sum Is Where Its Partial Sums Settle · Sometimes Time Has Holes, and Apparently That's Ok (I) · What If a Machine Writes the Proof? · Things and Arrows

325–265 BCEuclid

Of Alexandria. The Elements set the form the whole ladder follows: definitions, postulates, then proofs that cite only what came before. His first common notion is the chain technique; his endless primes and his fifth postulate run through floors one to five.

Advanced Euclid’s chain technique · Euclid’s greatest-common-divisor technique · Euclid’s prime-divides-a-factor technique · Euclid’s factor-that-shares-nothing technique · Euclid’s ratio · Euclid’s triangle technique · Euclid’s line · Euclid’s circle · Euclid’s two-sides-and-the-angle technique · Euclid’s same-product technique · Euclid’s equilateral technique · Euclid’s rim-half-centre technique · Euclid’s outside-angle technique

Met Nikolai Lobachevsky, Carl Friedrich Gauss and Eugenio Beltrami over Euclid's fifth postulate. Lobachevsky built a geometry in which it fails; the St Petersburg Academy turned down his outline of 1829. Resolved Gauss thought highly of his work. Beltrami gave the geometry a concrete surface in 1868, a key step in its acceptance.

287–212 BCArchimedes

Of Syracuse. He weighed figures against each other to find their areas and then proved what the weighing showed, counted the grains of sand that would fill the world, and gave the postulate that sorts the amounts that can be compared from those that cannot.

Advanced Archimedes’ work · Archimedes’ buoyancy technique · Archimedes’ ball technique

276–194 BCEratosthenes

Librarian at Alexandria, who measured the earth by a shadow and a well. Archimedes wrote to him the letter called The Method, lost until 1906.

Built into Every Under-Estimate Is Below Every Over-Estimate · Where Is the Balance Point of a Shape? · How Can a Small Map Be True to the Land?

262–190 BCApollonius

Of Perga. His Conics cut a cone with a plane and named the three curves ellipse, parabola and hyperbola by whether a square falls short of, equals or exceeds a rectangle; he found the touching lines of every one.

Advanced Apollonius’ hyperbola technique

190–120 BCHipparchus

Measured the sun's year to a few minutes, made the first chord tables, and used the stereographic projection on which the astrolabe is built.

Built into Why Does a Calendar Need Leap Years? · cos and sin as Coordinates · Spaces Flat up Close

10–75Heron

Of Alexandria. His Metrica finds a square root by averaging a guess with the number over the guess, the step the ladder calls the averaging step, and gives the area of a triangle from its three sides.

Built into Square Root by Hand · Homing in on a Root With Tangent Lines, and How Errors Spread · The Two Triangles · Sequences by Hand

85–165Ptolemy

Of Alexandria. The Almagest carried the circles riding on circles for fourteen centuries, with chord tables and the sum formulas for chords; his scale with the ratio five to four is the ladder's just third.

Built into Can a Scale Be Perfectly in Tune? · Circles and Conics · The Sum Formulas From Turns · Multiplying Geometries · The Fifth Postulate on Trial

160–210Sextus Empiricus

The sceptic whose reports preserve the Stoic logic of Chrysippus, the oldest telling of the if-then shapes the ladder's first rung builds.

Built into And, and the Ors · If, and Exactly When

200–284Diophantus

Of Alexandria. His Arithmetica asked for whole-number answers, and it was in the margin of his copy that Fermat wrote his famous claim.

Built into Can Two Cubes Ever Add to a Cube? · Which Whole Numbers Are the Area of a Right Triangle With Fraction Sides?

290–350Pappus

Of Alexandria. He named analysis, sorted problems by the curves they need, told the story of Archimedes moving the earth, and knew that a ratio of four points on a line survives projection.

Built into Playing the Challenge Game · How Does a Small Push Lift a Heavy Weight? · Curves by Turning and Reach · Perspective

411–485Proclus

The fifth-century commentator on Euclid, who reports the Epicureans' donkey that knows two sides of a triangle beat the third, and who tried to prove the fifth postulate.

Built into Absolute Value and Distance on the Line · The Triangle Inequality in the Plane

598–670Brahmagupta

In 628 he set out the techniques for fortunes, debts and zero itself, and solved x² − 92y² = 1 by composing answers.

Built into Zero, Negatives, and the Forbidden Division · Limits Add, Multiply and Divide · Continued Fractions

790–850al-Khwārizmī

In ninth-century Baghdad he wrote the book of al-jabr whose problems the ladder's eighth rung still works, and whose name gives us the word algorithm.

Built into What a Recipe for Answers Is Made Of · Roots, Powers and the Quadratic Formula · What Is the Quickest Way to Sort a List? · Triangles and Parallels by Hand

965–1039Ibn al-Haytham

About the year 1000 he studied burning spheres and the gathering of rays, knew the value of (p − 1)!, and tried to prove the fifth postulate.

Built into How Does a Lens Bend Light? · Undoing on a Clock

1048–1131Omar Khayyam

Poet and geometer. He worked on the calendar of 1079 with eight leap years in every thirty-three, and studied the four-sided figure that hides the fifth postulate.

Built into Why Does a Calendar Need Leap Years? · The Fifth Postulate on Trial

1170–1250Fibonacci

Leonardo of Pisa. His book of reckoning of 1202 opens with the nine Indian figures and the sign 0, gives a method for unit fractions that always succeeds, and poses the rabbits whose counts carry his name.

Built into Writing Numbers · Fractions by Hand, and Decimals · Recursion Revisited: Sequences · What Is a Payment Next Year Worth Today? · Why Do Sunflower Seeds Spiral? · Remembering Answers · Undecidable Everywhere · Clock Arithmetic · Which Whole Numbers Are the Area of a Right Triangle With Fraction Sides?

1288–1348Ockham

William of Ockham, the schoolman, who stated the not-through techniques in words five centuries before De Morgan and argued over first and last instants.

Built into Rewriting by the Rulebook · Can These Meetings Fit in Three Rooms?

1323–1382Nicole Oresme

In Paris around 1350 he proved by grouping that one plus a half plus a third and so on passes every bound, and drew the first graphs of changing quantities.

Advanced Oresme’s grouped-halves technique

1350–1425Madhava

Of Sangamagrama, in Kerala. Around 1400 he found the endless sums for sine, cosine and the arctangent, with correction terms that speed the sum for π, three centuries before Europe.

Built into Running Trig Backwards · Rebuilding From Point One · Polynomials That Match Every Slope · Term by Term · Wave Recipes by Hand
