How Many Cheap Seats Should an Airline Sell?

How many cheap seats should an airline sell? Holiday travellers book months ahead and want low fares; business travellers book late and pay several times as much. Sell every seat cheap early and the late payers find the plane full. Hold too many back and the plane flies with empty seats. 30·logi asked how many tickets to sell; this page asks at what price. It weighs one more held-back seat as 13·econ weighed one more of anything, finds a rule that needs only one chance, and uses the bell curve of rung 30 when the late demand is uncertain.

Hold back y seats for the high fare. The y-th held seat earns the high fare only if at least y late travellers come; sold cheap now, it earns the low fare for sure. So hold it back while the high fare times P(late demand ≥ y) is at least the low fare. Read y off the chance function of late demand: a count for an even spread, the bell curve for a bell.

30logi22One More Held Seat

Compare holding the y-th seat with selling it early, as 13·econ’s one-more test does.

\text{sold early: } 100 \text{ for sure} @@ early demand fills it
\text{held: } 300 \text{ if } D \ge y,\ 0 \text{ if not} @@ it is the y-th late seat
\text{held, on average: } 300 \cdot P(D \ge y) @@ averages as integrals (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hold it while } P(D \ge y) \ge \tfrac{100}{300}} @@ the one-more test

Held-seat technique: hold back one more seat while the chance that late demand reaches it is at least the low fare over the high fare. P(D ≥ y) falls as y grows, so the rule picks out one y: the last seat that passes.

30logi23An Even Spread of Late Travellers

Say the number of late travellers is equally likely to be any of 0, 1, …, 39.

P(D \ge y) = \dfrac{40 - y}{40} @@ the counts from y to 39
\dfrac{40 - y}{40} \ge \dfrac13 \iff y \le 26.7 @@ take from both sides (5g)
y = 26:\ \tfrac{14}{40} = 0.35 \ge 0.33;\ \ y = 27:\ 0.325 < 0.33 @@ the last seat that passes
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hold } 26,\ \text{sell } 74 \text{ early}} @@ not the 19.5 late travellers expected on average

The average late demand is 19.5, yet the rule holds 26. Because a late seat is worth three early ones, it pays to hold seats that will probably go empty. The best number held depends on the fares’ ratio and the whole spread of late demand, not on its average alone.

30logi24A Bell of Late Travellers

Late demand is more often bell-shaped: say its average is 20 and its spread 8. Find y with P(D ≥ y) = ⅓.

P(Z \ge z) = \tfrac13 \text{ for the standard bell} @@ the bell curve (30j)
z \approx 0.43 @@ from its table
y = 20 + 0.43 \cdot 8 \approx 23.4 @@ stretch and slide the standard bell
\htmlClass{new}{\text{hold } 23} @@ above the average, by 0.43 spreads

If the fares were 100 and 200, the ratio ½ would put y at the average itself, z = 0; for 100 and 150, below it. Held seats sit above the average late demand when the high fare is more than twice the low, and below when less.

30logi25Fences, and Many Fares

The rule assumed each traveller is early or late by nature. Business travellers would happily pay 100 if they could, so airlines put up fences: the cheap fare needs booking weeks ahead, or a Saturday night away, or allows no refunds.

\text{late travellers who could buy down} @@ fences keep them out
\text{more than two fares} @@ one held number per fare, nested
\text{travellers who do not show} @@ 30·logi’s overbooking, on top
\htmlClass{new}{\text{prices changed as bookings come in}} @@ the held numbers redone every night

With many fares, each fare keeps back seats for all the fares above it, by the same comparison of a chance with a ratio of fares. Airlines recompute these numbers every night as bookings arrive, and the same reckoning prices hotel rooms, rental cars and concert seats.

Accepted names

Setting how many seats to sell at each fare is revenue management or yield management. The held seats are a protection level, the early limit a booking limit, and the rule of 30logi2 is Littlewood’s rule. Fences make price discrimination work; nested limits for many fares come from the EMSR methods, and changing prices as bookings come in is dynamic pricing.

Who said it first

Ken Littlewood, at the British airline BOAC, published the two-fare rule in 1972. American Airlines built revenue management into its booking system in the 1980s, after deregulation in 1978 brought fare wars.
