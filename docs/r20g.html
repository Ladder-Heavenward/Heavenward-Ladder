20g · proof

Handedness

A flag drawn on paper can be slid and turned anywhere on the page, and it still reads the same way round. Flip it, and no slide or turn will bring it back. This step puts a number on “which way round”. It proves that slides and turns keep the number and flips change its sign. Then it counts: a string of flips that is even can never equal one that is odd.

A cross product of the vectors from the first corner of a triangle to the other two is positive when the corners go round anticlockwise. Slides and turns keep it; flips turn its sign. Flips change its sign. So an even number of flips is never an odd number.

20g1Which Way Round

Take three points A, B, C. Walk from A to B to C and back to A. Either you turn left at each corner, going round anticlockwise, or you turn right, going round clockwise.

From here on, D(A, B, C) = (B − A)₁(C − A)₂ − (B − A)₂(C − A)₁, where (B − A)₁ is the first coordinate of B − A.

Which-way-round technique: D(A, B, C) is twice the triangle’s area, with a sign that tells which way round the corners go. It is the 18g triangle-area before the size was taken. Positive is anticlockwise, negative is clockwise, and zero means A, B, C lie on one line.

D((0,0), (1,0), (0,1)) = 1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1 @@ anticlockwise: the floor, then up
D((0,0), (0,1), (1,0)) = 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1 @@ the same corners, the other way round
\htmlClass{new}{D(A, C, B) = -D(A, B, C)} @@ the two products swap places

From here on, which way round a figure’s corners go is called its orientation.

20g2Slides and Turns Keep It

Turns-keep-orientation technique: slides and turns keep D. A slide keeps every displacement B − A and C − A, so D is unchanged. A turn about the origin sends u = B − A and v = C − A to R(u) and R(v), and the cross terms cancel.

R(u)_1R(v)_2 - R(u)_2R(v)_1 = (au_1 - bu_2)(bv_1 + av_2) - (bu_1 + au_2)(av_1 - bv_2) @@ R(x, y) = (ax − by, bx + ay)
= (a^2 + b^2)(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1) @@ share out; the ab terms cancel
\htmlClass{new}{D(RA, RB, RC) = D(A, B, C)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a² + b² = 1; turns about other points are slide, turn, slide back (20c4)

20g3Flips Change the Sign

Flips-reverse technique: a flip changes the sign of D, and keeps its size. A flip through the origin is F(x, y) = (px + qy, qx − py), with p² + q² = 1 (20e1).

F(u)_1F(v)_2 - F(u)_2F(v)_1 = (pu_1 + qu_2)(qv_1 - pv_2) - (qu_1 - pu_2)(pv_1 + qv_2) @@ 20e1
= -(p^2 + q^2)(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1) @@ share out; the pq terms cancel
\htmlClass{new}{D(FA, FB, FC) = -D(A, B, C)} \quad \blacksquare @@ p² + q² = 1; other lines: slide, flip, slide back

20g4Odd and Even Flips

Flip-parity technique: a string of flips keeps D when it has an even number of flips, and changes its sign when it has an odd number. Each flip changes the sign once. So no string of an even number of flips equals a string of an odd number.

F_k \circ \dots \circ F_1:\ D \mapsto (-1)^k D @@ 20g3, k times
\text{slides, turns: two flips (20e)} @@ keep orientation
\text{flips: one flip} @@ reverse it
(x, y) \mapsto (x + 1, -y):\ \text{three flips (20e.4.1)} @@ reverses it, and keeps no point
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every isometry keeps orientation or reverses it, never both}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 20f: each is 0, 1, 2 or 3 flips

From here on, a flip followed by a slide along the flip’s own line is called a glide. The move (x, y) ↦ (x + 1, −y) is the flip over the floor, then the slide by (1, 0). Footprints in sand go by glides.

This is 19e again. Each swap of cards changed odd to even. Here each flip changes anticlockwise to clockwise.

20g.4.5seamA philosopher in 1768 pointed at a right hand and a left hand. They agree in every length and every angle, yet the glove of one will never fit the other. The accepted names

D(A, B, C) is the signed area (doubled), or the determinant of B − A and C − A. Moves that keep orientation are called orientation-preserving, direct or proper; those that reverse it are orientation-reversing, opposite or improper. A glide is often called a glide reflection.

Who said it first

In 20g.4.5, the philosopher is Immanuel Kant, “Concerning the ultimate ground of the differentiation of directions in space” (1768), on incongruent counterparts. By the Prolegomena (1783) he used the same hands to argue the opposite: that space is a form of our seeing, not a thing in itself. The letter-writers of 20c.4.5 are Leibniz and Clarke.
