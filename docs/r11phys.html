Why Do Mountains Have Roots?

Why do mountains have roots? The earth’s outer rock, the crust, is lighter than the hot rock beneath it, the mantle, which flows very slowly over millions of years. So the crust floats on the mantle, the way ice floats on water. A mountain range is a thick piece of crust, and like a tall iceberg it rides higher above and sinks deeper below. This page balances columns of rock by weight with 5j’s units, finds the depth of a mountain’s root as its height times a ratio of densities, sees land rise again after an ice sheet melts, and checks with 11a that the root follows the height without jumps.

Deep enough down, every column of rock of the same width weighs the same, or the mantle would flow from under the heavier one. A mountain of height h adds crust above, so it must push a root of depth r down into the denser mantle, with h·ρ_c = r·(ρ_m − ρ_c).

11phys1Columns That Weigh the Same at Depth

Picture two columns of rock, each one square metre across, reaching from the surface down to a level deep in the mantle. Over millions of years the mantle flows like very thick syrup, so if one column pressed down harder than the other, mantle would creep out from under it until they matched.

From here on, on this page, the level of balance is a depth below which the mantle has evened things out: every column reaching down to it has the same mass above each square metre. Mass is density times volume (5j), and for a column one square metre across, volume is just height.

\text{mass} = \rho \cdot \text{volume} = \rho \cdot \text{height} \cdot 1 \text{ m}^2 @@ density times volume (5j)
\text{layers add: } \rho_1 h_1 + \rho_2 h_2 + \cdots @@ stack the layers
\htmlClass{new}{\text{equal at the level of balance, for every column}} @@ or the mantle flows

11phys2Light Rock Floats on Heavy

The crust’s rock has density about 2.8 tonnes per cubic metre and the mantle’s about 3.3. Lighter on heavier: the crust floats.

Ice floats on water with about nine tenths of it below the surface, because ice’s density is about nine tenths of water’s. Crust floats on mantle with 2.8/3.3, about 85%, of its thickness below the mantle’s level. The same balance of columns gives both.

11phys3The Root Under a Mountain

Compare a column through lowland crust of thickness T with a column through a range standing h above the lowland. The range’s column has the same crust T, plus h on top, plus a root r reaching down where the lowland column has mantle.

\text{lowland: } \rho_c T + \rho_m r @@ crust, then mantle down to the level
\text{mountain: } \rho_c h + \rho_c T + \rho_c r @@ all crust
\rho_c h + \rho_c r = \rho_m r @@ equal; take away ρ_c T from both
\htmlClass{new}{r = h \cdot \dfrac{\rho_c}{\rho_m - \rho_c} = h \cdot \dfrac{2.8}{0.5} = 5.6\,h} @@ the root, from the height

Equal-columns technique: where the crust floats in balance, a range h above the lowland has a root about 5.6h deep, because the root’s missing weight must make up for the range’s added weight. A plateau 5 km high has a root about 28 km deep: its crust is far thicker than the lowland’s. Geologists timing earthquake waves through high plateaus find crust much thicker than under lowlands, as the technique says.

11phys4Land Rises Back Once Its Ice Melts

Twenty thousand years ago, ice sheets about 3 km thick lay over much of northern North America and northern Europe. Ice, at density 0.9, pressed the land down into the mantle.

\rho_{\text{ice}}\, H = \rho_m\, d @@ the ice’s weight against the mantle pushed aside
d = H \cdot \dfrac{\rho_{\text{ice}}}{\rho_m} = 3 \text{ km} \cdot \dfrac{0.9}{3.3} @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{d \approx 0.8 \text{ km}} @@ once balance was reached

When the ice melted, the land began to rise back, slowly, as the mantle flowed in underneath. It is still rising: land around Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia rises about a centimetre a year. A surveyor with a satellite receiver bolted to the bedrock there reads the rise directly, year after year.

11phys5The Root Changes With the Height and Never Jumps

The root depth is a function of the height: r(h) = 5.6h. A polynomial, so continuous (11d): a small change in height makes a small change in root.

r(h) = 5.6\,h @@ continuous (11d)
|r(h) - r(h_0)| = 5.6\,|h - h_0| @@ 
\text{for } |r(h) - r(h_0)| < \varepsilon:\ \ |h - h_0| < \varepsilon / 5.6 @@ continuity’s challenge met (11a)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{wear 100 m off a range: the root rises about 560 m}} @@ erosion lifts the mountain back

As a range wears down, its root rises with it, so the range keeps rising back as it erodes, until the root is gone. That is why old, worn ranges still stand. The rise is continuous only once the mantle has had time to flow: over a few thousand years it lags, which is the rebound of footing 4.

Accepted names

Floating balance of the crust is isostasy, and the level of balance is the compensation depth. Roots made by thicker crust are Airy isostasy; the land rising after the ice is post-glacial rebound.

Who said it first

George Airy proposed mountain roots in 1855, to explain why plumb lines near the Himalaya were pulled toward the mountains less than expected; John Pratt offered an explanation by density differences in the same years.
