Why Is a Used Car Worth Less the Moment You Drive It Away?

Why is a used car worth less the moment you drive it away? Nothing about the car changed. What changed is who knows what: the seller knows whether the car is good, and the buyer can only guess. Markets where one side knows more can fall apart, with only the worst goods left for sale. This page averages over what the buyer cannot see, as rung 30 averages chances, watches a market unravel, and then finds two cures: a costly act that only the better side can afford, and a menu of offers that makes each side reveal itself by what it picks.

A buyer who cannot see quality pays at most the average worth of what is offered. If that is below what the best sellers will take, they leave, the average falls, and the next best leave. A signal works when it costs the better type less: then a level exists that only the better type will pay for. A menu works when each type prefers the offer meant for it.

31econ21Who Knows What

Terry is selling cars on a lot. Half are good and half are lemons, and only the sellers know which is which. Each car is worth more to a buyer than to its seller.

From here on, on this page, a buyer who cannot tell the cars apart pays at most the average worth of the cars offered, averaged over the chances as in 30g. Every trade would make both sides better off, if the buyer could see.

31econ22The Market Unravels

Offer every car at one price, since the buyer cannot tell them apart.

\text{both kinds offered: average worth } \tfrac12 \cdot 1200 + \tfrac12 \cdot 500 = 850 @@ averages (30g)
850 < 1000:\ \text{good sellers will not sell} @@ they leave
\text{only lemons offered: average worth } 500 @@ the buyer knows who stayed
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only lemons sell, at about } 500} @@ every good-car trade is lost

Unravelling technique: when buyers cannot see quality, the price falls to the average, the best sellers leave, and the average falls again, until only the worst goods trade. No one cheated; the missing information alone destroyed the good trades.

31econ23Information Bought and Promised

If the trades are worth having, someone will try to restore what the buyer cannot see. Each way costs something and changes the averages.

\text{an inspection: the buyer sees the car} @@ worth paying for, up to the trade’s gain
\text{a warranty: the seller pays if the car fails} @@ cheap for a good car, dear for a lemon
\text{a name to keep: a dealer sells many cars} @@ cheating once costs future sales (23·econ)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each makes quality pay for the seller who has it}} @@ the good cars return

Hidden quality can be repaired by buying the information, or by a promise that costs the seller of a bad car more than the seller of a good one. The second idea is general.

31econ24A Signal Only the Better Side Can Afford

An employer cannot see which job seekers are able. An able worker produces 2, a less able one 1, in a year’s wages. Study costs both, but costs the able less: a year of it costs the able 1 and the less able 2. Say employers pay 2 to anyone with at least y years, and 1 to others.

\text{less able: study } y \text{ only if } 2 - 2y \ge 1 \iff y \le \tfrac12 @@ otherwise take 1 and skip it
\text{able: study } y \text{ if } 2 - y \ge 1 \iff y \le 1 @@ the same test, at cost 1
\tfrac12 < y \le 1:\ \text{only the able study} @@ the signal sorts them
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the employer’s guess is right, and stays right}} @@ a stuck pair, as in 7·econ

Signal technique: an act that costs the better type less can sort the types, if its level is too dear for the worse type and still worth it for the better. Here study sorts even if it teaches nothing: its cost does the sorting. Whether real schooling sorts, teaches, or both, is argued over still.

31econ25A Menu That Makes Each Side Choose

Turn it round: now the insurer cannot see who drives carefully. It offers a menu, and lets each driver pick.

\text{A: full cover, high price} @@ every claim paid
\text{B: you pay the first } 500 \text{ of each claim, low price} @@ a deductible
\text{careful drivers rarely claim: B costs them little} @@ they pick B
\htmlClass{new}{\text{reckless drivers often claim: they pick A}} @@ the choice reveals the type

Menu technique: when one side cannot see the other’s type, offer a menu built so that each type prefers the offer meant for it; the choice then reveals the type. The price is that the careful drivers get less cover than they would if the insurer could see.

31econ26Moves to Watch Out For

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

\text{the buyer pays } 850 \text{ and both kinds sell}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-s">out of sequence</span> at 850 good sellers leave; then the average is 500 (31econ22)
\text{the lemons drove the good cars out by cheating}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> no one cheated; hidden information did it (31econ22)
\text{study sorts only if it teaches}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> its cost sorts, if it costs the able less (31econ24)
\text{any level of study sorts}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> only ½ < y ≤ 1 here (31econ24)
\text{one policy for everyone is fairest}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> it draws the reckless and drives the careful away, as the lemons did (31econ25)

Accepted names

One side knowing more is asymmetric information; the unravelling is adverse selection, and the used-car case the market for lemons. A costly act that sorts is a signal, and the sorting a separating equilibrium. A menu that makes each type reveal itself is screening, and the condition that each type picks its own offer is incentive compatibility.

Who said it first

George Akerlof published “The Market for Lemons” in 1970, Michael Spence the job-market signal in 1973, and Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz insurance screening in 1976. Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz shared the Nobel memorial prize in economics in 2001.
