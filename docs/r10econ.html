Can Interest Added More Often Grow Without End?

Can interest added more often grow without end? A bank that pays six percent a year can add it once, at the end of the year. Or it can add half of it at midyear, and the other half at the end, on the larger sum. Or a twelfth each month, or a share each day. Each time interest is added, it starts to earn interest of its own. So adding it more often always pays a little more. Then does adding it a million times a year, or every second, pay without limit? People asked because banks competed on how often they added interest, and because a lender who adds it every day can quote a rate smaller than what he really charges. What makes it hard is that two things pull against each other. The pieces get smaller as their number grows, and the number of times they pile up grows. This page proves that the year's growth rises every time the interest is cut finer. It proves that the growth never passes a ceiling, and finds the least ceiling as a limit, in rung 10's sense. And it shows why adding interest daily is barely better than adding it monthly.

Cut a year's interest into more and more equal parts, each added on what is there already. The year's growth rises with every finer cut. It also stays below a ceiling, found by running the same cut backwards, as a loss taken in the same parts. A run of numbers that rises and stays below a ceiling heads to the least number above all of it, so the growth has a limit. The shortfall from that limit shrinks in proportion to one over the number of parts. By the time interest is added monthly, nearly all of the gain is in.

10econ1A Year's Interest, in N Parts

From here on, on this page, r is a yearly rate written as a real number (6% is 0.06), and n is a whole number at least 1, the number of equal parts the year's interest is cut into. Each part adds r/n of what is there. So a year multiplies a sum by u_n = (1 + r/n)ⁿ, by 9h's steps-that-multiply with n steps. For a fixed r, u is a sequence (9·cb4).

10econ2One Plus a Power

One-plus-a-power technique: a power of one plus some amount is at least one plus that many of the amount. Let x be a real with x ≥ −1, and let n be a whole number at least 1. Then (1 + x)ⁿ ≥ 1 + nx. When x ≠ 0 and n ≥ 2, the ≥ is >.

(1 + x)^1 = 1 + 1 \cdot x @@ the base, n = 1
(1 + x)^{n+1} = (1 + x)^n (1 + x) @@ the step: suppose the line for n (4c)
\ge (1 + nx)(1 + x) @@ multiply the hypothesis by 1 + x ≥ 0: the order is kept (8g)
= 1 + (n + 1)x + \htmlClass{new}{n x^2} @@ spread (8d)
\ge \htmlClass{keep}{1 + (n + 1)x} \quad \blacksquare @@ nx² ≥ 0 (square-never-negative); > when x ≠ 0, which gives the strict form from n + 1 = 2 on

Read at x = r/n, it says u_n ≥ 1 + r. Interest added in parts pays at least what it pays once. It is the first of three uses on this page.

10econ3Finer Cuts Pay More

Finer-pays technique: cutting the year's interest one part finer always pays a little more. Let s be a real, s ≠ 0. Let n be a whole number at least 1, with n + s > 0. Then (1 + s/n)ⁿ < (1 + s/(n + 1))ⁿ⁺¹. The plan: write the new factor as the old one times a ratio, and bound the ratio's power below by one-plus-a-power.

q = \tfrac{1 + s/(n+1)}{1 + s/n} = \tfrac{n\,(n + 1 + s)}{(n + 1)(n + s)} @@ one bottom (5h); 1 + s/n = (n + s)/n is positive
q = 1 - \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{s}{(n + 1)(n + s)}} @@ (n + 1)(n + s) − n(n + 1 + s) = s: multiply out and collect
q^{n+1} \gt 1 - \tfrac{(n + 1)\,s}{(n + 1)(n + s)} = \tfrac{n}{n + s} @@ one-plus-a-power, strict: the amount is not 0, the power is at least 2, and the amount is at least −1, since s < (n + 1)(n + s) when s > 0
\big(1 + \tfrac{s}{n+1}\big)^{n+1} = \big(1 + \tfrac{s}{n}\big)^{n+1} q^{n+1} @@ 1 + s/(n + 1) = (1 + s/n) · q; power-of-a-product
\gt \big(1 + \tfrac{s}{n}\big)^{n} \cdot \tfrac{n + s}{n} \cdot \tfrac{n}{n + s} = \htmlClass{keep}{\big(1 + \tfrac{s}{n}\big)^{n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply the line above by the positive (1 + s/n)ⁿ⁺¹ (8g); cancel

With s = r, a positive rate, the year's growth u_n rises with n. With s = −r, a loss taken in parts, (1 − r/n)ⁿ rises too, once n > r: a loss cut finer loses less. Both readings are used next.

10econ4But Never Past a Ceiling

Run the cut backwards. Interest of r/n multiplies by 1 + r/n. A loss of r/n multiplies by 1 − r/n, and dividing by that multiplies by a little more than 1 + r/n. That gives a ceiling.

Ceiling technique: however finely the year is cut, the growth stays below one over the matching loss at any fixed cut. Let r > 0. Let m be a whole number with m > r. Then u_n < (1 − r/m)^(−m) for every whole n ≥ 1.

n \ge m:\ \ \big(1 + \tfrac{r}{n}\big)\big(1 - \tfrac{r}{n}\big) = 1 - \tfrac{r^2}{n^2} \lt 1 @@ difference of squares; r ≠ 0
1 + \tfrac{r}{n} \lt \tfrac{1}{1 - r/n} @@ divide by 1 − r/n, positive since n ≥ m > r (8g)
u_n \lt \big(1 - \tfrac{r}{n}\big)^{-n} @@ powers-keep-order (8f)
\big(1 - \tfrac{r}{m}\big)^m \lt \big(1 - \tfrac{r}{n}\big)^n \text{ for } n \gt m @@ finer-pays with s = −r, n − m times (induction, 4c)
u_n \lt \htmlClass{new}{\big(1 - \tfrac{r}{m}\big)^{-m}} @@ positive-reciprocal (8g); for n = m, the line before last directly
n \lt m:\ \ u_n \lt u_m \lt \big(1 - \tfrac{r}{m}\big)^{-m} \quad \blacksquare @@ finer-pays, m − n times; then the case n = m

So at 100%, with m = 2, the growth never reaches (½)⁻² = 4, however often the interest is added. At 5%, with m = 1, it never reaches 1/0.95 ≈ 1.0526: interest of 5% a year, added every second, never pays 5.27% in a year. A larger m gives a lower ceiling: at 100%, m = 1000 gives about 2.7196.

10econ5The Ceiling Is a Limit

Now rung 10. The sequence u rises, and it has an upper bound. 8c says the set of its terms has a least upper bound. The next technique says the sequence heads there.

Rising-and-capped technique: a sequence that never falls and never passes some bound heads to the least of its upper bounds. Let u be a sequence of reals with u_n ≤ u_(n+1) for every n, and let the set of its terms have an upper bound. Let L be its least upper bound (8c). Then u_n → L.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0 @@ speak of everything (10f6's definition)
L - \varepsilon \text{ is not an upper bound} @@ it is less than L, the least one
u_N \gt L - \varepsilon \text{ for some } N @@ not every term is at most L − ε (7e)
n \ge N:\ \ L - \varepsilon \lt u_N \le u_n \le L @@ never falls, step by step from N (4c); L is an upper bound
|u_n - L| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ unpack-the-bars (8h)

From here on, on this page, C(r) names the limit of u_n = (1 + r/n)ⁿ as n → ∞: the ceiling at rate r. It exists by rising-and-capped, since finer-pays makes a rise and the ceiling technique caps it.

Two facts pin it down. Every u_n is below C(r): u_n < u_(n+1) ≤ C(r). And C(r) is at most every ceiling of the ceiling technique, because it is the least upper bound. So C(r) is trapped.

\big(1 + \tfrac{1}{10\,000}\big)^{10\,000} \lt C(1) \le \big(1 - \tfrac{1}{10\,000}\big)^{-10\,000} @@ n = m = 10 000
2.71814 \lt C(1) \lt 2.71842 @@ worked by machine, rounded outward
C(1) \approx \htmlClass{new}{2.718} @@ to three places: both ends round to it

So the answer to the title is no. Interest added more often always pays more, and never more than a fixed ceiling. At 100% a year, that ceiling is about 2.718 times the start. The ladder can trap it as tightly as you like. It has no shorter name for it yet.

10econ6Why Daily Is Barely Better Than Monthly

The trap also says how fast u_n closes in. The ceiling technique at m = n gives u_n < C(r) ≤ c_n, where c_n = (1 − r/n)^(−n). The gap between the two ends is small, and one-plus-a-power measures it.

Shortfall technique: the shortfall from the ceiling, as a share of it, is less than r² over n − r². Let r > 0, and let n be a whole number greater than r and than r². Then C(r) − u_n < C(r) · r²/(n − r²).

\tfrac{c_n}{u_n} = \big(1 - \tfrac{r^2}{n^2}\big)^{-n} @@ (1 + r/n)(1 − r/n) = 1 − r²/n², to the power n
\big(1 - \tfrac{r^2}{n^2}\big)^{n} \ge 1 - \tfrac{r^2}{n} \gt 0 @@ one-plus-a-power, with x = −r²/n² ≥ −1; n > r²
\tfrac{c_n}{u_n} \le \tfrac{n}{n - r^2} @@ positive-reciprocal (8g)
C(r) - u_n \le c_n - u_n \le u_n \cdot \tfrac{r^2}{n - r^2} @@ C(r) ≤ c_n; times u_n, positive
\lt \htmlClass{keep}{C(r) \cdot \tfrac{r^2}{n - r^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ u_n < C(r)

The bound shrinks in proportion to one over n. So does c_n − u_n: both ends of the trap close on C(r), by the squeeze (10d) with one-over-n and constant-factor (10f). Here is $10 000 for a year at 5%.

The ceiling itself is $10 512.71, to the cent, by the trap at n = 8760. Going from monthly to daily, 30 times as many additions, earns about a dollar. Going from daily to every second earns less than four cents. Most of what finer cuts can ever pay is in by monthly. The shortfall bound needs r² to be small against n. At 5%, r² is 0.0025, and even yearly adding is close.

For a borrower the same numbers run the other way. Most credit cards in the United States add interest daily. A card that quotes 24% a year, added daily, multiplies a balance carried a year by (1 + 0.24/365)³⁶⁵ ≈ 1.2711: 27.1%, not 24%. Added monthly it would be 26.8%. No adding, however often, passes about 27.12%.

10econ8Running It Backwards

Handed a yearly rate and a number of parts, find the year's growth: (1 + r/n)ⁿ. Handed a year's growth g and the number of parts, find the quoted rate: 1 + r/n is the positive nth root of g (8f), so r = n(ⁿ√g − 1). Handed a rate and a wish (to come within some amount of the ceiling), solve the shortfall technique's bound for n. Handed a ceiling you must stay under, choose m larger than r and compute (1 − r/m)^(−m).

Who said it first

Interest on interest was worked out in tables by Babylonian scribes, and argued over by moralists for two thousand years after. In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked what a year's interest of the whole sum becomes if it is added at every moment, and showed it lies between two and a half and three times the sum. One-plus-a-power is named after him too, from his work of 1689, though it appears earlier in Isaac Barrow's lectures. The rising-and-capped technique was stated as a property of the reals by Bernard Bolzano (1817) and made a foundation by Richard Dedekind (1872), whose cuts are 8a's.

The United States Truth in Lending Act of 1968 made lenders quote an annual percentage rate, the r of this page, without the interest on interest. The Truth in Savings Act of 1991 made banks quote savings as an annual percentage yield, the year's growth less 1, with it. The two acts are the two sides of 10·econ.9.4.

Accepted names

One-plus-a-power is Bernoulli's inequality. Rising-and-capped is the monotone convergence theorem for sequences; a sequence that never falls is increasing (or non-decreasing), and one with an upper bound is bounded above. The quoted r is the nominal rate or APR; the year's growth less 1 is the effective annual rate or APY. Adding interest is compounding, and adding it at every moment is continuous compounding, which rung 17 takes up.
