Why Does a Thin Post Snap Sideways?

Why does a thin post snap sideways? Press down on a short block of wood and it holds until it crushes. Press down on a long thin ruler and, well before anything crushes, it suddenly bows out sideways and gives way. Nothing about the material changed; the shape decided. This page writes the bow of a post as a rate statement of rung 24, finds that a straight post can bow only at one load, and sees how that load falls with length and rises with the right shape. Last it finds the length past which a steel rod gives way by bowing before it can crush.

A post bowed by y feels a bending pull P·y, and its stiffness EI answers with EI·y″: so y″ = −(P/EI)y, a steady swing in x. With both ends held on the line, a bow fits only if the swing’s half wave fits the length: P = π²EI/L². The load falls as 1/L², and I, the area’s spread from its middle line, decides which way it bows. Short posts crush, long posts bow.

24eng21A Post Pressed From Both Ends

Mike builds a frame with long steel posts, each pressed from above by a load P and free to turn at both ends. If a post bows sideways by y(x) at height x, the load, now off the post’s middle line by y, bends it there with a turning effect P·y.

From here on, on this page, the post’s stiffness is E·I: E says how stiff the steel is, and I how far the post’s cross section is spread from its middle line, with I = ∫ s² dA over the cross section, s the distance from the middle line. A bent post pushes back in proportion to its stiffness times its bend, y″, as 16·eng’s beams did.

EI\,y\prime\prime = -P\,y @@ the post’s push back balances the load’s bending
\htmlClass{new}{y\prime\prime = -\tfrac{P}{EI}\,y} @@ a steady swing, in height instead of time
y(0) = 0,\ \ y(L) = 0 @@ both ends held on the line

24eng22Only One Load Lets It Bow

Solve as in 24h, with n = √(P/EI), and ask the far end to sit on the line too.

y = A\cos(nx) + \tfrac{B}{n}\sin(nx) @@ the steady-swing technique (24h)
y(0) = 0 \Rightarrow A = 0 @@ cos 0 = 1
y(L) = 0:\ \tfrac{B}{n}\sin(nL) = 0 @@ a bow needs B ≠ 0
\sin(nL) = 0,\ \text{least: } nL = \pi @@ around the circle (21)
\htmlClass{new}{P = \dfrac{\pi^2 E I}{L^2}} @@ the bowing load

Bow technique: a post free to turn at both ends stays straight under any load below π²EI/L²; at that load it can bow, in half a sine wave, and any push sideways sends it out. Below the load every bow is pulled back straight. Above it, the slightest crookedness grows.

24eng23Length and Ends

The bowing load holds L squared below the line. Holding the ends firmly instead of letting them turn shortens the half wave.

L \to 2L:\ P \to P/4 @@ twice as long, a quarter the load
\text{both ends clamped: the half wave spans } L/2 @@ the bow turns back at each end
P = \pi^2 EI/(L/2)^2 = 4\pi^2 EI/L^2 @@ four times the pinned load
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one end clamped, one free: the wave spans } 2L,\ P/4} @@ a flagpole bows easiest

The bowing load falls as the square of the length that bows; clamping the ends shortens that length and multiplies the load. Braces halfway up a post do the same, halving the length that can bow.

24eng24Shape Decides the Way It Bows

A plank 10 cm wide and 2 cm thick bows the easy way, across its thin side. Find I both ways, with 16’s areas: for a rectangle b wide and h thick, about its middle line,

I = \textstyle\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} s^2\, b\, ds = \dfrac{b h^3}{12} @@ area is a change (16c)
\text{thin way: } \tfrac{10 \cdot 2^3}{12} \approx 6.7 \text{ cm}^4 @@ b = 10, h = 2
\text{wide way: } \tfrac{2 \cdot 10^3}{12} \approx 167 \text{ cm}^4 @@ b = 2, h = 10
\htmlClass{new}{25 \text{ times the load, the same wood}} @@ it always bows the thin way

Spread technique: a post bows about the line its cross section is least spread from, and spreading the same material further out, as a tube, raises I and the load without adding weight. That is why bicycle frames, scaffold poles and bones are hollow.

24eng25Crush or Bow?

A short post does not bow: it crushes when the pressure P/A reaches the steel’s limit σ. Set the bowing load equal to the crushing load to find where one gives way to the other. For a solid round rod of radius r, I/A = r²/4.

\dfrac{\pi^2 E I}{L^2} = \sigma A @@ bows and crushes at once
L = \pi \sqrt{\dfrac{E I}{\sigma A}} = \pi \cdot \tfrac{r}{2} \sqrt{\dfrac{E}{\sigma}} @@ I/A = r²/4
\text{steel: } E/\sigma \approx 200\,000/250 = 800,\ \sqrt{800} \approx 28.3 @@ in megapascals
\htmlClass{new}{L \approx 44\,r} @@ longer than about 22 diameters, it bows first

Below the crossover length a post fails by crushing, and the material decides; above it, it fails by bowing, and the shape and length decide. A stronger steel lifts the crushing limit and leaves the bowing load where it was, so for slender posts it buys nothing.

24eng27A Crack and the Stress That Runs It

A post may also break by a crack. A crack of half-length a in a plate pulled with stress σ frees stored stretch energy around it, and making the crack longer needs new surface, γ per area of each face. The crack runs when growing frees more than it costs.

\text{freed: } \dfrac{\pi\sigma^2 a^2}{E},\ \ \text{spent: } 4a\gamma @@ two faces, each of length 2a
\dfrac{d}{da}:\ \dfrac{2\pi\sigma^2 a}{E} = 4\gamma @@ the crack runs when the freed slope passes the spent slope
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma_{\text{break}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E\gamma}{\pi a}}} @@ four times the crack, half the strength

Crack-length technique: a crack runs when the stress reaches √(2Eγ/(πa)), so the breaking stress falls as one over the square root of the crack’s length, and a part is only as strong as its worst crack. Shown on glass: rods scratched with cracks of measured length break at stresses that fall as 1/√a, and fresh, unscratched fibres are many times stronger than bulk glass. That is why inspectors measure cracks in bridges and wings, not only loads.

Accepted names

Bowing sideways under a push is buckling, and π²EI/L² is Euler’s critical load. E is Young’s modulus, I the second moment of area, and EI the flexural rigidity. The length that bows, set by the ends, is the effective length, and L over the spread √(I/A) is the slenderness ratio. The crushing limit is the yield strength.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler found the bowing load in an appendix on elastic curves to his Methodus inveniendi, 1744.
