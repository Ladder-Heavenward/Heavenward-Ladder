Can a Lock Be Proved Safe?

Can a lock be proved safe? 9·crypt’s pad hides everything, and its key must be as long as the message. Every lock in daily use has a short key, so it cannot hide everything from an attacker with unlimited time. What can be asked is less: that no attacker who runs a limited number of steps can tell the locked message from chance by more than a sliver. Proofs of that kind exist, and each has one shape. It turns any attacker on the lock into an attacker on a simpler thing. So a proof of safety is an arrow, arrows in a row compose, and what they carry is counted: steps added, and a factor lost. What no arrow gives is a place to start. Not one map has been proved hard to tell from chance, and a proof for any would settle the open question of 41·now2.

Edge of a tester D between sources X and Y: |P(D says yes on X) − P(D says yes on Y)|. A detour never wins: edge(X, Z) ≤ edge(X, Y) + edge(Y, Z). A map g goes first: the edge of D on g(X), g(Y) is the edge of D∘g on X, Y. A stretcher run m times loses at most mε; the lock m ⊕ G(k) loses at most 2ε. An arrow with (t₁, c₁) after one with (t₂, c₂) has (t₁ + t₂, c₁c₂).

inf87.1A Game Says What Safe Means

9·crypt4’s one-key-each technique let the attacker run for ever and still learn nothing, and 9·crypt5’s short-key technique showed the price: a key as long as the message. Ask for less, and say it as a game. A source hands out lists of bits by chance. A tester is any method that reads one list and says yes or no; it may toss coins. Give a tester D a list from source X or from source Y, without saying which. Its edge between the two is the gap between its chances of saying yes:

edgeD(X, Y) = |P(D says yes on X) − P(D says yes on Y)|.

A tester that cannot tell the sources apart at all has edge 0. From here on, on this page, X and Y are (T, ε)-alike when every tester that runs at most T steps has edge at most ε between them. A source is even when every list of its length is equally likely.

Edge-detour technique: for one tester and three sources, edge(X, Z) ≤ edge(X, Y) + edge(Y, Z). Map-first technique: for a map g on lists, the edge of D between g(X) and g(Y) is the edge of “g, then D” between X and Y; so if X and Y are (T, ε)-alike and g takes t steps, then g(X) and g(Y) are (T − t, ε)-alike.

p_X = P(D \text{ says yes on } X),\quad \text{edge}_D(X, Y) = |p_X - p_Y| @@ the definition
|p_X - p_Z| = |(p_X - p_Y) + (p_Y - p_Z)| \le |p_X - p_Y| + |p_Y - p_Z| @@ the triangle technique (8h3): the detour
P(D \text{ says yes on } g(X)) = P(D \circ g \text{ says yes on } X) @@ g, then D, is one tester
\text{edge}_D(g(X), g(Y)) = \text{edge}_{D \circ g}(X, Y) @@ the same for Y
\htmlClass{new}{D \text{ runs } T - t \text{ steps} \Rightarrow D \circ g \text{ runs } T \text{ steps} \Rightarrow \text{edge}_D(g(X), g(Y)) \le \varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ X and Y are (T, ε)-alike

So for each tester the edge behaves as a distance between sources (34a1), except that two different sources may be at edge 0. And a map on lists never stretches it, which is what ∞Η asked of a map between spaces with a distance. Why the limit on steps? Take the map G(s₁s₂) = s₁ s₂ (s₁ ⊕ s₂), from two bits to three. The tester “is the third bit the ⊕ of the first two?” says yes on every G(s), and on an even list of three bits with chance ½. Its edge is ½. Against testers with no limit, nothing with a short seed is like chance.

inf87.2One More Bit, Then Many

From here on, a stretcher is a fast map G from lists of n bits to lists of n + 1 bits. It is (T, ε)-safe when G of an even seed and an even list of n + 1 bits are (T, ε)-alike. One more bit is little. Run it again and again: from the seed s₀, let G(s₀) be s₁ followed by one bit b₁, let G(s₁) be s₂ followed by b₂, and so on m times. Put out b₁ b₂ … b_m and then s_m: n + m bits from n. Call this map G_m.

Stretch-again technique: if G is (T, ε)-safe and takes t steps, then G_m and an even list of n + m bits are (T − mt, mε)-alike.

H_i:\ i \text{ even bits, then } b_{i+1} \dots b_m s_m \text{ run from an even seed } s_i,\quad i = 0, \dots, m @@ m + 1 sources; H₀ is G_m of an even seed, H_m is even
g_i(z):\ i - 1 \text{ even bits, then the last bit of } z\text{, then the run from the first } n \text{ bits of } z @@ a map on lists of n + 1 bits; at most mt steps
H_{i-1} = g_i(G(s)),\qquad H_i = g_i(U) @@ s an even seed, U an even list of n + 1 bits
\text{edge}_D(H_{i-1}, H_i) \le \varepsilon \text{ for } D \text{ of } T - mt \text{ steps} @@ map-first; G is (T, ε)-safe
\htmlClass{new}{\text{edge}_D(H_0, H_m) \le \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m} \text{edge}_D(H_{i-1}, H_i) \le m\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ edge-detour, m − 1 times

Each neighbour pair differs in one use of G, and that use is where the safety of G is spent. With ε = 2⁻⁸⁰ and m = 1000, the loss is at most 1000 · 2⁻⁸⁰, below 2⁻⁷⁰. The m + 1 sources in the middle are never built by anyone. They stand in the proof only, between the two that matter.

inf87.3A Lock With a Short Key

Take a key k of n bits and a message m of L bits, with L far above n. Stretch the key to L bits, G_L(k) for short, and use that as 9·crypt3’s pad: the locked message is c = m ⊕ G_L(k). Whoever holds k stretches it the same way and takes the pad off.

Stretched-pad technique: if G_L of an even key and an even list of L bits are (T, ε)-alike, then for any two messages m₀ and m₁ of L bits, the locked m₀ and the locked m₁ are (T − L, 2ε)-alike.

m_0 \oplus U \text{ is even, and so is } m_1 \oplus U:\ \text{edge } 0 @@ U an even pad: the one-key-each technique (9·crypt4)
\text{edge}_D(m_0 \oplus G_L(k),\ m_0 \oplus U) \le \varepsilon @@ map-first with g = “⊕ m₀”, which takes L steps
\text{edge}_D(m_1 \oplus U,\ m_1 \oplus G_L(k)) \le \varepsilon @@ the same with m₁
\htmlClass{new}{\text{edge}_D(m_0 \oplus G_L(k),\ m_1 \oplus G_L(k)) \le \varepsilon + 0 + \varepsilon = 2\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ edge-detour, twice

So a key of 128 bits locks a message of a million bits, and a tester of limited steps can hardly tell which of two messages was locked. With G itself (T, 2⁻⁸⁰)-safe, the stretch-again technique gives ε ≤ 10⁶ · 2⁻⁸⁰ for the pad, and the lock loses at most 2 · 10⁶ · 2⁻⁸⁰, below 2⁻⁵⁹. The short-key technique of 9·crypt5 is not broken. An attacker with 2¹²⁸ runs of G to spend tries every key, and the limit T is what keeps her out. And one key still locks one message (9·crypt6).

inf87.4Read It With Arrows

Both proofs did one thing. They took a tester against the larger thing and made from it a tester against the smaller. From here on, on this page, a (t, c)-arrow from a pair of sources (X, Y) to a pair (X′, Y′) is a way to turn any tester D into a tester D′ that runs at most t steps more, with edgeD′(X′, Y′) ≥ edgeD(X, Y)/c. The stretched-pad proof is an (L, 2)-arrow from the two locked messages to the pad and chance. The stretch-again proof is an (mt, m)-arrow from the pad and chance to G and chance.

Arrow-row technique: a (t₁, c₁)-arrow followed by a (t₂, c₂)-arrow is a (t₁ + t₂, c₁c₂)-arrow; and safety runs back along an arrow: if (X′, Y′) are (T, ε)-alike, then (X, Y) are (T − t, cε)-alike.

D \mapsto D' \mapsto D'':\ \text{steps } T,\ T + t_1,\ T + t_1 + t_2 @@ the two ways, one after the other
\text{edge}_{D''} \ge \text{edge}_{D'}/c_2 \ge \text{edge}_D/(c_1 c_2) @@ each arrow’s condition
D \text{ runs } T - t \text{ steps} \Rightarrow D' \text{ runs } T \text{ steps} \Rightarrow \text{edge}_{D'}(X', Y') \le \varepsilon @@ (X′, Y′) are (T, ε)-alike
\htmlClass{new}{\text{edge}_D(X, Y) \le c \cdot \text{edge}_{D'}(X', Y') \le c\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ the arrow’s condition, turned round

So the lock rests on G by one arrow, (L + mt, 2m), read off its two pieces: steps add and factors multiply, as ∞P’s losses added along a row. See what the arrows let hide. The tester is never opened: an arrow uses it whole, so what an attack is made of is out of sight, and the proof covers attacks nobody has thought of. The chain H₀, …, H_m is out of sight as well, with the order of its rows. What is left is the plan: the lock is safe if G is. 41k4’s map between questions was an arrow of this kind with no numbers on it. Here the numbers are the point, since a factor c of 2⁴⁰ turns a safety of 2⁻⁸⁰ into 2⁻⁴⁰.

inf87.5Where No Arrow Starts

Every arrow ends at something taken as safe. Is anything proved safe outright? Take any map G from n bits to n + 1 bits and one tester: given a list y, it asks whether some seed s has G(s) = y.

Seed-search technique: the tester “is there a seed s with G(s) = y?” has edge at least ½ between G of an even seed and an even list of n + 1 bits.

P(\text{yes on } G(s)) = 1 @@ s itself is such a seed
G \text{ reaches at most } 2^n \text{ of the } 2^{n+1} \text{ lists} @@ one list for each seed
P(\text{yes on an even list}) \le 2^n / 2^{n+1} = \tfrac12 @@ each list equally likely
\htmlClass{new}{\text{edge} \ge 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12} \quad \blacksquare @@ the definition

Trying every seed takes 2ⁿ runs of G, far over any limit T in use. But the tester’s question has a short certificate, the seed, checked by one run of G, so it is one of 41h2’s questions. If every such question could be answered fast, this tester would be fast and no stretcher would be safe. So a proof that some stretcher is safe would be a proof that P ≠ NP, which is open (41·now2). Every lock with a short key rests on a condition that is posited and has not been proved.

  • By 1984 safety had been defined by a game of telling apart, and a lock was proved to meet it if a problem about numbers on a clock is hard. A stretcher was built in the same years whose bits cannot be guessed if another clock problem is hard.
  • In 1999 it was proved that a stretcher can be built from any function that is fast to run and hard to undo, so that the one exists exactly when the other does.
  • In 2020 it was proved that such functions exist exactly when one question is hard for a fair share of lists: how short a program prints a given list within a time limit.
  • In 2000 a gap was found in the proof, given in 1994, for a padding in wide use, and the lock built on it was then proved safe by another road, with a factor that is not small. A paper posted in 2004 and printed in 2007 argued that proofs of this kind are often of limited use for the locks in practice, and can mislead.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞年.oq.1Is any stretcher safe? No map has been proved to be one, and a proof would settle whether P = NP. What is known is which other hard things would give one.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

9·crypt told how the pad was proved to hide everything, and that its key must be as long as the message. A lock with a short key cannot be proved safe in that way.

By 1984 safety had a definition, a game of telling apart, and proofs took the shape of this page: an attacker on the lock is turned into a solver of a problem believed hard. In 1999 the stretcher was tied to functions that are hard to undo, each giving the other.

Since 2000 the proofs themselves have been checked and disputed, their gaps and their factors counted, and in 2020 the existence of functions that are hard to undo was shown to be the same question as the hardness of finding short programs.

The accepted names

A tester is a distinguisher, and its edge is its advantage. Sources that are (T, ε)-alike are computationally indistinguishable; even is uniform. A stretcher is a pseudorandom generator, its input the seed. The chain H₀, …, H_m is a hybrid argument. The lock m ⊕ G(k) is a stream cipher, and its safety here is indistinguishability of encryptions, which matches semantic security. An arrow is a security reduction, a proof by arrows is provable security, and the factor c is the tightness of the reduction: a small c is a tight reduction. A function that is fast to run and hard to undo is a one-way function. The shortest program that prints a list within a time limit is its time-bounded Kolmogorov complexity. The padding of 1994 is optimal asymmetric encryption padding, OAEP, and its proofs are in the random oracle model, where one part of the lock is taken as an ideal chance function.
