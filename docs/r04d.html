4d · proof

Completing the proof

Since 0c you have argued in sentences. Each sentence came with a reason: it was given, or supposed, or it followed from sentences before it by a tech. Those arguments convinced, and they were right. But they used a thing the ladder had never built: the proof itself. Collections came on rung 2. Order came with pairs at 2e. Counting came at 4a, induction at 4b, and lists came with functions at 4c. Every part is now in hand. This step starts with questions, and each one picks up your last answer. Work them in order. By the end you will have built the thing the ladder has been holding in trust, and then the step gives it a syntax.

Argue something from scratch. Then look at what your argument is made of: sentences, what each stands under, and an order. Collect every line any argument could write, and see what kind of set that is. Then write it down the short way.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 0c (unpack, transform, pack), 1a (supposing, reporting, cases), 1d (carry an if through, prove an if), 2a (membership, parts), 2c (gather and merge), 2e (pairs in order), 3a (each number is the set of the numbers before it), 4a (the counting numbers), 4b (induction) and 4c (a function is its pairs; the smallest-closed-set technique). 4d8What you built

Stands on 4d1 to 4d7 (the chain).

The syntax · footings 4d8 to 4d17; read them once the chain is done

Look back at the chain. In 4d2 every sentence turned out to carry two things: the statement, and the collection of suppositions it stands under. That collection may be empty, and the last sentence's is.

A line of a proof is a pair: the suppositions it stands under, and the statement it says.

Example: in the fish argument, the sentence “the fish is a salmon” is the line ({the fish is a smolt}, the fish is a salmon). The last sentence is (∅, if the fish is a smolt, the fish swims).

Write the pair as (S, R), with S the collection of suppositions and R the statement. The pair is 2e's pair, so (S, R) and (R, S) are different things. Two lines are the same line exactly when both parts match.

In 4d3 the lines got numbers, and the argument became a set of pairs (k, line): a function. In 4d5 you wrote the rule for leaning. In 4d6 and 4d7 you collected every line at once. You found a set the rules cannot get out of, whose lines standing under nothing are the statements the givens force.

That set is what the ladder has been holding in trust since 0c. Footings 4d9 to 4d17 state it once and name it.

4d9A proof is a list

Stands on 4d8 (a line is a pair), 3a and 4c (a function is its pairs).

A list of n lines is a function from n to lines. 3a showed that the number n is the set of the numbers before it: 4 is {0, 1, 2, 3}. So a list of four lines is a function L from {0, 1, 2, 3}, and L(2) is line 2.

Which lines come before line k? Exactly the numbers that are members of k, because k is the set of the numbers before it. The order of the lines costs nothing new.

Now the rule. Fix a collection G of given statements. A list L of n lines is a proof from G when every line L(k) = (S, R) is one of these:

  • Given. R is a member of G, or an axiom, or a technique already earned. S may be anything.
  • Supposed. R is a member of S. You may suppose anything, as long as it is kept in S.
  • Moved. R comes by one move from given statements and from lines numbered before k, and every one of those lines stands under suppositions that are a part of S.
  • Dropped. R comes by a tech that ends a supposition. It leans on earlier lines that stand under S with one supposition added, and on lines under S itself if it needs them, and the added supposition leaves. As 1a said, each joining word brings its own way. Prove an if takes (S with P added, Q) to “if P then Q”: the report. Refute takes (S with P added, ⊥) to “not P”. Cases takes (S, P or Q), (S with P added, R) and (S with Q added, R) to R. Use a some takes (S, some x is P) and (S with “a is P” added, R) to R, for a name a used nowhere else.

The proof reaches R when its last line is (∅, R): the statement, under no supposition at all.

A proof is a list of lines, each one given, supposed, moved from lines above it, or dropped, and its foot stands under no supposition.

Example: with no givens at all, take L(0) = ({P}, P) and L(1) = (∅, if P then P). Line 0 is supposed: P is in its S. Line 1 is dropped: prove an if takes line 0 to it, and P leaves S. The foot stands under nothing, so this two-line list reaches “if P then P”.

4d10The old citing rule, as a part

Stands on 4d9 (the four kinds of line) and 2a (part of).

The fourth bullet of 4d9 is 1a's dropping, every way of it, now with nothing held in trust. The third bullet holds the old citing rule.

Citing rule: a line may lean on a line before it only if that line's suppositions are a part of its own.

A line inside a supposition may lean on lines outside it, because ∅ is a part of every collection. A line outside may never lean on a line inside, because the inner line carries a supposition the outer line does not have.

Nesting is no longer a habit we trust. A line two suppositions deep is a line whose S has two members, and nothing more. And 1a's empty row is a line whose S is ∅: nothing supposed, so what stands on it stands on nothing tried.

4d11The identity of a statement, earned

Stands on 4d9 (a proof from G) and 0c (the one-sentence argument).

0c called the one-sentence argument the identity of a statement, as the self-sameness axiom is the identity of a name. On 0c that could only be said. Now it can be earned.

Plan: write a list with one line, and check that the line is given.

Proof. Let P be any statement, and let G be {P}, the collection whose only member is P. Let L be the list with one line, L(0) = (∅, P). That one line is given, since P is a member of G. So L is a proof from G. Its last line is (∅, P), so L reaches P. ∎

Identity technique: every statement is reached by a proof from itself.

The proof reaches P exactly as P was given: no line was moved, so nothing was added and nothing was lost. The foot is as so, or as not so, as the given was.

Could the identity technique be made an axiom, now that it is earned? Some ways of writing proofs do lay it down as an axiom, the first one every other proof starts from. On the ladder it cannot be one any more, and it does not need to be. An axiom is laid down with nothing beneath it. The identity technique now has something beneath it: the definition of a proof, built from lists, which were built from the numbers, which were built from sets. What is earned is a technique, and it is cited by name like any other. The self-sameness axiom stays an axiom: nothing on the ladder lies beneath it.

4d12The column, at last

Stands on 4d9 (a proof is a list) and 4d10 (the citing rule).

Writing every line as a pair is slow. So the ladder writes a proof as a column: one line per row, the statement on the left, the reason on the right, and the suppositions drawn as an indent.

Each supposition still open pushes the row one step to the right. A row says “assume P” where P joins S, and the indent closes where P leaves S. The space an indent holds is called a box.

Example: here is the fish argument from 4d1 as a column.

\qquad \text{assume the fish is a smolt} @@ supposed: S is {the fish is a smolt}
\qquad \text{the fish is a salmon} @@ every smolt is a young salmon, a given; same S
\qquad \text{the fish is a fish} @@ every salmon is a fish, a given; same S
\qquad \text{the fish swims} @@ every fish swims, a given; same S
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{if the fish is a smolt, the fish swims}} \quad\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ reported: prove an if; S is empty again

Read it back to pairs and you get the list you wrote in 4d3. The column is a way of writing; the list of pairs is the object. Every column from here on is one of these lists.

4d13Everything the givens let in

Stands on 4d9, 4c (the smallest-closed-set technique), 4b (induction) and 2a (double inclusion).

Which statements can G reach? One answer: those that are the foot of some proof from G. There is a second answer, and it has the same shape as 4c.

Call a collection of lines closed when it holds every given line and every supposed line, and holds every line that is moved or dropped from lines it holds.

Example: with G = {P}, the collection holding only (∅, P) is not closed. It leaves out the supposed lines, such as ({Q}, Q).

Closed collections exist. The lines that appear in proofs from G form one: to move from lines of two different proofs, write the proofs one after the other and add the new line at the foot. Sift the lines that belong to every closed collection. As in 4c, the result is closed, and it is a part of every closed collection. It is the smallest one.

Two-pictures technique: the lines some proof reaches are exactly the lines in the smallest closed collection.

Plan: show each collection is a part of the other, then close with double inclusion.

First, every line of every proof is in every closed collection. Fix a closed collection C and a proof. Prove by induction, as 4b taught, that for every k all the lines numbered before k are in C. For 0 there are none. Suppose all the lines before k are in C. Line k is given, supposed, moved from givens and those lines, or dropped from some of them. Each way, C holds it, since C is closed. So all the lines before k + 1 are in C.

Second, the lines reached by proofs form a closed collection, as above, so the smallest one is a part of them. Each is a part of the other, so by 2a's double inclusion they are the same.

So there are two pictures of a proof. One is a list you can write down and check line by line. The other is the smallest collection the rules cannot get out of. They pick out the same statements. The first is how you work. The second is how you prove things about every proof at once, as the next part does.

4d14A supposition never hurts

Stands on 4d9 (the four kinds of line) and 4b (induction).

One fact the sentences used all along, without saying so: anything earned outside a supposition stays earned inside it. Here it is earned.

More-suppositions technique: if a proof from G holds the line (S, R), then for any statement P some proof from G holds the line (S with P added, R).

Example: the given line (∅, Q) becomes ({P}, Q), and it is still given, since a given line may stand under anything.

Plan: add P to every line of the proof, then check each line keeps its kind, by induction on line numbers.

Proof. Take the proof, and add P to the suppositions of every line. A given line is still given, since S may be anything. A supposed line is still supposed, since its statement is still in its S. A moved line still leans on lines before it whose suppositions are a part of its own, because P was added to both sides.

A dropped line still drops. The lines it came from gain P too, so (S with Q added, R) becomes (S with Q and P added, R), and the same Q is dropped. (If P happens to mention the fresh name of a use-a-some, rename that name first.) Every line keeps its reason, so the new list is a proof. ∎

That is why a box may cite the lines outside it. It is no longer a rule to follow. It is a technique, earned: the more-suppositions technique.

4d15The tables, again

Stands on 4d13 (the two pictures), 1f (the tables agree with the techs) and 3c (cannot be proved).

1f checked each tech one row at a time, and said plainly that this covered only the argument in front of you. Now a proof is an object, and the same check covers every proof at once.

Plan: fix a row, or a world, where the givens are true. Then prove by induction on line numbers that every line (S, R) of every proof from G has this property: wherever all of S is true, R is true.

A given line is true, by the choice of row. A supposed line has R in S. A moved line is true because each tech keeps what is true, which is 1f's table.

A dropped line is true too, one way of dropping at a time. Prove an if: wherever S is true, either P is false, and then “if P then Q” is true, or P is true, and then Q is true by the line it came from. Refute: wherever S is true, P cannot be true, since then ⊥ would be, so “not P” is true. Cases: wherever S is true, P or Q is true, and either way R is true. Use a some: wherever S is true, something is P; call it a, and R is true, since R does not mention a.

So the foot, which stands under nothing, is true wherever the givens are true. ∎

Soundness technique: whatever a proof reaches from true statements is true.

This is what 4d7 found by hand. Turned round, it is 3c's method: if some world has every given true and R false, then no proof from G reaches R. The basement writes 3c's argument out as a proof.

4d16The shape, seen again

Stands on 4d12 (the column) and 1i (the shape of an argument).

1i read the shape of an argument off its goal: an if is reached by supposing its first half, an every by letting a name be anything, an or by cases. In a column, the shape is the indent.

Each supposition the goal asks for pushes the rows one step right, and each report brings them back. The goal's outermost mark decides the last row. The next mark in decides the indent before it, and so on.

Example: here is the goal “if P, then if Q, then P and Q”, read off its marks.

\qquad \text{assume } P @@ supposed: the outer if asks for it
\qquad\qquad \text{assume } Q @@ supposed: the inner if asks for it
\qquad\qquad P \land Q @@ join, on the two suppositions
\qquad Q \Rightarrow (P \land Q) @@ reported: the inner supposition is dropped
\htmlClass{new}{P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow (P \land Q))} \quad \blacksquare @@ reported: the outer supposition is dropped

Read the indent from the left: two steps in, then out, then out. That is the goal's two ifs, nested, drawn on the page. As pairs, the middle line is ({P, Q}, P ∧ Q): the indent was S all along.

Accepted names

A proof written as a list of lines is called a derivation. Proofs built from suppositions that open and close are called natural deduction; a line written as a pair of suppositions and a statement is called a sequent, often written S ⊢ R. Closing a supposition is called discharging it. The smallest closed collection of statements reached from G is G's deductive closure. The identity technique is the identity (or reflexivity) of ⊢, written P ⊢ P; the way of writing proofs called the sequent calculus lays it down as its identity axiom. The more-suppositions technique is called weakening. 4d.4.2 is the deduction theorem.

Cannot be proved, earned (3c)

3c said in plain words that infinity cannot be argued from the axioms of rungs 0 to 2. Here it is as a proof. Let G be those axioms, and let W be 3c's world, the sets of the layers. 3c checked in words that every member of G is true in W, and that no set of W is inductive.

\text{assume a proof from } G \text{ reaches } \exists x\,(x \text{ inductive}) @@ supposed, aiming to refute
\quad \text{every member of } G \text{ is true in } W @@ 3c's check, “every earlier tool stays inside”
\quad \exists x\,(x \text{ inductive}) \text{ is true in } W @@ soundness (“the tables, again”), on the two lines above
\quad \neg\exists x\,(x \text{ inductive}) \text{ is true in } W @@ 3c's check, “no set of the world is inductive”
\quad \bot @@ clash
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no proof from } G \text{ reaches } \exists x\,(x \text{ inductive})} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute

The same column, with foundation in place of infinity and 2f's world in place of W, earns 2f's claim that foundation does not follow from the other axioms. A proof about proofs is an ordinary proof, once proofs are objects.
