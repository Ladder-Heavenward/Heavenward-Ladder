How Fast Is the Tide Rising?

How fast is the tide rising? Anyone who keeps a boat on this coast asks it. A sailboat waiting to cross the bar at Moss Landing needs a few more feet of water. A crabber on a Puget Sound beach wants to know how long the sand stays dry. The tide table answers a different question. It gives the height of the water at certain times: the times of high and low water, or one reading each hour. How fast the water climbs between readings is not printed anywhere. It is hard because the rate changes all the time. Near low water the sea barely moves, and a few hours later it climbs a foot in twenty minutes. This page reads the rate from a real table as chords, finds the rate at an instant from a function fitted to the low and the high, and proves why the middle hours of a rise run fastest. It ends with the sailors' old rule of twelfths, and with a rise from Seattle that the rule gets wrong.

The average rate over an hour is the rise in that hour. The best reading of the rate at an instant from an hourly table is the centred chord: the rise over the two hours around it, halved. For the rate at an instant itself, fit the smoothest rise that starts and ends at rest. Its rate is zero at low and high water and greatest exactly halfway, at one and a half times the range over the time of the rise. The sailors' rule says the six hours of a rise climb one, two, three, three, two and one twelfths of the range. The fitted rise agrees to within a hundredth of the range. A rise that is quicker at its start than at its end breaks both.

The Words

Take Monterey on the evening of 21 July 2024. The predictions below are NOAA's, for its station on the Monterey wharf, in feet above the chart's zero, at Pacific standard time. Low water came at 3:36 p.m., at 2.77 ft. High water came at 9:47 p.m., at 6.47 ft. In between, the table gives one height each hour.

From here on, on this page, t is the time in hours, and h(t) the height of the water in feet. The low and the high are L and L + R: R is the range of the rise. T is how long the rise takes, from low water to high. Monterey's evening: L = 2.77, R = 3.70, T = 6.18 (6 hours 11 minutes).

A tide table is a function of time, known only at the times it lists. Between them, the water rises without jumps, but the table says nothing about how.

Chords from a table

The rise from 6 p.m. to 7 p.m. is 4.743 − 3.830 = 0.913 ft. Over a run of one hour, that is a chord of slope 0.913 ft an hour (13a). It is the average rate of that hour, nothing more. The whole rise averages R/T = 3.70/6.18 ≈ 0.60 ft an hour.

The chords climb, then fall: 0.34, 0.69, 0.91, 0.91, 0.64, 0.16. The middle hours of the rise run fastest, and the first and last run slowest. The table shows it. It does not say why, or how fast the water climbs at 7 p.m. exactly.

The chord from 6 to 7 belongs to 6:30 more than to either end. For the rate at 7 p.m. itself, take the chord from 6 to 8, which has 7 at its middle. Centred-chord technique: for a function made of a constant, a multiple of t and a multiple of t², the chord over a stretch centred on t has exactly the slope at t. For other functions it is a guess, usually a close one.

At Monterey, the centred chord at 7 p.m. is (5.651 − 3.830)/2 ≈ 0.91 ft an hour. That is a guess at the rate at an instant, made from two readings. To do better needs a function for every instant, not a list.

A function for the whole rise

Near low water and near high water the sea stands almost still for a while. Sailors call it slack water. So guess a rise that starts at rest at L, ends at rest at L + R, and has no corners. The plainest polynomial that does it has degree 3.

From here on, on this page, s = (t − t₀)/T is the share of the rise gone by, where t₀ is the time of low water: s runs from 0 at low water to 1 at high. The smooth rise is h(t) = L + R(3s² − 2s³). Its accepted name is a smoothstep.

h(t_0) = L,\ \ h(t_0 + T) = L + R(3 - 2) = L + R @@ s = 0 and s = 1: the rise starts and ends where the table says
\tfrac{d}{ds}(3s^2 - 2s^3) = 6s - 6s^2 @@ polynomial-slope (13d)
h'(t) = R \cdot (6s - 6s^2) \cdot \tfrac1T @@ constant-factor; multiple-inside (13j) and displaced-slopes (13d): s is t/T less a constant
\htmlClass{new}{h'(t) = \tfrac{6R}{T}\,(s - s^2)} @@ the rate of the smooth rise, in feet an hour
h'(t_0) = 0 = h'(t_0 + T) @@ at s = 0 and s = 1: at rest at low and high water

For Monterey's evening, 6R/T = 6 · 3.703/6.183 ≈ 3.59. At 7 p.m., s = (19 − 15.6)/6.183 ≈ 0.55, so h′ ≈ 3.59 · (0.55 − 0.30) ≈ 0.89 ft an hour. The centred chord said 0.91. The model also puts the water at 4.90 ft at 7 p.m., where the table says 4.74. Across the evening, model and table never differ by more than 0.18 ft.

Why the middle hours run fastest

Fastest-at-mid-tide technique. Let h be the smooth rise. Its rate is greatest exactly halfway through the rise, where it is 3R/(2T), one and a half times the average. And the rate a share s into the rise equals the rate a share s before its end.

s - s^2 = \tfrac14 - \big(s - \tfrac12\big)^2 @@ completing the square (8i)
s - s^2 \le \tfrac14,\ \text{ with equality only at } s = \tfrac12 @@ square-never-negative (8f), and a square is 0 only at 0
h'(t) \le \tfrac{6R}{T} \cdot \tfrac14 = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{3R}{2T}} @@ the rate of the smooth rise; 6R/T &gt; 0
(1 - s) - (1 - s)^2 = s - s^2 \quad \blacksquare @@ expand: the rate at s and at 1 − s agree

So the rise is slow at both ends and quickest in the middle for one plain reason: it must start at rest and end at rest, and it must cover R in T. At Monterey the greatest rate is 3 · 3.703/(2 · 6.183) ≈ 0.90 ft an hour, at about 6:41 p.m. The table's fastest hours, 6 to 7 and 7 to 8, rose 0.91 ft each.

The rule of twelfths

Sailors on this coast learn an older rule, with no function in it. For a rise of about six hours, the water climbs 1/12 of the range in the first hour, 2/12 in the second, 3/12 in the third, 3/12 in the fourth, 2/12 in the fifth and 1/12 in the sixth. Its accepted name is the rule of twelfths. It is a law, read from tides: it has a range, and it has exceptions.

T = 6:\ \ \text{hour } k \text{ rises } R\big(f(\tfrac k6) - f(\tfrac{k-1}6)\big),\ f(s) = 3s^2 - 2s^3 @@ the smooth rise, hour by hour
\tfrac{12}{R} \times \text{rise}:\ 0.89,\ 2.22,\ 2.89,\ 2.89,\ 2.22,\ 0.89 @@ worked; they add to 12
\text{the rule}:\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 2,\ 1 @@ whole twelfths, easy to add in a cockpit
\htmlClass{new}{\text{running totals differ by under } 0.01R} @@ after hours 1, 2, 3: 1, 3, 6 twelfths against 0.89, 3.11, 6

In the middle two hours the rule climbs R/4 an hour, and the smooth rise's greatest rate is 3R/(2 · 6) = R/4 as well. At Monterey R/4 ≈ 0.93 ft an hour; the table's fastest hours rose 0.91. The rule of twelfths is the smooth rise rounded to whole twelfths.

A rise the rule gets wrong

Seattle, 5 June 2023, from NOAA's Seattle station on Elliott Bay. Low water at 11:26 a.m., −3.66 ft. High water at 7:09 p.m., 12.06 ft. The range is 15.7 ft, and the rise takes 7 hours 43 minutes, not six. The table's hourly rises from noon: 1.75, 2.85, 3.28, 3.10, 2.43, 1.48, 0.54 ft. The fastest hour, 2 to 3 p.m., comes before the middle of the rise at 3:17.

The smooth rise puts the water at 3.31 ft at 3 p.m.; the table says 4.51. The water passed half its range before half the time had gone. This rise is quicker at its start than at its end, and no rise that is the same both ways round can follow it. The rule of twelfths fails the same way. Puget Sound's tides are mixed: two highs and two lows a day, of different sizes. The rule and the smooth rise fit a tide that rises the same both ways round, in about six hours, and the further a tide is from that, the worse they do.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

13a set beside the slope a second kind of care. Things do not jump between states; they pass through a middle. A tide table lists heights: a before and an after for each hour. The rate of the tide is the middle between them. It is what a skipper waiting at a bar needs, and the table never prints it.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

At Seattle the water rose 3.28 ft between 2 and 3 p.m. That is a before and after. The smooth rise says that near the middle of a rise the water climbs at one and a half times its average rate, for a short while. A skipper who reads only the average, 2 ft an hour, is wrong by a foot an hour when it matters most.

At 3 p.m. on 5 June 2023, was the water at Seattle rising faster than a foot every twenty minutes?

Running It Backwards

Handed the greatest rate of a smooth rise and its time T, the range is R = 2T/3 times that rate. Handed one hour of a six-hour rise under the rule of twelfths, the range is that hour's rise over its twelfths: a third hour of 1.8 ft means a range of 7.2 ft. Handed a centred chord and one reading, the other reading is the first plus twice the chord times the half-run.

Who said it first

The rule of twelfths is a sailors’ rule, taught for generations in British and Commonwealth seamanship books; who first wrote it down is not known. The famous scientist of 13·nav.4.4 is Galileo Galilei, in the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632), who held that the tides were the sloshing of the seas as the earth spins on its axis and travels around the sun; Johannes Kepler had already blamed the moon. Isaac Newton’s Principia (1687) gave the moon’s and sun’s pull as the cause. William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) built the first tide-predicting machine in 1872, a set of pulleys that added up regular swings; the United States used such machines to print its tables until 1966.

Galileo wondered that so great a mind as Kepler’s should have lent his ear and his assent to the moon’s rule over the waters, and to other occult properties and such childish ideas.

Galileo Galilei, Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632), Fourth Day; paraphrase

Accepted names

The rise is the flood and the fall the ebb; the still water near low and high is slack water, or the stand. NOAA’s zero for these heights is mean lower low water, the chart datum: the average of each day’s lower low water over nineteen years. A tide with two highs and two lows a day of different sizes is a mixed tide. Fitting regular swings to a port’s records has the accepted name harmonic analysis. The smooth rise is a smoothstep, or the cubic Hermite step.
