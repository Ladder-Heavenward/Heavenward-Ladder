26h · proof

Residues

A loop round a hole totals something. One over z round zero totals two pi i, and every other power totals nothing. So near a trouble, only one number matters: how much of one over the distance the function holds there. This step names that number, and proves that a loop’s total is two pi i times the sum of those numbers, one for each trouble inside. The total of a whole loop comes down to a few pieces of arithmetic, done at a few points.

Near a pole, a function is a few negative powers plus a power sum. Only the one-over-distance term survives a loop. So the loop total is two pi i times the sum of those coefficients, the residues, at the troubles inside.

26h1Near a pole

From here on, f has a pole of order m at w when f has a complex slope near w but not at w, and g(z) = (z − w)ᵐ f(z), given the right value at w, has a complex slope at w with g(w) ≠ 0. The residue of f at w is the coefficient of 1/(z − w) in the pieces below: Res(f, w) = b₁. Accepted names: a pole, its order, its residue.

Pole-split technique: near a pole of order m, f(z) = b_m/(z − w)ᵐ + ⋯ + b₁/(z − w) + h(z), where h has a complex slope at w. The first part is the trouble part of f at w.

g(z) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 0} g_k (z-w)^k \text{ near } w @@ rebuilt in the plane (26g2)
f(z) = \frac{g(z)}{(z-w)^m} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \frac{g_k}{(z-w)^{m-k}} + \sum_{k \ge m} g_k (z-w)^{k-m} @@ divide each piece by (z − w)ᵐ
\htmlClass{new}{b_j = g_{m-j},\ \ h(z) = \textstyle\sum_{k\ge m} g_k(z-w)^{k-m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ h is a power sum, so it has a slope at w (23e)

For a pole of order 1, the residue is the simplest to find: b₁ = g(w) = the value of (z − w)f(z) at w. For f = p/q with q(w) = 0 and q′(w) ≠ 0, it is p(w)/q′(w).

26h2The residue technique

Residue technique: if f has a complex slope on an open disc holding the circle C and its inside, except at poles w₁, …, wₙ inside C, then ∮_C f = 2πi (Res(f, w₁) + ⋯ + Res(f, wₙ)).

f - (P_1 + \cdots + P_n) \text{ has a slope on the disc} @@ Pⱼ is the trouble part at wⱼ (26h1); each Pⱼ has a slope away from wⱼ
\oint_C \big(f - \textstyle\sum P_j\big) = 0 @@ the disc-undoing technique (26f2)
\oint_C \frac{dz}{(z-w_j)^k} = 0 \text{ for } k \ge 2 @@ an undoing −1/((k − 1)(z − wⱼ)ᵏ⁻¹) with a slope on C (26e2)
\oint_C \frac{dz}{z-w_j} = 2\pi i @@ the kernel of the rim technique (26g1): wⱼ inside C
\htmlClass{new}{\oint_C f = 2\pi i\textstyle\sum_j \operatorname{Res}(f, w_j)} \quad \blacksquare

A whole loop’s total, which is a limit of sums along a curve, is fixed by a few coefficients read off at a few points. This is the tool 26k turns on real integrals no antiderivative can reach.

26h3Counting roots by a loop

Root-count technique: if p is a polynomial with no root on the circle C, then (1/2πi) ∮_C p′/p is the number of roots of p inside C, counted with repeats.

p = w_n\textstyle\prod (z - r_j) \Rightarrow \frac{p'}{p} = \sum_j \frac{1}{z - r_j} @@ the root technique (25d4); the product rule, divided by p
\operatorname{Res}\big(\tfrac{1}{z-r_j}, r_j\big) = 1 @@ a pole of order 1 (26h1)
\htmlClass{new}{\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C \frac{p'}{p} = \#\{j : r_j \text{ inside } C\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the residue technique (26h2); terms with rⱼ outside have a slope on the disc and total 0

So a loop can count the roots inside it without finding any of them. With C a very large circle, it counts all n of them: the root technique a third time.

26h4Illegal moves

\operatorname{Res}\big(\tfrac{1}{z^2}, 0\big) = 1\,? @@ there is no 1/z piece: the residue is 0
\oint_{|z|=1} \frac{dz}{z-2} = 2\pi i\,? @@ the pole at 2 lies outside the circle; the total is 0
\operatorname{Res}\big(\tfrac{e^z}{z^2}, 0\big) = e^0 = 1 \text{ by putting } z = 0 \text{ in } e^z\,? @@ that rule is for poles of order 1; here g = e^z, and b₁ = g₁ = 1, which agrees only by luck: for e^(2z)/z² it is 2
\oint_C f = 2\pi i\textstyle\sum \operatorname{Res} \text{ for any } f\,? @@ only where the troubles inside are poles and f has a slope elsewhere on the disc

The accepted names

The trouble part is the principal part of the Laurent series at a pole; the residue technique is the residue theorem; the root-count technique is the argument principle, for polynomials.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy introduced residues in 1826 and built a calculus of them over the next decades. Pierre Alphonse Laurent gave the expansion with negative powers in 1843. The argument principle is also Cauchy’s, from 1831.
