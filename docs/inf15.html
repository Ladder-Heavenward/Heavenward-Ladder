Can a Chemical Plant Be Written as Algebra?

Can a chemical plant be written as algebra? A net has places that hold tokens and transitions that eat some tokens and make others. Rung 27’s balanced reaction is one transition: the places are the species, the tokens are molecules. The states a net can reach from a start are the arrows of a category made freely from the net, and two firings that do not touch can be run side by side, in either order. Nets with some places left open as ports glue along those ports, as spaces glued in rung 47, and the rate rule of rung 13 is read off the net transition by transition. Whether a given state can be reached at all is decidable, and since 2021 it is known to take more steps than any tower of powers.

A net: places P, transitions T, each t with an input list in(t) and an output list out(t) over P. A marking m fires t when m ≥ in(t), giving m − in(t) + out(t). A weight y with y · (out(t) − in(t)) = 0 for every t keeps y · m fixed on every run. Runs are the arrows of the free category on the net, side by side is adding markings. Gluing open nets along shared places is a colimit (47e3). Mass action: dx/dt = Σₜ kₜ x^{in(t)} (out(t) − in(t)).

inf15.1A Net and Its Firing Rule

Take three places a, b, c and two transitions. Transition t₁ eats one token from a and one from b and makes one in c: a + b → c. Transition t₂ eats one token from c and makes two in a: c → 2a. A marking lists the tokens in each place, (a, b, c). A transition can fire when the marking holds its inputs; firing takes the inputs away and puts the outputs in. Each firing changes the marking by a fixed list: out(t) − in(t).

Conserved-weight technique: if y · (out(t) − in(t)) = 0 for every transition t, then y · m is the same for every marking m reached from a start. This is 27·chem.1’s atom count turned round: there the reaction was fixed and the amounts were found; here the reactions are fixed and the weights are found.

t_1: (-1, -1, 1),\quad t_2: (2, 0, -1) @@ out − in, for each transition
-y_1 - y_2 + y_3 = 0,\quad 2y_1 - y_3 = 0 @@ a weight that each firing keeps
y_3 = 2y_1,\ \ y_2 = y_1 @@ solve
\htmlClass{new}{y = (1, 1, 2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ any stretch of it is conserved too

inf15.2Reachable States by Hand

Rule-out technique: a marking whose conserved weight differs from the start’s cannot be reached.

\{(1,1,0),\ (0,0,1),\ (2,0,0)\} @@ every marking reachable from (1, 1, 0), by firing each enabled transition in turn
y \cdot (1,1,0) = 2 @@ the start, with y = (1, 1, 2)
y \cdot (0,0,2) = 4 \ne 2 @@ a candidate
\htmlClass{new}{(0,0,2) \text{ is not reachable}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the conserved-weight technique

The test only rules out. The marking (0, 2, 0) also has weight 2 and is not reachable. Deciding reachability in general needs far more than weights: a 1981 proof showed it can always be decided, and the work since 2019 shows how long that must take.

inf15.3Runs Are Arrows

Make a category: its things are markings, and an arrow from m to m′ is a run, a list of firings that takes m to m′. Running one run after another is composing, and the empty run is the arrow that does nothing. Nothing else is assumed, so this is the category made freely from the net, as 47b2 made a group freely from letters. Two runs can also be set side by side: from m and n to m′ and n′, a run from m + n to m′ + n′.

Side-by-side technique: two firings that both can fire from one marking, and do not need each other’s tokens, reach the same marking in either order.

(1,1,1) \xrightarrow{t_1} (0,0,2) \xrightarrow{t_2} (2,0,1) @@ t₁ first
(1,1,1) \xrightarrow{t_2} (3,1,0) \xrightarrow{t_1} (2,0,1) @@ t₂ first
(1,1,0) + (0,0,1) \to (0,0,1) + (2,0,0) @@ the two firings set side by side
\htmlClass{new}{t_2 \circ t_1 = t_1 \circ t_2 = t_1 \otimes t_2} \quad \blacksquare @@ one arrow, three ways of writing it

From (1, 1, 0) the two orders are not the same: t₂ cannot fire first, because it needs the token t₁ makes. Side by side is for firings that do not feed each other.

inf15.4Open Nets Glue

Cut the net in two. Piece A has places a, b, c and transition t₁; piece B has places a, c and transition t₂. The places a and c are left open as ports, a set C = {a, c} that sits in both pieces. Gluing A and B along C is the shape A ← C → B, and the glued net is its colimit, as 47e3 glued spaces: places joined along the ports, transitions kept from both.

Glued-weight technique: a weight on the glued net is conserved exactly when its part on each piece is conserved there.

\text{on A: } -y_a - y_b + y_c = 0 @@ t₁ lives in A
\text{on B: } 2y_a - y_c = 0 @@ t₂ lives in B
\text{glued: both, since each transition lies in one piece} @@ the conserved-weight test runs over transitions
\htmlClass{new}{y = (1,1,2) \text{ on the whole}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two pieces’ answers agree on the ports a and c

So a plant can be checked piece by piece, provided the pieces agree on what flows through the ports. The 2018 work on open nets makes this a map of categories from glued nets to their reachable behaviour at the ports.

inf15.5The Rate Rule Off a Net

13·chem.6 found a rate that is a constant times a concentration. The general rule, mass action, gives each transition a rate constant kₜ and runs it at kₜ times the product of the concentrations of its inputs, each to the power of how many it eats. The concentrations then change by Σₜ kₜ x^{in(t)} (out(t) − in(t)).

Rate-keeps-weight technique: a conserved weight of the net stays constant along the rate rule.

\dot a = -k_1 ab + 2k_2 c,\ \ \dot b = -k_1 ab,\ \ \dot c = k_1 ab - k_2 c @@ mass action on t₁ and t₂
\tfrac{d}{dt}(a + b + 2c) = -k_1ab + 2k_2c - k_1ab + 2k_1ab - 2k_2c @@ the weight (1, 1, 2)
= 0 @@ each transition’s term is a multiple of out − in, and y kills it
\htmlClass{new}{a + b + 2c \text{ is constant}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the count of a-atoms, if c is a pair of them

The rule adds one term per transition, so gluing two open nets adds their rates on the shared places. Read that way, taking the rate rule is a map of categories from open nets to open systems of rate equations; the 2017 paper made this exact.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞τ.oq.1Which questions about nets stay feasible? Reachability is decidable, and since 2021 it is known to need, in the worst case, a number of steps that outgrows every tower of powers; narrower questions and nets of fixed size are mapped one by one. How to treat nets with chance, where transitions fire at random rates, in the same glued and arrow-drawn way is still being worked out.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In August 1939, at thirteen, a German student drew nets to describe chemical processes; his 1962 dissertation, “Communication with Automata”, set them out, and they became a standard way to draw machines that run in parallel.

In 1981 reachability in nets was shown decidable. In 2019 five authors showed it is not bounded by any tower of powers of fixed height, and in 2021 two independent papers showed it is exactly as hard as a function that outgrows every tower of powers.

In 2017 two mathematicians gave open reaction networks with mass action a map of categories to their steady behaviour, and in 2018 to 2020 open nets with ports were glued as colimits, with runs as the arrows of a category made freely.

The accepted names

A net is a Petri net; places and transitions keep those names; a marking is a marking; a conserved weight is a P-invariant (place invariant). The category of runs is the free commutative monoidal category on the net, side by side its tensor. A net with ports is an open Petri net, glued by pushout in a decorated cospan category. The rate rule is the law of mass action, the equations the rate equation; the map of categories is black-boxing. Reachability being Ackermann-hard is stated for vector addition systems, the same thing as nets.

Who said it first

Carl Adam Petri, Kommunikation mit Automaten (Darmstadt, 1962). Ernst Mayr (STOC 1981), reachability decidable. Wojciech Czerwiński, Sławomir Lasota, Ranko Lazić, Jérôme Leroux and Filip Mazowiecki, “The reachability problem for Petri nets is not elementary” (STOC 2019).
