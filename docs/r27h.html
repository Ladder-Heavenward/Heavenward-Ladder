27h · support

Measuring sticks for arrows

A ruler laid along a direction reads off one number from each arrow, and it reads sums as sums. Such a reading is itself a straight map, from arrows to numbers. The readings of a space form a space of their own, of the same dimension. And a reading that takes two arrows at once, straight in each, is how lengths and angles will be measured.

The straight readings of a space of dimension n form a space of dimension n. A reading of two arrows that is straight in each is a grid, read as a row times the grid times a column.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 27c (straight maps), 27d (grids) and 27b (bases). The techniques of this rung come next, from 27i.

27h1Readings form a space

Stands on 27c1 and 27b3.

A reading of a space V is a straight map from V to the numbers. Readings add and stretch value by value, so they form a space of arrows (27a2). Accepted name: the readings form the dual space, V*.

Reading-basis technique: if v₁, …, vₙ is a basis of V, the readings f₁, …, fₙ with fᵢ(vⱼ) = 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise are a basis of V*; so V* has dimension n.

f(t_1v_1 + \cdots + t_nv_n) = t_1f(v_1) + \cdots + t_nf(v_n) @@ a reading keeps mixes; its values on the basis fix it
f = f(v_1)f_1 + \cdots + f(v_n)f_n @@ both sides agree on every vⱼ, and so on every arrow
s_1f_1 + \cdots + s_nf_n = 0 \Rightarrow s_j = (\textstyle\sum s_if_i)(v_j) = 0 @@ read each vⱼ
\htmlClass{new}{f_1..f_n \text{ build } V^* \text{ and are free}} \quad \blacksquare

Each fᵢ reads off the i-th number of an arrow in the basis vⱼ. For lists of numbers with the eⱼ, a reading is a row: f(x) = a₁x₁ + ⋯ + aₙxₙ.

Question

  1. 27h1.1doFind the rows f₁ = (a, b) and f₂ = (c, d) with fᵢ(vⱼ) = 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise.
    Find f₁(3, 5) and f₂(3, 5).
    Check that f₁(3, 5)·v₁ + f₂(3, 5)·v₂ = (3, 5).

27h2Readings of two arrows

Stands on 27h1 and 27d2.

Two-slot technique: if B(u, v) is a number for each pair of lists, straight in u when v is held, and straight in v when u is held, then B(x, y) = xᵀGy, where G is the grid with Gᵢⱼ = B(eᵢ, eⱼ). B(x, y) = B(y, x) for all x, y exactly when G = Gᵀ.

B(x, y) = B(\textstyle\sum_i x_ie_i,\ \sum_j y_je_j) @@ the eⱼ build every list
= \textstyle\sum_i\sum_j x_i\,B(e_i, e_j)\,y_j @@ straight in each slot, one at a time
\htmlClass{new}{B(x, y) = x^{\mathsf T}Gy} @@ a row times a grid times a column (27d2)
B(x,y) = B(y,x)\ \forall x,y \iff G_{ij} = G_{ji} @@ take x = eᵢ, y = eⱼ; and back by the line above
\blacksquare

With G = I, B(x, y) = x₁y₁ + ⋯ + xₙyₙ. In the plane and in space this reading returns lengths: B(x, x) is the square of the distance of 18a. The technique step 27i takes it further, to the angle between two arrows.

Question

  1. 27h2.1doFind G, and check G = Gᵀ.
    Find B((1, 2), (3, −1)) two ways: from the formula, and as xᵀGy.
    Write B(x, x) as 2(x₁ + ½x₂)² plus a number times x₂², and find whether B(x, x) can be 0 for x ≠ 0.

27h3Illegal moves

Stands on 27h1 and 27h2.

f(x) = x_1 + 1 \text{ is a reading}\,? @@ it sends 0 to 1; a reading is straight
B(x, y) = x_1y_1 \text{ is straight in the pair } (x, y)\,? @@ doubling both x and y multiplies it by 4: it is straight in each slot, not in the pair
f_1 \text{ depends only on } v_1\,? @@ fᵢ(vⱼ) = 0 for every j ≠ i ties it to the whole basis; change v₂ and f₁ changes
B(x, x) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0 \text{ for every two-slot reading}\,? @@ with G having rows (1, 0), (0, −1), B((1, 1), (1, 1)) = 0

Question

  1. 27h3.1bend
    1. Find f(0, 0), f(1, 0) + f(0, 1) and f(1, 1).
      Find which demand of a reading f breaks.
    2. Find B((1, 0), (1, 0)) and B((2, 0), (2, 0)).
      Find what doubling x alone does, and what doubling the pair does.
    3. Find f₁ for the basis v₁, v₂, and for the basis v₁, w₂.
      Find whether f₁ changed, though v₁ did not.
    4. Find B(x, x) for x = (1, 1), (2, 1) and (1, 2).
      Find every x with B(x, x) = 0, and why G = I gives 0 only for x = 0.

The accepted names

A reading is a linear functional (or covector), and the readings form the dual space V*; the fᵢ are the dual basis. A two-slot reading is a bilinear form; one with B(x, y) = B(y, x) is symmetric, and G is its Gram matrix. With G = I it is the dot product.

Who said it first

Readings were used throughout the nineteenth century without a name for their space. Hermann Weyl and Stefan Banach made the dual space a standard tool in the 1920s.
