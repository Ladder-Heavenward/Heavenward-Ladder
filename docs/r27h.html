27h · support

Measuring Sticks for Vectors

A ruler laid along a direction reads off one number from each vector, and it reads sums as sums. Such a reading is itself a straight map, from vectors to numbers. The readings of a space form a space of their own, of the same dimension. And a reading that takes two vectors at once, straight in each, is how lengths and angles will be measured.

The straight readings of a space of dimension n form a space of dimension n. A reading of two vectors that is straight in each is a grid, read as a row times the grid times a column.

27h1Readings Form a Space

A reading of a space V is a straight map from V to the numbers. Readings add and stretch value by value, so they form a space of vectors (27a2). Accepted name: the readings form the dual space, V*.

Reading-basis technique: if v₁, …, vₙ is a basis of V, the readings f₁, …, fₙ with fᵢ(vⱼ) = 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise are a basis of V*; so V* has dimension n.

f(t_1v_1 + \cdots + t_nv_n) = t_1f(v_1) + \cdots + t_nf(v_n) @@ a reading keeps mixes; its values on the basis fix it
f = f(v_1)f_1 + \cdots + f(v_n)f_n @@ both sides agree on every vⱼ, and so on every vector
s_1f_1 + \cdots + s_nf_n = 0 \Rightarrow s_j = (\textstyle\sum s_if_i)(v_j) = 0 @@ read each vⱼ
\htmlClass{new}{f_1..f_n \text{ build } V^* \text{ and are free}} \quad \blacksquare

Each fᵢ reads off the i-th number of a vector in the basis vⱼ. For lists of numbers with the eⱼ, a reading is a row: f(x) = a₁x₁ + ⋯ + aₙxₙ.

A straight map f from V to W turns readings round: for a reading r of W, r ∘ f is a reading of V. Write f*(r) = r ∘ f. Turn-round technique: (g ∘ f)* = f* ∘ g*, and the identity of V gives the identity of V*. r ∘ (g ∘ f) = (r ∘ g) ∘ f by 9b3, which is f*(g*(r)); and r ∘ id = r. ∎ The reading basis matches V with V*, but another basis gives another matching: that matching is made with a choice. V is matched with its readings of readings, V**, with none. Double-reading technique: v ↦ (r ↦ r(v)) matches V with V**. It is straight. It loses nothing: for v ≠ 0, grow v to a basis (27b) and take the reading that is 1 at v. V** has the dimension of V, by the reading-basis technique twice, so lost plus reached (27c2) makes it onto. ∎

27h2Readings of Two Vectors

Two-slot technique: if B(u, v) is a number for each pair of lists, straight in u when v is held, and straight in v when u is held, then B(x, y) = xᵀGy, where G is the grid with Gᵢⱼ = B(eᵢ, eⱼ). B(x, y) = B(y, x) for all x, y exactly when G = Gᵀ.

B(x, y) = B(\textstyle\sum_i x_ie_i,\ \sum_j y_je_j) @@ the eⱼ build every list
= \textstyle\sum_i\sum_j x_i\,B(e_i, e_j)\,y_j @@ straight in each slot, one at a time
\htmlClass{new}{B(x, y) = x^{\mathsf T}Gy} @@ a row times a grid times a column (27d2)
B(x,y) = B(y,x)\ \forall x,y \iff G_{ij} = G_{ji} @@ take x = eᵢ, y = eⱼ; and back by the line above
\blacksquare

With G = I, B(x, y) = x₁y₁ + ⋯ + xₙyₙ. In the plane and in space this reading returns lengths: B(x, x) is the square of the distance of 18a. The technique step 27i takes it further, to the angle between two vectors.

27h3The Readings That Are 0 on a Space

Let W be a space inside V, with dim V = n and dim W = k. Some readings of V are 0 at every vector of W.

Zero-readings technique: the readings of V that are 0 on W form a space of dimension n − k. And for a straight map f from V to U, f* loses exactly the readings of U that are 0 on what f reaches.

w_1..w_k,\ v_{k+1}..v_n \text{ a basis of } V @@ grow a basis of W (27b3)
f_1, \ldots, f_n \text{ its reading basis} @@ the reading-basis technique (27h1)
r = r(w_1)f_1 + \cdots + r(v_n)f_n @@ both sides agree at each basis vector
r = 0 \text{ on } W \iff r(w_1) = \cdots = r(w_k) = 0 @@ a reading keeps mixes
\iff r \text{ is a mix of } f_{k+1}, \ldots, f_n @@ read the second line
f^*(r) = r \circ f = 0 \iff r(f(v)) = 0 \text{ for every } v @@ the meaning of f* (27h1)
\htmlClass{new}{\dim\{r : r = 0 \text{ on } W\} = n - k} \quad \blacksquare @@ lines 4 and 5: the last n − k of the fᵢ are a basis of it; and line 6 says f* loses the readings that are 0 on what f reaches

Put the two halves together. If f reaches a space of dimension r inside U, and dim U = m, then f* loses a space of dimension m − r. By lost-plus-reached (27c2), f* reaches a space of dimension m − (m − r) = r. So a map and its turned-round map reach the same number of directions. For grids, f is A and f* is Aᵀ acting on rows: the columns of a grid and its rows build spaces of the same dimension.

In space, the readings that are 0 on a plane through 0 form a line of readings: all the stretches of one reading a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + a₃x₃, whose row (a₁, a₂, a₃) stands at right angles to the plane.

27h4A Reading That Misses None of a Few Vectors

For one vector v ≠ 0 there is a reading that is not 0 at v: grow v to a basis and take the reading that is 1 at v (27h1). For several vectors at once one reading still serves.

Misses-none technique: if v₁, …, vₖ are vectors other than 0 in a real space V, some reading r of V has r(vᵢ) ≠ 0 for every i.

r(v_i) \neq 0 \text{ for } i \lt k @@ suppose a reading serves the first k − 1 vectors: induction on k
s(v_k) \neq 0 @@ a reading that is not 0 at vₖ (27h1)
(r + t\cdot s)(v_k) = r(v_k) + t\,s(v_k) @@ this is 0 for at most one t
(r + t\cdot s)(v_i) = r(v_i) + t\,s(v_i),\ i \lt k @@ 0 for at most one t each: none if s(vᵢ) = 0, since r(vᵢ) ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{r + t\cdot s \text{ serves all } k \text{, for some } t} \quad \blacksquare @@ at most k real numbers t fail, and there are more reals than k

Read the other way round: the readings that are 0 at vᵢ form a space of dimension n − 1 (27h3), and the technique says that finitely many such spaces never fill the whole space of readings. The same proof shows that a real space is never the union of finitely many smaller spaces inside it. The last line counted numbers, so it needs a field with more than k members. Over a field with few members it can fail.

27h5The Grid of a Two-Slot Reading in a Basis

27h2 gave a two-slot reading of lists the grid G with Gᵢⱼ = B(eᵢ, eⱼ). In any space with a basis v₁, …, vₙ, a two-slot reading B has the grid Gᵢⱼ = B(vᵢ, vⱼ), and B(u, v) = xᵀGy for the lists x of u and y of v, by the same proof.

Two-slot grid technique: if Q is the grid whose columns are the lists of a second basis in the first, the grid of B in the second basis is QᵀGQ. The dimension of what G reaches is the same in every basis, and it is n exactly when the only u with B(u, v) = 0 for every v is u = 0.

x = Qx',\ \ y = Qy' @@ old lists from new ones: Q undoes the change table (27b5)
B(u, v) = (Qx')^TG\,(Qy') = x'^T(Q^TGQ)\,y' @@ (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ, entry by entry
G' = Q^TGQ @@ the grid of B in a basis is the only one that does this (27h2)
\dim(G \text{ reaches}) = \dim(Q^TGQ \text{ reaches}) @@ Q and Qᵀ are undoable: they match what is reached (27d5)
x^TGy = 0 \text{ for all } y \iff x^TG = 0 \iff G^Tx = 0 @@ put y = e₁, …, eₙ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only } u = 0 \iff G \text{ undoable}} \quad \blacksquare @@ Gᵀ loses only 0 exactly when it is undoable (27d3), and det Gᵀ = det G (27g1)

The dimension of what G reaches is the size of the two-slot reading B. When the size is n, B loses nothing: no vector other than 0 reads 0 against every vector. The dot, with G = I, loses nothing. The reading B(x, y) = x₁y₁ on the plane has size 1, and it reads 0 whenever x = (0, 1).

A grid changes by PAP⁻¹ when it stands for a map (27d4), and by QᵀGQ when it stands for a two-slot reading. The two agree only when Qᵀ = Q⁻¹. So the determinant of G is not a property of B: det(QᵀGQ) = (det Q)² det G. Whether it is 0 is.

27h6Two-Slot Readings That Change Sign

A two-slot reading B changes sign when B(u, v) = −B(v, u) for all u and v. Then B(v, v) = 0 for every v, and its grid has G = −Gᵀ: 0 down the middle, and each entry below the middle the negative of the one across from it.

Sign-change technique: the two-slot readings of a real space of dimension n that change sign form a space of dimension n(n − 1)/2, and the size of each is even.

G = -G^T \iff g_{ii} = 0,\ g_{ji} = -g_{ij} @@ read B(vᵢ, vⱼ) = −B(vⱼ, vᵢ)
\text{free choice of } g_{ij},\ i \lt j:\ n(n-1)/2 \text{ numbers} @@ the places above the middle; the space of such grids (27d6)
\det G = \det(-G^T) = (-1)^n\det G @@ n columns each change sign (27e1); det Gᵀ = det G (27g1)
n \text{ odd} \Rightarrow \det G = 0 @@ so B loses nothing only when n is even
N = \{u : B(u, v) = 0 \text{ for all } v\},\ \ V = N + W \text{ in one way} @@ grow a basis of N; W is built by the added vectors (27b4)
\htmlClass{new}{\dim W \text{ is even}} \quad \blacksquare @@ B on W loses nothing, so line 4 applies to it; and dim W is the size of B

Why does B on W lose nothing? If w in W reads 0 against all of W, then against any v = u + w′, with u in N, it reads B(w, u) + B(w, w′) = −B(u, w) + 0 = 0. So w is in N as well as W, and w = 0. And in a basis of N followed by a basis of W, the grid of B is 0 outside the corner for W, so its size is that of the corner, dim W.

On the plane the readings that change sign are the stretches of one: B(x, y) = x₁y₂ − x₂y₁, the signed area of 15e and the 2 × 2 determinant. In space they form a space of dimension 3, the dimension of space itself, and in no other dimension do the two numbers agree: n(n − 1)/2 = n only for n = 3, apart from 0.

27h7Maps That Keep a Reading

A straight map T from V to itself carries a two-slot reading B to the reading B′(u, v) = B(Tu, Tv), whose grid is TᵀGT by the proof of 27h5. T keeps B when B′ = B, that is, when B(Tu, Tv) = B(u, v) for all u and v.

Keepers technique: T keeps B exactly when TᵀGT = G. If B loses nothing, every map that keeps B is undoable with determinant 1 or −1, and the maps that keep B form a group.

B(Tu, Tv) = (Tx)^TG\,(Ty) = x^T(T^TGT)\,y @@ lists x of u and y of v (27h5)
T \text{ keeps } B \iff T^TGT = G @@ a two-slot reading has one grid (27h2)
(\det T)^2\det G = \det G,\ \ \det G \neq 0 @@ det-product (27e3), det Tᵀ = det T (27g1); B loses nothing (27h5)
\det T = 1 \text{ or } -1 \Rightarrow T \text{ undoable} @@ the nonzero-det technique (27e4)
B(STu, STv) = B(Tu, Tv) = B(u, v) @@ S and T keep B: so does ST
\htmlClass{new}{B(T^{-1}u, T^{-1}v) = B(u, v):\ \text{a group}} \quad \blacksquare @@ put T⁻¹u and T⁻¹v into the second line; I keeps B (19a)

For the dot, G = I, and the condition reads TᵀT = I: the maps that keep lengths and angles, which 27m takes up. For the signed area of the plane, G has rows (0, 1), (−1, 0), and TᵀGT = (det T)·G, so the keepers are exactly the grids of determinant 1. For the reading x₁y₁ − x₂y₂ the keepers include the grids with rows (c, s), (s, c) where c² − s² = 1.

Two readings B and B′ are carried into each other when B′(u, v) = B(Pu, Pv) for some undoable P. They then have the same size (27h5), and their groups of keepers are matched by T ↦ P⁻¹TP.

f_1 \text{ depends only on } v_1\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> fᵢ(vⱼ) = 0 for every j ≠ i ties it to the whole basis; change v₂ and f₁ changes
B(x, x) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0 \text{ for every two-slot reading}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> with G having rows (1, 0), (0, −1), B((1, 1), (1, 1)) = 0

The accepted names

A reading is a linear functional (or covector), and the readings form the dual space V*; the fᵢ are the dual basis. A two-slot reading is a bilinear form; one with B(x, y) = B(y, x) is symmetric, and G is its Gram matrix. With G = I it is the dot product. The readings that are 0 on W are the annihilator of W. The dimension of what a grid reaches is its rank; that rows and columns agree is “row rank equals column rank”. A two-slot reading is a bilinear form; its size is its rank; one that loses nothing is non-degenerate; grids tied by G′ = QᵀGQ are congruent. A two-slot reading that changes sign is alternating, or skew-symmetric; its grid is a skew-symmetric matrix. The maps that keep a two-slot reading are its isometries; for the dot they form the orthogonal group, and for a reading that changes sign and loses nothing, the symplectic group.

Who said it first

Readings were used throughout the nineteenth century without a name for their space. Hermann Weyl and Stefan Banach made the dual space a standard tool in the 1920s.
