34·cb18 · newsflash for 18

Lines of Equal Height

Rung 18 laid a grid over land that was already there and read distances off a small map. 18·nav ended on the contour map, the brown lines that join points of equal height, and called it the inverse of a graph: the heights are on the land, and the map draws where each height is reached. This page comes back to those lines with the tools of rung 34. A contour line is a level set of the height, so it is closed, it is a smooth curve wherever the ground is not flat, and the uphill direction crosses it square. Its spacing gives the slope. A map printed on paper that has stretched in the rain still shows which hill is which, because stretching keeps the pieces of every level set. And the flat places, the peaks, the pits and the passes, are not free to come in any numbers. On an island the peaks minus the passes plus the pits is always one, and on a whole planet it is two: the characteristic of 34f, read off the land.

A contour line is where the height equals one value, so it is a closed set, and where the uphill arrow is not zero it is a smooth curve that the uphill arrow crosses at a right angle. Lines drawn every so many metres of height crowd where the ground is steep, and the slope is the interval over the spacing. A same-shape map of the land carries level sets to level sets with the same pieces. Flooding an island from the sea up, or uncovering it from the top down, adds a disc at each peak and each pit and a band at each pass, so peaks minus passes plus pits is the characteristic of the land.

34·cb18.1A Contour Is a Level Set

Let h be the height of the ground above sea level at each point of a map, continuous and smoothly sloped. The contour at height c is the level set {h = c}. By 34j’s strict tests it is closed, the land above it is open, and the land at or above it is closed. Where the uphill arrow ∇h is not zero, 28k’s level-plane technique makes the contour a smooth curve whose touching line is square to ∇h. So the steepest way up crosses every contour at a right angle, and water running downhill does too.

Spacing-slope technique: where contours drawn every Δ of height lie a distance s apart on the ground, the slope there is about Δ/s, and the steepest way up runs straight across them.

h(p + u) \approx h(p) + \nabla h(p)\cdot u @@ the best flat map (28b), for a small step u
u \perp \text{the contour},\ |u| = s:\ \ \Delta \approx |\nabla h(p)|\,s @@ from one contour to the next, along the uphill arrow
\htmlClass{new}{|\nabla h(p)| \approx \Delta / s} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope is the size of the uphill arrow (28k)
  
\text{a map at } 1{:}24\,000,\ \text{contours every } 40 \text{ ft} = 12.19 \text{ m} @@ a USGS map of the slopes of Mount Rainier
\text{lines } 1 \text{ mm apart on the map}:\ s = 24 \text{ m} @@ the scale
\Delta / s = 12.19 / 24 = 0.51 @@ the spacing-slope technique

34·cb18.2A Map Kept up to Stretching

A paper map that has been rained on in a pack has stretched a little, and not evenly. Its distances are wrong. It still shows which contour rings which summit, which pass joins which two valleys, and which way to walk downhill to reach the lake. Rung 18’s map was true to the land by similar triangles. This one is true to the land up to stretching.

Stretch-keeps-contours technique: if φ is a same-shape map of the land onto the paper, then the contours of the stretched map h ∘ φ⁻¹ are the images of the contours of h, with the same number of pieces, nested in the same way.

\{h \circ \varphi^{-1} = c\} = \{q : h(\varphi^{-1}(q)) = c\} = \varphi(\{h = c\}) @@ q = φ(p) for exactly one p, since φ is a bijection
\varphi \text{ matches the pieces of } \{h = c\} \text{ with the pieces of its image} @@ the kept-by-shape technique (34e2), on each piece
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi(\{h > c\}) = \{h \circ \varphi^{-1} > c\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines, with > for =: what rings what is kept

A subway map is the same kind of map. The lines of the SkyTrain in Vancouver are drawn straight, with stations evenly spaced, and nothing about the distances is right. Which station lies on which line, and where lines meet, is kept. That is all a rider needs.

34·cb18.3Peaks, Pits and Passes

A flat point of the height is where ∇h = 0. By 28l’s second-derivative test, a clean flat point, where the grid of second slopes has no zero determinant, is a peak, a pit, or a pass: a saddle, where the ground rises two ways and falls the other two. Near a clean flat point the land looks like −x² − y², x² + y², or x² − y².

Take an island, with height 0 on its shore and above 0 inside, and only clean flat points, all at different heights. Uncover it from the top down: let the sea fall from above the highest peak to 0, and watch the land above the water.

Flood-count technique: on an island, peaks minus passes plus pits is 1; on a whole planet, it is 2.

\text{between flat points, the land above the water changes only by stretching} @@ held in trust: proved much higher up the ladder, by sliding down the uphill arrows
\text{past a peak: a new disc of land} @@ near the peak, land above c is a small disc: χ rises by 1
\text{past a pass: a band joins the land along two arcs} @@ near x² − y², two strips of land meet: 2 new edges, 1 new face, χ falls by 1
\text{past a pit: a disc closes a hole in the land} @@ the last water in a hollow drains: 1 new face, χ rises by 1
\text{at height } 0:\ \text{the whole island, a disc},\ \chi = 1 @@ the characteristic of a disc, one face, as on 34f2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{peaks} - \text{passes} + \text{pits} = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ on a planet the whole sphere is uncovered, and χ = 2

Wizard Island, the cinder cone that rises out of Crater Lake in Oregon, has a crater at its top. Its rim has one highest point, a peak, and one lowest point, a pass. The crater floor is a pit. 1 − 1 + 1 = 1. An island with two summits joined by a saddle has 2 − 1 + 0 = 1. And on the whole earth, counting the sea floor, the peaks and pits outnumber the passes by exactly two.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

Every contour map is drawn from heights measured with some error, and it shows nothing smaller than its interval. A hummock 3 m high on a slope mapped every 12 m is not on the map. So the map’s count of peaks, pits and passes is not the land’s.

A count that every small error changes in cancelling pairs is exact even when the map that gives it is rough.

But the difference is never odd. A small bump on a slope adds a peak and a pass together: 1 − 1. A small hollow adds a pit and a pass: −1 + 1. Every feature too small for the map comes in a pair that cancels.

So the count is exact where the map is not. The map is a cloud of heights, each with its error, and the count of flat points, weighed by the flood-count technique, sits outside the cloud.

A survey of San Juan Island finds 14 peaks, 2 pits and 15 passes. Is that possible? What would you check before trusting each number?

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A contour is a level curve, or isoline. A clean flat point is a non-degenerate critical point, and a height with only such points is a Morse function. The flood-count technique is the Morse relation on a surface: maxima minus saddles plus minima equals the Euler characteristic. The drop from a summit to its highest pass toward higher ground is a mountain’s topographic prominence. A map kept up to stretching is a topological map.

Who said it first

Contour lines were drawn on sea charts in the 1500s and 1700s and on land maps from the 1770s; Charles Hutton used them to compute the mass of Schiehallion in 1774. Arthur Cayley in 1859 and James Clerk Maxwell in 1870 counted the peaks, pits and passes of a landscape. Marston Morse built the general theory in the 1920s. Harry Beck drew the first stretched subway map, of London, in 1931.

Wrong moves, worked

“The map shows 3 peaks and 1 pass on the island, so 3 − 1 + 0 = 2, and the count is wrong.” Not allowed: the count is a fact about the land; the map has missed a pass, or drawn a peak that is a shoulder. Between two peaks of one island there is always a pass, however low.
