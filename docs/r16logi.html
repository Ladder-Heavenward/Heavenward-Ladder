16·logi · logistics

How Much Is in the Tank Right Now?

How much is in the tank right now? A town's water sits in a tank on a hill. A pump fills it from the wells, and the town draws it down through every tap. Nobody stands on the roof with a ruler. The meters measure how fast water goes in and how fast it goes out, and the level has to be worked out from those. The question matters most at the worst moment. On a hot evening the town is watering its gardens, and a fire may start. Then the tank has to hold enough, and the operator has to know it will. What makes it hard is that the two rates change through the day, and the lowest level does not come when the town draws hardest. This page reads the level as the start plus the area under what goes in less what comes out. It finds when the tank is lowest, how full it must start so that it never runs dry, and the steady pumping that keeps one day from draining the next.

The level now is the level at the start plus the area under the inflow less the outflow since. The tank is lowest where the outflow falls back to the inflow, or at an end. It never runs dry exactly when it starts with at least its deepest dip, the most that outflow ever gets ahead of inflow. It must hold the swing between its highest and lowest, plus what is kept back for fires. And a pump slower than the day's average outflow loses ground every day, however full the tank started.

16logi1The Level from the Rates

From here on, on this page, V(t) is the water in the tank, in cubic metres, t hours after midnight. in(t) and out(t) are the rates in from the pump and out to the town, in cubic metres an hour. The tank has straight sides and a floor of 400 m², 20 m by 20 m, so the depth of water is V/400 metres.

Tank-level technique: the water now is the water at the start plus the area under in less out since. If in and out are continuous on [0, T], then V(T) = V(0) + ∫₀ᵀ (in(t) − out(t)) dt. It is 16·then's net-change, with Q = V.

A model summer day for a coastal town of about 8000 people. The town draws out(t) = 40 + t(24 − t)/2: 40 m³/h at midnight, rising to 112 at noon as the gardens are watered. Over the day that is 2112 m³, about 260 litres a person. The pump runs steadily at 88 m³/h.

16logi3The Least Start, and the Size of the Tank

Never-dry technique: a tank never runs dry if and only if it starts with at least its deepest dip. Precisely: let in and out be integrable on [0, T], and let D be the greatest value of ∫₀ᵗ (out − in) for t in [0, T]. Then V(t) ≥ 0 for every t in [0, T] exactly when V(0) ≥ D.

G(t) = \textstyle\int_0^t (\text{out} - \text{in}) \text{ is continuous on } [0, T] @@ area-never-jumps (16a)
D = G(t^*) \text{ for some } t^* \text{ in } [0, T],\ \ D \ge G(0) = 0 @@ highest-and-lowest (12c)
V(t) = V(0) - G(t) @@ the tank-level technique; change-for-any-integrable (16c) if the rates jump
V(0) \ge D \Rightarrow V(t) \ge V(0) - D \ge 0 \text{ for every } t @@ G(t) ≤ D
V(0) \lt D \Rightarrow V(t^*) = V(0) - D \lt 0 @@ dry at t*
\htmlClass{new}{V \ge 0 \text{ on } [0, T] \iff V(0) \ge D} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines together

For the model day, D = 64√3 ≈ 110.9 m³: the deepest dip comes at the evening trough. The tank must also hold the morning crest. If it is full at 5:04 in the morning, it must hold the swing, from crest to trough, 128√3 ≈ 221.7 m³.

A town keeps water back for fires as well, the stock kept back of 14·logi. A fire code may ask for 1500 US gallons a minute for two hours, about 680 m³. So this tank needs at least 680 + 221.7 ≈ 902 m³, a depth of about 2.25 m on its 400 m² floor, and the operator should never let the evening trough eat into the 680.

16logi4The Refill That Keeps It from Running Dry

Let the pump run at a steady r m³/h, and let every day draw the same. Over a day the level changes by ∫₀²⁴ (r − out) = 24(r − 88), since 88 is the day's average outflow (16g). Pump-rate technique: a pump slower than the average outflow runs any tank dry in the end. At r = 87 the tank loses 24 m³ a day. No start saves it, since the level after n days is the start less 24n. At r ≥ 88 the never-dry start of the first day keeps every day after it safe.

A delivery that comes by truck, as fuel does, is 14·logi's lead time again, with a rate that changes. A tank that only empties, called at time t with a lead time L, is not dry when the truck arrives exactly when V(t) is at least ∫ₜ^{t+L} out. The level at which to call changes with the hour.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the ledger, kept by the hour

1a's ledger, again. 16·then kept a lake's books every second. A water tank is the same books, but the operator is judged on the worst second, not the total. Over the model day the books close: the pump puts back exactly what the town took. And yet the tank swung by 221.7 m³ inside the day.

A ledger that balances at the end of the day can still go below zero in the middle of it.

That is why the never-dry technique asks for the deepest dip, not the day's total. The total says whether the tank drifts from day to day. The dip says whether it survives the evening.

A tank's level is the same at midnight every night for a week, yet it ran dry one evening. How can both be so?

Who said it first

Rippl showed how to find the storage a reservoir needs to give a steady draw: add up, month by month, the inflow less the draw, plot the running total, and read off the greatest fall from a high point to a later low point.

W. Rippl, The capacity of storage-reservoirs for water-supply, Minutes of Proceedings of the Institution of Civil Engineers (1883); paraphrase

Rippl's running total is this page's N(t), and his greatest fall is the swing. Engineers still call the running-total plot a mass curve, or Rippl diagram, and the storage that evens out a day's draw the equalizing storage.

Accepted names: the tank-level technique is the net change theorem again, or a mass balance; the deepest dip and the swing are the operating storage; what is kept back for fires is the fire reserve, sized from a required fire flow and its duration; the call level for a truck is the reorder point of 14·logi.

In 16·logi.6.4: on 14 November 2021 the Trans Mountain pipeline was shut as atmospheric-river floods and slides cut it and the highways between the Lower Mainland and the interior. British Columbia limited non-essential drivers to 30 litres a visit from 19 November to 14 December; the pipeline restarted on 5 December.
