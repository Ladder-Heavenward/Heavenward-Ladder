31l · technique

Correlation and Regression

Two lists of numbers that rise together, like the July temperature in Sacramento and the sales of ice cream there, give a correlation and a fitted line. Both are easy to compute and easy to misread. Seen with the rung’s geometry they are one angle. Take each list away from its own average, and treat what is left as an arrow with one place for each day. The correlation is the cosine of the angle between the two arrows. The fitted line is a shadow of one on the other, and there are two shadows, one each way, which is why the line that predicts sales from heat is not the line that predicts heat from sales. A third list that both arrows lean towards, such as the number of people at the beach, can make the angle small with no link between the first two at all. Take its shadow out of both, and the angle that is left is the honest one.

The correlation is the cosine of the angle between the two lists of deviations. Each fitted line is a shadow of one list on the other, and the two slopes multiply to the correlation squared. A third list’s shadow, taken out of both, leaves the angle between what it does not explain.

31l1A Correlation Is a Cosine

Take paired data and the deviation lists u = (x₁ − x̄, …, xₙ − x̄) and v = (y₁ − ȳ, …, yₙ − ȳ), arrows of lists of n numbers. 8·stat’s correlation was r = u · v/(|u| |v|), and 8·stat bounded it by the dot bound. 31a4 gives the bound its meaning.

Correlation-angle technique: r is the cosine of the angle between the deviation arrows u and v, and r² is the share of the spread of y that the best line accounts for. The first part is 31a4’s angle with the dot. The second is 13·stat’s residuals technique read as 31b4’s square-sum: the shadow of v on u has squared size r²|v|², and the miss carries the rest.

u = (-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.5),\ \ v = (-0.5, -1.5, 1.5, 0.5) @@ the deviations of 8·stat
u\cdot v = 3,\ \ |u|^2 = |v|^2 = 5 @@ the dot
r = \tfrac{3}{5} = 0.6,\ \ \theta = \arccos 0.6 \approx 53.1^\circ @@ the correlation-angle technique
\htmlClass{new}{r^2 = 0.36} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line takes 36% of the spread of y, and the miss keeps 64%

So r = 0 means right angles, and r = ±1 means one arrow lies along the other. A correlation of 0.6 is an angle of 53°, which is far from lying along: most of v is at right angles to u.

31l2Two Shadows, Two Lines

The line of y on x is the shadow of v on u: its slope is b = (u · v)/|u|² = r σ_y/σₓ. The line of x on y is the shadow of u on v, with slope b′ = r σₓ/σ_y, read as how much x changes for one unit of y.

Two-lines technique: the two slopes multiply to r², so the two lines are the same line exactly when r = ±1; in standard units, where each list is measured in its own spread widths from its own average, the line of y on x predicts r times the units of x.

b = \tfrac{u\cdot v}{|u|^2},\ \ b' = \tfrac{u\cdot v}{|v|^2} @@ two shadows (31b1)
bb' = \tfrac{(u\cdot v)^2}{|u|^2|v|^2} = r^2 \le 1 @@ the product bound (31a3)
\text{the line of } x \text{ on } y, \text{ drawn in the } (x, y) \text{ plane, has slope } 1/b' @@ x = b′y read the other way
\htmlClass{new}{b = 1/b' \iff bb' = 1 \iff r = \pm 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ otherwise the line of x on y is steeper
  
r = 0.5 \text{ between a first and a second test score} @@ a model class
\text{first score } 2 \text{ widths above average} @@ standard units
\htmlClass{new}{\text{predicted second score: } 0.5 \times 2 = 1 \text{ width above}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two-lines technique; and a second score 2 widths up predicts a first score 1 width up

Each line pulls towards the middle, both ways at once. Nothing has happened to the students. The pull is the angle between two arrows, which is not 0.

31l3What the Line Does Not Say

A small angle between u and v can come from a third list w that both lean towards: heat drives ice cream sales and beach crowds, and the crowds drive drownings. To see what is left, take the shadow of w out of both, and measure the angle between the misses.

Take-out technique: the correlation of x and y with w taken out is the cosine of the angle between the misses of u and v from their shadows on the deviation arrow of w, and it equals (r_xy − r_xw r_yw)/√((1 − r_xw²)(1 − r_yw²)).

|u| = |v| = |w| = 1 \text{ first} @@ stretch each deviation arrow; correlations do not change
\tilde u = u - r_{xw}w,\ \ \tilde v = v - r_{yw}w @@ the closest-point technique: u · w = r_xw
\tilde u\cdot\tilde v = r_{xy} - r_{xw}r_{yw} - r_{yw}r_{xw} + r_{xw}r_{yw} = r_{xy} - r_{xw}r_{yw} @@ straight in each slot; w · w = 1
|\tilde u|^2 = 1 - r_{xw}^2,\ \ |\tilde v|^2 = 1 - r_{yw}^2 @@ the square-sum technique
\htmlClass{new}{r_{xy\cdot w} = \tfrac{r_{xy} - r_{xw}r_{yw}}{\sqrt{(1 - r_{xw}^2)(1 - r_{yw}^2)}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cosine of the misses’ angle

With r_xy = 0.6 between sales and drownings, r_xw = 0.8 between sales and heat, and r_yw = 0.75 between drownings and heat, the take-out correlation is (0.6 − 0.6)/… = 0. All of the link went through heat. Taking out a list that was measured is a technique. Taking out one that was not measured is impossible, and a line through data can never show that no such list exists.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

30q reported an average with its width. A correlation from a sample is an estimate too, and it wobbles. For a bell-shaped cloud of n pairs, its width is about (1 − r²)/√n.

A correlation from a sample is the middle of a cloud and is reported with its width, about (1 − r²)/√n.

With 30 pairs and r = 0.6, the width is about 0.12, so the honest report is 0.6 give or take 0.12: an angle somewhere between about 43° and 61°. With 100 pairs and r = 0.5, it is 0.5 give or take 0.075.

A study of 25 streams in the Willamette Valley finds r = 0.3 between shade and trout numbers. Is that angle clearly short of a right angle? What width would you report?

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

r is Pearson’s correlation coefficient, here read as the cosine of the angle between centred data vectors. The share r² is the coefficient of determination. The line of y on x and the line of x on y are the two regression lines; the pull to the middle is regression to the mean. The take-out correlation is the partial correlation, and a third list that drives both is a confounder (or lurking variable). The width (1 − r²)/√n is the large-sample standard error of r for bivariate normal data.

Who said it first

Francis Galton found the pull to the middle in the heights of parents and children in 1886, and named correlation in 1888. Karl Pearson gave the formula for r in 1896, and George Udny Yule the partial correlation in 1897. Ronald Fisher found the exact spread of r for bell-shaped data in 1915.

A proof that is not allowed

“Taller parents have children closer to the average, so in a few generations everyone will be the same height.” Not allowed: the pull runs both ways, children of the tallest children have parents closer to the average too, and the spread of the whole list stays put. The angle says how two lists lean, not that either is shrinking.
