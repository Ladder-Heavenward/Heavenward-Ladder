Arrows Matter More Than Things

Every floor of the ladder built a kind of thing and then the maps between things of that kind: sets and functions, groups and the maps that keep products, spaces and continuous maps, spaces of vectors and linear maps, fields and the maps of rung forty three. Each time, the same few facts came back. Maps run in a row, brackets do not matter, and there is a map that does nothing. Each time, the most useful thing to know about a construction was not what it was made of but how maps went in and out of it: a pair is whatever has a first and a second part, a free group is whatever a map out of it is fixed by the letters of, a glued space is whatever a map out of it is a pair of maps that agree. This rung takes that seriously. It writes down the common facts once, as a category, and maps between categories, and maps between those. Then it pins things down by their arrows alone: products and sums, meeting places and gluings, the free and the forgetful, tensor products. Each is fixed up to exactly one matching, and the rung proves that this is all a thing is: two things with matching arrows into them are matched. The last steps work such descriptions by hand, and read proofs as programs.

A category is things and arrows that run in a row. Maps of categories carry matched things to matched things, and natural maps between them are the ones made without choosing. A universal property pins a thing down up to exactly one matching, and a thing is known by the arrows into it.

What it means to say it

To say that arrows matter more than things is to say that, in a category, everything that can be proved about a thing from its arrows is kept by every matching, that the useful constructions are described by their arrows alone, and that such a description fixes the thing up to exactly one matching. It does not say the things are not there. It says that what a thing is made of is the business of one build, and that what it does is the business of every build.

A thing of a category is pinned down, up to exactly one matching, by the arrows into it; so every construction given by a universal property is unique in the one sense that matters, and every statement made with arrows is carried across a matching. The rung proves it, and the few steps held in trust say so where they stand.

Where it comes from

Groups and their maps, spaces and their maps, were in use for a century before anyone named the pattern. In 1945 two mathematicians wrote down categories, maps of categories and natural maps, to say exactly what “natural” meant in a paper on topology. Universal properties were set out around 1948 to 1950, and the duality of products and sums of groups was noticed in 1950. The fact that a thing is known by its arrows was told on a railway platform in Paris in 1954. Adjunctions were defined in 1958. From 1960 algebraic geometry was rewritten in the language, and in 1964 a categorical foundation for the whole of mathematics was proposed, in place of rung 2’s sets. In 1969 a note showed that typed programs and proofs are one thing, and since 2009 a foundation in which matched things are equal has been built. The names are in the cellars of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves eight things.

First, a category is things, arrows between them, running in a row with brackets free, and a do-nothing arrow at each thing. A group is a category with one thing, and an order is a category with at most one arrow between two things. Two things are matched when an arrow between them can be undone, and which arrows the category has decides which things are matched.

Second, a map of categories sends things to things and arrows to arrows, keeping the two rules, and carries matched things to matched things. The loop group of a space is one, which is why loops tell the circle from a disc; forgetting and freeing are two more, and readings of vectors turn arrows round.

Third, a natural map between two maps of categories is a family of arrows that closes every square. A space of finite dimension is matched naturally with its readings of readings, and matched with its readings only by a choice.

Fourth, a product is a thing with an arrow to each of two things through which every rival runs in exactly one way, and a sum is the same with arrows turned round. Any two products, or sums, are matched by exactly one arrow that agrees.

Fifth, limits and colimits are products and sums for any picture: a sift is a limit, classes are a colimit, and gluing spaces is a colimit, so a map out of a glued space is a pair of maps that agree.

Sixth, an adjunction matches the arrows out of F(A) with the arrows into U(B), naturally. Freeing is left of forgetting, pairs are left of functions, and between two orders an adjunction is a fixing correspondence like rung 46’s; a left map carries sums to sums.

Seventh, the natural maps from the arrows into A to any map F are matched with F(A), and so two things with naturally matched arrows into them are matched: a thing is known by its arrows.

Eighth, the tensor product of two spaces is pinned down by the maps straight in each slot; its dimension is the product of theirs, most of its members are sums of several products, and over a ring some tensor products are nothing.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 47i checks universal properties in three places where a first guess fails: the dividing order, groups, and fields, where some pairs have no product at all. 47j gives programs types and finds that typed programs are proofs, and that cutting a detour from a proof is running its program. The newsflash returns to rung 0’s sameness and answers rung 3’s question of which set is three. The computing annex reads types as things and programs as arrows, and finds that a program written for every type is a natural map, fixed almost entirely by its label; a database join is a limit. The research fronts ask when two things are truly the same, in a foundation where matched means equal, and whether mathematics is one theory, in the programme of 1967 whose geometric form was proved in 2024.

What you can do because you know it

You can see the same proof in sets, groups, spaces and spaces of vectors and do it once. You can pin a construction down by what maps in and out of it, prove that it is unique in the only sense it can be, and check by counting arrows when a guess is not the thing it claims to be. You can tell a natural matching from one that needed a choice, read a free construction as left of a forgetful one, and read a proof as a program.

And you can say exactly what this does not show: not that the things do not matter, but that nothing a statement made with arrows says can tell two matched things apart.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down in the lessons; 47·now’s univalence axiom is laid down on that page alone. The rung spends rung 2’s sets, pairs and classes, rung 9’s functions, rung 19’s groups, rung 27’s spaces of vectors, rung 34’s spaces and loops, rung 35’s sizes, and the fields, rings and correspondence of rungs 43 to 47. The things of a category such as all sets are described, never gathered into one set. Four things are held in trust: that typed programs all stop (47j), that the gluing rule of 34i carries gluing to gluing (47e), that a program written for every type is carried along by every program (47·comp), and on the research fronts the circle’s full computation and the 2024 proof. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say arrows matter more than things, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
