39b · proof

The wave that is only 1 or 0

Clip two probes to a wire and an oscilloscope draws what the wire is doing: a line that rises and falls as time goes by. That line is a function, one height for each moment. The plainest such line is a turning, the shadow of a point going round the circle, and the last step read its two extreme shares as true and false. This step asks for less. Keep only whether the shadow is on the near side or the far side, and write one for the near side and zero for the far. The result is a wave that is only ever one or zero: a square wave, the beat of every clock in every chip. The rung on sums of turnings already knows its recipe, so the recipe is borrowed, not worked again. Then comes the surprise: the very first turning of that recipe, alone, read against the halfway mark, gives back every one and every zero. And then the honest part. No wire is ever exactly at one level. A wire is read against a threshold, and noise sometimes carries it across. How often can be said in numbers, and that is what makes reading by threshold both inexact and rigorous.

A trace is a function of time. The sign of a turning, read as one or zero, is the square wave, and its recipe is the square recipe slid a quarter turn. Read against the halfway mark, the first turning alone gives back every bit. Noise misreads a level with a chance the bell curve gives.

39b1A trace

A wire’s voltage at each moment t is one number v(t): a function of time in 9a’s sense, which the oscilloscope draws with time across and voltage up. The turning v(t) = V cos(2πft) is the across share of a point that goes round the circle f times a second (21c1, with θ = 2πft); V is its size and f its frequency, in turns a second, named the hertz, Hz. The socket in a Seattle kitchen gives 60 Hz; a chip’s clock gives billions.

A trace is a function, so everything rung 32 built for functions applies to it. Sliding it later by s multiplies its turnings’ coefficients by a turning of their own (32h2), and stretching time keeps the coefficients. So a recipe found for one size, one frequency and one start serves every other.

39b2The sign of a turning, read as 1 or 0

Let s(θ) be 1 when cos θ > 0 and 0 when cos θ < 0, and ½ at the two quarter turns where cos θ = 0, as 32h1’s halfway technique sets a jump. s is 1 on the near half of the circle and 0 on the far half: a square wave of 1s and 0s, one 1 and one 0 a turn.

Sign-wave technique: the sign of cos θ, read as 1 or 0, is ½ + (2/π)(cos θ − cos 3θ/3 + cos 5θ/5 − ⋯): one half, and the odd speeds alone, each over its speed, with signs taking turns.

q(x) = 1 \text{ on } (0, \pi),\ -1 \text{ on } (-\pi, 0):\ \ s(\theta) = \tfrac12\big(1 + q(\theta + \tfrac\pi2)\big) @@ θ + π/2 is in (0, π) exactly when cos θ > 0 (21c); 39a’s marks from ±1
q(x) = \tfrac4\pi\textstyle\sum_{n\ \mathrm{odd}} \tfrac{\sin nx}{n} @@ the square-recipe technique (32h1)
\sin\big(n(\theta + \tfrac\pi2)\big) = \sin\tfrac{n\pi}{2}\,\cos n\theta \text{ for odd } n @@ the sum formula (21d); cos(nπ/2) = 0 for odd n
\sin\tfrac{n\pi}{2} = 1, -1, 1, \ldots \text{ for } n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots @@ a quarter, three quarters, five quarters of a turn
\htmlClass{new}{s(\theta) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac2\pi\big(\cos\theta - \tfrac{\cos 3\theta}{3} + \tfrac{\cos 5\theta}{5} - \cdots\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ halve, and add ½

Nothing in this proof is new except the reading. The ½ is the wave’s average, its 0th turning: on for half the time. The rest is rung 32’s square, slid a quarter turn so that it lines up with the cosine, and squeezed from ±1 into {0, 1}.

39b3The first turning knows every bit

Keep only the first two terms: u(θ) = ½ + (2/π) cos θ. It is a smooth turning, swinging from ½ − 2/π ≈ −0.137 to ½ + 2/π ≈ 1.137. It is nothing like a square. Read it against the halfway mark ½ all the same.

First-turning technique: read against the threshold ½, the first turning of the sign wave alone gives back every 1 and every 0 of the wave.

u(\theta) > \tfrac12 \iff \tfrac2\pi\cos\theta > 0 @@ take ½ from both sides
\iff \cos\theta > 0 @@ 2/π is above 0: multiplying keeps the order (5g)
\iff s(\theta) = 1 @@ the reading of 39b2
\htmlClass{new}{u > \tfrac12 \iff s = 1,\ \ u < \tfrac12 \iff s = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines with <

So a wire that passes only the lowest frequency of a square wave still carries all its bits, provided the reader looks only at which side of the threshold the trace is on. The higher turnings make the edges sharp. They tell the reader when a change happened more exactly, and nothing about which bit it was. That is the first sign of the split 39c makes: the shape of a wave and the message on it are different things.

39b4A level read against a threshold

A real wire does not sit at 0 V or 3.3 V. It sits near one of them, plus noise: the warmth of the wire, the motor in the next room, the wire beside it. Model the noise as 30j’s bell, centred at 0 with spread σ. A level is read as 1 when it is above the threshold and as 0 when below, and the gap from the level to the threshold is the margin d.

Threshold-reading technique: a level with margin d, under bell noise of spread σ, is misread with the chance that the bell lies more than d/σ spreads to one side.

v = 0.6 + Z \cdot 0.1 \text{ V},\ \ Z \text{ a standard bell},\ \ \text{threshold } 1.2 \text{ V} @@ a 0 sent at 0.6 V, spread σ = 0.1 V; the margin is d = 0.6 V
\text{misread} \iff v > 1.2 \iff Z > 6 @@ subtract 0.6, divide by 0.1
P(Z > 6) \approx 9.9 \cdot 10^{-10} @@ the bell’s tail (30j), worked by machine
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a billion bits a second: about one misread a second}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average count adds (30g1): 10⁹ × 9.9 · 10⁻¹⁰
P(Z > 7) \approx 1.3 \cdot 10^{-12}:\ \ \text{one misread in about 13 minutes} @@ one more spread of margin: a factor of nearly 800
Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

Every reading of a wire is a guess under the large case. The voltage is a crowd of causes no one tracks, and the reader keeps only one fact about it: which side of the threshold it fell on.

The reading is wrong at a rate, and the rate is known.

The bell does not make the wire exact. It makes the error exact: one in a billion at six spreads, one in a trillion near seven. Engineers buy margin with power, and buy it by the spread.

A rule of thumb on an Oregon coast radio link says a dry day gives twice the margin of a stormy one. What would you measure to turn that rule into a misread rate?

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A trace on an oscilloscope is a waveform; the square wave of 1s and 0s with equal time on and off has a duty cycle of 50%. Its recipe is its Fourier series. Reading a level against a threshold is slicing, or decision, and the chance of a misread is the bit error rate; d/σ is tied to the Q-factor of a link. The tail of the bell beyond z is Q(z), the complementary error function halved: Q(z) = ½ erfc(z/√2).

Who said it first

Ferdinand Braun built the first cathode-ray oscilloscope in 1897. The two-valued reading of a relay’s contacts as logic is Claude Shannon’s, in his master’s thesis of 1937. The contest between computing with smooth levels and with two values ran from Vannevar Bush’s differential analyser (1931) to the digital machines of the late 1940s; analog computers stayed in use for control and simulation into the 1970s, and a revival for low-power learning machines is under way.

A proof that is not allowed

“The first turning reads back every bit. So a wire that passes only the first turning carries everything the square wave carries.” Not allowed as stated: it carries every bit of this one wave, whose changes come at a fixed beat. A wave whose changes come at other times needs other frequencies to place them, and 39c counts how many changes a wire can carry.
