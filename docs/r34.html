Rung 34 · theory

Nearness survives without distance

Since rung 8 the ladder has said what it means for numbers to come close: a list settles when its distance to a point falls below every bound, and a function is continuous when close inputs give close outputs. Every one of those definitions measured a distance. This rung asks what is left when the distance is taken away. It starts by writing the old definitions for any space with a distance, and finds that each one can be said using only which sets are open. Then it keeps the open sets and throws the distance away. What survives is surprising: continuity, being in one piece, being finitely covered, the intermediate values of rung 12 and the highest values of rung 15, all of it, now true of spaces no ruler could measure. A stretching that matches open sets with open sets keeps every one of these, so they are facts about shape. Then the rung counts shape. A surface has a characteristic, a number no cutting changes, and every closed surface is on one short list. A loop that can be slid but not broken counts the holes it goes round, and that count proves that every continuous map of a disc into itself keeps a point. The techniques that follow do it all by hand, and the annexes find it in contour maps, in light, in a cell’s DNA, and in the shape of space.

Continuity needs only which sets are open, so being in one piece, being finitely covered and keeping points apart survive any stretching that matches open sets. Surfaces fall into one list, told apart by a count. And loops, slid without breaking, count holes: the circle’s loops are the whole numbers, and so a map of the disc to itself keeps a point.

What it means to say it

To say that nearness survives without distance is to say that every statement the ladder has made about coming close, settling, continuity and its consequences, can be made using only the family of open sets, and stays true in any space that has such a family, measured or not. A distance makes open sets, and many different distances make the same ones. What depends only on the open sets does not see the difference.

Continuity needs only which sets are open; shape survives stretching, and loops can count holes. The rung proves it, and with it a new kind of fact: a count that no stretching can change.

Where it comes from

The count V − E + F for the faces of a solid was noticed in the middle of the eighteenth century, and the arguments over which solids it holds for ran through the next. A puzzle about crossing seven bridges once each was settled by a count of the same kind in 1736. Sets of points with no distance in sight were studied from the 1870s, when the endless sums of rung 32 forced the question of which sets of points a series can fail on. Spaces given by their neighbourhoods alone were laid down in 1914, and by their open sets in the 1920s. Loops that slide were introduced in 1895, with the question of 1904 that 34·now follows to its answer a century later. Surfaces were listed by the 1860s and proved complete in 1907 and again in the 1920s, and the point that stays put was proved in 1910–12. The names belong to the steps, and each step’s basement gives them.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things, each from the ones before.

First, in a space with a distance every ball is open, by the ball-open technique, and a map is continuous exactly when it pulls open sets back to open sets, by the pull-back technique. A part is closed exactly when it keeps its limits, by the keeps-limits technique.

Second, the open sets alone are enough. Two distances can make the same family, by the same-opens technique; continuity chains, by the pull-back-chain technique; and a space that keeps points apart lets a list settle at one point at most, by the one-limit-apart technique. Maps out of glued spaces are tested upstairs, by the glued-map technique.

Third, being in one piece and being finitely covered are statements about open sets. A part of the line is in one piece exactly when it is a stretch, by the one-piece-is-a-stretch technique, and continuous maps keep pieces whole, by the image-one-piece technique: rung 12’s intermediate values. A part of the line is finitely covered exactly when it is closed and bounded, by the closed-and-bounded technique, and continuous maps keep covers finite, by the image-covered technique: rung 15’s highest values. That every product of finitely covered spaces is finitely covered is as strong as choice, by the products-choose technique.

Fourth, a same-shape map keeps all of these, by the kept-by-shape technique, so they are facts about the shape. Taking out a point and counting pieces tells shapes apart, by the point-out technique.

Fifth, every closed surface is the sphere, a sum of tori, or a sum of cross-caps, by the surface-list technique, told apart by the characteristic of the word-count technique.

Sixth, the loops at a point, slid without breaking, form a group, by the loop-group technique, and the circle’s is the whole numbers, by the circle-loops technique. So no map presses the disc onto its rim, and every map of the disc to itself keeps a point, by the kept-point technique. The loops of a glued space are named by letters and relations, by the presented-map technique and the turn-about-count technique.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Rung 34 carries three green boxes. 34e carries connections, not families: a shape is known by how its points connect, not by what it is made of or how far apart its points are. 34·cb18 carries the cloud: a contour map is rough, and still its count of peaks less passes plus pits is exact, because every feature too small for the map comes in a cancelling pair. 34·bio carries two sizes of truth: every ring of DNA has a whole-number linking count, and the tube of rings averages a count that no ring can have.

What follows

With those six, the techniques compute. 34j tests open sets and continuity by hand, at the edges where the definitions bite. 34k sews the torus, the one-sided bottle and the cross-cap from a square, and reduces their words. 34l counts the roots of a polynomial inside a loop by its winding. 34m tells knots apart by counts no move changes, and shows the overhand knot is not its mirror. The annexes take the counts into contour maps of an island, into the flux of a charge and the waves of light, into the linking of a cell’s DNA and the enzymes that cut it, and into the question whether loops decide the shape of a closed three-dimensional space. Rung 35 asks whether infinities come in sizes, and the families of open sets of this rung are among the first sets it must count.

What you can do because you know it

You can tell when a definition depends only on the open sets, and carry it into any space that has them. You can prove a part of a space in one piece, or finitely covered, and use it to find an intermediate value or a highest value without a formula. You can tell two shapes apart by a count, list every closed surface from its word, count the windings of a loop and the roots inside it, and prove that a continuous map of a disc to itself keeps a point.

From here on, a count that stretching cannot change is a fact about the shape, and the first thing to ask of any space is what its open sets decide.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 6’s axiom of choice, at 34d, where every product of finitely covered spaces is finitely covered only if choice is true, and at 34a, where a limit is kept by picking a point from each of countably many balls. It spends rung 8’s least upper bound, rung 12’s intermediate values, rung 15’s highest values, rung 19’s groups and rung 26’s complex slopes. Held in trust, each where it is used: that a closed curve in the plane has an inside and an outside; that every surface can be cut into triangles, and any two cuttings have a common finer one; the details of lifting slides; that the sphere’s loops slide to a point; the gluing rule for loops; that the far-point map of the sphere does not slide to the do-nothing map; and that the twist, the poke and the slide join every two drawings of a knot. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say nearness survives without distance, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
