Can a Battery Be Safe, Cheap and Last for Decades?

Can a battery be safe, cheap and last for decades? A phone battery holds the energy of a small firecracker, a car’s holds as much as a few hundred kilograms of explosive, and both are asked to give it back slowly, a hundred thousand times, for ten or twenty years, without ever giving it back all at once. The lead cells under a car bonnet have done a modest version of this since the 1860s. The lithium cells in a phone, sold since 1991, hold about five times as much for their weight, and in 2013 and 2016 they grounded a fleet of airliners and recalled millions of phones. Three wishes pull against each other. More energy for the weight means more reactive metals and higher voltages, and higher voltages break down any liquid that carries the charge, while the liquids that survive them burn. Cheaper means common metals, and the common ones are heavy or weak. Longer life means slower side reactions, and every side reaction a cell can have it eventually has. This page weighs a cell by its reaction, finds the ceiling each chemistry cannot pass, finds why water is ruled out and what replaced it, finds the temperature past which a cell cannot cool itself, times how long a fast charge takes to grow a spike of metal, and counts how a cell fades over decades. When cells with a solid in place of the liquid will be sold in cars is argued every year.

A cell’s change moving n moles of electrons through E volts gives nFE, so the starting stuff of mass M caps the energy at nFE/M: 170 Wh/kg for lead and acid, about 370 for the lithium cell as used, 2600 for lithium and sulfur. Water splits at 1.23 V, so cells above it carry their charge in carbonate liquids, which burn. Heat made as e^(−E/RT) against heat lost in proportion to the warming has a last resting temperature, lost at a fold where the warming is RT²/E, a few kelvins. At a current i the salt at a metal face runs out after πDc₀²/(4J²), hours at a slow charge and minutes at a fast one. A film that grows as fast as lithium can cross it thickens as √t, so a cell fades as the square root of its age. This i is not 9·cb8's i.

inf40.1A Ceiling Set by the Reaction

25·cb7 found that a cell moving n moles of electrons through E volts hands out nFE joules, F = 96 485 coulombs per mole. Every gram of the starting stuff must be carried, so the change itself sets a ceiling on the energy per kilogram, before any case, wire or spare liquid is added.

Mass-cost technique: a cell whose change moves n moles of electrons through E volts, and whose starting stuff for that change weighs M kilograms, holds at most nFE/M joules per kilogram. It is the energy technique divided by the mass the change uses up; 1 Wh is 3600 J.

\mathrm{Pb} + \mathrm{PbO_2} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2SO_4} \to 2\,\mathrm{PbSO_4} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} @@ lead gives two electrons each, lead in PbO₂ takes two (6·chem)
E = 1.685 - (-0.356) = 2.041 \text{ V} @@ the cell technique, with the two measured pulls of lead in acid
nFE = 2 \times 96\,485 \times 2.041 = 393.9 \text{ kJ} @@ the energy technique, n = 2
M = 207.2 + 239.2 + 2 \times 98.08 = 642.6 \text{ g} @@ the starting stuff, by 5·chem’s molar masses
\htmlClass{new}{393.9/0.6426 = 613 \text{ kJ/kg} = 170 \text{ Wh/kg}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ceiling

Real lead batteries give 30 to 40 Wh/kg, a fifth of the ceiling. The acid must stay dilute, the plates must keep a lead frame that never reacts, and only part of each plate can be reached before its surface clogs with sulfate. Every chemistry pays such a fee; the ceiling says which ones are worth paying it for.

The cell’s change is one transition of ∞15.1’s nets, and the electrons it moves are a weight that every firing keeps. That is why the energy a kilogram can hold is fixed by the balanced change alone. The plan is the transition; where in the cell the atoms sit, and the order in which they react, are let go.

inf40.2Per Gram of the Metal

Split the ceiling into a charge and a voltage. 25·cb7 counted It/(nF) moles of metal for a charge It. Read backwards: each gram of a stuff that gives n electrons per unit of molar mass M grams holds a fixed charge, and battery makers count it in milliamp hours, 3.6 coulombs each.

Per-gram technique: a stuff of molar mass M grams that takes or gives n electrons per unit holds 26 801 n/M milliamp hours per gram.

\tfrac{96\,485 \text{ C}}{3.6 \text{ C per mAh}} = 26\,801 \text{ mAh per mole of electrons} @@ the counting technique
\text{lithium: } 26\,801/6.94 = 3862,\ \ \text{sodium: } 26\,801/22.99 = 1166 @@ one electron each
\text{graphite holding lithium as } \mathrm{LiC_6}:\ 26\,801/72.07 = 372 @@ per gram of carbon
\text{sulfur, taking two as } \mathrm{Li_2S}:\ 2 \times 26\,801/32.06 = 1672 @@ n = 2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{lithium metal holds } 10.4 \text{ times what graphite does, per gram}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 3862/372

The phone’s cell stores lithium in graphite on one side and in cobalt oxide on the other, at about 3.7 V. Only half the lithium may leave the oxide before its layers buckle, so the change is ½LiC₆ + Li₀.₅CoO₂ → 3C + LiCoO₂, and the mass-cost technique gives about 370 Wh/kg. Good cells reach 250 to 300. Swap graphite for lithium metal and sulfur for the oxide, at about 2.2 V, and the ceiling is 2600 Wh/kg. Petrol holds about 12 800 Wh/kg, and an engine turns about a third of it into motion.

Sodium sits a column below lithium, 0.33 V weaker at −2.71 V and three times heavier. It is also about a thousand times more common in the earth’s crust, 2.4% against about 20 parts per million, and it does not react with aluminium, so the cheap aluminium foil can carry current on both sides of the cell where lithium needs copper on one. Sodium does not fit between graphite’s sheets; it is stored in a disordered carbon at about 300 mAh/g. Sodium cells, in cars on sale in China since early 2024, give about 140 to 175 Wh/kg.

inf40.3Why Not Water

A liquid between the two sides carries charge as ions and must not itself take or give electrons. Water is cheap, safe and carries ions well. But water can be pulled apart, into hydrogen at one side and oxygen at the other, and the energy technique says at what voltage.

Water-window technique: a cell whose two sides differ by more than 1.23 V can split the water of its liquid, so a cell in water above 1.23 V lives only as long as its sides are slow at making gas.

\mathrm{H_2O} \to \mathrm{H_2} + \tfrac12\mathrm{O_2},\ \ \Delta G^\circ = +237.1 \text{ kJ} @@ measured, at 25 °C, for liquid water
n = 2 @@ two hydrogens go from +1 to 0
E = \tfrac{237\,100}{2 \times 96\,485} @@ the energy technique, run backwards
\htmlClass{new}{E = 1.229 \text{ V}} \quad \blacksquare @@ any pair of sides further apart than this can push the change

The lead cell, at 2.04 V, lives past the window because lead is very slow at making hydrogen; overcharge it and it bubbles, which is why old car batteries were topped up with water. Lithium, at −3.04 V, takes water apart on contact. So the lithium cell carries its ions in a liquid with no water at all: lithium salt in carbonates, small molecules of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen that hold out to about 4.3 V on the high side. On the low side not even they hold. Graphite full of lithium sits below where they break, and in the first charge a skin of their broken pieces forms on the graphite and then stops ions of the liquid from reaching it, though not lithium. That skin is what lets the cell exist, and it is where most of a cell’s ageing happens (inf40.6). The carbonates are also, in plain words, a fuel: one of them catches fire from a spark at room temperature.

inf40.4The Last Resting Temperature

A warm cell makes heat by side reactions, the skin breaking down first, and loses heat to the air. 24·then found a reaction over an energy barrier E going at a pace in proportion to e^(−E/RT). Let the cell make heat at B e^(−E/RT) and lose it at h(T − T₀), the air at T₀. It rests where the two match. 24k found two resting places that meet and vanish as a knob turns; here the knob is the air’s temperature, or how well the pack is cooled.

Tangent-runaway technique: as T₀ rises, the lower resting temperature, the one that holds, is lost when the curve of heat made touches the line of heat lost, and there T − T₀ = RT²/E.

Be^{-E/RT} = h(T - T_0) @@ a resting temperature: made equals lost
\tfrac{d}{dT}Be^{-E/RT} = \tfrac{E}{RT^2}\,Be^{-E/RT} = h @@ at the touching point the slopes match too (13: the chain rule)
\tfrac{E}{RT^2}\,h(T - T_0) = h @@ put the first line into the second
\htmlClass{new}{T - T_0 = RT^2/E} \quad \blacksquare @@ past this, made beats lost at every T: the fold of 24k, with no resting place left
  
E = 130 \text{ kJ/mol},\ \ T \approx 353 \text{ K (80 °C)} @@ a barrier of the size measured for the skin breaking, held in trust
\tfrac{8.314 \times 353^2}{130\,000} = 8.0 \text{ K} @@ the tangent-runaway technique
\text{a cell of } 3.6 \text{ V} \times 3.0 \text{ Ah} = 10.8 \text{ Wh} = 38.9 \text{ kJ},\ \ 46 \text{ g},\ \text{about } 1 \text{ J/(g K)} @@ the common cylinder cell, 18 mm across
\htmlClass{new}{38\,900/46 \approx 850 \text{ K}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the rise if its stored energy all turned to heat, before the liquid burns

So a cell sitting only some eight kelvins above its last resting temperature has nowhere to rest, and its own stored energy is enough to take it past 600 °C. In a pack the cell next to it warms in turn. 17·chem counted a chain that grows when each event leads on average to more than one: a pack is designed so a failing cell sets off fewer than one neighbour, by gaps, barriers and cooling plates, and a pack that fails that test burns end to end.

inf40.5When the Salt Runs Dry

Charging puts lithium back onto the negative side. If that side is lithium metal, the 10.4 times of inf40.2, the metal should grow as a flat layer. It often grows as spikes instead, which can cross to the other side and short the cell. One cause can be timed. The current draws lithium ions out of the liquid at the face, and fresh salt arrives from further off only by spreading, by 33a’s rule c_t = Dc_xx with salt in place of heat. Write J for the moles of salt taken from each square metre of face each second, J = i(1 − t₊)/F, where t₊ is the share of the current the lithium ions carry themselves.

Run-dry technique: a steady draw J at a flat face of a liquid with salt c₀ and spreading D brings the salt at the face to c₀ − 2J√(t/(πD)), so the face runs dry at t = πDc₀²/(4J²).

\text{a draw } J\,ds \text{ at time } s \text{ spreads as a bell, its spread } 2D(t - s) @@ 33b’s spreading-bell technique
\text{at the face: } -\tfrac{2J\,ds}{\sqrt{4\pi D(t - s)}} @@ the face is a wall, so the bell is folded back: twice the height of a free bell
c(0, t) = c_0 - \tfrac{2J}{\sqrt{4\pi D}}\int_0^t \tfrac{ds}{\sqrt{t - s}} = c_0 - \tfrac{2J}{\sqrt{4\pi D}}\cdot 2\sqrt t @@ the draws add (33a: the rule is straight); 16’s undoing
= c_0 - 2J\sqrt{t/(\pi D)} @@ tidy
\htmlClass{new}{c(0, t) = 0 \iff t = \pi D c_0^2/(4J^2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the face runs dry
  
D = 3 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}^2/\text{s},\ \ c_0 = 1000 \text{ mol/m}^3,\ \ t_+ = 0.38 @@ a common lithium liquid, held in trust
i = 10 \text{ A/m}^2:\ \ J = 10 \times 0.62/96\,485 = 6.4 \times 10^{-5},\ \ t = 5.7 \times 10^4 \text{ s} \approx 16 \text{ h} @@ 1 mA per cm², a slow charge
\htmlClass{new}{i = 100 \text{ A/m}^2:\ \ t = 570 \text{ s} \approx 9.5 \text{ min}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ten times the current, a hundred times sooner

Once the face runs dry, lithium lands where ions still reach it, the bumps that stick out into the liquid, and a bump that gets more grows more: the spike feeds itself. Spikes also grow well before the face runs dry, so this time is an upper bound on safety, not a promise. A solid in place of the liquid was hoped to stop them by force. A rule of 2005 said a solid more than about twice as stiff against shearing as lithium, near 9 GPa, would hold a flat face. The best ceramic, at about 60 GPa, let lithium through anyway, along the joins between its grains and in hairline cracks, and often at currents below 1 mA per cm². The bulk of a good solid passes current easily: 30 μm of one that conducts 1 mS per cm loses only 3 mV at 1 mA per cm². The trouble is at the faces, where the solid must stay pressed to a metal that grows and shrinks with every charge.

inf40.6Fading as a Square Root

The skin of inf40.3 does not stop growing; it slows. The liquid’s pieces must cross the skin to reach the graphite and break, and each break uses up some of the cell’s lithium for good. A thicker skin is crossed more slowly.

Square-root-fade technique: if a skin of thickness x grows at a pace k/x, then x² = x₀² + 2kt; so once it is well past its first thickness, the lithium it has used up grows as √t.

x' = k/x @@ the pace is in proportion to how fast the pieces cross, so to 1/x
(x^2)' = 2xx' = 2k @@ the product rule
x^2 = x_0^2 + 2kt @@ 16: the slope is the constant 2k
\htmlClass{new}{x \approx \sqrt{2kt} \text{ for } 2kt \gg x_0^2,\ \text{and lithium lost} \propto x} \quad \blacksquare @@ the skin holds lithium in proportion to its thickness
  
\text{lost } = 4\% \times \sqrt{t/\text{year}} @@ a cell kept at 25 °C that loses 4% in its first year, for example
t = 4,\ 9,\ 25 \text{ years}: 8\%,\ 12\%,\ 20\% @@ the square-root-fade technique
\text{at } 45\,°\text{C}: k \times 4,\ \text{so loss} \times 2 @@ the ten-degree technique twice, and √4 = 2
\htmlClass{new}{80\% \text{ left after } 25 \text{ years at } 25\,°\text{C, after about } 6 \text{ at } 45\,°\text{C}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 8% √t = 20% at t = 6.25

That is sitting still. Use adds its own wear. Each charge swells the graphite by about a tenth, cracks the skin, and exposes fresh carbon that grows new skin. Cells of the cheaper iron phosphate kind are rated for 6000 full charges or more, sixteen years at one a day. The catch is the test: a claim of thirty years cannot be checked in a lab before the cell is sold. Makers run cells hot and fast, fit the square root and the count of charges, and read the curves forward, and whether the curves stay square roots for decades, or bend down at the end when the skin starts to choke the pores, is learned from the field, late.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞40.oq.1Can a lithium metal side survive a thousand fast charges? It would give the cell about half again the energy of today’s best, and every major maker has tried. The spikes appear at currents below the run-dry time, in liquids and in solids, and the layers proposed to stop them are each shown to work in some cells and fail in others. Nobody has a rule that says, from the materials alone, whether a face will stay flat.
∞40.oq.2When will cells with a solid in place of the liquid be sold in ordinary cars, and at what price? Makers in Japan, Korea, China and the United States have each named years, and most of the years named before 2020 have passed. Some cells now sold as solid still hold some liquid. The open part is not whether a solid cell works in a lab, it does, but whether one can be made by the million, pressed together at the faces, at a price near a liquid cell’s.
∞40.oq.3What is the skin, exactly? It forms in the first charge, a few nanometres thick, from a liquid that had a dozen kinds of molecule and an unknown trace of water, and it decides how long the cell lives. It is mixed, changes with temperature, and is damaged by the tools used to look at it. Could a skin be designed, laid down on purpose, rather than grown and hoped for?
∞40.oq.4Can the life of a cell over twenty years be told from a test of a few months? Fits of the square root and of the count of charges work until they do not, and a cell can fade gently for years and then drop quickly when its pores clog or lithium starts to plate. Since 2019 groups have trained models on the first hundred charges of many cells to forecast the rest. Whether those forecasts carry over to cells and uses they were not trained on is open.
∞40.oq.5How much storage, and of what kind, does a grid run mostly on sun and wind need? Cells of four hours now even out day and night in places like California and South Australia. A still week of winter needs days, and for that iron with air, salt water that flows through tanks, pumped water and hydrogen are all being built and argued for. The answer depends on prices, on weather over decades and on what else the grid can do, and nobody has settled it.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Cells are old and the rechargeable kind nearly as old. The first pile of metal discs and wet card was shown in 1800; the lead cell, still in every petrol car, came in 1859. For a century the lead cell was the only rechargeable cell anyone relied on, because it is cheap, it forgives abuse, and almost all of its lead is recycled. Its weakness was the ceiling of inf40.1, and in the 1970s oil crises a race for a lighter cell began.

The first lithium cell that could be recharged came from an oil company in the 1970s: lithium metal against titanium sulfide, about 2.5 V. It ran into the spikes of inf40.5 and caught fire, and a Canadian maker that sold metal lithium cells in phones had to recall them in 1989 after fires. Two moves made the cell safe enough. In 1980 a group in Oxford found that cobalt oxide could give up lithium at about 4 V, and in 1985 a group in Japan paired it with a carbon that holds lithium without any metal at all. Sony sold the first such cells in 1991. The chemistry prize of the Swedish academy went to three of the people involved in 2019.

Safety has been argued at each step since. Millions of laptop cells were recalled in 2006 after metal specks in manufacture shorted them. In January 2013 two battery fires grounded the new Boeing airliner worldwide for three months, and the fix was a steel box and a vent rather than a new chemistry. In 2016 a phone was recalled worldwide after fires traced to two different faults, in cells from two different makers. For electric cars the numbers are disputed: counts from insurers and fire services in several countries suggest they catch fire less often per car than petrol cars, while fire crews point out that a lithium fire is harder to put out and can start again hours later. Both can be true.

Cost fell faster than almost anyone forecast. Surveys of pack prices put them above $1000 per kWh in 2010 and near $115 in 2024. Part of the fall came from abandoning cobalt: the iron phosphate cathode, found in 1997, holds less, but it is cheap and much harder to set on fire, and after its key patents ran out around 2022 it became the cathode of most cars sold in China. Cobalt had an ethical cost too: most of it is mined in the Democratic Republic of Congo, part of it by hand, and reports from 2016 on documented children in the mines. Lithium has its own arguments, about the water drawn from the salt flats of Chile and Argentina and who decides about it. The price of lithium rose about tenfold between 2020 and late 2022 and then fell back by more than four fifths, which made the case for sodium first urgent and then less so.

The solid cell has been promised for a long time. A Japanese car maker spoke in 2017 of solid cells in its cars early in the 2020s, and now names 2027 to 2028. An American start-up went public in 2020 at a valuation larger than many car makers on the strength of lab cells, and has since sent sample cells to a German car maker; its critics argued its tests were too gentle, and its defenders that every new cell starts small. Chinese makers sold cars from 2024 with cells called semi-solid, holding less liquid than usual, and in 2025 a Chinese standards effort moved to stop such cells being called solid at all. Each side points to the faces of inf40.5: the optimists say the problem is engineering now, the sceptics that pressing a ceramic to a metal that breathes is a problem nobody has solved at scale.

What is argued now is less whether cells will improve than which wish to give up. A car maker wants energy for the weight; a grid wants cheapness and decades, and cares little for weight; a plane wants all three. Sodium, iron phosphate and solid cells each give up a different thing. The long view, held by some, is that the cheap and long-lived cells will win the largest market, the grid, and the light ones the smallest; others think a light enough cell would change transport so much that it is worth any price. Neither side can yet point to a cell that is safe, cheap and lasts for decades, all at once.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The mass-cost ceiling is the theoretical specific energy, and the per-gram count the theoretical specific capacity; F is the Faraday constant. The pulls of lead in acid are the standard reduction potentials of PbSO₄/Pb and PbO₂/PbSO₄. The water-window technique gives the electrochemical stability window of water; the lead cell survives past it by the overpotential for hydrogen on lead. The carbonates are ethylene, dimethyl and diethyl carbonate with the salt LiPF₆, and the skin is the solid electrolyte interphase, SEI. The tangent-runaway technique is Semenov’s criterion for thermal explosion, the e^(−E/RT) rule is Arrhenius’s, and a chain from cell to cell is thermal runaway propagation. The spikes are dendrites; the run-dry time is Sand’s time, t₊ the transference number, and the 2005 rule the Monroe–Newman criterion. The ceramic is the garnet LLZO, Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂, and other solids are sulfides such as Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂. Fading while sitting is calendar ageing and with use cycle ageing; the cheaper cathode is lithium iron phosphate, LFP, and the sodium store is hard carbon.

Who said it first

Alessandro Volta showed the pile in 1800, and Gaston Planté the lead–acid cell in 1859. M. Stanley Whittingham built the lithium–titanium disulfide cell at Exxon in the 1970s; Moli Energy recalled its lithium metal cells in 1989. John Goodenough, with Koichi Mizushima and coworkers at Oxford, found lithium cobalt oxide in 1980; Akira Yoshino at Asahi Kasei built the carbon–cobalt oxide cell in 1985, and Sony commercialised it in 1991. Whittingham, Goodenough and Yoshino shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2019. Goodenough’s group with Akshaya Padhi reported lithium iron phosphate in 1997. Nikolay Semenov gave the runaway criterion in 1928, Henry Sand the depletion time in 1901, and Charles Monroe and John Newman the stiffness criterion in 2005. The Boeing 787 was grounded in January 2013, and the Samsung Galaxy Note 7 recalled in 2016. Toyota is the car maker of 2017, QuantumScape the start-up of 2020, with Volkswagen’s PowerCo receiving its samples; CATL announced sodium cells in 2021. The 2019 forecast from early charges was by Kristen Severson, Peter Attia, William Chueh, Richard Braatz and coworkers.

A proof that is not allowed

“Lithium is the lightest metal and the strongest giver of electrons, so a lithium metal cell must be the best cell.” Not allowed: the mass-cost technique ranks the starting stuff, not the cell. A cell is judged also by the spikes it grows, the liquid it needs and the skin it ages through, and the best ceiling has so far made one of the hardest cells to build.

A resting place, in 3b’s marks

inf40.4’s “the resting temperature that holds” is 3b2’s □ with a width: necessity inside a stated range of T₀, nothing promised outside it.
