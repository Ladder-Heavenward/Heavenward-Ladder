How Far Away Is the Horizon?

How far away is the horizon? Stand on the beach and the sea ends in a sharp line. Climb a hill and the line moves away, and ships that were hidden come into view. A lookout at the masthead sees a coast long before the crew on deck. For a navigator the question is practical: from how far off will a lighthouse show at night, and how far away is the land whose top just shows? What makes it hard is that the answer comes in two pieces of very different sizes, one from the height of the eye and one from the height squared, and the second is almost always too small to matter. Dropping it is a guess, and the guess must be held to account. Then the air itself bends the line of sight, and the bending is a matter of weather. This page shows, with rung twenty-one’s tangent square to its radius and rung twenty-three’s binomial series, how far the horizon is, how wrong the short formula can be, how far off a light first shows over the sea, and how much the air adds.

The line of sight to the horizon just grazes the sea, so it is square to the earth’s radius there. That makes a right corner, and the distance comes out as a square root with two terms under it. One term is twice the earth’s radius times the height of the eye. The other is the height squared, and for any eye on earth it is tiny beside the first. Dropped, it changes the distance by a share smaller than the height over four earth radii. Two people at two heights see each other across the sum of their two horizons. Air bends the line of sight gently along the curve of the sea, as if the earth were a larger ball, and that adds a few percent, by an amount the weather sets.

23nav1The Line of Sight Grazes the Sea

Take the earth as a ball of radius R = 6371 km with its centre C, as 22·nav did. An eye E is at height h above the sea. The line of sight to the horizon just touches the sea at a point T. A line that touches a circle is square to the radius there (21q).

CT \perp ET @@ tangent square to radius (21q)
|CE|^2 = |CT|^2 + |ET|^2:\ \ (R + h)^2 = R^2 + d^2 @@ the right-triangle technique (18a), d = |ET|
d^2 = 2Rh + h^2 @@ multiply out; R² goes from both sides
\htmlClass{new}{d = \sqrt{2Rh + h^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ d > 0

Sight-line technique: from a height h above a ball of radius R, the line of sight to the horizon is √(2Rh + h²) long. A walker on the sand at Cannon Beach, eyes 1.7 m up, sees the horizon 4654 m away. From the summit of Mount Constitution on Orcas Island, 734 m up, the sea’s horizon is 96.7 km away.

23nav2Dropping the Tiny Term

Under the root, 2Rh is huge and h² is small. From 1.7 m, 2Rh is about 21.7 million square metres, and h² is under 3. Take out the big term, and hold the small one to account.

d = \sqrt{2Rh}\,\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{h}{2R}} @@ take 2Rh out from under the root
\sqrt{1 + x} = 1 + \tfrac{x}{2} - \tfrac{x^2}{8} + \cdots @@ the binomial series (23h), power ½: the size of the leftover
1 \le \sqrt{1 + x} \le 1 + \tfrac{x}{2} \ \ (x \ge 0) @@ square each side: 1 ≤ 1 + x ≤ 1 + x + x²/4
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt{2Rh} \le d \le \sqrt{2Rh}\,\big(1 + \tfrac{h}{4R}\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ x = h/(2R)

Horizon technique: the horizon is about √(2Rh) away, and dropping the h² term shortens it by a share less than h/(4R). With R = 6371 km and h in metres, √(2Rh) ≈ 3.570√h km, or 1.927√h nautical miles (21·nav). From 1.7 m, the share is under 7 × 10⁻⁸: a third of a millimetre on 4.65 km. From Mount Constitution it is under 3 × 10⁻⁵: about 2.8 m on 96.7 km.

The sight line is not the distance over the water. Let φ be the turn at C from the point below the eye to T. Over the water the distance is s = Rφ (21j), and the sight line is d = R tan φ.

\tan\varphi = \tfrac{d}{R},\ \ \sin\varphi = \tfrac{d}{R + h} @@ the right corner at T
\sin\varphi \le \varphi \le \tan\varphi @@ the small-arc technique (21f)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{d}{1 + h/R} \le s \le d} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply by R

Over-water technique: the distance over the water to the horizon is a little shorter than the sight line, by a share less than h/R. From Mount Constitution, s ≈ 96.70 km against d ≈ 96.71 km. Every number on this page is a fence, and its width is a share of h/R.

23nav3A Light Seen From a Ship

A lighthouse lamp at height h₁ and an eye at height h₂ can see each other while the straight line between them clears the sea. At the farthest such place, the line just grazes the sea at one point T. It is square to the radius CT there, from both sides, so the two sight lines to T lie on one line.

\text{lamp to } T:\ d_1 = \sqrt{2Rh_1 + h_1^2};\ \ \text{eye to } T:\ d_2 = \sqrt{2Rh_2 + h_2^2} @@ the sight-line technique, twice
\text{both are square to } CT \text{ at } T, \text{ so they lie on one line} @@ tangent square to radius (21q)
D = d_1 + d_2 @@ the line from lamp to eye
\htmlClass{new}{D \approx \sqrt{2Rh_1} + \sqrt{2Rh_2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the horizon technique, twice

Lighthouse technique: a light at height h₁ first shows over the sea to an eye at height h₂ at about √(2Rh₁) + √(2Rh₂). The lamp of Heceta Head Light, on the Oregon coast, stands about 62 m above the sea. From a bridge 15 m up, it first shows at 28.1 + 13.8 ≈ 41.9 km, about 22.6 nautical miles. The heights add under separate roots, not under one.

Short of that distance, the lamp shows. Past it, part of the tower hides. An eye at height h₂ looking toward a tower D away sees the sea end at d₂. The tower stands D − d₂ past the eye’s horizon, so a stretch of height about (D − d₂)²/(2R) at its foot lies below the eye’s line of sight. That is the horizon technique run backwards.

23nav4The Air Bends the Sight Line

Air is a little thicker near the sea, and light runs a little slower in it. So, by least time, a sight line near the sea bends gently down, along the curve of the sea. Take as a measured law that it bends along a circle of radius ρ, about 7R in ordinary weather. That figure is a law found under the large case, the statistician’s guess: it is an average over many days, and no law says what tomorrow’s air will do.

First, how far a circle of radius ρ falls below its tangent line, at a distance x along it. The right-triangle technique gives ρ − √(ρ² − x²), and the horizon technique’s fence, run the same way, shows that this is x²/(2ρ), too small by a share under x²/ρ². Call it the sag.

\text{at } x \text{ from } T, \text{ the sea falls } \tfrac{x^2}{2R} \text{ below the line touching it at } T @@ the sag of a ball of radius R
\text{the bent sight line falls } \tfrac{x^2}{2\rho} \text{ below the same line} @@ the sag of a circle of radius ρ
\text{its height above the sea: } \tfrac{x^2}{2R} - \tfrac{x^2}{2\rho} = \tfrac{x^2}{2R'},\ \ \tfrac{1}{R'} = \tfrac1R - \tfrac1\rho @@ subtract
\htmlClass{new}{d \approx \sqrt{2R'h}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the eye sees the sea as a ball of radius R′

Bent-sight technique: air that bends the sight line along a circle of radius ρ makes the sea look like a ball of radius R′, with 1/R′ = 1/R − 1/ρ. With ρ = 7R, R′ = 7R/6, and every horizon moves out by √(7/6) ≈ 1.080: about 8 percent. In nautical miles, 1.927√h becomes about 2.08√h, with h in metres.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, at sea

The horizon technique drops a term worth less than a millionth for any eye on a ship. The air adds about eight percent on an ordinary day. So the arithmetic is not where the doubt lives. The doubt is in the air.

The horizon’s distance is a fence, and the weather sets its width.

On a summer morning, warm air can lie over cold water, as it often does on the Salish Sea and off the Golden Gate. Then the sight line bends much more strongly, ρ falls toward R, and far islands and ships rise into view that the tables call hidden. When ρ = R the sea looks flat, and the horizon goes as far as the air stays that way. On a hot day over warm sand the bending runs the other way, and the horizon comes in.

On a cold June morning at Point Roberts, a ferry’s hull shows clearly at a distance where the table says its lowest 20 m should be hidden. Was the table wrong, or the air?

The accepted names

d is the distance to the horizon, and the horizon technique is its usual approximation d ≈ √(2Rh). The angle by which the horizon lies below the level line is the dip of the horizon. The lighthouse technique gives a light’s geographic range; the range printed on charts also depends on the light’s brightness. The bending of light by air near the ground is terrestrial refraction, and ρ = 7R is often written as a refraction coefficient k = R/ρ ≈ 0.14 (values from 0.13 to 0.17 are in use). R′ is the effective earth radius. Strong bending that lifts far objects into view is looming, and the bent-sight technique with ρ ≤ R describes ducting, or a superior mirage.

Who said it first

Al-Biruni measured the earth’s radius about 1020 from the dip of the horizon seen from a mountain near the Indus, the method of 23·nav.6.4. In 23·nav.6.5, Samuel Rowbotham (1838) watched a flag along the Old Bedford River in England and argued that the water was flat. In 1870 Alfred Russel Wallace took John Hampden’s wager on it, set markers of equal height along the same canal, and saw the middle one stand higher; the referee paid Wallace, and the quarrel ran on for years. Later sightings along the canal showed how strongly the air above the water can bend the sight line.
