Why Fly Over the Pole to Asia?

Why fly over the pole to Asia? Board a plane in Seattle for Tokyo and look at the map on the seat back. Tokyo is south of Seattle, yet the plane sets off north-west, climbs past Alaska, and only then turns down toward Japan. A flight from Seattle to Dubai sets off almost due north and passes within a short walk, as such things go, of the North Pole. On a flat map both look like long detours. People asked why for as long as ships have crossed oceans, and the answer is that the earth is round and a flat map is not. What makes it hard to see is that every flat map stretches the far north, so the shortest way looks bent. This page works with rung twenty-nine’s triangles on a sphere. The pole and two cities make a triangle whose sides are great circles. From it come the heading a plane should first fly, how far north its route will reach, and why some routes cross the pole itself. The same triangle, with a star in place of one city, is how a ship found its place before satellites. And the angles of a large enough triangle, measured, say which geometry the earth has.

Join the pole to each city and the two cities to each other by great circles, and you have a triangle on the sphere. Two of its sides are the cities’ distances from the pole, and the angle at the pole is the difference of their longitudes. Rung twenty-one’s rule for the distance between two cities is this triangle’s rule for its third side. The same rule, used a second time on the triangle, gives the heading on which the route leaves the first city. Along a great circle, the reach from the axis times the share of the heading that runs east never changes, so the route is highest where it runs due east or west, and passes over the pole when it starts due north. That happens when the far city sits on the opposite side of the earth’s axis, and from the west coast that side runs through the Persian Gulf. A star straight above some point of the earth makes the same triangle with the pole and a ship. The angles of a triangle on the earth add to more than two right angles, by its area over the square of the radius. And a screen that shows the distance to a city uses a form of the rule that never subtracts two nearly equal numbers.

29nav1The Pole and Two Cities

Take the north pole N, Seattle P and Tokyo Q, and join each pair by a great circle (29b). That is 29i’s triangle. Its side NP is π/2 − φ₁ and its side NQ is π/2 − φ₂, where φ is latitude, and its angle at N is the gap in longitude. 29i’s great-circle technique gives the distance and the heading h on which the route leaves P, from north toward Q’s side. Seattle’s airport is at 47.45° N, 122.31° W, and Narita, for Tokyo, at 35.76° N, 140.39° E.

\cos\delta \approx 0.4305 - 0.5487 \times 0.1271 \approx 0.3608,\ \ \delta \approx 1.2017 @@ the great-circle technique (29i2): sin φ₁ sin φ₂ ≈ 0.4305, cos φ₁ cos φ₂ ≈ 0.5487
R\delta \approx 6371 \times 1.2017 \approx 7656 \text{ km} @@ the earth taken as a ball of radius 6371 km
\cos h = \tfrac{\sin\varphi_2 - \sin\varphi_1\cos\delta}{\cos\varphi_1\sin\delta} \approx 0.5052,\ \ h \approx 59.7^\circ @@ the second half of the great-circle technique
\psi = \ln(\sec\varphi + \tan\varphi):\ \ \psi_1 \approx 0.9432,\ \ \psi_2 \approx 0.6691 @@ the strip technique (22·nav): drawn heights over R on the heading-keeping map

The shortest route from Seattle to Tokyo sets off north of west, though Tokyo lies south of Seattle, and it is about 6% shorter than holding one heading. Its heading on a compass is 360° − 59.7° = 300.3°. The steady heading would hold 260.8° the whole way: a straight line on 22·nav’s map, and a curve on the globe.

29nav2How Far North, and When Over the Pole

A plane flying a great circle does not hold its heading: Seattle to Tokyo starts at 300° and arrives near 226°. Something else is held. Take any point X of the route beyond P, at latitude φ, where the route’s heading is h_X, from north toward the same side as h_P.

\text{triangle } NPX:\ \ NP = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi_P,\ \ NX = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi,\ \ \angle P = h_P,\ \ \angle X = \pi - h_X @@ at X the way back to P is the way ahead turned half round
\tfrac{\sin h_P}{\sin NX} = \tfrac{\sin(\pi - h_X)}{\sin NP} @@ the sphere-sine technique (29i3): each angle against the side it faces
\htmlClass{new}{\cos\varphi_P\,\sin h_P = \cos\varphi\,\sin h_X} \quad \blacksquare @@ the complement technique (21e), and sin(π − h) = sin h

Kept-product technique: along a great-circle route, the reach from the axis, cos φ, times the sine of the heading, sin h, is the same at every point. Where sin h_X = 1 the route runs due east or west and is highest: that is 29i’s farthest-north technique, cos φ_max = cos φ_P sin h_P. Where the product is 0, the route leaves due north or due south, and runs through the pole. By the great-circle technique, that happens when the far city’s longitude is the same as the near one’s, or 180° away: on the opposite side of the axis.

Seattle to Tokyo: cos 47.45° × sin 59.7° = 0.584, so the route tops out at 54.3° N, near the Alaska Peninsula at 160.8° W. Vancouver to Seoul tops out at 59.6° N, over the Bering Sea. The longitude opposite Seattle’s is 57.7° E, which runs through the Persian Gulf. Dubai, at 25.25° N, 55.36° E, is 177.7° away: the route leaves Seattle on a heading of 2.2°, nearly due north, reaches 88.5° N within 170 km of the pole, and comes down the far side. Vancouver to Delhi reaches 78.3° N. From the west coast, the further west in Asia, the nearer the pole.

29nav3The Pole, the Star and the Ship

At any moment a star stands straight overhead at one point of the earth, its ground point. The ground point’s latitude is the star’s, δ, read from tables; its longitude comes from the time. A ship at latitude φ measures the star’s height H above the horizon. Straight up from the ship and straight toward the star differ by 90° − H, and that is the angle at the centre between the ship and the ground point. The pole, the ground point and the ship make a triangle: sides 90° − φ and 90° − δ from the pole, and t, the difference of their longitudes, at the pole.

\cos(90^\circ - H) = \sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos t @@ the great-circle technique (29i2), for the ship and the ground point
\htmlClass{new}{\sin H = \sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos t} \quad \blacksquare @@ the complement technique (21e)

Star-circle technique: every place that sees a star at height H lies on the circle of points 90° − H from its ground point, so a navigator works out H for a guessed place and moves toward the star by the difference, one nautical mile for each minute of arc. A nautical mile, 1852 m, is a minute of arc of a great circle. The pole star has δ ≈ 89.4°, so sin H ≈ sin φ: its height is the latitude, to within about 0.6°.

A ship guesses it is at 48.0° N, 125.0° W, off the west coast of Vancouver Island. Arcturus has δ = 19.1° N, and at the moment of the sight t = 30°. At the guessed place, sin H = 0.791, so H = 52°15.4′, with the star to the south-west, at 230.5°. The sextant reads 52°27.4′: 12′ higher. So the ship is 12 nautical miles nearer the ground point than the guess, along 230.5°, on a line across that heading. A second star gives a second line, and the ship is where they cross.

29nav4Which Geometry Does the Earth Have?

On a flat sheet a triangle’s angles add to π (18f). On a sphere of radius R, the excess-area technique (29b) says they add to π plus area/R². Take the triangle of the pole, Seattle and Tokyo.

\text{at the pole: } 360^\circ - 262.70^\circ = 97.30^\circ @@ the longitudes are 122.31° W and 140.39° E
\text{at Seattle: } 59.66^\circ @@ the great-circle technique’s heading, west of north (29·nav.1)
\text{at Tokyo: } 45.99^\circ @@ the great-circle technique’s heading from Tokyo toward Seattle, east of north
\text{sum} - 180^\circ = 22.95^\circ = 0.4005 @@ in turns measured on the circle of radius 1 (21)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = 0.4005\,R^2 \approx 16.3 \text{ million km}^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the excess-area technique (29b)

The angles of a triangle on the earth add to more than two right angles, and the excess, read with the area, gives the radius. No new technique is needed: 29b’s, turned round, reads R² = area/excess. A big enough triangle needs no satellite to show that the earth curves.

Anexact, yet rigorous · a measurement picks the geometry

Whether the ground is flat or curved is not settled by logic. Rung 29 built flat, round and saddle-shaped surfaces, each free of contradiction. Which one the earth’s skin is, near you, is found by measuring. Surveyors on the three volcanoes Mount Rainier, Mount St. Helens and Mount Adams would have a triangle with sides of 54, 75 and 81 km and an area of about 1970 km². Its excess is 1970/6371² of a radian: 10.0 seconds of arc. A good theodolite reads angles to about a second. So the excess is there to be found, but only just, and a flat survey would miss it by those 10 seconds.

Which geometry a surface has is a measured fact: measure a triangle’s angles and its area, and its excess says how the surface curves, within what the instruments can see.

The measuring goes further. The earth is flatter at the poles by about 1 part in 298, so the ball of radius 6371 km is itself a guess, and route lengths worked on it can be off by up to about half a percent. Airlines plan on a squashed ball. The test of a geometry is the size of what it gets wrong, set against what the instruments can see.

A surveyor measures a triangle of three peaks in the Olympic Mountains, about 30 km a side, and finds that its angles add to 180° to the nearest second. Does that show the ground is flat there?

29nav5Distance on the Screen

A phone shows the distance from where you stand to a pin on the map. It knows two latitudes and two longitudes. The great-circle technique gives cos γ, and then γ. For two places across a city, γ is tiny and cos γ is 0.99999998: a number that must be stored to many places before 1 − cos γ, the part that carries the distance, shows at all.

1 - \cos\gamma = 2\sin^2\tfrac{\gamma}{2} @@ the half-arc technique (21k)
1 - \cos\gamma = 1 - \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\cos\Delta\lambda - \sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 @@ the great-circle technique (29i2)
= \big(1 - \cos(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1)\big) + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\,(1 - \cos\Delta\lambda) @@ the difference technique (21e): cos(φ₂ − φ₁) = cos φ₁ cos φ₂ + sin φ₁ sin φ₂
\htmlClass{new}{\sin^2\tfrac{\gamma}{2} = \sin^2\tfrac{\varphi_2 - \varphi_1}{2} + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\,\sin^2\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the half-arc technique on each bracket; divide by 2

Half-angle distance technique: the square of the sine of half the distance is the square of the sine of half the latitude difference plus cos φ₁ cos φ₂ times the square of the sine of half the longitude difference, a form that never subtracts two nearly equal numbers. From the Space Needle (47.6205° N, 122.3493° W) to Pike Place Market (47.6097° N, 122.3422° W), cos γ = 0.999999978746. Kept to seven figures it rounds to 1, and the distance comes out as 0. The half-angle form gives sin²(γ/2) = 1.063 × 10⁻⁸, and 1.31 km.

The accepted names

A triangle of great circles is a spherical triangle; with the pole, the star’s ground point and the ship it is the navigational (PZX) triangle. The heading a route leaves on is the initial course, or forward azimuth. That cos φ sin h is kept along a great circle is Clairaut’s relation, and the highest point is the vertex. The steady heading is a rhumb line, or loxodrome. The ground point is the star’s geographical position, δ its declination, t the local hour angle and H its altitude; moving toward the star by the difference is the intercept method, and the line it gives a line of position. The half-angle form is the haversine formula. The angle sum’s excess is the spherical excess.

Who said it first

Pedro Nunes showed in 1537 that a steady heading is not a great circle, and spirals in toward the pole. Gerardus Mercator drew the map on which it is straight in 1569. Alexis Clairaut proved the kept product for curves on surfaces of revolution in 1743. Thomas Sumner found the line of position by accident off the Irish coast in 1837, and Adolphe Marcq de Saint-Hilaire gave the intercept method in 1875. James Inman printed tables of the haversine for sailors in 1835. Carl Friedrich Gauss surveyed a triangle of three German peaks, Hohenhagen, Brocken and Inselsberg, in the 1820s, with an excess of about 15 seconds of arc; whether he meant it as a test of the geometry of space is still argued. Scandinavian Airlines flew the first scheduled route over the polar region, Copenhagen to Los Angeles, in 1954. Routes straight across the Arctic between North America and Asia opened to airliners around 2001. In 29·nav.7.5, great-circle sailing was taught from the sixteenth century, yet most ships held steady headings until steam, and sailing ships often took composite courses that followed the great circle up to a safe latitude and then ran along it.
