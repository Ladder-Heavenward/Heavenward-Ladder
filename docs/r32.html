Every Periodic Function Is a Sum of Turnings

Rung 31 made functions into arrows, with lengths, angles and shadows, in a space with endlessly many directions. This rung picks a list of directions in that space, the turnings einx, and shows they are at right angles to one another and leave no direction out. Then every repeating function is the sum of its shadows on them, and its energy is shared out exactly among them. The rung asks when the sum gives back the function at each point, finds the halfway rule at a jump and an overshoot that never dies, and repairs it by averaging. It lets the repeat stretch until it is gone, and gets the transform, which breaks any signal into frequencies and keeps its energy. Two prices follow: a signal cannot be both short and sharp in frequency, and a signal with no frequency above B is fixed by samples 1/(2B) apart. The techniques then do the arithmetic of waves by hand and by machine, and the annexes take it into the bell, spectra, violins, crystals, photos, headphones, tides, stepped mechanics and fast scans.

What it means to say it

A periodic function is one that repeats: f(x + 2π) = f(x) for every x. A turning is the function einx for a whole number n, a point going round the circle n times while x goes once round. To say that every periodic function is a sum of turnings is to say that each such f is c0 + c1eix + c−1e−ix + ⋯, with one number cn for each turning, and that the number is the shadow of f on that turning.

The turnings are a right-angled list that leaves no direction out, so every periodic function with finite energy is the sum of its shadows on them, and its energy is the sum of the squares of their sizes. The rung proves it, and then says in what sense the sum returns the function at a point, which is a harder and more interesting question.

Where it comes from

A vibrating string was written as a sum of sines in the middle of the eighteenth century, and the argument over whether every shape could be so written ran for fifty years. A study of heat in 1807 claimed it could, with the coefficients as totals, and was not believed. The proof that the series returns a function with a slope came in 1829, and the overshoot at a jump was found twice, in 1848 and again in 1899, after a machine drew it. The averaged sums that always settle came in 1900. The transform for signals that never repeat grew out of the same study of heat, the sampling technique was proved for telegraph and radio in the 1920s to 1940s, and the fast way to compute the coefficients was found in 1965, and then found to have been written down in 1805. The names belong to the steps, and each step’s cellar gives them.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things, each from the ones before.

First, the turnings are at right angles to one another, each of size one, so a coefficient is a shadow, found once and kept however many turnings are used; a real wave is a sum of cosines and sines.

Second, the coefficients of a wave with finite energy fade, the turnings come near every continuous wave at every input at once, and so the energy is kept: the sum of |cn|² is the energy of f. This proves the equality held in trust from 31h, and it gives 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯ = π²/6, the total rung 22 found but could not name.

Third, the partial sum at a point is a weighted average of f near it. Where f has a slope the sum returns f(x); at a jump it returns the halfway value; and near the jump the sums overshoot by about nine per cent of the jump, however many turnings are used.

Fourth, blending one function with another multiplies their coefficients, a blend is as smooth as the smoother weight, and the averages of the partial sums settle at every continuous f everywhere at once.

Fifth, letting the repeat grow without end turns the coefficients into the transform. It is bounded and continuous, the bell e−πx² is its own transform, the transform can be undone, and it keeps energy.

Sixth, a slope becomes a multiple of the frequency, so the widths of a signal in time and in frequency multiply to at least 1/(4π), and only bells reach it.

Seventh, a signal with no frequency above B is fixed by its samples 1/(2B) apart, and rebuilt between them; sampled more slowly, a high frequency takes the samples of a low one.

Four of the second kind of care return on this rung, each now inside a technique. 32b carries the ledger again: the energy of a wave is shared out exactly among its turnings, nothing made and nothing lost, as size was shared among countably many pieces on rung 30. 32f carries the cloud again: a measurement short in time is wide in frequency, so a width is part of what a reading reports, not a flaw in the instrument. 32·stat carries the middle of a change again: the bell is the one shape whose turnings fade into its own spread, which is why sums pass through it. 32·nav carries laws with a known exception rate again: a tide table is a law of turnings stated with the size of what it leaves out, the weather.

What follows

With those seven, the techniques compute. Square, sawtooth and triangle waves are written out by hand. Filters become multiplications of coefficients. Rate rules with constant coefficients become algebra, one frequency at a time. The coefficients of N samples are found in about N log N steps by splitting in halves, and the CD rate of 44 100 samples a second is read from the ear’s top frequency. Oscillating totals are found to live near the points where the phase stops turning. The annexes take these into why the bell keeps turning up, what a spectrum tells us, the sound of a violin, X-rays in a crystal, photos stored small, headphones that cancel noise, tides predicted years ahead, stepped mechanics that keep the area in its plane, and signals rebuilt from fewer samples than 32g asks for.

What you can do because you know it

You can write any repeating signal as a list of turnings, read its energy from the list, and say where its series returns it and where it overshoots. You can blend and filter by multiplying, break a signal that does not repeat into frequencies and back, say how short and how sharp it can be at once, and choose how often to sample it so nothing is lost.

From here on, a wave is a list of numbers, and most of what is done to waves is done to the list.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 9’s cosine and sine, rung 15’s area and its evenly-continuous technique, rung 22’s endless sums, rung 26’s turnings and power-to-sum technique, rung 30’s totals and their limits, and rung 31’s products, shadows and complete spaces. Two steps are held in trust. On 32·stat, the step from matching average-functions to matching limits is carried from 30j. On 32l, the size of the error left by stationary phase is stated, not proved. And one warning is given without proof: some continuous wave has a series that fails to settle at a point, which is proved much higher up the ladder. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say every periodic function is a sum of turnings, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
