Why Does Cloth Stretch on the Bias?

Why does cloth stretch on the bias? Pull a square of woven cloth along its threads and it barely gives. Pull it corner to corner, on the bias, and it stretches easily, narrowing as it goes. Dressmakers cut on the bias to make cloth drape and cling; engineers cutting sails and tents must allow for it. This page finds threads stiff along themselves and loose between; splits a pull at an angle along each thread with 27’s maps; writes the stretch as a quadratic form, as 31e did; finds the softest way as 31d’s square axes; and treats twisted yarn as a helix with 21’s sines.

A pull at angle θ to the warp splits into a pull along each set of threads and a shear between them. The threads hardly stretch, but the shear, which only turns the crossings, is easy. The stretch per pull is cos⁴θ/E + sin⁴θ/E + sin²θ cos²θ/G, a quadratic form in (cos²θ, sin²θ). With G much smaller than E, it is softest at 45°: the bias.

35eng1Threads Are Stiff Along Themselves and Loose Between

Sarah designs fabric structures: tents and sails. Woven cloth has warp threads one way and weft threads square to them. A pull along the warp stretches the warp threads themselves, which are stiff. But the crossings are not glued: pushed sideways, the squares between threads lean over into diamonds easily.

From here on, on this page, E is the cloth’s stiffness along either set of threads, and G its stiffness against leaning, the shear stiffness: for Sarah’s cloth, E = 100 and G = 2, in the same units, newtons per centimetre of width for a full stretch.

35eng3Stretch as a Quadratic Form

Each part stretches by its pull over its stiffness, and the stretch along the strip collects each part turned back along it.

\dfrac{1}{E(\theta)} = \dfrac{\cos^4\theta}{E} + \dfrac{\sin^4\theta}{E} + \dfrac{\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta}{G} @@ threads not pulling on each other sideways

35eng4The Bias Is the Softest Way

\theta = 0:\ \dfrac{1}{E(0)} = 0.01,\ \ E(0) = 100 @@ 
\theta = 45°:\ \dfrac{0.25 + 0.25}{100} + \dfrac{0.25}{2} = 0.13,\ \ E(45°) \approx 7.7 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on the bias the cloth is 13 times softer}} @@

The grid’s square axes (31d) lie along (1, 1) and (1, −1), with numbers 1/E + 1/(2G) and 1/E − 1/(2G). With G much smaller than E, the stretch is greatest where c² = s², at 45°.

Bias technique: woven cloth is stiff along its threads and softest at 45° to them, where the pull becomes pure leaning of the crossings; how much softer is set by E against G. Sarah tests strips cut at 0°, 15°, 30° and 45° on a pulling machine, and their stiffnesses fall on the curve E(θ) this form gives.

35eng5Twisted Yarn Is a Helix

A thread is itself spun from short fibres twisted together. Each fibre on the surface winds round as a helix (21r), at an angle to the thread’s length.

\text{one turn: } 2\pi r \text{ round, } h \text{ along}:\ \tan\alpha = \dfrac{2\pi r}{h} @@ unrolled, a right triangle (21h)
r = 0.1 \text{ mm},\ h = 1 \text{ mm}:\ \tan\alpha \approx 0.63,\ \alpha \approx 32° @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each fibre carries the thread’s pull at } \cos\alpha \approx 0.85} @@

Twist holds fibres together by friction but tilts them off the thread’s line, so too much twist weakens a thread as too little does. Spinners choose the twist for each use: tight for strong sewing thread, loose for soft knitting yarn.

Accepted names

Different stiffness in different directions is anisotropy; cloth with two square axes is orthotropic; G is the in-plane shear modulus; and the leaning of crossings is trellising. The bias cut is the cut at 45° to the grain; α is the twist angle.

Who said it first

The dressmaker Madeleine Vionnet made the bias cut famous in the 1920s and 1930s (not yet revisited for the dates).
