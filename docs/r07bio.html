How Many Fish Are in the Lake?

How many fish are in the lake? Nobody can drain a lake and count. A biologist can catch some fish, mark them and let them go, then come back and catch again. The share of marked fish in the second catch says what share of the whole lake was marked, and that gives the lake’s size from one proportion. This page sets up the count, solves it with 5j’s ratios, lists what must be true for it to work, and meets the case where 5e’s forbidden division turns up: a second catch with no marked fish at all.

Mark M fish and release them. Later catch C fish, of which R carry marks. If the marked fish have mixed in evenly, R is to C as M is to the whole lake N: R : C = M : N. Cross-multiply: N = M × C ÷ R.

7bio1Catch, Mark, Release

Jean is surveying the trout in a mountain lake. In June she nets 100 trout, clips a small fin on each so it can be recognised, and lets them all go.

From here on, on this page, M is the number of fish marked and released, C the number caught the second time, R the number of those that carry a mark, and N the number of fish in the lake, which is what we want.

The marked fish are now somewhere in the lake among all the others. If Jean waits long enough for them to spread out, and not so long that many fish are born or die, the lake’s mix is fixed: M of its N fish are marked.

7bio2The Marked Share of the Second Catch

Two weeks later Jean nets 80 trout. Of these, 10 have a clipped fin.

\text{marked share of the catch: } R : C = 10 : 80 @@ what Jean counted
\text{marked share of the lake: } M : N = 100 : N @@ what she wants to know
\htmlClass{new}{R : C = M : N} @@ if the catch is a fair sample of the lake (6·stat)

The second catch is a sample of the lake. If every fish was equally likely to be caught, marked or not, the sample’s share of marked fish is right on average, as 6·stat showed for a fair sample.

7bio3The Estimate From One Ratio

Solve the proportion by cross-multiplying, as 5j did.

\dfrac{10}{80} = \dfrac{100}{N} @@ the proportion
10 \cdot N = 100 \cdot 80 @@ cross-multiply (5j)
\htmlClass{new}{N = \dfrac{M \cdot C}{R} = \dfrac{100 \cdot 80}{10} = 800} @@ about 800 trout

Mark-share technique: mark M fish, catch C later, and count R marked; if the marked fish have mixed in evenly, the lake holds about M × C ÷ R fish. The answer is a guess from a sample, so a second survey would give a nearby number, not the same one.

7bio4What Must Be True for It to Work

The proportion R : C = M : N rests on four things. If any fails, the estimate leans one way.

what must holdif it failsthe estimate
no fish born, die, arrive or leave between catchesN changesis for neither lake
marks stay on and are always seenR is too smalltoo big
marked fish mix in evenlyR depends on where the net goeseither way
marked and unmarked fish are equally easy to catchnet-shy marked fish make R too smalltoo big

Each row is a statement, and the estimate stands only on the and of all four. In the field, a biologist checks what can be checked: a second mark on some fish to see how many marks fall off, nets spread around the whole lake, and a short gap between catches.

7bio5When No Marked Fish Come Back

Suppose Jean’s second net of 80 holds no marked fish. Then R = 0, and N = 100 × 80 ÷ 0 asks for 5e’s forbidden division. The proportion 0 : 80 = 100 : N has no N at all.

What the catch says is that the lake is big compared with 100 and 80; it says nothing about how big. Biologists use a small correction that adds one to each count, so the division is never by 0.

N \approx \dfrac{(M + 1)(C + 1)}{R + 1} - 1 @@ the correction
R = 10:\ \dfrac{101 \cdot 81}{11} - 1 \approx 743 @@ close to 800 when R is not small
\htmlClass{new}{R = 0:\ \dfrac{101 \cdot 81}{1} - 1 = 8\,180} @@ a number, but only a floor of a guess

When R is 0, the honest result is “much more than M × C”, not a number. Jean’s fix in the field is a bigger second catch, which makes R = 0 unlikely if the lake is the size she thinks.

Each line below is a move that looks right and is not. The tag says what kind of mistake it is; the words say which footing catches it.

N = \dfrac{M \cdot R}{C}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-a">out of arrangement</span> R : C = M : N gives N = M × C ÷ R (7bio3)
\text{marks fell off, so the estimate is too small}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> lost marks make R too small, so the estimate is too big (7bio4)
R = 0,\ \text{so } N = 0\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> no marked fish means a big lake, and the division by 0 is forbidden (7bio5)
\text{catch again the same day}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-s">out of sequence</span> the marked fish need time to mix in (7bio1)
\text{800 trout exactly}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> the estimate comes from a sample; a second survey gives a nearby number (7bio3)

Accepted names

This is mark and recapture, and M × C ÷ R is the Lincoln and Petersen estimator. The corrected count is Chapman’s estimator. A lake with no births, deaths or movement between catches is a closed population.

Who said it first

C. G. Johannes Petersen used marked plaice to study fish in Danish waters in the 1890s, and Frederick Lincoln used bird bands to estimate waterfowl in North America in 1930. Douglas Chapman gave the corrected count in 1951.
