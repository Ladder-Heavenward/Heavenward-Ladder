28·∎ · conclusion

The derivative is a flat map in every direction

With several inputs, the slope at a point is the best flat map, whose grid is the grid of partial slopes. Slope grids multiply along chains; an undoable slope grid means a local undoing; an equation fixes a function; at a best point on a constraint the uphill arrows line up; totals over regions change by the determinant of the slope grid; and totals inside trade for totals round the edge.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 28 asked what a slope is when a function has several inputs. A function of several inputs has a slope at a point when one straight map matches it better than any step shrinks, and then that map’s grid is the grid of its partial slopes. The price is the demand of continuity: partial slopes alone can miss a jump, and mixed slopes can disagree.

28a turned one knob at a time and found that was not enough. 28b defined the best flat map and found its grid. 28c chained flat maps. 28d swapped the order of the knobs. 28e undid a curved map near a point, and 28f fixed a function by an equation. 28g lined up the uphill arrows on a constraint. 28h totalled over rectangles, 28i changed the variables, and 28j traded totals inside for totals round the edge. This page puts the claim together.

The claim, in full

If f(a + h) = f(a) + L(h) + r(h) with L straight and |r(h)|/|h| → 0, then L is unique, its grid is [∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ (a)], and continuous partial slopes guarantee such an L.

D(f ∘ g)(a) = Df(g(a)) Dg(a). If fxy and fyx are continuous, they are equal.

If det Df(a) ≠ 0, f can be undone near a, with D(f⁻¹) = (Df)⁻¹. If F(a, b) = 0 and Fy(a, b) ≠ 0, F(x, y) = 0 fixes y = g(x) near a, with g′ = −Fx/Fy. At a best point of f on g = 0 with ∇g ≠ 0, ∇f = λ∇g.

A continuous f on a rectangle has ∬R f, equal to either total of totals; ∬T(Q) f = ∬Q f(T) |det DT|; and ∮∂R F · dr = ∬R (Qx − Py), with the flux out equal to the total of the spread.

The proof

Each part below is a technique already proved on a step of this rung. The proof cites them by name and joins them.

f_x, f_y \text{ exist} \not\Rightarrow f \text{ continuous} @@ the knobs-miss-jumps technique (28a)
L \text{ unique},\ \ \text{column } j = \partial f/\partial x_j(a) @@ the one-flat-map and slope-grid techniques (28b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{continuous partials} \Rightarrow Df(a) \text{ exists}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the smooth-knobs technique (28b)
  
|Ah| \le c\,|h| @@ the stretch-bound technique (28c)
\htmlClass{new}{D(f\circ g)(a) = Df(g(a))\,Dg(a)} @@ the chain-of-flat-maps technique (28c)
\htmlClass{new}{f_{xy} = f_{yx}} \text{ when both are continuous} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d); the order-matters technique shows the demand is needed
  
\det Df(a) \ne 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ undoable near } a,\ \ D(f^{-1}) = (Df)^{-1} @@ the local-undo and undo-slope techniques (28e)
F_y \ne 0 \Rightarrow y = g(x),\ \ g' = -F_x/F_y @@ the fixed-by-an-equation and implicit-slope techniques (28f)
\htmlClass{new}{\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g \text{ at a best point on } g = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uphill and lines-up techniques (28g)
  
\textstyle\iint_R f = \int\!\int f\,dy\,dx = \int\!\int f\,dx\,dy @@ the rectangle-total and either-order techniques (28h)
\textstyle\iint_{T(Q)} f = \iint_Q f(T)\,|\det DT| @@ the area-scale, near-flat and change-of-variables techniques (28i)
\htmlClass{new}{\oint F\cdot dr = \iint (Q_x - P_y),\ \ \text{flux out} = \iint (P_x + Q_y)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uphill-total, swirl and spread techniques (28j)

So the rung’s claim holds: with several inputs, the slope at a point is the best flat map, and chains, undoings, equations, constraints, totals and boundaries all follow from it.
