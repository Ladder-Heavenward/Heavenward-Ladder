20h · proof

Sorting geometries by the moves they keep

This rung has met four families of moves: the slides, the slides and turns, the isometries, and the similarities. Each sits inside the next, and each is a group. Each also keeps some facts about a figure and loses others. This step turns that round. A geometry is a choice of moves, and its facts are exactly what those moves keep. More moves keep fewer facts.

Slides sit inside slides and turns, which sit inside isometries, which sit inside similarities, each a part-group of the next. A fact belongs to a geometry when every one of its moves keeps it. If one group sits inside another, every fact the larger one keeps is a fact the smaller keeps too.

20h1What each family keeps

Kept-facts technique: each family of moves keeps some facts about every figure and can change the others. A slide keeps every displacement, so it keeps lengths and directions. A turn keeps lengths and D but changes directions. A flip keeps lengths but changes the sign of D. A stretch by k keeps angles and the ratio of any two lengths, but multiplies every length by k.

\text{slides: displacement } B - A @@ S(B) − S(A) = B − A (20d1)
\text{slides and turns: lengths, and } D @@ 20d2, 20g2
\text{isometries: lengths} @@ 20d; D only up to sign (20g3)
\text{similarities: angles, ratios of lengths, “is a circle”} @@ 20a2, 20a3
\htmlClass{new}{\text{more moves, fewer facts kept}} @@ each family holds the one before

20h2Families inside families

Nested-families technique: the slides, the slides and turns, the isometries, and the similarities are each a part-group of the next. Each holds the one before. Each passes the part-group test.

20h3A geometry is a choice of moves

From here on, a geometry is a group of moves of the plane, and a fact of that geometry is a fact about figures that every move in the group keeps.

Fewer-facts technique: if one group of moves sits inside another, every fact of the bigger geometry is a fact of the smaller one. If every move of H keeps a fact, so does every move of G, since each move of G is a move of H.

G \subset H,\ \ \text{every } h \in H \text{ keeps the fact} @@ suppose
g \in G \Rightarrow g \in H \Rightarrow g \text{ keeps it} @@ G sits in H
\htmlClass{new}{\text{facts of } H \subset \text{facts of } G} \quad \blacksquare

So the old geometry, with the isometries, has more facts than the geometry of shape, with the similarities. Length is a fact of the first. Only ratios of lengths are facts of the second.

A fifth family goes beyond the similarities. From here on, a pull is a move that stretches in one direction only, such as (x, y) ↦ (2x, y). The pulls together with the similarities, and every move built from them, make a bigger group. The picture above offers its pulls. Its geometry has fewer facts still.

20h4Each geometry sorts figures

Same-in-a-geometry technique: in each geometry, two figures are the same when a move of its group carries one onto the other, and this sorts all figures. The proof is 20d4’s, word for word. Doing nothing, undoing and combining are in every group.

\text{slides: a figure and its slid copies} @@ same size, same way round, same direction
\text{isometries: congruent figures} @@ triangles sorted by three sides (20f4)
\text{similarities: similar figures} @@ triangles sorted by two angles (20f4)
\text{similarities: every circle with every circle} @@ 20a3: one sort holds them all
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a bigger group, fewer and larger sorts}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each sort of G lies inside one sort of H

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Sorting geometries by their groups of moves is the Erlangen program. The facts a group keeps are its invariants. The slides are the translations; the slides and turns, the proper or direct isometries; the pulls belong to the affine maps. The old geometry, with the isometries, is Euclidean geometry, after Euclid.

Who said it first

The program is Felix Klein's, in his Erlangen inaugural programme (1872). In 20h.4.5, the physicist is Hermann von Helmholtz, “On the facts that underlie geometry” (1868), and the mathematician is Bernhard Riemann, “On the hypotheses that underlie geometry” (lecture of 1854, printed 1868). Their argument was settled, in a sense, by Einstein's general relativity (1915), in which rulers do change as they move.
