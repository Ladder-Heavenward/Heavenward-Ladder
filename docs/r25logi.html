Why Does a Five-Minute Delay Miss the Deadline?

Why does a five-minute delay miss the deadline? A factory that holds no spare parts saves money every day, until one late delivery at the start of the line stops every station after it, and the day’s order ships late. This page models the line as a chain of handoffs, lets each late handoff pass its delay on with some chance, adds up how far one delay spreads as 22’s geometric sum, finds why an early slip predicts a missed deadline when there is no slack, and weighs where one buffer of stock does the most good, as 14·logi weighed stock against orders.

A delay at one station passes to the next with chance r, and is absorbed with chance 1 − r. The stations it reaches add up as 1 + r + r² + … = 1/(1 − r). With no slack, r is near 1, so a slip at station k reaches the end with chance r^(24 − k). A buffer catches what arrives; it does more good the later it sits, but costs more there.

25logi1Just in Time: a Chain of Handoffs

Claire runs an assembly line with 24 stations. Each station waits for the part from the one before, works on it, and hands it on. To save money, no station keeps a stock of finished parts: each part arrives just as it is needed.

From here on, on this page, a station is one of the 24 handoffs in order, and the deadline is the moment the last one must finish.

With no stock between stations, a station that finishes late makes the next one start late. Whether the next one makes up the time depends on how much slack it has.

Say a late part reaches a station. If the station has slack, a few spare minutes in its work, it absorbs the delay and hands on in time. If not, it hands on late.

25logi3How Far One Delay Spreads

1 + r + r^2 + r^3 + \cdots = \dfrac{1}{1 - r} @@ the geometric sum (22e)
r = 0.5:\ 2 \text{ stations};\ \ r = 0.9:\ 10;\ \ r = 0.97:\ 33 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{as } r \to 1, \text{ one delay reaches the whole line}} @@ the line has only 24 to reach

Knock-on series technique: if each station passes a delay on with chance r, one delay reaches on average 1/(1 − r) stations; when 1/(1 − r) is longer than the line, a delay anywhere usually reaches the end.

25logi4With No Slack, a Small Early Slip Predicts a Missed Deadline

A delay at station k must pass 24 − k more stations to reach the deadline.

chance r of passing ondelay at station 4 reaches the deadlineat station 20
0.900.90²⁰ ≈ 0.120.90⁴ ≈ 0.66
0.970.97²⁰ ≈ 0.540.97⁴ ≈ 0.89
0.990.99²⁰ ≈ 0.820.99⁴ ≈ 0.96

On a lean line, with r close to 1, a five-minute slip at the fourth station misses the deadline more often than not. That is why a line manager watching a just-in-time line treats the first late handoff of the day as a warning for the last, and acts at once rather than waiting to see if it clears.

25logi5Where One Buffer Does the Most Good

A buffer is a small stock of finished parts at one station: when a delay arrives there, the stock covers it. Say a delay is equally likely to start at any station, and r = 0.97. A buffer at station j catches every delay that reaches j.

\text{chance a delay reaches } j = \dfrac{1}{24}\,(1 + r + \cdots + r^{j-1}) = \dfrac{1}{24} \cdot \dfrac{1 - r^j}{1 - r} @@ from stations 1 to j
j = 6:\ 0.23;\ \ j = 12:\ 0.43;\ \ j = 18:\ 0.59;\ \ j = 24:\ 0.72 @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the gain flattens as } j \text{ grows, while the stock’s value grows with each station}} @@

A buffer later in the line catches more delays, but each later station adds less, while stock held there is worth more and costs more to hold; the best place is where one more station’s gain just pays for its extra holding cost (14·logi, 14h). Planners of lean lines place a few such buffers before the slowest stations and leave the rest bare.

Accepted names

A line with no stock between stations is just-in-time production; a buffer of stock is safety stock, and delays passed down a line are knock-on or propagated delays. The study of where buffers go is buffer allocation.

Who said it first

Just-in-time production grew up in Japanese car factories after the Second World War, chiefly under Taiichi Ohno (not yet revisited).
