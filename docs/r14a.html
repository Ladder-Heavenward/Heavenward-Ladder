14a · proof

Zero Slope at a Top or Bottom

13k's slope-sign-near says that a positive slope at an input pushes the values up just to its right and down just to its left. Turn that about. Take an input where no nearby value is higher. The slope there cannot be positive, since slope-sign-near would find a higher value just to the right. And it cannot be negative, since slope-sign-near would find one just to the left. So if there is a slope there, it is 0. That is the whole proof, and it is short because 13k did the work. What it buys is a search. Rung 12 promised that a function continuous on a closed interval reaches a greatest value, and said nothing of where. This step cuts the places to look down to a short list.

A positive slope at an input finds a higher value just to its right. A negative slope finds one just left. At a top neither can happen, so a slope there is 0. The greatest value on a closed interval is at an end, or at a top, and a top is where the slope is 0 or missing.

14a1Greatest Among the Neighbours

12c's greatest value is greatest among all the inputs of a set. A top is greatest only among its neighbours: a hill on a road is the highest point near it, and there may be higher hills further on. Said of a function:

From here on, f has a top at c when some neighbourhood of c (10b) lies inside f's domain and f(x) ≤ f(c) for every x in it. And f has a bottom at c when some neighbourhood of c lies inside f's domain and f(c) ≤ f(x) for every x in it. Their accepted names are a local maximum and a local minimum, and either is a local extremum. 12c's greatest and least values on a whole set are then called the global maximum and minimum, or the absolute maximum and minimum, to tell them apart.

The neighbourhood must lie inside the domain, so an end of a closed interval is never a top or a bottom in this sense, even when it gives the greatest value. Some books allow an end to be a local maximum; this ladder does not, because the technique below is false there.

Take 12c's p(x) = x³ − 3x + 1. 12c found p(x) − 3 = (x + 1)²(x − 2), which is at most 0 for every x < 2. On the neighbourhood of −1 of reach 1, every input is below 2, so p(x) ≤ 3 = p(−1) there: p has a top at −1. Likewise p(x) + 1 = (x − 1)²(x + 2) is at least 0 for x > −2, so p has a bottom at 1. On [−2, 2] the greatest value 3 is reached at −1 and at 2. The first is a top. The second is an end. Now look at the slopes: polynomial-slope gives p′(x) = 3x² − 3, so p′(−1) = 0, p′(1) = 0, and p′(2) = 9.

14a2The Proof, Every Line Cited

Flat-top technique: if f has a top or a bottom at c, and f has a slope at c, then f′(c) = 0.

\text{call } \delta:\ |h| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; f(c + h) \le f(c) @@ f has a top at c: the neighbourhood of reach δ
\text{suppose } f'(c) \gt 0 @@ a supposition for contradiction (7e)
\text{call } r:\ 0 \lt h \lt r \;\Rightarrow\; f(c + h) \gt f(c) @@ slope-sign-near (13k), its first half
h_1 = \tfrac12 \min(r, \delta) @@ positive, and below both r and δ
f(c + h_1) \gt f(c) \text{ and } f(c + h_1) \le f(c);\ \ \bot @@ the two lines above, at h₁; three-way-order on ℝ
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\,(f'(c) \gt 0)} @@ refute
\text{suppose } f'(c) \lt 0 @@ a second supposition
\text{call } r_2:\ -r_2 \lt h \lt 0 \;\Rightarrow\; f(c + h) \gt f(c) @@ slope-sign-near turned about: a negative slope pushes the values up on the left
h_2 = -\tfrac12 \min(r_2, \delta);\ \ f(c + h_2) \gt f(c) \ge f(c + h_2);\ \ \bot @@ the same squeeze, on the left; three-way-order on ℝ
\htmlClass{new}{\neg\,(f'(c) \lt 0)} @@ refute
f'(c) = 0 @@ three-way-order on ℝ: exactly one of f′(c) < 0, f′(c) = 0, f′(c) > 0, and two are refuted
  
-f \text{ has a top at } c @@ f(c) ≤ f(x) on the neighbourhood, so −f(x) ≤ −f(c) there (negative-reverses-order)
(-f)'(c) = -f'(c) @@ constant-factor for slopes (13d), with the constant −1
-f'(c) = 0,\ \ f'(c) = 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ the column above, for −f

Where each hypothesis was spent. The neighbourhood inside the domain: at h₁ and at h₂, which must both be inputs, one on each side of c. The slope at c: in both uses of slope-sign-near. Rung 7's either-way principle: inside three-way-order on ℝ, at the last line of the first column. The refutations themselves are refutation proper, which needs no such principle.

The proof uses only that f(c) is at least every nearby value. It never uses that f(c) is greatest on the whole domain. So it says nothing about which top is highest, and nothing about what happens at an end, where one of h₁ and h₂ is not an input. It is also why 13·stat found both slopes 0 at the best line: the total squared miss is least there, in each letter with the other kept fixed.

An astronomer measuring wine barrels noticed that near the shape that holds the most, a small change of shape on either side changes the volume hardly at all. At first the loss cannot be felt.

14a3The Inputs Worth Testing

Flat-top points at a short list. A top or bottom where f has a slope has slope 0. A top or bottom where f has no slope has no slope. Either way it is on the list below.

From here on, c is a critical point of f when some neighbourhood of c lies inside f's domain, and either f′(c) = 0 or f has no slope at c. Its accepted name is critical point; some books say critical number. A critical point where f′(c) = 0 is also called a stationary point, and its value f(c) a critical value.

Flat-top, said with the new word: every top and every bottom is a critical point. It does not say the reverse, and the reverse is false. x³ has slope 3 · 0² = 0 at 0, so 0 is a critical point. But (h)³ > 0 for every h > 0 and h³ < 0 for every h < 0. So every neighbourhood of 0 holds an input with a higher value and one with a lower value. x³ has neither a top nor a bottom at 0. And |x| has a bottom at 0, since 0 ≤ |x|, and no slope there by 13f's size-has-no-slope-at-0: a critical point of the second kind.

14a4The Candidates on a Closed Interval

Candidates technique: if f is continuous on [α, β], then its greatest value on [α, β] is reached at α, at β, or at a critical point of f inside (α, β). Likewise its least value.

\text{call } c:\ \forall x \in [\alpha, \beta]\ (f(x) \le f(c)) @@ highest-and-lowest (12c)
c = \alpha \;\lor\; c = \beta \;\lor\; \alpha \lt c \lt \beta @@ three-way-order on ℝ, twice, with c ∈ [α, β]
\text{if } \alpha \lt c \lt \beta:\ \delta = \min(c - \alpha,\ \beta - c) \gt 0 @@ the neighbourhood of c of reach δ lies inside [α, β]
f \text{ has a top at } c @@ f(x) ≤ f(c) on that neighbourhood, by the first line
\htmlClass{new}{c \text{ is a critical point}} \quad \blacksquare @@ flat-top if f has a slope at c; if not, ︿ “critical point”

So the greatest value on [α, β] is the largest of a short list of values: f(α), f(β), and f at each critical point inside. Its accepted name is the closed interval method, or the candidates test. The list must be finite for the test to finish. It is finite for every polynomial that is not constant, since its slope is a polynomial with at most finitely many zeros (9g's at-most-n-zeros).

p(x) = x^3 - 3x + 1 \text{ on } [0, 2] @@ 12c found its extremes from two factorings
p'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1) @@ polynomial-slope; zero at 1 and −1, and only 1 is inside (0, 2)
p(0) = 1,\ \ p(1) = -1,\ \ p(2) = 3 @@ the three candidates
\text{greatest } \htmlClass{new}{3} \text{ at } 2,\ \ \text{least } \htmlClass{new}{-1} \text{ at } 1 @@ candidates: compare the three
  
f(x) = x^4 - 8x^2 \text{ on } [-1, 3] @@ continuous: polynomials-are-continuous (11d)
f'(x) = 4x^3 - 16x = 4x(x - 2)(x + 2) @@ polynomial-slope; −2 is outside, 0 and 2 inside
f(-1) = -7,\ \ f(0) = 0,\ \ f(2) = -16,\ \ f(3) = 9 @@ the ends and the critical points
\text{greatest } 9 \text{ at } 3,\ \ \text{least } -16 \text{ at } 2 @@ the greatest is at an end, where f′(3) = 60
  
g(x) = x^2 - 2|x| \text{ on } [-3, \tfrac12] @@ continuous: 11b, with |x| continuous as a joint of −x and x (11e)
g'(x) = 2x + 2 \text{ for } x \lt 0,\ \ 2x - 2 \text{ for } x \gt 0 @@ slopes-decided-near: g agrees with x² + 2x left of 0 and with x² − 2x right of 0
\text{no slope at } 0:\ \text{one-sided slopes } 2 \text{ and } -2 @@ 13f: one-sided slopes that disagree (two-side-slopes)
\text{critical points inside: } -1 \text{ and } 0 @@ 2x − 2 = 0 only at 1, which is outside
g(-3) = 3,\ g(-1) = -1,\ g(0) = 0,\ g(\tfrac12) = -\tfrac34 @@ the four candidates
\text{greatest } 3 \text{ at } -3,\ \ \text{least } -1 \text{ at } -1 @@ the sharp bend at 0 is a candidate and wins nothing

The test does not find tops. It finds a list that contains every top, and compares values. On a closed interval that is enough for the greatest and least values, because highest-and-lowest says they are reached and candidates says where they can be. Which critical points are tops, which are bottoms, and which are neither waits for 14f.

14a6Running It Backwards

Handed a function continuous on a closed interval and asked for its greatest and least values, do four things. Find its slope function. List the inputs inside where the slope is 0 or missing. Add the two ends. Then compare the values: the greatest and least are among them. Handed a claimed top at c, check that c has a neighbourhood in the domain, and that the slope there is 0 or missing. If the slope is anything else, slope-sign-near finds a higher value on one side. Handed a critical point and asked whether it is a top, flat-top is silent. Write the gap f(x) − f(c) and find its sign near c, as 12c did, or wait for 14f. Found upward; written downward.

14a.4.4seam One early mathematician wanted to split a length b into two parts whose product is greatest. He wrote the product for each given pair of parts, treated the two as nearly equal, cancelled, divided by e, and struck out what still held e, to reach half of b. Who said it first

Kepler, measuring wine barrels, noticed that near the shape that holds the most, a small change of shape on either side changes the volume hardly at all: at first the loss cannot be felt.

Kepler, Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum (1615); paraphrase

The flat-top technique is Fermat’s; its accepted name is Fermat’s theorem on stationary points. The standing question’s method of greatest and least is Pierre de Fermat’s (about 1636), and the attack of 1638 is René Descartes’s.

Accepted names: a top is a local maximum and a bottom a local minimum, and either is a local extremum.
