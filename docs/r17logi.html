17·logi · logistics

How Many Will We Sell Next Week?

How many will we sell next week? Every shop that orders ahead has to guess. 14·logi set the order size and the stock kept back, given a forecast; this page makes the forecast. Last week alone jumps about too much, and the average of every week ever moves too slowly. A rule that nudges the old forecast toward each new week does both jobs, and unrolled it weighs past weeks by a falling ratio. Rung 17’s logs then say how long one week’s sales go on counting, and the last step shows what the rule does when sales keep rising.

Write the new forecast as the old one plus a share a of the miss. Unroll it: each past week gets a weight, the weights fall by the factor 1 − a, and they add up to 1. Take logs to find after how many weeks a weight has halved. If sales rise steadily, find the steady miss, and add a second nudged number for the rise.

17logi1Last Week, or Every Week?

Claire runs a bike shop and orders tyres every Monday for the week ahead. Here are six weeks of sales.

week123456
tyres sold384440365030

Guessing last week’s number each time would have forecast 50 for week 6, and sold 30. Guessing the average of every week so far is steadier, but if the town opens a cycle path and sales jump, an average of a year of weeks takes months to notice. From here on, on this page, the forecast F is the number guessed before the week, the sales D are what happens, and the miss is D − F.

17logi2Nudge the Forecast

Claire keeps one number, her forecast, and after each week moves it a share a of the way toward what was sold. With a = 0.2 and a forecast of 40 going into week 7:

F_{\text{new}} = F + a\,(D - F) @@ move a share of the miss
D = 50:\ 40 + 0.2 \cdot 10 = 42 @@ a week above the forecast pulls it up
D = 30:\ 42 + 0.2 \cdot (-12) = 39.6 @@ a week below pulls it down
\htmlClass{new}{D = 45:\ 39.6 + 0.2 \cdot 5.4 = 40.68} @@ one stored number, updated each week

The rule rearranges as Fnew = aD + (1 − a)F: a mix of this week’s sales and the old forecast. With a = 1 it is last week alone; with a near 0 it barely moves.

17logi3Weights That Fall by a Ratio

Write the forecast after week t as Ft+1, and put the rule inside itself again and again.

F_{t+1} = aD_t + (1 - a)F_t @@ the rule
= aD_t + a(1 - a)D_{t-1} + (1 - a)^2 F_{t-1} @@ the rule again, for F_t
= \textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a(1 - a)^i D_{t-i} + (1 - a)^n F_{t-n+1} @@ n times
\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a(1 - a)^i = a \cdot \dfrac{1 - (1 - a)^n}{a} = 1 - (1 - a)^n @@ the finite-ratio-sum technique (15i)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the weights, with } (1 - a)^n \text{ on the old forecast, add to } 1} @@ a true average of the past

Smoothing technique: the nudged forecast is an average of every past week, with the weight on each week falling by the factor 1 − a as it ages. Since (1 − a)n heads to 0 (15i), the first forecast fades away.

17logi4How Long a Week Counts

A week’s weight halves after k weeks when (1 − a)k = ½. Rung 17’s logs bring the k down from the power.

(1 - a)^k = \tfrac12 @@ the weight has halved
k \ln(1 - a) = -\ln 2 @@ the log-of-a-power technique (17c)
k = \dfrac{\ln 2}{-\ln(1 - a)} @@ ln(1 − a) < 0, by log-rises (17a)
a = 0.2:\ k = 0.693/0.223 \approx 3.1 @@ about three weeks
\htmlClass{new}{a = 0.5:\ k = 1} @@ one week

Choosing a is choosing how long the past counts. A large a follows a real change quickly and jumps with every odd week; a small a stays calm and is slow to see a change. Shops try several and keep the one whose misses were smallest on old weeks.

17logi5When Sales Keep Rising

Say sales grow by g tyres every week, and the forecast has settled so that it also grows by g each week. Then the miss settles too.

F_{t+1} - F_t = a\,(D_t - F_t) @@ the rule
g = a\,(D_t - F_t) @@ the forecast grows by g
D_t - F_t = g/a @@ the miss, every week
\htmlClass{new}{g = 2,\ a = 0.2:\ \text{always } 10 \text{ short}} @@ the forecast trails a rise

The rule averages the past, and on a rise the past is lower. On a steady rise the nudged forecast trails by g/a every week. The fix keeps a second number, the rise per week, nudged toward each new week’s rise in the same way, and adds it to the forecast.

\text{the weights } a, a(1 - a), \ldots \text{ add to more than } 1\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-c">outside the collect</span> with the old forecast’s share they add to exactly 1 (17logi3)
\text{a large } a \text{ is always better: it follows faster}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-l">legal, with a cost</span> it follows faster and jumps with every odd week (17logi4)

17logi7Forecast Error, Bias and the Nudge Size

A forecast is judged by its errors, sales less forecast. If they lean one way, the forecast is biased; their size, ignoring sign, says how far off it runs. And the nudge of 17logi2 can be chosen by running the forecast over past weeks for a few nudge sizes and keeping the one whose errors are smallest.

\text{errors } 4, -2, 7, 3, 8:\ \text{mean } 4 @@ biased low
\text{mean size } \tfrac{4 + 2 + 7 + 3 + 8}{5} = 4.8 @@ 
\text{after a jump, nudge } a:\ \text{share of the gap closed in } k \text{ weeks} = 1 - (1 - a)^k @@ ratios (17logi3)
\htmlClass{new}{a = 0.3:\ 51\% \text{ in 2 weeks},\ 76\% \text{ in 4}} @@ the lag

Error-check technique: track the mean error for bias and the mean size of error for accuracy; choose the nudge size that gives the smallest errors on past weeks, knowing that a nudge a closes the share 1 − (1 − a)ᵏ of a jump in k weeks. Shown in a shop’s records: when sales jumped and stayed up, a forecast with nudge 0.3 had covered about half the jump after two weeks and three quarters after four, as the ratio said.

Accepted names

The nudging rule is simple exponential smoothing, a is the smoothing constant, and the weights falling by a ratio are why it is called exponential. The weighted average age of the weeks it uses is (1 − a)/a. The forecast with a level and a rise is Holt’s linear method; adding a seasonal number gives the Holt-Winters method.

Who said it first

Robert G. Brown used the nudging rule for naval stock control in the 1950s. Charles Holt added the nudged rise in a 1957 report for the Office of Naval Research, and Peter Winters the seasons in 1960.
