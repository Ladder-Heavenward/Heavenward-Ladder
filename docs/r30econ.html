Can you beat the market by watching prices?

Can you beat the market by watching prices? Every trading day the shares of the big companies of Puget Sound, the software house in Redmond, the bookseller turned everything store in Seattle, the warehouse club in Issaquah, change hands millions of times, and their prices move. Charts of those prices look as if they have shapes in them: runs up, runs down, floors and ceilings. People have asked for well over a century whether the shapes can be read, so that you buy before the rise and sell before the fall. If they could, the reader would grow rich, and a great many people have tried. What makes the question hard is that a chart made by tossing a coin each day also looks as if it has shapes in it. The eye finds patterns in pure luck. So the question is not whether a pattern can be seen, but whether it would still be there if it were only luck. This page takes rung thirty’s random walks and fair games to it. It finds how slowly the steady rise of prices shows through the daily wobble, why no rule built from past prices can change the average of the next change if the game is fair, how to test whether a pattern in past prices is real, why a crowd of people who each try a rule will always throw up some winners, and where the bell curve, which this page leans on, fails on the worst days.

Think of the day’s change in a price as a step of a random walk, with a small steady drift and a much larger wobble. Over many days the drift adds up in proportion to the number of days, but the wobble adds up only like the square root of that number, because spreads of independent steps add, not their widths. So the drift does show through in the end, but only after many years. If the market is a fair game once the drift is taken out, then the next change, averaged with everything about the past known, is nothing, and so it is unrelated to anything you could build from the past. That is the claim to test. Multiply each day’s change by the next one and average: if the claim holds, the result sits near nothing, within a width of one over the square root of the number of days, and a pattern smaller than about two widths cannot be told from luck. Try twenty such rules and one will pass the test by luck more often than not, and among hundreds of fund managers some will beat the market year after year by luck alone. Finally, the bell curve for a month’s change is a good guide to an ordinary month and a poor one to a crash.

30econ1The drift and the wobble

Measure a price by its log (17), so that a day’s change D is close to its change in percent, and a rise of 1% counts the same at any price. Take the changes D₁, D₂, … of successive days as independent, each with average μ and spread width σ. A broad index of large American shares has moved, over long stretches, by about σ = 1.2% on a typical day, with a drift of about μ = 0.04% a day, near 10% a year over 252 trading days. A single company’s shares wobble more, often 1.5% to 2% a day.

\log P_t - \log P_0 = D_1 + D_2 + \cdots + D_t @@ the changes add, since logs turn products into sums (17)
E[\log P_t - \log P_0] = t\mu @@ the average-adds technique (30g)
\operatorname{Var}(\log P_t - \log P_0) = t\sigma^2,\ \ \text{width } \sigma\sqrt t @@ the root-n technique (30n), with the drift taken out of each step
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\text{drift}}{\text{width}} = \tfrac{t\mu}{\sigma\sqrt t} = \tfrac{\mu}{\sigma}\sqrt t} \quad \blacksquare @@ the drift outruns the wobble, but only like √t

Drift-and-width technique: over t days the drift grows like t and the wobble like √t, so the drift stands out against the wobble by (μ/σ)√t, which is tiny for a day and grows only slowly. With μ = 0.04% and σ = 1.2%, one day’s drift is a thirtieth of its wobble. A year’s drift, about 10%, is half its width, 19%. For the drift to stand two widths clear takes t = (2σ/μ)² = 3600 trading days: about 14 years.

So a year in which the index fell tells you almost nothing about whether its drift is up. And a price that rose for a month is about what a random walk does, often.

30econ2If the game is fair, the past is silent

Take the drift out: let X_n be the log price less nμ. The claim to test is that X is a fair game (30k): with everything up to day n known, the average of the next change X_{n+1} − X_n is 0. Let f be any number built from prices up to day n: yesterday’s change, a ten-day average, a pattern on a chart.

E[X_{n+1} - X_n \mid \text{the past}] = 0 @@ the fair game of 30k
E\big[f\,(X_{n+1} - X_n)\big] = \textstyle\sum_h f(h)\big(E(X_{n+1} \mid h) - X_n(h)\big)P(h) = 0 @@ whole-chance over the histories h, as in 30k’s stopping-keeps proof: f is fixed by h
\operatorname{Cov}\big(f,\ X_{n+1} - X_n\big) = E[f\,(X_{n+1} - X_n)] - E f \cdot 0 = 0 @@ co-spread (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no number built from the past has any co-spread with the next change}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every such f

Past-is-silent technique: in a fair game, anything built from the past, from yesterday’s change to the shape of a chart, has co-spread 0 with the next change. So, if the claim holds, no chart reading can tell the average of tomorrow’s change. 30k’s no-system technique says more: no rule of buying and selling that stops in bounded time changes the average gain at all.

A favourite rule: sell when the price is 10% up, or 5% down, to lock in gains and cut losses. Take steps of 1% in the log price. By 30k’s ruin-chance technique, starting 5 steps above the floor with the ceiling 15 steps above it, the ceiling comes first with chance 5/15 = 1/3. The average gain is (1/3)(10%) + (2/3)(−5%) = 0, and by 30n’s exit-time technique the wait averages 5 × 10 = 50 days. The rule changes the shape of the gains, many small losses and a few large wins, and leaves their average where it was. Each sale costs a fee, so on average it loses.

30econ3The test that a pattern is luck

To test the claim, take f to be yesterday’s change. Suppose the changes D₁, …, D_{n+1} are independent with average 0 and width σ. Form the lag product average r = (D₁D₂ + D₂D₃ + ⋯ + D_nD_{n+1})/(nσ²). If yesterday helped, r would sit away from 0.

E[D_iD_{i+1}] = E D_i \cdot E D_{i+1} = 0 @@ the independent-averages-multiply technique (30g)
E[D_iD_{i+1}\,D_jD_{j+1}] = 0 \text{ for } i < j @@ D_{j+1} is independent of the other three and averages 0
\operatorname{Var}(D_iD_{i+1}) = E[D_i^2]\,E[D_{i+1}^2] = \sigma^4 @@ independent-averages-multiply again
\operatorname{Var}\big(\textstyle\sum_i D_iD_{i+1}\big) = n\sigma^4 @@ the spread-of-a-sum technique (30g): every co-spread is 0
\htmlClass{new}{E\,r = 0,\ \ \text{width of } r = \tfrac{\sqrt n\,\sigma^2}{n\sigma^2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by nσ²

Lag-check technique: if the daily changes are independent, the lag product average r has average 0 and width 1/√n, so an r within about two widths of 0 cannot be told from luck. The central limit technique (30j) makes r close to a bell for large n, so by 30s’s bell-test technique a value beyond 1.96/√n turns up by luck about one time in twenty.

Ten years is about n = 2520 days, and 1/√2520 = 0.020. A measured r of 0.03 is 1.5 widths from 0: luck explains it easily. To see an r of 0.03 at two widths takes n = (2/0.03)² ≈ 4400 days, over 17 years, and over 17 years the market itself changes. Tests of this kind, run on prices since the 1950s, have found lag products small enough that, after a trader’s fees, no rule built on them pays.

30econ4Many tries make winners

One test at the one-in-twenty line is wrong by luck one time in twenty. But no one tests one rule. Suppose m rules are tested on the same prices, and each has chance α of passing by luck. Take the tests as independent.

P(\text{at least one passes by luck}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m @@ the at-least-one technique (30l), every rule false and the tests independent
E(\text{number passing}) = m\alpha @@ the average-adds technique (30g), one 0-or-1 count for each try
1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 0.64,\ \ 20 \times 0.05 = 1 @@ twenty rules at α = 0.05
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the rule that passed is likely the luckiest of the twenty}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and it is the one written up

Among m independent tries that each pass by luck with chance α, at least one passes with chance 1 − (1 − α)^m, and on average mα pass, so a winner among many tries is no evidence by itself. No new technique is needed: it is 30l’s at-least-one technique, read as a warning.

The same holds for people. Let 500 fund managers each beat the index in a year with chance ½, by luck, independently. A given manager beats it in at least 8 years of 10 with chance (45 + 10 + 1)/1024 ≈ 5.5%: about 27 of them, each with a fine record. Some manager beats it ten years running with chance 1 − (1023/1024)⁵⁰⁰ ≈ 39%. The yearly scorecards of fund returns against the index find that, over fifteen years, most managers of funds of large American shares, around nine in ten, trail the index after fees.

30econ5The bell, and the worst days

A month’s change is a sum of 21 daily changes, so by the central limit technique (30j) it is close to a bell with average 21μ and width σ√21. For the index, the width is 1.2% × √21 ≈ 5.5%. Banks use such a bell to set aside money: the loss over 10 days that is passed with chance only 1%.

P(Z < -2.326) = 0.01 @@ the bell, from its table
\text{a day: } 2.326 \times 1.2\% = 2.79\% @@ the width of one day
\text{ten days: } 2.326 \times 1.2\% \times \sqrt{10} = 8.83\% @@ the drift-and-width technique; the drift is ignored
P(|Z| > 4) = 6.3 \times 10^{-5} @@ the bell: one day in 15 800, about once in 63 years of 252 days
\htmlClass{new}{\text{19 October 1987: } -20.5\% \text{ in one day, about } 17 \text{ widths}} \quad \blacksquare @@ on the bell, chance below 10^(−60)

The bell fits ordinary months well and the worst days badly: falls of four widths and more have come dozens of times since 1950, where the bell allows about one. The central limit technique asks for independent steps with finite spread. Prices in a panic are neither: one fall brings forced sales, and the next.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

The bell is the shape every sum of many small independent parts passes through on its way, and a month of prices is such a sum. A bank that sets aside 8.83% of its holdings, to cover a ten-day loss passed with chance 1%, has made a decision with a stated chance of being wrong. That is rigour of the second kind: it does not say the loss will not come, it says how often it should.

A loss line drawn from the bell is a decision with a stated chance of being wrong; count how often it is passed, and keep the count beside the line.

So the stated chance must itself be checked against what happens. Count the days on which the loss passed the line. If it is 1 day in 100, the bell has earned its place. On the worst days of 1987 and 2008 it was passed far more often than 1 in 100, and the rules that banks follow were rewritten to look further into the tail.

Over 250 trading days a bank’s one-day line at 1% was passed on 7 days. On average it should be passed on 2.5. Is that bad luck, or a wrong line?

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

That prices follow a random walk is the random walk hypothesis; that they take in what is known, so that no reading of public information beats them, is the efficient market hypothesis, in weak, semi-strong and strong forms. The lag product average is the lag-1 autocorrelation. Testing many rules and reporting the one that passed is data snooping, or the multiple comparisons problem. The loss line is value at risk. Days far out in the tail, more often than the bell allows, are fat tails.

Who said it first

Louis Bachelier modelled the prices of French government bonds as a random walk in his thesis of 1900. Alfred Cowles asked in 1933 whether stock market forecasters can forecast, and found that they could not beat chance. Maurice Kendall found in 1953 that weekly changes in British share prices had almost no lag correlation. Paul Samuelson proved in 1965 that properly anticipated prices fluctuate randomly, and Eugene Fama gave the efficient market its name and its forms between 1965 and 1970. Benoit Mandelbrot showed in 1963 that changes in cotton prices had far fatter tails than the bell. Burton Malkiel’s book of 1973 brought the random walk to a wide public. Robert Shiller argued in 1981 that share prices swing far more than the dividends behind them justify. Fama, Lars Peter Hansen and Shiller shared the economics prize in 2013, which is 30·econ.7.5. The scorecards of active funds against their indexes have been published by S&P Dow Jones Indices since 2002.
