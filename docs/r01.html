Rung 1 · theory

Making expressions

We have ideas, sure, but we can’t prove anything yet. Of course not: we still need to earn techniques for connecting ideas here in this rung, for selecting ideas next, for ordering them later. We’re foreclosing a much larger system than what we did in rung 0, but the payoff, you see, is worth every moment.

The ground floor gave you statements, names, and the sameness mark with its techniques. Every statement there stood alone: “Tibbles is grey”, “Rex is Rex”. But the statements people actually reason with are built out of others. “Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown.” “If it rains, the street is wet.” “Ann is not at home.” “Every cat is an animal.” “Somebody left the gate open.” This rung is about those joining words, and its claim is a small, strong one: reasoning is nothing but a short list of techs on statements built with these words. Every proof on the whole ladder, from here to the calculus, is made of those techs and no others.

None of this is new to you. You have joined lines with “and”, “or”, “not” and “if” since you could talk, and you already know how they combine: when “Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown” is so, and when it is not. This rung only writes down what you already do, in marks small enough to carry a long way.

So a proof answers a question about the arrangement: given these statements, does that one follow? The answer is true, or false, or none at all, when the givens can’t reach it. A question that wants true, false or nothing is almost always a question about an arrangement, and a proof is how you answer it.

The rung has two goals. The first is to take rung 0's “so” and “not so” and make them true and false: a value every statement has, in every case. The second is the operators that feed on true and false directly. “Not”, “and”, “or”, “if”, “every” and “some” take statements in and give a statement back, and they are what the statements of an argument are built with.

Each joining word is a word before it is a mark. Learn what the word lets you build, what it lets you take apart, and which tempting step it refuses. Then write the mark.

The survey · what this rung claims, and what you can do once you know it

A plane assembled in midair

This rung is in foreclosure: not only its techs but the proof itself is partly built, and rung 4 finishes it; 1a7 says what is held in trust. We are assembling the plane in midair, so some questions ask for an argument before every piece of it is built.

What it means to say the claim

Take any argument you have ever found convincing. “Ann is either at the bakery or at the library. She is not at the library. So she is at the bakery.” Why does it convince? Not because of Ann, or bakeries. Put anything you like in the places of the names and the plain statements, and it convinces just the same: “The key is either in my coat or in my bag. It is not in my bag. So it is in my coat.” What carries the conviction is the shape. The shape is made by the little words: either, or, not, so. A good argument is good because of its shape and not its subject. The oldest book of logic that has come down to us says it this way: when certain things are laid down, something different from them “necessarily results”.

“Necessarily results” is the whole business. Not “probably”, not “usually”: if the laid-down lines are true, the conclusion cannot fail. On this ladder that phrase has a precise home. 1a1 says what a line is, and 4d what a proof's reasons may be. The ground floor had only the techniques of sameness, and 1a adds unpack. This rung's claim is that the rest of reasoning, all of it, is a handful of further techs. Each tech is attached to a joining word. Once those are laid down, nothing else will ever be needed. A proof of a technique about the calculus, far up the ladder, will be an argument whose every line cites a tech built on this rung, or a technique that was itself proved by them.

Here are the joining words, as words. Each gets its own step, where it is given a mark by a line of the shape “from here on, this mark means…”. That is exactly how 0a and 0b brought in theirs, and 1a its unpack and pack. On this page the words stay words, and no new mark appears.

  • not: “Ann is not at home.” It denies a statement. With it comes the idea of the totally false: a statement that is never true, the thing you reach when you have said something and its denial together.
  • and: “Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown.” It joins. Both parts are claimed.
  • or: “Ann is at the bakery or at the library.” It offers options, and on this ladder it always allows that both may be true; “this or that but not both” has to be said in full.
  • if, then: “If it rains, the street is wet.” A promise: whenever the part after “if” is true, so is the part after “then”. It promises nothing about dry days.
  • exactly when: “The light is on exactly when the switch is up.” A promise each way.
  • every: “Every cat is an animal.” A statement about anything you might be handed.
  • some: “Some cat is asleep.” A statement that there is an example, without saying which.

Where the techs come from

The techs are not invented; they are read off the words. Ask of each word two questions. What does it take to be allowed to say a statement built with it? What are you allowed to do once you have one? Those are the word's techs, the tech that makes it and the tech that uses it. For “and” the answers are nearly too plain to write. To say “P and Q” you need P and you need Q. Having “P and Q”, you may keep either part. For “or” they are less even, and that unevenness is the heart of the rung. To say “P or Q” it is enough to have P. But having “P or Q” does not let you keep a part. You must reason by cases, supposing each option in turn and reaching your conclusion from each. For “if” the techs are the ones everyone already uses and hardly anyone can name. Having “if P then Q” and having P, you may write Q. To prove “if P then Q”, you suppose P for a while and reach Q under that supposition.

That last idea, supposing something for a while, is new on this rung and recurs on every step. From 1b3 on, an argument may open a supposition, reason inside it, and close it with one line that stands outside; refuting is the same shape aimed at the totally false. Nearly everything subtle on this rung is about what may and may not be carried out of a closed supposition.

“Every” and “some” have techs that match “and” and “or”, writ large. To speak of everything, speak of anything: prove the statement of a thing about which you assumed nothing in particular, and you have proved it of every thing. To use an every, apply it to the thing in your hand. To speak of something, show an example. To use a some, give the promised thing a fresh name and reason about it, using nothing but what was promised. The last of these is where most everyday mistakes about “some” live, and 1e names them.

What you can do because you know this

You can build compound statements and take them apart with a clear conscience, because each tech is written down and can be cited by name beside a line. You can refute: show that a supposition leads to something totally false, and conclude its denial. You can speak of everything by proving a thing of anything, and speak of something by producing it. You can say exactly what it would take for a statement to fail. Push a “not” inward through the words, and each word turns into its partner as the “not” passes. And you can spot, and name, the tempting steps that look like techs and are not. Here is a short list, learned on this rung and never forgotten. Concluding the “if” part from the “then” part. Concluding “not then” from “not if”. Picking a side of an “or”. Generalising from the thing you happened to look at. Giving the thing a “some” promises a name that is already in use. Each of these convinces people daily, and each fails for a reason you will be able to state.

There is also a thing you will be told you cannot yet do, and the telling is deliberate. Having refuted “not P”, you have “not not P”. On this rung there is no tech from that to P. It looks as if there should be; most books allow it without comment. This ladder withholds it, because it is not read off the word “not” the way the other techs are. It is a further principle. Rung 7 is where that principle is laid down and its cost examined. Until then, a proof that reaches “not not P” stops there, and says so. You will find that a surprising amount of mathematics never needs the missing step. Knowing exactly where it is needed is a kind of sight.

Think of guesswork as a fine machine. The steps look at its pieces and their motion. The questions ask you to be the mechanic. The ladder makes designers.

That is a first handful of techs. The ladder has seen only the tip of what you will do with them.

And 1·comp asks what a computer checks when it reads if: the condition only, read left first, stopping as soon as the left side fixes the answer.

Which step does what

The order follows the words, each step leaning only on the ones before it. 1a comes first because every other page needs it. It says what an argument is and builds the box a proof on this rung is made in, opened on a supposition and closed with one line outside it. It builds boxes inside boxes. And it builds arguments that start with nothing given and reach statements that are always so, which a technique from nothing may then write anywhere. 1b builds “not”, and with it the totally false, written with a mark of its own, as the statement that is never true. Its techs are refute, clash, and the strange but harmless rule that when nothing is true, everything is permitted. It also gives “is not the same thing as” its mark, as “not” applied to the sameness of 0b, and it marks the deliberate gap. 1c builds “and” and “or”: join, keep either part, add an option, reason by cases, and the word “disjunct” for a side of an “or”. 1d builds “if” and “exactly when”: carry an if through, prove an if by supposing its hypothesis, and the words “hypothesis” and “conclusion”. 1e builds “every” and “some”, with the letter after each as a placeholder that may be renamed. It gives the techs for each, why their order matters when both appear, and how a “not” passes through them.

Those are the proof steps, and with them the rung's claim is established. The rest is support and technique. 1f gives each joining word a table: for every way its letters can come out, what the whole comes to. The tables agree with the techs exactly, failing only at the deliberate gap, which is the best evidence that the gap is real. 1g is the rulebook. It holds techniques, each with a name, for rewriting a statement into another that says the same thing. It says which directions this rung licenses and which wait for rung 7. 1h is practice with “every” and “some” by hand, including the oldest lines of logic and a diagram of how they oppose one another. 1i is about the shape of proofs: straight through, turned around, split into cases, killed by an example, refuted. It ends the rung's new techs with a benchmark line.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 1a carries a green box: the ledger; a description whose books don't balance is wrong, even when every statement in it is true. 1e carries another: two sizes of truth. Say whether a statement is about each one, some one, or the crowd as a whole.

Rung 2 asks what a collection is. Every principle there has to be stated with “every”, “if”, “and” and “not”. That is why logic comes earliest and comes as a block. It is also why you will see this rung's techs cited, by name, in every proof above it.

Who said it first

Aristotle, in Athens, was the earliest thinker we know of to say that a good argument is good because of its shape and not its subject. The phrase “necessarily results” is his:

“A syllogism … is a line in which, certain things being laid down, something else different from the premises necessarily results, in consequence of their existence.”

Aristotle, Prior Analytics I.1 (24b18), trans. O. F. Owen, 1853

1h's oldest lines of logic are Aristotle's four forms (every, no, some, some not), and their diagram is the square of opposition.
