37e · proof

Fading, bouncing, passing through

Thunder close by is a crack; thunder ten kilometres away is a low rumble. The same sound left the cloud, and the air on the way took the high part and let the low part through. This step follows a sound through what it travels in and into what it meets. The air takes a fixed share of it every metre, more of the high notes than the low. At a wall, or at the surface of a lake, a share bounces back and a share goes on, and the shares are fixed by how stiff and heavy each side is. A share that bounces comes back late, and if it is late enough the ear hears it as an echo.

A medium takes a fixed share of a sound each metre, so the sound fades by a fixed number of decibels a metre, faster for higher notes. At a boundary the share that bounces depends only on how unlike the two sides are. A bounce from far enough away is heard as an echo.

37e1A sound fades

Spreading (37c3) thins a sound without taking any of its power. A medium also takes power: each swing of the air loses a little to heat. Let I(x) be the intensity of a sound running along a pipe, where spreading plays no part.

From here on, a medium takes the share α per metre when the intensity’s rate along the pipe is I′(x) = −α I(x), with α > 0. That is 24a3’s growth at its own rate, with a minus sign.

Fading technique: a medium that takes a fixed share per metre makes a sound fade by the same number of decibels every metre.

I' = -\alpha I,\ \ I(0) = I_0 @@ the share per metre, and the start
I(x) = I_0\, e^{-\alpha x} @@ 24a3 and 17f2: the only solution
10\log_{10}\tfrac{I(x)}{I_0} = -10\,\alpha x \log_{10} e @@ 17c2, powers
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a loss of } 4.343\,\alpha \text{ dB per metre}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 10 log₁₀ e ≈ 4.343

37e2High notes fade first

What follows is laid down, measured: the share air takes grows with frequency, roughly as its square. In air at 20 °C and half-saturated with water, a 1000 Hz note loses about 0.005 dB a metre, a 4000 Hz note about 0.03, and an 8000 Hz note about 0.1.

Far-is-low technique: the air passes low notes and takes high ones, so a far sound is the near sound with its top cut away. Proof: by the fading technique each frequency loses its own number of decibels per metre, and the numbers above grow with frequency.

1000 \text{ Hz}: 0.005 \times 10\,000 = 50 \text{ dB} @@ weakened, still heard
8000 \text{ Hz}: 0.1 \times 10\,000 = 1000 \text{ dB} @@ gone
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the crack is lost, the rumble arrives}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the far-is-low technique

So the air is a filter (32i2), one that keeps the low frequencies. 37h2 gives filters of that kind a name.

37e3At a boundary, a share bounces

A sound runs along a pipe and meets a change of medium: air to water, say. From here on, the impedance of a medium is Z = ρc, its density times its speed. For air Z₁ ≈ 413; for water Z₂ ≈ 1000 × 1480 = 1 480 000. Two facts are held in trust at the boundary: the pressure is the same on both sides, and the air moves at the same speed on both sides. A sound’s motion is its pressure divided by Z, with a minus sign for a sound running back.

Bounce technique: at a boundary met head on, the share of the intensity that bounces back is ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(Z₂ + Z₁))², and the rest goes on. It is the same shape as 36·phys5’s share of light at a pane.

p_{\mathrm{in}} + p_{\mathrm{back}} = p_{\mathrm{on}} @@ equal pressure
(p_{\mathrm{in}} - p_{\mathrm{back}})/Z_1 = p_{\mathrm{on}}/Z_2 @@ equal motion
p_{\mathrm{back}} / p_{\mathrm{in}} = (Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1) @@ put the first into the second and solve
\htmlClass{new}{R = \big(\tfrac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}\big)^2,\ \ 1 - R = \tfrac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ intensity is the square over Z (37c1)

Air to water: 1 − R = 4 × 413 × 1 480 000 / (1 480 413)² ≈ 0.0011. About a tenth of one percent goes in, a loss of about 29.5 dB, and the same going out. A shout from the dock barely reaches a swimmer under the surface of Flathead Lake.

37e4Echoes

A sound that bounces off a cliff d metres away comes back after it has run 2d metres, so its delay is 2d/c. What follows is laid down, measured: the ear joins a bounce to the sound that made it when the delay is under about 50 milliseconds, and hears a separate echo when it is longer.

Echo technique: a single wall is heard as an echo only if it is more than about 8.6 m away. Proof: 2d/c > 0.05 s exactly when d > 0.05 × 343/2 ≈ 8.6 m.

t = 2d/c @@ out and back
d = 170 \text{ m}:\ t = 340/343 \approx 0.99 \text{ s} @@ about a second
\htmlClass{new}{d = ct/2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the depth sounder of 37·nav runs this backwards

In a room the bounces come from every wall, many times, and each comes back a little weaker. They are joined to the sound, not heard apart, and make it ring on after it stops; 37·eng measures how long.

The accepted names

The share per metre is the attenuation coefficient, and its loss in air is atmospheric absorption, tabulated in the standard ISO 9613-1. Z = ρc is the specific acoustic impedance, and the bounce technique gives the reflection and transmission coefficients at normal incidence. The joining of bounces under about 50 ms is the precedence effect, or Haas effect. The ringing on in a room is reverberation.

Who said it first

George Gabriel Stokes worked out in 1845 that the loss of sound to the air’s stickiness grows as the square of the frequency; the larger loss to the water in the air was found by Vern Knudsen in the 1930s. Helmut Haas measured the joining of echoes in 1949.

A proof that is not allowed

“The pressure of a sound going from air into water is nearly doubled at the surface, since p_on/p_in = 2Z₂/(Z₁ + Z₂) ≈ 2. So the sound in the water is stronger.” Not allowed: intensity is the square of pressure over Z (37c1), and Z₂ is 3600 times Z₁; the doubled pressure carries a thousandth of the power.
