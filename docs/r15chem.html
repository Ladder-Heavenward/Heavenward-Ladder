Why Does a Squeezed Gas Push Back?

Why does a squeezed gas push back? Squeeze a gas into half the room and it pushes back twice as hard. Warm it and it pushes harder still. For most gases, most of the time, one short law ties pressure, volume, temperature and the amount of gas together. The same law, read for sugar or salt dissolved in water, gives the pressure that drives water across the skin of a cell. The law is not quite right, and the way it fails says something about molecules. This page states the law, corrects it, and then asks rung 15's question of it: how much work does a gas do as it pushes a piston out? The answer is the area under its pressure curve.

For an ideal gas, pressure times volume is fixed by the amount and the temperature. The corrected law adjusts the volume for the room the molecules take, and the pressure for their pull on each other. The work of an expanding gas is the area under its pressure, read against volume. The area under one over the volume squared comes out exactly by a collapse, and the area under one over the volume only by squeezing.

Pressure

From here on, the pressure on a flat wall is the force pressing on it divided by its area (15a's box-area axiom for a box-shaped wall): p = F/A, in newtons per square metre. Its accepted name is pressure; the unit is the pascal, Pa = N/m². Two other units are common. The bar is 10⁵ Pa. The atmosphere, 1 atm = 101 325 Pa, is the air's pressure at sea level on an average day.

At the summit of Mount Shasta, 4322 m up, the air's pressure is about 0.60 atm. The air above a square metre at sea level weighs about 101 kN; at the summit, about 61 kN.

The ideal gas law

Three measured laws, each a physical premise. The pressure-volume law (Boyle's), 1662: at a fixed temperature, p times V stays the same as a fixed amount of gas is squeezed or let out. The volume-temperature law (Charles's and Gay-Lussac's), around 1787 and 1802: at a fixed pressure, V is proportional to the temperature counted from absolute zero, in kelvin (11·chem). The equal-volumes law (Avogadro's), 1811: equal volumes of any gases, at the same pressure and temperature, hold equal numbers of molecules. Together:

pV = nRT, with n the amount in moles (5·chem), T in kelvin, and R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), or 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K). Its accepted name is the ideal gas law; R is the gas constant, and a gas that obeys the law exactly is an ideal gas.

V = \tfrac{nRT}{p} = \tfrac{1 \cdot 8.314 \cdot 273.15}{101\,325} \approx 0.0224\ \text{m}^3 @@ one mole at 0 °C and 1 atm: 22.4 L
p = \tfrac{nRT}{V} = \tfrac{1 \cdot 0.08314 \cdot 300}{1.0} \approx 24.9\ \text{bar} @@ one mole of CO₂ held in 1 L at 300 K, if it were ideal

The correction for real molecules

In 1873 a physicist argued that the law fails for two reasons a chemist could see. The molecules take up room themselves, so the room they have to move in is less than V. And they pull on each other, as 14·chem's curve said they do when they are not too close, so the pressure on the wall is less than it would be. His corrected law:

(p + a n²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT, or p = nRT/(V − nb) − a n²/V². The two constants (a) and (b) are measured for each gas; for CO₂, (a) ≈ 3.64 L²·bar/mol² and (b) ≈ 0.0427 L/mol.

\text{ideal: } p = 24.9\ \text{bar} @@ above
\tfrac{nRT}{V - nb} = \tfrac{24.94}{1 - 0.0427} \approx 26.05 @@ less room: a higher pressure
\tfrac{a n^2}{V^2} = 3.64 @@ the pull between molecules: a lower one
\text{corrected: } p \approx \htmlClass{new}{22.4\ \text{bar}} @@ the measured value is close to this; the ideal law is about 11% high

The maker of the corrected law argued that the gas and the liquid are not two different kinds of matter, but one, with the same molecules of the same size pulling on each other in the same way. A single equation, with the room the molecules take and their mutual pull put in, should describe both.

Work is an area

A second physical premise. When a gas pushes a piston out slowly, so that its pressure stays the same throughout it, consider the work it does as its volume goes from V₁ to V₂. It is W = ∫ p dV, with p as a function of V. Its accepted name is the pressure–volume work. At a fixed pressure it is a box, p(V₂ − V₁). In general it is the area under the gas's curve in the p–V plane.

The two parts of the corrected law's p ask for two areas: one under 1/(V − nb), one under 1/V². The second comes out exactly.

Inverse-square-area technique: for 0 < u < v, ∫ᵤᵛ dx/x² = 1/u − 1/v.

\text{on } [x_{i-1}, x_i]:\ \ \tfrac{1}{x_i^2} \le \tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}x_i} \le \tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}^2} @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: x_{i−1} ≤ x_i, all positive
\tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}x_i}\,\Delta x_i = \tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}} - \tfrac{1}{x_i} @@ Δxᵢ = xᵢ − xᵢ₋₁; divide through
L \le \textstyle\sum \Big(\tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}} - \tfrac{1}{x_i}\Big) \le U @@ 1/x² is decreasing: mᵢ = 1/xᵢ², Mᵢ = 1/xᵢ₋₁²
\textstyle\sum \Big(\tfrac{1}{x_{i-1}} - \tfrac{1}{x_i}\Big) = \tfrac1u - \tfrac1v @@ collapsing-sum (15g), for every partition
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_u^v \tfrac{dx}{x^2} = \tfrac1u - \tfrac1v} \quad \blacksquare @@ exactly-one-between (15c), with 1/x² integrable by continuous-is-integrable (15f)
  
W_{\text{pull}} = \textstyle\int_{V_1}^{V_2} \tfrac{a n^2}{V^2}\,dV = a n^2\Big(\tfrac{1}{V_1} - \tfrac{1}{V_2}\Big) @@ areas-scale; inverse-square-area
\text{CO}_2,\ 1\ \text{mol},\ 1\ \text{L} \to 2\ \text{L}:\ \ 3.64 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1.82\ \text{L·bar} @@ 1 L·bar = 10⁻³ m³ · 10⁵ Pa = 100 J
= \htmlClass{new}{182\ \text{J}} @@ work the gas does not do on the piston, because its molecules hold on to each other

The first area, under nRT/V for an ideal gas at a fixed temperature, has no formula on the ladder yet. It can be squeezed. For ∫₁² dV/V, ten equal pieces give a lower sum of 0.6688 and an upper sum of 0.7188. So the work of one mole of ideal gas doubling its volume at 300 K is between 0.6688 and 0.7188 times RT = 2494 J: between about 1668 J and 1793 J.

Osmotic pressure

A membrane that lets water through but not sugar, set between pure water and sugar water, lets the water move toward the sugar. The flow stops only if the sugar side is pressed with an extra pressure Π. Π is a Greek capital letter, the chemists' usual name for this pressure. Here it is only a letter, and claims nothing. A botanist measured Π in 1877 with membranes of copper ferrocyanide laid inside clay pots. In 1887 a chemist saw that those numbers obeyed the gas law, a third physical premise:

Π = cRT, with c the concentration of dissolved particles, in moles per cubic metre (13·chem's concentration, times 1000 from mol/L). Its accepted name is osmotic pressure.

\text{blood: } c \approx 300\ \text{mol/m}^3,\ T = 310\ \text{K} @@ the dissolved particles in blood plasma, at 37 °C
\Pi \approx 300 \cdot 8.314 \cdot 310 \approx 7.7 \times 10^5\ \text{Pa} \approx 7.6\ \text{atm} @@ a drip of 0.9% salt water matches it
\text{seawater: } c \approx 1100\ \text{mol/m}^3,\ T = 288\ \text{K} @@ the salt splits into two kinds of particle in the water
\Pi \approx 2.6 \times 10^6\ \text{Pa} \approx \htmlClass{new}{26\ \text{atm}} @@ the least pressure that pushes fresh water out of seawater through a membrane

A sockeye salmon leaving the Strait of Georgia for the Fraser River crosses from water at about 26 atm of osmotic pressure to water at nearly none. Its gills must stop pumping salt out and start pumping it in.

Who said it first

Van der Waals argued that the gas and the liquid are not two different kinds of matter, but one, with the same molecules of the same size pulling on each other in the same way; and that a single equation, with the room the molecules take and their mutual pull put in, should describe both.

van der Waals, Over de continuiteit van den gas- en vloeistoftoestand (1873); paraphrase

The pressure-volume law is Robert Boyle’s (1662); the volume-temperature law is Jacques Charles’s and Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac’s; the equal-volumes law is Amedeo Avogadro’s (1811). The corrected law is Johannes van der Waals’s (1873). The membranes of 1877 are Wilhelm Pfeffer’s, and Jacobus van ’t Hoff read his numbers as the gas law in 1887; the accepted name of Π = cRT is van ’t Hoff’s law. The corrected law’s accepted name is the van der Waals equation, and its two constants are the van der Waals constants. In the closer, the chemist and the physicist are Wilhelm Ostwald and Ernst Mach.
