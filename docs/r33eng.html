Why Do Corners Crack?

Why do corners crack? A concrete wall with a doorway cut in it cracks from the top corners of the door, slanting up and away, years after it was poured. A steel bracket bent into an L breaks on the inside of the bend, not the outside. The windows of an airliner are rounded at every corner, and the first jet airliners, whose openings had sharper corners, broke up in the air. What makes the question hard is that the corner is not where the load is. The weight is spread over the whole wall, the twist over the whole shaft. Something about the shape gathers it. This page finds what, with the balanced functions of 33d. A twisted bar’s stress comes from a potential with a source, and near a corner the potential looks like one of the real parts of rung 26, a power of the distance from the corner. Whether that power makes the stress fade or grow depends only on how wide the corner is. Then the page puts the bar on a grid, as 33i did, and watches the grid fail to settle at the one place that matters, and a round hole double the stress around it.

The stress in a twisted bar is the slope of a potential that is 0 on the edge. Near a corner of opening α the potential goes as a power π/α of the distance, so the stress goes as the power π/α − 1: it fades at an outside corner and grows past every bound at an inside one. A grid confirms it by never settling there, and a round hole in a sheet that slides doubles the stress at its edge. Stress at a point is one symmetric grid, whose two axes carry the greatest pull and push, and a beam sags in proportion to the fourth power of its span.

33eng1A Twisted Bar

Twist a long straight bar, a drive shaft, by a fixed angle per metre. Each cross-section turns, and the steel in it is sheared. A measured law, held in trust, packed into one function: there is a stress potential φ(x, y) on the cross-section with Δφ = −2 inside (in units set by the steel and the twist), φ = 0 on the edge, and the shear at each point has size |∇φ| and runs along the level curves of φ. So φ is 33j’s still state with an even source, held at 0 on the edge.

Round-shaft technique: in a round shaft of radius R, φ = (R² − r²)/2, and the shear is r: greatest at the skin and 0 at the centre.

\tfrac1r\big(r\,\varphi^{\prime}\big)^{\prime} = -2 @@ the round-rate technique (33g1), with no turn
r\,\varphi^{\prime} = -r^2,\ \ \varphi = -\tfrac{r^2}{2} + C @@ finite at the centre, as on 33j4
\varphi(R) = 0 \Rightarrow \varphi = \tfrac{R^2 - r^2}{2} @@ the edge
\htmlClass{new}{|\nabla\varphi| = |\varphi^{\prime}(r)| = r} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uphill vector (28k) points along the reach

So in a round shaft the stress is gentle and even: it grows steadily to the skin. A shaft with a flat cut into it, or a square bar, or an L-shaped bracket, is not round, and the edge bends the level curves. Where the edge turns a corner, the level curves have to turn with it.

33eng2The Power at a Corner

Put the tip of a corner at 0, with the steel filling the wedge 0 < θ < α. Very near the tip the source −2 hardly matters against what the edges force, and φ is close to a balanced function that is 0 on both faces of the wedge.

Corner-stress technique: φ = r^(π/α) sin(πθ/α) is balanced in the wedge and 0 on both faces; its stress is (π/α) r^(π/α − 1), which heads to 0 at the tip when α < π and passes every bound when α > π.

z^{\pi/\alpha} = r^{\pi/\alpha}e^{i\pi\theta/\alpha} @@ a power of z by the logarithm of 26c, with θ in (0, α): one value, with a complex slope in the open wedge
\varphi = \operatorname{Im} z^{\pi/\alpha} = r^{\pi/\alpha}\sin\tfrac{\pi\theta}{\alpha} \text{ is balanced} @@ the real-part technique (33d1), on −iz^(π/α)
\varphi = 0 \text{ at } \theta = 0 \text{ and } \theta = \alpha @@ sin 0 = sin π = 0
|\nabla\varphi|^2 = \varphi_r^2 + \tfrac{1}{r^2}\varphi_\theta^2 = \big(\tfrac{\pi}{\alpha}\big)^2r^{2\pi/\alpha - 2} @@ the slopes along the reach and round the turn; sin² + cos² = 1
\htmlClass{new}{|\nabla\varphi| = \tfrac{\pi}{\alpha}\,r^{\pi/\alpha - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the power is above 0 when α < π, below 0 when α > π

An outside corner of a square bar has α = π/2: the stress goes as r, and fades at the tip. The corners of a square shaft are the safest steel in it. An inside corner, the inner bend of an L, has α = 3π/2: the stress goes as r^(−1/3), and the closer to the tip, the larger, without bound. A crack is a corner with α = 2π: r^(−1/2). Real steel is not endless in strength, so at an inside corner it gives: it yields, or it cracks. The corner crack of the doorway is the r^(−1/3), written in concrete.

33eng3The Grid That Never Settles

Put an L-shaped bar on a grid: the square from −1 to 1 each way with the quarter x > 0, y < 0 cut away. Its inside corner is at 0. Solve Δφ = −2 by 33i’s four-neighbour rule, with the source added: each inside mark is the average of its four neighbours plus h²/2. Read the stress next to the corner as φ at the mark (−h, 0) over h.

h = \tfrac18:\ 1.176,\ \ h = \tfrac1{16}:\ 1.544,\ \ h = \tfrac1{32}:\ 1.976 @@ the stress next to the corner; solved on a computer
h = \tfrac1{64}:\ 2.504,\ \ h = \tfrac1{128}:\ 3.162 @@ still growing
\text{ratios: } 1.312,\ 1.280,\ 1.268,\ 1.263 @@ each halving of the gap
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the ratios head to } 2^{1/3} \approx 1.260} @@ while φ at (−½, ½) settles: 0.2592, 0.2610, 0.2617, 0.2620, 0.2620

Grid-corner technique: near an inside corner of 3π/2, the grid’s stress at the mark next to the tip grows by 2^(1/3) each time the gap is halved, and never settles. The reason is a scaling. The corner shape φ = r^(2/3) sin(2θ/3) obeys φ(hx, hy) = h^(2/3) φ(x, y). A quotient read across marks a gap h apart is φ at those marks over h, so it is h^(2/3)/h = h^(−1/3) times the same quotient at h = 1. Halve h and it grows by 2^(1/3).

So the grid is honest. Away from the corner, two runs at h and h/2 agree, as 33i’s cloud asks. At the corner they never agree, and that disagreement is the warning. An engineer who printed the stress at the corner from one run would print a number that only says how fine the grid was. Breaking a shape into small pieces, triangles instead of squares, has an accepted name, the finite element method, and it meets the same corner in the same way.

33eng4A Round Hole, and a Rounded Corner

A wide sheet slides along itself: each line across it is shifted by w(x, y) out of the sheet, with w = (τ/μ)y far away, so the shear far away is τ. A measured law, held in trust: w is balanced, and at a free edge no shear crosses it, so the slope of w across the edge is 0. Cut a round hole of radius R at 0. Here μ (little mu) means the steel's stiffness under shear.

Round-hole technique: w = (τ/μ)(r + R²/r) sin θ is balanced outside the hole, has no slope across its edge, matches the far sheet, and its shear at the edge is 2τ|cos θ|: a round hole doubles the stress beside it.

w = \tfrac{\tau}{\mu}\operatorname{Im}\big(z - \tfrac{R^2}{z}\big) = \tfrac{\tau}{\mu}\big(r + \tfrac{R^2}{r}\big)\sin\theta @@ Im(−R²/z) = (R²/r) sin θ; balanced by the real-part technique (33d1)
w_r = \tfrac{\tau}{\mu}\big(1 - \tfrac{R^2}{r^2}\big)\sin\theta = 0 \text{ at } r = R @@ no slope across the edge
w \to \tfrac{\tau}{\mu}\,r\sin\theta = \tfrac{\tau}{\mu}\,y \text{ far away} @@ R²/r heads to 0
\mu|\nabla w| = \tfrac{\mu}{R}|w_\theta| = \tau\big(1 + 1\big)|\cos\theta| \text{ at } r = R @@ at the edge only the slope round the hole is left
\htmlClass{new}{\text{greatest shear } 2\tau,\ \text{at } \theta = 0 \text{ and } \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two sides of the hole across the sliding

So a round hole doubles the stress, whatever its size, but no more. A round hole is a corner with no tip. A sharp inside corner rounded off to a radius ρ behaves, a little way from the curve, like the corner-stress technique’s r^(−1/3), so the stress on the curve is capped near ρ^(−1/3) times a number set by the load. Halve ρ and the peak grows by 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26; make ρ ten times larger and the peak falls by 10^(1/3) ≈ 2.15. That is why door frames, brackets and window openings are cut with radii instead of corners. For a sheet stretched rather than slid, the measured factor at a round hole is 3, not 2, and the same rounding rule holds.

33eng6A Push per Area on Every Face

16·eng called a push per unit of area across a cut its stress, and used one cut, straight across the beam. A cut can face any way. In a flat sheet, write the push per area on a cut whose vector out of the cut is n, of length 1, as σn. Two measured laws of 12·eng make σ a grid, and a symmetric one. The balance of forces on a small triangle cut from the sheet makes the push on its slanted face the sum of the pushes on the other two, so n ↦ σn adds and stretches. The balance of turning effects on a small square makes the shear along its top equal the shear along its side, or the square would spin: the grid’s two corner numbers agree.

Stress-axes technique: at every point of a sheet there are two cuts at right angles that carry no shear, only a straight pull or push, and these two are the largest and smallest straight stresses on any cut; a sheet in pure shear τ has them at 45° to the shear, a pull τ and a push τ.

\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{xx} & \tau \\ \tau & \sigma_{yy} \end{pmatrix} @@ a symmetric grid: the corner numbers agree
\sigma u_1 = \lambda_1 u_1,\ \allowbreak \ \sigma u_2 = \lambda_2 u_2,\ \allowbreak \ u_1 \perp u_2 @@ the right-angled-stretches technique (31d2): on these cuts the push is straight
n = c_1u_1 + c_2u_2,\ \allowbreak \ c_1^2 + c_2^2 = 1:\ \ n\cdot\sigma n = \lambda_1c_1^2 + \lambda_2c_2^2 @@ the straight part on any cut lies between λ₂ and λ₁
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & \tau \\ \tau & 0 \end{pmatrix}:\ \ \lambda = \pm\tau,\ \allowbreak \ u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,\ \allowbreak \pm 1) @@ pure shear, as in the sliding sheet of 33·eng.4
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{greatest }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{pull, } \tau,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{acts at } 45^\circ \text{ to }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{the }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{shear}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the stress-axes technique

Concrete and chalk hold a push well and a pull badly, and they crack straight across the greatest pull. A wall carries its weight round a doorway as shear beside the top corners, which the corner-stress technique gathers there; the greatest pull runs at 45° to that shear, and the crack runs across the pull, slanting up and away from the corner. Twist a stick of chalk and it breaks along a spiral at 45° to its length, for the same reason: 33·eng.1’s twisted shaft is in pure shear. Mild steel is the other way round. It gives first to shear, and a twisted steel bar fails straight across.

33eng7Stretch per Length, and How Far a Beam Sags

A rod pulled by F stretches by ΔL. Two rods side by side share F, and a rod twice as long stretches twice as far, so a measured law is put per area and per length: the strain ε = ΔL/L, the stretch per unit of length, is in proportion to the stress, σ = Eε, while the material springs back. E is the material’s stiffness, about 200 GPa for steel and 11 GPa for Douglas fir along the grain. A spring of 16·phys is such a rod, with k = F/ΔL = EA/L. A steel rod 2 m long and 1 cm² across, pulled by 10 kN, carries 100 MPa, a strain of 5 × 10⁻⁴, and stretches 1 mm.

In a bent beam a flat cut across it stays flat, a measured law of thin beams, so a strip at depth y below the middle is stretched by y/ρ for each unit of length, where ρ is the radius of the bend. With σ = Eε, that earns 16·eng’s stretch law, stress in proportion to depth, from the stiffness.

Bent-beam technique: a beam of stiffness E whose cut has I = ∫y² dA bends under a bending moment M to the curvature 1/ρ = M/(EI); for a rectangle b wide and h deep, I = bh³/12, and an even load w on a span L sags (5/384)wL⁴/(EI) at the middle.

\sigma(y) = E\,\tfrac{y}{\rho} @@ flat cuts stay flat; σ = Eε
M = \textstyle\int y\,\sigma(y)\,dA = \tfrac{E}{\rho}\int y^2\,dA = \tfrac{EI}{\rho} @@ each strip turns about the middle (16·eng)
I = \textstyle\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} b\,y^2\,dy = \tfrac{bh^3}{12} @@ the area-under-a-power technique (15i5)
z'' = \tfrac{M}{EI} = \tfrac{w}{2EI}\,x(L - x),\ \allowbreak \ z(0) = z(L) = 0 @@ for a gentle bend the curvature is the second slope of the sag z; 16·eng’s M
\htmlClass{new}{|z(\tfrac L2)| = \tfrac{5}{384}\,\tfrac{wL^4}{EI}} \quad \blacksquare @@ undo the slope twice (16), the two ends fixing the two constants

16·eng’s joist in Salinas, 38 mm by 235 mm, Douglas fir, 4.2 m span, 0.96 kN/m: I = 4.11 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴, and it sags about 8.6 mm at the middle, inside the 11.7 mm, a 360th of the span, that floors are commonly held to so that plaster below them does not crack. Laid flat, I shrinks by (235/38)² ≈ 38, and the sag would grow 38 times, if the wood held at all. The sag grows as the fourth power of the span: a joist 20% longer sags twice as much. So a long floor is stopped by its bounce before its strength.

The accepted names

The stress potential is Prandtl’s stress function for the torsion of a prismatic bar, and Δφ = −2 (times 2Gθ′, with G the stiffness of the steel against shear and θ′ the twist per length) is its Poisson equation. The shear is τ = |∇φ|. The corner-stress technique gives the stress singularity at a re-entrant corner, r^(π/α − 1); for a crack the stress intensity factor measures the size of the r^(−1/2) term. The ratio of greatest stress to far stress is the stress concentration factor: 2 for a round hole in anti-plane shear, 3 for a round hole in a plate under tension (Kirsch, 1898), and 1 + 2a/b for an elliptical hole (Inglis, 1913). Rounding a corner is adding a fillet. Breaking a shape into small triangles and solving on them is the finite element method, and a run of finer grids is a mesh convergence study. The stress grid is the stress tensor, its axes the principal directions and λ₁, λ₂ the principal stresses; the shear at 45° is the greatest shear stress. Strain is the stretch per unit of length, E is Young’s modulus, and σ = Eε is Hooke’s law for a material; flat cuts that stay flat are the assumption of Euler–Bernoulli beam theory, I is the second moment of area, and 1/ρ = M/(EI) the moment–curvature relation. The sag of a beam is its deflection, and L/360 a common deflection limit.

Who said it first

Adhémar Barré de Saint-Venant solved the twisting of bars in 1855, and Ludwig Prandtl gave the stress function and its soap-film picture in 1903. Ernst Gustav Kirsch found the factor 3 at a round hole in 1898. Charles Inglis gave the oval hole in 1913, and Alan Griffith explained in 1921 why cracks grow from the energy they release. Max Williams gave the stresses at the corners of wedges in 1952, and George Irwin the stress intensity factor in 1957. The airliner of 1954 was the de Havilland Comet; the inquiry found fatigue cracks grown from the corners of cut-outs in the cabin roof. Richard Courant proposed solving on triangles in 1943, and engineers at Boeing and elsewhere built the finite element method in the 1950s. Robert Hooke stated that the stretch is in proportion to the force in 1678, and Thomas Young measured the stiffness of a material as one number in 1807. Augustin-Louis Cauchy showed in 1822 that the push on every cut through a point is fixed by one symmetric grid. Jacob Bernoulli and Leonhard Euler worked out the bending of beams in the 1700s, with flat cuts that stay flat.

Wrong moves, worked

“Steel near the corner is under endless stress by the corner-stress technique. Steel cannot bear endless stress. So every L-bracket breaks at once.” Not allowed: the technique is for a material that stays elastic however hard it is pushed. Real steel yields a little at the tip, the corner rounds itself on a tiny scale, and the stress is capped. What the technique does say is that the tip is where the yielding, and later the cracking, begins.
