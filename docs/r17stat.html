17·stat · statistics

Why do so many numbers start with 1?

Why do so many numbers start with 1? Take the people in each county of Oregon, the lengths of rivers, the prices in a shop, or the powers of two. Read only the first digit of each. You might guess that each of the nine digits comes first about as often as any other. It does not. In many lists about three numbers in ten start with 1, and fewer than one in twenty start with 9. People noticed this in old books of log tables, whose first pages were worn far more than their last. What makes it hard is that no one list explains it. A county is not a river, and a price is not a power. This page reads the first digit off a log scale, with 17’s logs and 15·stat’s chances as areas. It finds the share of each digit when a list is spread evenly on that scale. Then it checks the guess on real lists, finds where it fails, and uses it to spot figures that someone made up.

Put the decimal point after the first digit, and the log of the number keeps only its part after the point. The first digit is 1 exactly when that part is below the log of 2, which is about three tenths. If that part lands anywhere from 0 to 1 with no place favoured, the chance of a first digit is the gap between the logs of that digit and the next. A change of units moves every log by the same amount, and an even spread stays even. Lists that cover many powers of ten often come close. Lists held inside one power of ten, or chosen by people, do not.

Worn pages

Before calculators, people multiplied by looking up logs in a printed book of tables. The book ran from numbers starting with 1 at the front to numbers starting with 9 at the back. In 1881 an astronomer noticed that the front pages of such books were far more worn than the back ones. People looked up numbers starting with 1 more often than numbers starting with 9. He guessed a rule for how much more often. Fifty-seven years later an engineer counted the first digits of more than twenty thousand numbers, from twenty different lists, and found the same rule.

From here on, on this page, log means log₁₀ (17g), so log 10 = 1 and log 2 ≈ 0.3010. The first digit of a positive number is its first digit that is not 0: the first digit of 0.0472 is 4.

The first digit lives in the log

Write a positive X as a number from 1 up to 10, times a power of ten: 47 300 = 4.73 × 10⁴. The power is ⌊log X⌋, by the floor technique (11f). What is left over is the part of log X after the point.

From here on, for X > 0, m(X) = log X − ⌊log X⌋. So 0 ≤ m(X) < 1, and X = 10m(X) × 10⌊log X⌋. Shifting the decimal point changes ⌊log X⌋ and leaves m(X) alone.

First-digit technique: a positive number starts with the digit d exactly when the part of its log after the point lies from log d up to log(d + 1). In marks: X has first digit d exactly when log d ≤ m(X) < log(d + 1).

17h built a ruler with each number placed at its log. The first-digit technique says: cut that ruler from 1 to 10, mark the nine digits, and the stretch for 1 is the widest. It runs from 0 to 0.301, nearly a third of the ruler. The stretch for 9 runs from 0.954 to 1, less than a twentieth.

Spread evenly on the log scale

Now a guess. Take a number from a list. Suppose its m lands by the density 1 on [0, 1] (15·stat): no part of the log ruler is favoured. Then the chance of each first digit is the width of its stretch.

Log-spread technique: if the part of the log after the point is spread evenly, a first digit d turns up with chance log(1 + 1/d). So 1 comes first about 30.1% of the time, and 9 about 4.6%.

\text{chance of first digit } d = \text{chance that } m \in [\log d,\ \log(d + 1)) @@ first-digit technique
= \textstyle\int_{\log d}^{\log(d + 1)} 1\,dt = \log(d + 1) - \log d @@ range-chance (15·stat), with the density 1
\htmlClass{new}{= \log\big(1 + \tfrac1d\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ log-of-a-quotient (17c)
\textstyle\sum_{d=1}^{9} \big(\log(d + 1) - \log d\big) = \log 10 - \log 1 = 1 @@ the sum collapses: the nine chances add to 1

The same width gives more. The chance that a number starts with 1, 2 or 3 is log 4 ≈ 0.602. The chance that its first two digits are 1 then 0 is log(11/10) ≈ 0.041.

Why an even spread?

Nothing so far says why a real list should be spread evenly on the log scale. Here is one reason, and it can be proved. Measure the rivers of Oregon in miles, and then in kilometres. Every length is multiplied by about 1.609. A rule about first digits that holds for real lengths should not care which unit was used.

Unit-change technique: multiplying every number by the same c > 0 keeps an even spread of m even. So the shares of the log-spread technique survive every change of units.

\log(cX) = \log X + \log c @@ log-of-a-product (17c)
s = m(c) \in [0, 1):\ \ m(cX) = m(X) + s \text{ when } m(X) + s \lt 1,\ \text{else } m(X) + s - 1 @@ the whole number ⌊log c⌋ only moves the point
m(cX) \in [u, v) \iff m(X) \in [u - s, v - s) \text{ or } m(X) \in [u - s + 1, v - s + 1) @@ for 0 ≤ u ≤ v ≤ 1; each piece cut to [0, 1)
\text{the pieces inside } [0, 1) \text{ have widths adding to } v - u @@ the second piece is the first moved up by 1; interval-cut-in-two (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{chance that } m(cX) \in [u, v) = v - u} \quad \blacksquare @@ range-chance: still the density 1

The technique goes one way. It says an even spread is kept. It does not say that a list kept by every change of units must be spread evenly. That turned-round claim is true for first digits, but its proof is much higher up the ladder.

A second reason is a guess, not a proof. When a list runs across many powers of ten, and its numbers pile up smoothly with no sharp peaks, the cuts at the whole numbers fall in every part of the pile. Then m comes out nearly even. Growth that multiplies, as in 17j, does this: a quantity that grows by a steady factor walks along the log ruler in equal steps.

Checking it on real lists

2^1, 2^2, \ldots, 2^{100}:\ \ 30,\ 17,\ 13,\ 10,\ 7,\ 7,\ 6,\ 5,\ 5 @@ counts of first digits 1 to 9; the law gives 30.1, 17.6, 12.5, 9.7, 7.9, 6.7, 5.8, 5.1, 4.6
\text{the } 36 \text{ counties of Oregon, people in } 2020:\ \ 9,\ 8,\ 3,\ 2,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 5,\ 1 @@ the law gives 10.8, 6.3, 4.5, 3.5, 2.9, 2.4, 2.1, 1.8, 1.6
\text{the whole numbers } 1 \text{ to } 999:\ \ 111 \text{ for each digit} @@ the law gives 301 for 1 and 46 for 9: it fails

The powers of two fit closely. Each step adds log 2 ≈ 0.301 to the log, and the steps never line up with the whole numbers, so m is pushed into every part of [0, 1). The counties fit roughly. There are only 36 of them, and with so few, counts of 8 or 5 where 6.3 or 1.8 are expected are not surprising. The whole numbers from 1 to 999 do not fit at all. On the log scale they crowd toward the top of each power of ten: there are 900 from 100 to 999, and only 9 from 1 to 9.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The first-digit law is a measured rule, and it comes with its range. It holds well for lists that run across several powers of ten and that come from growth, from multiplying, or from mixing many kinds of things: populations, river lengths, the amounts in a company’s books. It fails, in a known direction, for lists held inside one power of ten. The heights of adults, in centimetres, nearly all start with 1. It fails for numbers that people assign: telephone numbers, house numbers on one street, prices set at 9.99.

State the rule, the kind of list it holds for, and the kind it fails for.

A list that should follow the law and does not is a reason to look closer. A list that should not, and does not, tells you nothing.

A list of the depths of 50 lakes in Washington, in feet, has 12 starting with 1 and 9 starting with 9. Which part of the law would you check first: the lakes, or the count?

Spotting made-up figures

People asked to make up numbers tend to spread their first digits much more evenly than the law. They choose 1 too seldom and 7, 8 and 9 too often. So an auditor can count the first digits in a company’s books. A count far from the law is not proof of anything. It says which pages to read first.

Who said it first

Newcomb remarked that the first pages of tables of logarithms wear out much faster than the last ones, and that the first significant figure is oftener 1 than any other digit, and less often 9.

Simon Newcomb, “Note on the frequency of use of the different digits in natural numbers”, American Journal of Mathematics (1881); paraphrase

The engineer of 1938 is Frank Benford, a physicist at General Electric, who counted 20 229 numbers from 20 lists, from river areas to street addresses. The rule carries his name. Roger Pinkham (1961) showed that a first-digit rule that survives every change of units must be this one. Theodore Hill (1995) proved it in general, and showed why numbers drawn from a mix of many lists come close to it. Mark Nigrini brought the count into tax and fraud audits in the 1990s. In 17·stat.4.5, the election is Iran’s of 2009; Walter Mebane argued for second-digit tests of vote counts, and Joseph Deckert, Mikhail Myagkov and Peter Ordeshook (2011) argued that such tests cannot tell fraud from honest counts.

Accepted names: the first-digit law is Benford’s law, or the law of anomalous numbers; the first digit is the leading digit, or first significant digit; m(X) is the mantissa of log X; a list whose first digits are unchanged by every change of units is scale-invariant.
