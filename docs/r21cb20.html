21·cb20 · newsflash for 20·cb8

Multiply lengths, add angles

18f built the quarter turn, whose square is negative one, and halved it again and again to measure angles. 20·cb8 found that every pair of the plane multiplies by turning. Now that an angle also has an arc, the rule behind that multiplying can be said in one breath: lengths multiply and angles add. From it come all the powers of a turn and all the ways to split a whole turn into equal parts. Last comes this rung’s claim: turning by an angle is raising the quarter turn to a power.

Write each number of the plane by how far out it is and how far round. Then multiplying two of them multiplies the distances and adds the turns. The equal parts of a whole turn sit at the corners of a regular shape. Turning by any angle is the quarter turn raised to a power.

21·cb20.1Out and round

Out-and-round technique: every pair z ≠ 0 is r(cos θ + i sin θ), with r = |z| and θ its turn, fixed up to whole turns. This is the turn-and-reach technique, written with i.

z = a + bi = (a, b) @@ 20·cb8
(a, b) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta),\ \ r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = |z| @@ turn and reach (21r1)
\htmlClass{new}{z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ a + bi with a = r cos θ, b = r sin θ

From here on, θ is the turn of z, and r(cos θ + i sin θ) its out-and-round form.

21·cb20.2Lengths multiply, angles add

Multiply-and-add technique: multiplying two pairs multiplies their lengths and adds their turns; so the n-th power of cos θ + i sin θ is cos nθ + i sin nθ.

(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) @@ the pair product (20j2), with i² = −1
= (\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta) @@ share out
= \cos(\alpha + \beta) + i\sin(\alpha + \beta) @@ the sum technique (21d3)
\htmlClass{new}{r(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha) \cdot s(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = rs(\cos(\alpha + \beta) + i\sin(\alpha + \beta))} \quad \blacksquare @@ the numbers r and s come out front
(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta @@ induction on n (4c), one more factor each step

21·cb20.3Equal parts of a turn

Even-spacing technique: the numbers whose n-th power is 1 are cos(2πk/n) + i sin(2πk/n) for k = 0, 1, …, n − 1: n of them, at the corners of a regular n-sided shape. Every pair w ≠ 0 has exactly n n-th roots in the same way.

z^n = 1,\ z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) \Rightarrow r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta) = 1 @@ multiply-and-add
r^n = 1 \Rightarrow r = 1 @@ lengths: r > 0
n\theta = 2\pi k @@ turns: cos nθ = 1, sin nθ = 0 only at whole turns (21b)
\htmlClass{new}{z = \cos\tfrac{2\pi k}{n} + i\sin\tfrac{2\pi k}{n},\ \ k = 0, \ldots, n - 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ k and k + n give the same point

21·cb20.4An angle is a power of i

18f made iᵗ by halving i and multiplying, and measured an angle by the power it reaches. Now that power gets its coordinates. Quarter-power technique: iᵗ = cos(πt/2) + i sin(πt/2): the turn of t quarter turns is the point πt/2 round the circle.

i = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ the point at arc π/2 is (0, 1) (21b)
\big(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+2}} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+2}}\big)^2 = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+1}} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+1}} @@ multiply-and-add doubles the angle; both numbers on the left are at least 0
i^{1/2^n} = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+1}} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+1}} @@ so the left is the half (halving, 18f3); induction (4c) on n
i^{k/2^n} = \cos\tfrac{k\pi}{2^{n+1}} + i\sin\tfrac{k\pi}{2^{n+1}} @@ multiply-and-add, k times
\htmlClass{new}{i^t = \cos\tfrac{\pi t}{2} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{2},\ \ i^s \cdot i^t = i^{s + t}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every size t: an angle of t quarter turns has arc πt/2 (sizes are arcs, 21j2); turns add (multiply-and-add)

So 18f’s size, 21a’s arc and this call back’s turn are one number in three units: θ radians is t = 2θ/π quarter turns. Past a half turn the same formula carries on round the circle. The snag is the choice. iᵖ has q different q-th roots, and the rule picks one. So (i⁴)^{1/4} = 1^{1/4} = 1, while i^{4 · 1/4} = i.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The pairs a + bi are the complex numbers, and |z| is their absolute value. The turn θ is the argument of z, and r(cos θ + i sin θ) its polar form, often written r cis θ. Multiply-and-add for powers is de Moivre’s formula; the numbers with zⁿ = 1 are the n-th roots of unity. The value of iᵗ chosen here is its principal value.

Who said it first

The power formula is in Abraham de Moivre’s work (1707, 1722) and in Leonhard Euler’s Introductio (1748). In 21·cb20.5.5, the three are Caspar Wessel (1799), Jean-Robert Argand (1806) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1831), who gave them the name “complex” and said that if +1, −1 and √−1 had been called direct, inverse and lateral units, there would have been no mystery.
