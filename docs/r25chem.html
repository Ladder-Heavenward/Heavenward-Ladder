Why Does a Reaction Stop Halfway?

Why does a reaction stop halfway? Warm hydrogen with iodine vapour in a sealed flask and a purple colour fades as a new gas forms. Then it stops fading, with plenty of both starting gases left. Wait a day, a week: nothing more happens. Start instead from the new gas alone and it falls apart, until the same purple returns and the same mixture is reached from the other side. Chemists once thought each substance had a fixed urge to join each other one, so that a change either ran to the end or never started. Mixtures that stop halfway did not fit. What makes the question hard is that nothing visible is happening at the stopping point, yet the stopping point moves when the flask is warmed, squeezed or topped up. This page shows, with rung twenty-four’s statements on rates and rung twenty-five’s roots, why the mixture stops where it does, why only one of the roots of its equation is the answer, which way a pushed mixture runs, how the stopping point is tied to energy, and why a cold flask and a warm one can make different products from the same start.

A change and its reverse run together. The mixture stops when the two run at the same pace, and that fixes one ratio of amounts at rest. Finding the amounts means solving a polynomial. It may have several roots, some of them off the line, but exactly one lies where every amount is at least zero. Top up a product and the reverse runs faster than the forward, so the mixture pushes back. The ratio at rest is the energy of the change, read through a log. And a fast change can win at first while a slow one wins in the end. A salt dissolves until the product of its ions rests at Ksp, a shared ion cuts what dissolves, and acid dissolves a salt whose ion takes up H⁺.

25chem1Two Changes at Once

Glucose in water takes two forms, α and β, and each turns into the other. Dissolve pure α and the mixture drifts until about 36 parts in 100 are α and 64 are β. Then it stops changing. Write u for the amount of α and v for β, in mol/L, with u + v = c fixed. Each form turns into the other at a rate in proportion to how much of it there is: α to β at k_f u, β to α at k_r v.

u' = -k_f\,u + k_r\,(c - u) @@ α lost to β, less α won back
u' = -(k_f + k_r)\,u + k_r\,c @@ gather the u terms
u^* = \tfrac{k_r\,c}{k_f + k_r},\ \ \text{slope } -(k_f + k_r) \lt 0 @@ the state that holds (24k)
k_f\,u^* = k_r\,v^* @@ at rest, each change runs at the other’s pace
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{v^*}{u^*} = \tfrac{k_f}{k_r} = K} \quad \blacksquare @@ one ratio, whatever the start

Balance technique: a mixture stops changing when each change runs exactly as fast as its reverse, and that fixes the ratio of amounts at rest. For glucose, K ≈ 64/36 ≈ 1.78. For a change A + B ⇌ C + D, the forward pace is measured to be in proportion to [A][B] and the reverse to [C][D]. That is a law, with exceptions where the change runs in hidden steps. Then at rest [C][D]/([A][B]) = K. From here on, K is the ratio at rest: the products’ amounts over the starting stuff’s, each to the power of its count.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

2b asked whether a statement is about each one, some one, or the crowd as a whole. “The mixture has stopped” is a statement about the crowd. It is true of the flask.

A crowd at rest is not a crowd of things at rest.

It is false of every molecule in it. Each glucose molecule keeps flipping between its forms, thousands of times over the hours. The crowd stands still because the flips one way match the flips back. Mark a few molecules and you can watch them keep changing in a flask that does not change.

Oxygen from the air dissolves in a still pond, and the oxygen in the water holds steady all afternoon. Is any oxygen still crossing the surface?

25chem2One Root in the Window

Hydrogen and iodine make hydrogen iodide: H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI, with K ≈ 50 at 448 °C. Start with 1 mol/L of hydrogen and 2 mol/L of iodine. Let x be how far the change has run: x mol/L of each is used and 2x made. Chemists keep the count in a table of start, change and rest. Every amount must be at least 0, so x lies in the window 0 ≤ x < 1.

\tfrac{(2x)^2}{(1 - x)(2 - x)} = 50 @@ the ratio at rest
4x^2 = 50(2 - 3x + x^2) \iff 46x^2 - 150x + 100 = 0 @@ multiply out
x = \tfrac{150 \pm \sqrt{150^2 - 4 \cdot 46 \cdot 100}}{92} = \tfrac{150 \pm 64.03}{92} @@ the quadratic formula (8i)
x \approx 2.326 \text{ or } 0.934 @@ two roots, one outside the window
\htmlClass{new}{x \approx 0.934:\ [\mathrm{H_2}] \approx 0.066,\ [\mathrm{I_2}] \approx 1.066,\ [\mathrm{HI}] \approx 1.869} \quad \blacksquare @@ check: 1.869²/(0.066 · 1.066) ≈ 50

Why exactly one? On the window, Q(x) = 4x²/((1 − x)(2 − x)) starts at 0 and climbs past every ceiling as x nears 1: its top grows and its bottom shrinks toward 0. So by the in-between value technique some x gives 50, and since Q only climbs, only one does. The same holds for every change: the ratio climbs as the change runs.

Window technique: the amounts at rest are the one root of the ratio’s polynomial that lies in the window where every amount is at least 0; its other roots, on the line or off it, are not chemistry. Making sulfur trioxide, 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, with K about 280 at 1000 K, from 0.2 mol/L of SO₂ and 0.1 of O₂, gives 280(0.2 − x)³ = 2x², a cubic. 25d gives it three roots in the plane, and 25e puts any off the line in a flipped pair. Here x ≈ 0.146 is the root in the window, and the other two are 0.223 ± 0.069i.

25chem3A Pushed Mixture Pushes Back

Now the mixture is not at rest. From here on, Q is the same ratio as K, made from the amounts as they are now. Compare the two paces.

\tfrac{\text{forward pace}}{\text{reverse pace}} = \tfrac{k_f\,[A][B]}{k_r\,[C][D]} @@ the law of 25·chem.1
= \tfrac{k_f}{k_r} \cdot \tfrac{[A][B]}{[C][D]} = \tfrac{K}{Q} @@ K = k_f/k_r
Q \lt K \Rightarrow \text{forward faster} @@ the ratio K/Q is above 1
\htmlClass{new}{Q \lt K:\ \text{runs forward};\ \ Q \gt K:\ \text{runs back}} \quad \blacksquare @@ either way Q heads for K, since Q climbs as the change runs (25·chem.2)

Push-back technique: top up a product and Q rises past K, so the reverse runs faster until Q falls back to K. Squeeze the ammonia mixture N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ to half its volume and every amount doubles. Q is multiplied by 2²/(2 · 2³) = ¼, so it falls below K and more ammonia forms. In Netarts Bay, Oregon, water rich in carbon dioxide welled up in 2007 and 2008. Dissolved carbon dioxide makes acid, the acid takes carbonate ions, and oyster larvae at a hatchery there failed to build shells.

Two changes in a row add up to one change, and its Q is the product of their Qs. So their Ks multiply too.

25chem4The Ratio at Rest Is an Energy

25·cb7.3 found that a change can hand out ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q per mole. At rest it can hand out nothing, so ΔG = 0, and there Q = K.

0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln K @@ at rest
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K} @@ undo the log: K = e^(−ΔG°/RT)
\Delta G = RT\ln Q - RT\ln K = RT\ln\tfrac{Q}{K} @@ for any mixture
\Delta G^\circ_{1 + 2} = \Delta G^\circ_1 + \Delta G^\circ_2 \iff K_{1 + 2} = K_1 K_2 \quad \blacksquare @@ a log turns a product into a sum (17c)

Rest-energy technique: a change that hands out energy at the standard state has K above 1, and every 5.7 kJ per mole it hands out multiplies K by ten at 25 °C. A cell joins phosphate to glucose, ΔG° ≈ +13.8 kJ per mole, so K ≈ 0.004: it barely runs. Spending an ATP coin, ΔG° ≈ −30.5 kJ, in the same step gives ΔG° ≈ −16.7 kJ, so K ≈ 840. 19·bio’s coins are this technique at work.

25chem5Fast First, or Stable Last

A start A can become B or C. Suppose B forms faster, while C holds more firmly. In a cold flask neither change runs back. Each is fed by the same A, so B′ = k_B[A] and C′ = k_C[A], and B/C = k_B/k_C at every moment. In a warm flask both run back, B ⇌ A ⇌ C, and the mixture comes to rest.

\text{cold: } \tfrac{B'}{C'} = \tfrac{k_B}{k_C} \Rightarrow \tfrac{B}{C} = \tfrac{k_B}{k_C} @@ both start at 0 and grow in this ratio
\text{warm: } \tfrac{[B]}{[A]} = K_B,\ \ \tfrac{[C]}{[A]} = K_C @@ the balance technique, twice
\tfrac{[C]}{[B]} = \tfrac{K_C}{K_B} = e^{-(\Delta G^\circ_C - \Delta G^\circ_B)/RT} @@ the rest-energy technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cold: the faster wins;\ warm: the lower energy wins}} \quad \blacksquare

Control technique: where nothing runs back, products come in the ratio of their paces; where everything runs back, in the ratio of their Ks. Hydrogen bromide added to butadiene at −80 °C gives about four parts of one product to one of the other. At 40 °C the share flips to about one to four. A pre-step that runs back and forth fast is a small balance of its own. In 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂, a fast 2NO ⇌ N₂O₂ feeds a slow N₂O₂ + O₂ → 2NO₂, so the pace is k₂K₁[NO]²[O₂].

25chem7A Salt Dissolves Until a Product Rests

Silver chloride hardly dissolves. Shake it in water and a little of it falls apart, AgCl ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻, until the mixture rests. A measured law: adding more of a pure solid changes nothing at rest, so the solid’s amount does not enter the ratio, as the water does not enter an acid’s Ka (17·chem). The ratio at rest is then the product [Ag⁺][Cl⁻], written Ksp, and at 25 °C it is about 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. Write s for the amount dissolved, in mol/L.

Shared-ion technique: a salt that falls apart into one ion of each of two kinds dissolves s = √Ksp in pure water, and in water already holding c of one of its ions, the one root in the window of s(s + c) = Ksp, which is close to Ksp/c when c is far above √Ksp. Proof: each mole dissolved gives one of each ion, so the ratio is s · s in pure water and s(s + c) with the shared ion; s(s + c) climbs from 0 past every ceiling as s runs up from 0, so the window technique (25·chem.2) gives one root; and s(s + c) lies between sc and (s + c)², so when c is large the root is near Ksp/c.

s \cdot s = 1.8 \times 10^{-10} \Rightarrow s \approx 1.34 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol/L} @@ pure water: about 1.9 mg in a litre
s\,(s + 0.010) = 1.8 \times 10^{-10} @@ in water with 0.010 mol/L of table salt: the chloride is shared
s = \tfrac{-0.010 + \sqrt{0.010^2 + 7.2 \times 10^{-10}}}{2} @@ the quadratic formula (8i); the other root is below 0, outside the window
s \approx \tfrac{1.8 \times 10^{-10}}{0.010} = 1.8 \times 10^{-8} @@ s is tiny beside 0.010, so s + 0.010 is 0.010 to eight places
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{745 }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{times }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{less }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{dissolves}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the push-back technique, in numbers

The same window holds whatever the ions. Calcium fluoride gives two fluorides for each calcium, so s(2s)² = 4s³ = Ksp, a cubic with one root in the window. So a salt’s Ksp compares how much of two salts dissolves only when they fall apart the same way: the powers in the ratio differ, and so do the roots. Warm water moves Ksp, as it moves every K (25·chem.4), and most salts dissolve more when warm; the shared-ion technique then runs on the new Ksp.

25chem8Will It Settle Out, and Will Acid Take It Back?

Mix two clear solutions, one holding silver ions and one chloride, so that each is at 0.001 mol/L just after mixing. Then Q = 0.001 × 0.001 = 10⁻⁶, far above 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. By the push-back technique (25·chem.3) the change runs back, and solid silver chloride forms, a white cloud, until Q has fallen to Ksp. When Q is below Ksp nothing forms, however long one waits. Now let one of the ions take up H⁺. Magnesium hydroxide falls apart into one Mg²⁺ and two OH⁻, and OH⁻ joins H⁺ to make water, so acid pulls the ratio’s OH⁻ away.

Acid-dissolves technique: with the pH held fixed, magnesium hydroxide, Ksp = [Mg²⁺][OH⁻]², dissolves Ksp/[OH⁻]² mol/L, so each 1 the pH falls lets 100 times more dissolve, until the window holds no root and all of it dissolves. Proof: with [OH⁻] fixed by the pH, the ratio at rest is s[OH⁻]² = Ksp; a fall of 1 in pH is a fall of 10 in [OH⁻] by 17·chem’s tenfold technique, and [OH⁻] is squared.

\text{pure }\allowbreak \text{water}:\ s\,(2s)^2 = 5.6 \times 10^{-12},\ \allowbreak \ s \approx 1.12 \times 10^{-4} @@ a cubic, one root in the window
[\text{OH}^-] = 2s \approx 2.24 \times 10^{-4},\ \allowbreak \ \text{pH} \approx 10.35 @@ pH + pOH = 14 (17·chem)
\text{pH }\allowbreak \text{held }\allowbreak \text{at } 9:\ [\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-5},\ \allowbreak \ s = \tfrac{5.6 \times 10^{-12}}{10^{-10}} = 0.056 @@ 500 times what pure water takes
\text{pH } 8:\ s = 5.6,\ \allowbreak \ \text{pH } 7:\ s = 560\ \text{mol/L} @@ more than any litre can hold
\htmlClass{new}{\text{below }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{about }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{pH } 8.9,\ }\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{a }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{5 }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{g }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{spoonful }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{in }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{a }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{litre }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{dissolves }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{entirely}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 0.086 mol: the ratio never reaches Ksp, and no rest is left in the window

That is why milk of magnesia, a slurry of magnesium hydroxide, dissolves in the stomach’s acid near pH 2 and takes up its H⁺ as it goes. Limestone and the shells of 25·chem.3’s oysters are calcium carbonate, whose carbonate takes up H⁺ too: water made more sour by dissolved carbon dioxide dissolves shell that would otherwise rest, and the larvae build less. A salt whose ions take up no H⁺, as silver chloride’s do not, dissolves the same in acid as in water.

The accepted names

The mixture at rest is in chemical equilibrium, a dynamic equilibrium; K is the equilibrium constant and Q the reaction quotient. The forward pace in proportion to [A][B] is the law of mass action. The table of start, change and rest is an ICE table (initial, change, equilibrium). The push-back technique is Le Chatelier’s principle. ΔG° = −RT ln K is the van ’t Hoff isotherm, or the standard free energy relation. The glucose flipping is mutarotation, its forms anomers. The control technique is kinetic against thermodynamic control; the fast pre-step is a pre-equilibrium. A solution whose pH resists change is a buffer, and how much it resists, its buffer capacity. The ratio at rest of a dissolving salt is its solubility product, Ksp, and s its molar solubility. Less dissolving where one of the ions is already present is the common-ion effect, and a solid forming when Q passes Ksp is precipitation. A slurry of magnesium hydroxide is sold as milk of magnesia, an antacid.

Who said it first

Claude Louis Berthollet saw the Egyptian soda lakes on Napoleon’s expedition (1798–99) and argued, in 1803, that mass drives a change. Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage gave the law of mass action in 1864. Henry Le Chatelier stated the push-back in 1884, Jacobus van ’t Hoff tied K to energy in 1884–86, and Max Bodenstein measured hydrogen iodide in the 1890s. Fritz Haber made ammonia from the air in 1909. In 25·chem.9.5, Torbern Bergman’s tables of elective attractions (1775) ranked each substance’s urge to join each other one; Berthollet answered with the lakes. Walther Nernst set out the solubility product, and how a shared ion lowers solubility, in 1889.
