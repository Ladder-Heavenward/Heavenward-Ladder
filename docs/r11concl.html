11·∎ · conclusion

Where a Curve Is Headed Is Where It Is

A function is continuous at an input when it lands where it was headed: its limit there is its value. Build a function from continuous pieces by adding, multiplying, dividing and chaining, and it stays continuous wherever no bottom is 0.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 11e a joint doesn't break continuity is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 11 promised to say exactly what “drawing without lifting the pen” means. 11a laid down the line: the limit is the value. It gave forms for one side, for a set, and for an interval with its ends, and gave the word curve its line. It showed a function continuous nowhere, and one continuous at a single input. 11b carried continuity through adding, multiplying, and dividing by values that are not 0.

11c chained three gaps, with η in the middle, to prove continuous after continuous, and let a limit pass inside a continuous function. 11d proved every polynomial continuous by induction on the degree, and every rational function at each of its inputs. 11e showed that pieces glued at an input stay continuous when they arrive at one height. This page states the claim in full and joins those techniques.

The claim, in full

A function is continuous at an input when it lands where it was headed, and continuity survives adding, multiplying, dividing by what is not 0, and chaining.

Let f have real inputs and outputs, let w be an input of f, and let f be defined near w.

f is continuous at w when lim_{x→w} f(x) = f(w).

If f and g are continuous at w, so are f + g, f − g and fg, and so is f/g when g(w) ≠ 0.

If g is continuous at w and f is continuous at g(w), then f ∘ g is continuous at w.

Every polynomial is continuous at every real, and every rational function at every input.

Where f is one piece g just left of w and another piece h just right of it, f is continuous at w exactly when both pieces arrive at f(w).

On an interval with an end, continuity at the end is asked only from the side inside. The graph of f kept to the interval is a curve.

The Proof

The definition is 11a's line. The first headline part is 11b's: rung 10's limit techniques, with the values standing where the limits stood.

f, g \text{ defined near } w;\ \ w \in \operatorname{dom} f \cap \operatorname{dom} g @@ 11a's line for continuous, unpacked for each
f + g,\ fg \text{ defined near } w,\ \text{with } w \text{ an input} @@ 10c's lines: their domain is dom f ∩ dom g; a reach by lesser-below-both
\lim_{x \to w} (f + g)(x) = f(w) + g(w) = (f + g)(w) @@ limits-add (10c), with L = f(w) and M = g(w); 10c's line for f + g
\lim_{x \to w} (fg)(x) = f(w)\,g(w) = (fg)(w) @@ limits-multiply (10c), the same way
g(w) \ne 0:\ \ f/g \text{ defined near } w @@ away-from-zero (10c), with M = g(w): g(x) ≠ 0 near w
\lim_{x \to w} (f/g)(x) = \tfrac{f(w)}{g(w)} = \htmlClass{new}{(f/g)(w)} \quad \blacksquare @@ limits-divide (10c): each lands where it was headed. Continuous-sums, continuous-products and continuous-quotients (11b)

f − g is the same with limits-subtract. Nothing new was proved: rung 10 had done the work, and continuity only asked that the limits it was given be values.

The second part is 11c's chain of three gaps. The outer function answers the challenge with a middle gap η; the inner function, challenged with η, answers with δ. The first column of 11c checks that f ∘ g is defined near w, with w an input.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ r_0 = g(w) @@ the challenge is about the final output
\text{call } \htmlClass{new}{\eta}:\ r \in \operatorname{dom} f,\ |r - r_0| \lt \eta \Rightarrow |f(r) - f(r_0)| \lt \varepsilon @@ f is continuous at r₀ (continuity-unpacked, 11a)
\text{call } \delta:\ x \in \operatorname{dom} g,\ |x - w| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |g(x) - r_0| \lt \eta @@ g is continuous at w, challenged with η
\text{let } x \in \operatorname{dom}(f \circ g);\ \text{assume } |x - w| \lt \delta @@ x is an input of g, and g(x) an input of f (composite-domain)
|g(x) - r_0| \lt \eta,\ \text{so } |f(g(x)) - f(r_0)| \lt \varepsilon @@ g's promise; f's promise at r = g(x)
\htmlClass{new}{|(f \circ g)(x) - (f \circ g)(w)| \lt \varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ 9b's line: (f ∘ g)(w) = f(r₀); continuity-unpacked for f ∘ g

There is no “0 <” anywhere. g(x) may be exactly r₀ at inputs other than w, and then f's promise is asked at r₀ itself. A limit of f at r₀ would promise nothing there; continuity does.

The third part is 11d's induction on the degree. Peel off the constant term, and a polynomial is a constant plus x times one of degree one less.

\varphi(n):\ \text{for all reals } u_0, \cdots, u_n,\ x \mapsto \textstyle\sum_{k \le n} u_k x^k \text{ is continuous} @@ name the condition first (4c's shape): continuous at every real
\text{base: } \textstyle\sum_{k \le 0} u_k x^k = u_0,\ \text{continuous} @@ Σ's lines, power-zero; lines-are-continuous with step 0
\text{step: assume } \varphi(n);\ \ p(x) = u_0 + x \cdot q(x) @@ the induction hypothesis; peel-off-the-constant (11d), with q(x) = Σ_{k≤n} u_S(k) x^k
q \text{ continuous},\ \text{so } x \cdot q(x) \text{ continuous} @@ φ(n) at u₁, ⋯, u_S(n); x by lines-are-continuous; the first part (continuous-products)
p \text{ continuous};\ \text{so } \varphi(S(n)) @@ u₀ by lines-are-continuous; the first part (continuous-sums)
\htmlClass{new}{\forall n \in \mathbb N\ \varphi(n)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the induction technique (4c)

Then a rational function p/q is continuous at each of its inputs, where q is not 0, by the first part's quotients (rational-functions-are-continuous, 11d).

The last part is 11e's glued pieces. Continuity at w is a two-sided limit, and 10e split that into a left half and a right half.

f \text{ continuous at } w \Leftrightarrow \lim_{x \to w} f(x) = f(w) @@ 11a's line for continuous
\Leftrightarrow \lim_{x \to w^-} f(x) = f(w) = \lim_{x \to w^+} f(x) @@ two-sides-agree (10e), with L = f(w)
\Leftrightarrow \htmlClass{new}{\lim_{x \to w^-} g(x) = f(w) = \lim_{x \to w^+} h(x)} @@ f agrees with g just left of w and with h just right: agreeing-near, side by side (10e). The glued-functions technique (11e)
\lim_{x \to 0^-} (-x) = 0 = |0| = \lim_{x \to 0^+} x @@ for |x| at 0: line-limit, side by side, on each piece
\htmlClass{new}{|x| \text{ is continuous at } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above and glued-functions: a joint, whose pieces climb at the rates −1 and 1
