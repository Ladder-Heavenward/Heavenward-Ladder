Why Does a Narrowed Artery Matter so Much?

Why does a narrowed artery matter so much? Fatty plaque builds up inside the arteries of the heart over decades. For years it does nothing a person can feel. Then, often when the narrowing is already more than half the width of the artery, a climb up a flight of stairs brings pain in the chest. Doctors wanted to know why a narrowing seems harmless for so long and then matters so suddenly. What makes it hard is that blood is a fluid, and a fluid has a speed and a pressure at every point at once, each depending on where you are in all three directions. This page follows a fluid with the slopes of rung twenty-eight. Along a stream, pressure trades against speed. Down a tube, the flow goes as the fourth power of the width. A spinning stream keeps its spin. And the same tools of flux and circulation, turned on the fields of electric charges and currents, measure the skin of a blood cell and the field under a power line.

Following one small piece of a fluid with no friction, its pressure, its energy of motion and its height add to a fixed total, so where the stream speeds up through a narrowing its pressure drops. In a tube with friction between the layers, the speed is greatest in the middle and zero at the wall, and adding it over the cross section gives a flow that goes as the fourth power of the radius. Halve the radius and the flow at the same push falls to a sixteenth. A ring of fluid with no friction keeps its circulation as it moves, so a spinning column that is stretched thinner spins faster. The field of a charge sends the same flux through every closed surface around it, so in a metal at rest all the charge sits on the skin, and a thin oily skin stores charge in a way that measures how thick it is. The circulation of the field round a current is fixed by the current, and gives the field of a wire.

28phys1Pressure and Speed Along a Stream

Take a fluid of fixed density ρ with no friction, flowing steadily: the speed and the pressure at each place do not change in time. A small box of it feels more pressure on one face than on the face opposite. Add the pushes on all six faces, and the box is pushed by −∇p times its volume: down the uphill vector of the pressure. Follow one small piece along its path r(t).

\rho\,v' = -\nabla p - \rho g\,e_z,\ \ v = r'(t) @@ mass times acceleration, per unit volume; gravity pulls down along e_z
\rho\,v'\cdot v = -\nabla p\cdot r' - \rho g\,z' @@ dot both sides with the velocity
\rho\big(\tfrac12|v|^2\big)' = -\big(p(r(t))\big)' - \rho g\,z' @@ the roads-add technique (28c): the slope of p along the path is ∇p · r′
\htmlClass{new}{p + \tfrac12\rho|v|^2 + \rho gz \text{ is the same all along the stream}} \quad \blacksquare @@ its slope along the path is 0 (14)

Stream technique: along a stream of a steady fluid with no friction, pressure plus ½ρ times the square of the speed plus ρg times the height stays the same, so where the stream speeds up, its pressure drops. With the fluid at rest it is 16·cb12’s pressure-with-depth technique. A fluid that fills a tube cannot pile up, so the flow, area times speed, is the same at every cross section: squeeze the area by four, and the speed rises four times.

\rho \approx 1060 \text{ kg/m}^3,\ \ 1 \text{ mmHg} \approx 133.3 \text{ Pa} @@ blood; pressures in the clinic are read in millimetres of mercury
\tfrac12\rho v^2 \approx 530\,v^2 \text{ Pa} \approx 3.98\,v^2 \text{ mmHg} @@ v in metres a second
\htmlClass{new}{\Delta p \approx 4v^2 \text{ mmHg}} @@ the jet is much faster than the blood before it, so the upstream speed is dropped
v = 4 \text{ m/s through the heart’s outlet valve} \Rightarrow \Delta p \approx 64 \text{ mmHg} @@ a valve this tight is called severe

An ultrasound machine reads the speed of the jet from the shift in pitch of its echo, and the stream technique turns it into the pressure lost. Much of that pressure is never won back: beyond the narrowing the jet breaks up into eddies, and its energy of motion becomes heat.

28phys2The Fourth Power

Blood has friction between its layers. A measured law says that a layer sliding past its neighbour is dragged with a force μ times the rate at which the speed changes across the layers, for each square metre of contact. For blood μ ≈ 3.5 thousandths of a pascal second. Take a straight tube of radius R and length L, with a pressure drop Δp from end to end, and a flow that does not change along it.

\Delta p\,\pi r^2 = -\mu\,u'(r)\,2\pi rL @@ the core of radius r: the push on its ends balances the drag on its side
u'(r) = -\tfrac{\Delta p}{2\mu L}\,r,\ \ u(R) = 0 @@ the fluid at the wall does not move
u(r) = \tfrac{\Delta p}{4\mu L}\big(R^2 - r^2\big) @@ undo the slope (16): fastest in the middle
Q = \iint u\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R u(r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta @@ a double total in round measures, with its extra r (the change-of-variables technique, 28i)
\htmlClass{new}{Q = \tfrac{\pi R^4}{8\mu L}\,\Delta p} \quad \blacksquare @@ 2π · (Δp/4μL) · (R⁴/2 − R⁴/4)

Narrow-tube technique: a steady flow through a tube goes as the fourth power of its radius, Q = πR⁴Δp/(8μL), so halving the radius cuts the flow at the same push to a sixteenth. Two radii come from the area, one from the drag at the wall being nearer, and one from the speed in the middle being smaller.

So a narrowing does almost nothing until it does a lot. Take an artery of the heart 3.5 mm across, carrying about 1 mL a second. Its whole 10 cm length costs about 0.7 mmHg. Narrow one centimetre of it to half its width, and by the narrow-tube technique that centimetre costs about 1.1 mmHg: no one would notice. Narrow it to a quarter, and the same centimetre costs 18 mmHg, before the jet’s losses are counted. On a staircase the heart asks for about four times the flow, and every drop grows with it. The picture runs the numbers.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, through a fourth power

A narrowing’s width is read off an X-ray picture or an ultrasound, and every reading is a fence, a lower and an upper number. 28p says how a fence spreads through a formula: a share of error in R comes out of R⁴ four times as large.

Through a fourth power, a measured radius’s share of error comes out four times as large: state the flow with it.

So a width read to within 10% gives a flow known only to within about 40%. The narrow-tube technique is exact for its tube. The patient’s artery is a guess at the tube, and the guess passes through a fourth power.

A scan in Portland reads a narrowing as 1.2 mm across, give or take 0.1 mm. Within what share is its flow known, and would you send the patient to surgery on that reading alone?

28phys3A Spinning Stream Keeps Its Spin

28j measured the spin of a flow two ways: its circulation round a loop, ∮ v · dr, and its swirl Q_x − P_y at a point. A whole flow turning like a wheel, v = ω(−y, x), has swirl 2ω everywhere, and circulation 2πr²ω round a circle of radius r. A drain’s spin, v = Γ/(2πr) round the centre, has swirl 0 everywhere except at the centre, and circulation Γ round every circle about it.

Now let a loop of fluid move with the flow, its points r(s, t) labelled by s round the loop. With no friction and fixed density, 28·phys.1 says each point’s acceleration is −∇(p/ρ + gz): an uphill vector.

\Gamma(t) = \oint v\cdot r_s\,ds,\ \ v = r_t @@ the circulation round the moving loop; r_s and r_t are partial slopes (28a)
\Gamma'(t) = \oint v_t\cdot r_s\,ds + \oint v\cdot r_{st}\,ds @@ the product technique, inside the total
\oint v_t\cdot r_s\,ds = -\oint \nabla\big(\tfrac p\rho + gz\big)\cdot dr = 0 @@ the uphill-total technique (28j): end minus start, and a closed loop ends where it starts
r_{st} = r_{ts} = v_s \Rightarrow \oint v\cdot v_s\,ds = \oint\big(\tfrac12|v|^2\big)_s\,ds = 0 @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d), and a slope round a loop totals 0
\htmlClass{new}{\Gamma'(t) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ both totals vanish

From here on, the swirl of a flow’s velocity, carried into space, is its vorticity. Kept-circulation technique: in a fluid with no friction and fixed density, pushed only by pressure and gravity, the circulation round any loop moving with the fluid stays the same. A ring of air that shrinks keeps 2πr times its speed, so its speed grows as 1/r. Thunderstorms over the Sacramento Valley in spring carry a slowly turning column of air a few kilometres across. Stretched and narrowed to a tenth of its width, a ring turning at 10 m/s would turn at 100 m/s. Friction near the ground and in the eddies spends some of that, so real tornadoes need more than this sketch.

28phys4The Flux of a Field

16·phys measured the push between charges: K q Q/r², with K ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N m²/C². Divide the push on a small test charge by its charge, and the result at every point is a vector: the field of Q, E = KQ r/|r|³. Write ε₀ = 1/(4πK) ≈ 8.85 × 10⁻¹² for the constant the field is usually measured with.

\text{a sphere of radius } a:\ \ \oiint E\cdot n\,dA = \tfrac{KQ}{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2 = \tfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} @@ the field points straight out, the same size everywhere on it
\text{spread of } \tfrac{r}{|r|^3} = \sum_i\Big(\tfrac{1}{|r|^3} - \tfrac{3x_i^2}{|r|^5}\Big) = \tfrac{3}{|r|^3} - \tfrac{3|r|^2}{|r|^5} = 0 @@ partial slopes (28a), away from the charge
\text{flux through } S - \text{flux through the sphere} = \iiint \text{spread of } E\,dV = 0 @@ the spread technique (28j), on the space between them: proved there for a box; cut the space into small boxes, whose shared faces cancel (curved faces: 28n)
\htmlClass{new}{\oiint_S E\cdot n\,dA = \tfrac{Q_{\text{inside}}}{\varepsilon_0}} \quad \blacksquare @@ add over charges; a charge outside S sends in as much as it sends out

Enclosed-charge technique: the field’s flux out through any closed surface is the charge inside it over ε₀. In a metal, charges move freely. At rest, nothing pushes them, so the field inside the metal is 0, and every closed surface drawn inside it has flux 0 and holds no charge. All the charge of a metal at rest sits on its skin. A flat box straddling the skin finds the field just outside: σ/ε₀, where σ is the charge per square metre.

Some materials have no free charges, but their molecules stretch a little in a field and cancel part of it. A measured law says the field inside drops by a fixed factor κ. Two plates of area A, a gap d filled with such a material, and charges ±Q give a field σ/(κε₀), a voltage σd/(κε₀), and so a charge stored per volt of κε₀A/d.

That last line measured the skin of a cell before anyone could see it. A red blood cell stores about 0.81 microfarads of charge per volt for each square centimetre of its skin. Take κ ≈ 3 for an oily layer. Then d = κε₀/(C/A) = 3 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² / 0.0081 ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻⁹ m. A layer of oil a few molecules thick: electron microscopes, decades later, agreed.

28phys5The Field of a Current

24·then met the magnetic field through a loop of wire. A current makes such a field round itself. A measured law fixes its circulation: round any loop, ∮ B · dr = μ₀ times the current through the loop, with μ₀ ≈ 4π × 10⁻⁷ in SI units.

\oint B\cdot dr = \mu_0 I_{\text{through}} @@ the measured law
\text{a long straight wire: } B \text{ runs round circles about it, the same size at each radius} @@ turn the picture about the wire, or slide it along: nothing changes
B\cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I @@ the law, on a circle of radius r
\htmlClass{new}{B = \tfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = 2 \times 10^{-7}\tfrac Ir} \quad \blacksquare @@ in tesla, with I in amps and r in metres
\iint_D \text{swirl of } B = \oint_{\partial D} B\cdot dr = \mu_0\iint_D J\cdot n\,dA \Rightarrow \text{swirl of } B = \mu_0 J\cdot n @@ the swirl technique (28j), on every small rectangle D, in each of the three flat directions

Wire-field technique: a long straight current I makes a field μ₀I/(2πr) round it, falling off as one over the distance. A power line of the Bonneville grid carrying 1000 A, 15 m overhead, makes about 13 microtesla at the ground. The earth’s own field in Seattle is about 54. A coil of n turns a metre carrying I makes μ₀nI inside it, by a rectangle half in and half out. A hospital scanner’s 1.5 T needs about 1.2 million amp-turns a metre, carried in wire cooled until it loses its resistance.

The accepted names

The stream technique is Bernoulli’s equation, and Δp ≈ 4v² is the simplified Bernoulli equation of echocardiography. Friction between layers is viscosity, μ the dynamic viscosity, and the narrow-tube technique the Hagen–Poiseuille law. The kept-circulation technique is Kelvin’s circulation theorem. The enclosed-charge technique is Gauss’s law; a material whose molecules stretch is a dielectric, κ its dielectric constant, and charge per volt is capacitance, in farads. The circulation law for currents is Ampère’s law, and curl B = μ₀J its form at a point.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli published the trade of pressure for speed in his Hydrodynamica (1738), and Leonhard Euler wrote the equations of a fluid with no friction in 1757. Gotthilf Hagen (1839) and Jean Léonard Marie Poiseuille (1840), a physician studying blood, found the fourth power by experiment. William Thomson (Kelvin) proved the kept circulation in 1869. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the flux law in 1835 (published 1867), and André-Marie Ampère the circulation law in the 1820s. Hugo Fricke measured the skin of a red blood cell from its charge per volt in 1925, and found 3.3 nm. K. Lance Gould showed in 1974 how little flow a narrowing costs until it is severe. In 28·phys.7.5, Jean le Rond d’Alembert found in 1752 that the rules for a fluid with no friction give no drag at all; Martin Kutta (1902) and Nikolai Zhukovsky (1906) tied lift to circulation, and Ludwig Prandtl’s thin layer (1904) explained where the circulation comes from.
