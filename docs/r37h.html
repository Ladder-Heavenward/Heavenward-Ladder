37h · proof

Functions of Sound’s Own

A mixing desk in a studio is a wall of knobs, and every knob is a function from sounds to sounds. Some make a sound louder or softer, and some add sounds together. Some keep the low frequencies and drop the high ones, or the other way round, or keep only a band between. One kind adds the opposite of a sound to take it away. And one kind works on loudness itself, squeezing a wide range of levels into a narrow one or spreading a narrow one wide. This step defines each as a function in rung 9’s sense and proves what it does. The question it keeps asking is which of them can be undone, because what can be undone has lost nothing.

A gain multiplies a sound, a mix adds sounds. A filter multiplies each frequency by its own number: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass. Cancelling adds the opposite of a guess, and what is left is the guess’s error. A compressor squeezes levels above a threshold and an expander spreads them. A function that can be undone has lost nothing; one that cannot has.

37h1Louder, Softer, Together

From here on, the gain by g, for a real g > 0, is the function that sends a sound p to g·p, and the mix of sounds p and q is p + q. A gain by g changes the level by 20 log₁₀ g dB (37c2): a gain by 2 adds about 6 dB, a gain by 1/10 takes away 20.

Mixer technique: every chain of gains and mixes keeps adding and stretching, and so does the same thing to each turning separately. Proof: g(p + q) = gp + gq and g(kp) = k(gp), so each piece is one of 27c’s maps that keep adding and stretching; a chain of such maps is one too; and such a map sends the sum of a sound’s turnings to the sum of what it sends each turning to.

G_{1/g}(G_g(p)) = \tfrac1g\, g\, p = p @@ the gain by 1/g after it

37h2A Filter Keeps Some Frequencies

32i2 found that a filter that blends multiplies each turning by its own number H(f): its size |H(f)| is the gain at f and its angle the shift. The difference here is what that does to a sound in decibels: at frequency f the level changes by 20 log₁₀ |H(f)|.

From here on, a filter is low-pass when it keeps low frequencies and weakens high ones, and its corner is the frequency where its gain has fallen to 1/√2. The filter of 32i3, which mixes each new reading with its last output, is of this kind; so is the air (37e2). The plainest has |H(f)| = 1/√(1 + (f/f_c)²).

Filter-corner technique: the plainest low-pass filter takes about 3 dB at its corner, and above the corner it takes about 6 dB more for every octave.

f \gg f_c:\ |H(f)| \approx f_c / f @@ the 1 is small beside (f/f_c)²

37h3Low, High, and Between

A high-pass filter weakens low frequencies and keeps high ones; the plainest has |H(f)| = (f/f_c)/√(1 + (f/f_c)²). Put a high-pass and a low-pass in a chain and each turning is multiplied by both numbers in turn, so the chain’s gain is their product: a band-pass filter, which keeps a band between two corners. An old phone line is one, with corners near 300 Hz and 3400 Hz (37f4).

Undo-filter technique: a filter can be undone exactly when its gain is nowhere 0, and then the filter with gain 1/H(f) undoes it. Proof: if H(f) ≠ 0 for every f, multiplying each turning by H(f) and then by 1/H(f) returns it, and by the mixer technique each turning is handled alone. If H(f₀) = 0, the turning at f₀ and silence both come out as silence, so no function can tell which went in (9a: a function gives one output per input, so it cannot give two).

H(1000) \approx 1 @@ inside the band: kept
H(5000) \text{ small, not } 0 @@ weakened: a gain of 1/H gives it back, noise and all (37j7)
H \text{ is } 0 \text{ where the line passes nothing} @@ a hard cut
\htmlClass{new}{\text{weakened can be undone; cut cannot}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the undo-filter technique

37h4Cancelling

32·eng1 cancelled a turning by adding it half a turn late, and 32·eng2 found that a delay τ leaves 2 sin(π f τ) of each turning. The difference here is the function view: a canceller is the mix of a sound p with q, a guess at −p made from what was heard before. What reaches the ear is p + q, the error of the guess.

Best-guess technique: among guesses built from a fixed set of past readings by gains and mixes, the one that leaves the least energy is the closest point to −p in the flat piece they span, and what it leaves is at right angles to every one of them. Proof: 31b2’s closest point in a flat piece, with energy as the size (32b3).

So a canceller cancels exactly the part of a sound that its past predicts, and nothing else. A steady roar is predicted well from its last repeat (32·eng3); a voice is not, and comes through. A canceller loses the predictable part on purpose, and leaves the rest as it was.

37h5Compression and Expansion

Some functions act on a sound’s level, not its frequencies. A radio voice is kept loud enough to hear in a car and never so loud it hurts. Let the level of a sound in some stretch be x dB, and let T be a threshold and r ≥ 1 a ratio.

From here on, the compressor with threshold T and ratio r sends a level x to x when x ≤ T, and to T + (x − T)/r when x > T. The expander with threshold T and ratio r sends x to x when x ≤ T, and to T + r(x − T) when x > T. The limiter sends every x above T to T.

Level-squeeze technique: a compressor with a finite ratio is undone by the expander with the same threshold and ratio, and a limiter cannot be undone.

x > T:\ C(x) = T + (x - T)/r > T @@ the compressor keeps levels above T above T
E(C(x)) = T + r\big((x - T)/r\big) = x @@ the expander, applied to it
x \le T:\ E(C(x)) = x @@ both leave it alone
L(x) = T \text{ for every } x > T @@ the limiter: many levels, one output
\htmlClass{new}{E \circ C = \text{the identity};\ L \text{ has no undoing}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 9a: no function gives two outputs for one input

With T = −20 dB and r = 4, a level of −8 dB comes out at −17, and the expander takes −17 back to −8. Real compressors follow the level as it moves, with times for reacting and letting go, and that changes the recipe a little; the squeeze on levels is the heart of them. A compressor and an expander used as a pair is how an old phone network and a tape machine kept loud and quiet sounds both clear.

The accepted names

The corner is the cutoff, or −3 dB point; the plainest low-pass filter is a first-order filter, and its fall of 6 dB an octave is its roll-off. Undoing a filter is equalisation, or deconvolution. Cancelling by a best guess is active noise control with an adaptive filter. A compressor and expander used as a pair is companding; the phone networks used μ-law and A-law companding, and tape machines used Dolby noise reduction.

Who said it first

The best guess in the sense of 37h4, for signals that change in time, was worked out by Norbert Wiener in the 1940s; the first headphones that cancelled noise were patented by Paul Lueg in 1934 for sound in a duct.

A proof that is not allowed

“The compressor sends −8 dB to −17 dB and −17 dB is a level the compressor also sends to itself. So the compressor cannot be undone.” Not allowed: −17 dB is above T = −20, so the compressor sends it to −19.25, not to itself; no two inputs share an output when r is finite.
