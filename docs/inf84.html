How Many Examples Does Learning Need?

How many examples does learning need? A rule that is right on every example it was fitted to may be right by luck. The more rules there were to choose among, the likelier that one of them fits by luck, so the examples needed grow with the choice. They grow slowly: with the length of a rule’s name in bits, not with the number of rules. Even an endless family of rules can be learned from few examples when it can answer a sample in only a few ways, and in 2016 the number of examples that such a family needs was settled up to a constant. That is the classical answer, and the machines of today defy its use. They have more knobs than examples and can fit any answers at all, coin tosses included, and they still do well on fresh examples. Why they do is open.

Miss rate e(h): the chance h is wrong on a fresh example. Some rule of miss rate ≥ ε, out of N, fits m independent examples with chance at most N(1 − ε)ᵐ ≤ N·e^(−εm); so m ≥ (ln N + ln(1/δ))/ε is enough. Thresholds: m ≥ ln(1/δ)/ε. A family that answers at most d points in every way needs about (d + ln(1/δ))/ε. The walk downhill from 0 on ½|Xw − y|² reaches the fit of least size.

inf84.1A Bad Rule Can Fit by Luck

Examples come one at a time from a source, each drawn apart from the others, and each comes with its right answer. A rule h gives an answer to every example. Its miss rate e(h) is the chance that it is wrong on a fresh draw. The learner has a family of N rules, fixed before any example is seen, and picks one that is right on all m examples it was given. Call a rule bad when its miss rate is at least ε.

Lucky-fit technique: the chance that some bad rule of the family is right on all m examples is at most N(1 − ε)ᵐ, which is at most N·e−εm; so with m ≥ (ln N + ln(1/δ))/ε examples, that chance is at most δ.

P(h \text{ right on one example}) = 1 - e(h) \le 1 - \varepsilon @@ h is bad
P(h \text{ right on all } m) \le (1 - \varepsilon)^m @@ independent chances multiply (30l2)
P(\text{some bad rule right on all } m) \le N(1 - \varepsilon)^m @@ the chance of “or” is at most the sum (30f); at most N bad rules
(1 - \varepsilon)^m \le e^{-\varepsilon m} @@ 1 − x ≤ e^(−x): the graph of e^(−x) bends up, so it lies above its tangent at 0 (14g)
\htmlClass{new}{N e^{-\varepsilon m} \le \delta \iff m \ge \tfrac{\ln N + \ln(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}} \quad \blacksquare @@ take logs

So with that many examples, and with chance at least 1 − δ, every rule of the family that fits them all has a miss rate below ε. The learner need not be clever, only right on what it was shown. The two numbers ε and δ are both needed: the sample may itself be a freak, and δ is the chance allowed for that.

The count N enters only through its log. Name each rule by a row of k bits, a different row for each rule. That is a one-to-one map from the rules into the rows, so N ≤ 2ᵏ, as in ∞三, and ln N ≤ k·ln 2. The examples needed grow with the length of a rule’s name, not with the number of rules. For rules named by 1000 bits, with ε = δ = 1/100, 69 776 examples are enough, though there are 2¹⁰⁰⁰ rules.

inf84.2Endlessly Many Rules, Few Rows of Answers

Let the source draw a number x, with a density, and let the right answer be yes exactly when x ≥ c, for a cut c that the learner does not know. The family is every threshold rule, “yes exactly when x ≥ t”. There are endlessly many, and the last footing says nothing. Let the learner take for t the least example that was marked yes, t̂. If none was, it answers no to everything.

Least-yes technique: with m ≥ ln(1/δ)/ε examples, the least-yes rule has miss rate at most ε, with chance at least 1 − δ.

\hat t \ge c,\ \text{and the rule is wrong exactly on } c \le x < \hat t @@ every yes example is at least c
P(x \ge c) < \varepsilon:\ \text{miss rate below } \varepsilon @@ the misses lie at or above c
\text{else pick } b \text{ with } P(c \le x \le b) = \varepsilon @@ a density: the chance grows continuously with b (30m)
\text{some example in } [c, b] \Rightarrow \hat t \le b \Rightarrow \text{miss rate} \le \varepsilon @@ the misses lie in [c, b]
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{no example in } [c, b]) = (1 - \varepsilon)^m \le e^{-\varepsilon m} \le \delta} \quad \blacksquare @@ independent draws (30l2); m ≥ ln(1/δ)/ε

Why so few, for an endless family? On m examples the thresholds give only m + 1 different rows of answers. Sending a rule to its row of answers on the sample is a map, far from one-to-one, and what must be counted is the rows it reaches, not the rules. A family answers a set of points in every way when all 2ᵈ rows of yes and no on those d points are reached. Thresholds answer one point in every way and never two: with x < x′, no threshold says yes to x and no to x′. Half-planes answer three points in every way and never four.

  • In 1971 it was proved that if a family answers no set of more than d points in every way, the rows it reaches on m points number at most about mᵈ, not 2ᵐ, and that a sample then speaks for the source.
  • In 1989 it was proved that any learner needs at least about (d + ln(1/δ))/ε examples for such a family, and that any rule that fits needs at most that with d multiplied by ln(1/ε).
  • In 2016 the gap was closed: a learner was given that needs about (d + ln(1/δ))/ε, with no extra log. It does not return a rule of the family.

inf84.3Which Fit the Walk Picks

A machine with more knobs than examples has endlessly many settings that fit, and a count of all the rules it can express is hopeless. But the learner is not “any rule that fits”. It is the walk downhill of ∞9, and the walk chooses. Take the plainest case: n knobs w, m examples that are the rows of a grid X, with n > m, answers Xw, wanted answers y, and error f(w) = ½|Xw − y|². Start the walk at w = 0, with any steps η.

Least-size technique: every point of the walk is Xᵀa for some list a; and a fit of that form is the fit of least size: if w = Xᵀa and Xw = y, then every w′ with Xw′ = y has |w′| ≥ |w|.

\nabla f(w) = X^{\mathsf T}(Xw - y) @@ the wide-grid technique (∞9), with J = X
w_0 = 0 = X^{\mathsf T}0;\quad w_k = X^{\mathsf T}a \Rightarrow w_{k+1} = X^{\mathsf T}\big(a - \eta\,(Xw_k - y)\big) @@ induction on k (4c)
X(w' - w) = y - y = 0 @@ both fit
w \cdot (w' - w) = a^{\mathsf T}X(w' - w) = 0 @@ w = Xᵀa: the two are at right angles
\htmlClass{new}{|w'|^2 = |w|^2 + |w' - w|^2 \ge |w|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the square-sum technique (31a)

What Xᵀ reaches is a flat piece through 0, and a walk inside it cannot head to a point outside it. So when the walk reaches a fit, as ∞9 promises when every stretch of XXᵀ is above 0, it is the smallest fit of all. Nothing in the error asked for that: every fit has error 0. The preference lies in the walk and its start. Whether small is also good on fresh examples depends on the source, and for networks even the question of which fit the walk picks is open.

inf84.4What the Count Does Not Explain

  • In 2017 it was shown that the image networks in use fit their training examples exactly even when the right answers are replaced by answers drawn at random. So these families reach every row of answers that was tried on their own training sets, and a count of rows cannot be why the same networks do well when the answers are true.
  • In 2019 a second curve was drawn. As a machine is given more knobs, its error on fresh examples falls, then rises to a peak near the size at which it first fits its examples exactly, as the old picture says. Past that size it falls again.
  • In 2020, for straight machines fitted exactly by the fit of least size, upper and lower bounds that match were proved on how much worse than the best rule such a fit predicts. They tell when fitting noisy answers exactly does no harm.

The classical count is a proof, and it stands. It is a ceiling that holds for every source and for any learner that fits. The machines in use sit far under it, for reasons that must lie in the sources they meet and in the walk that trains them, and no account of those reasons is yet agreed.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1971 the rows of answers a family can reach were counted, and the largest set it answers in every way became the measure of a family.

In 1984 learning was given its present form as a question about chance: a rule probably right about most fresh examples, from a number of examples that can be afforded. In 1989 that number was tied to the measure of 1971, up to a log, and in 2016 the log was removed.

From 2017 the networks in use were found to lie outside the reach of these counts: they fit anything, and still learn. The curve of 2019 and the bounds of 2020 are first steps toward an account.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A rule is a hypothesis and a family a hypothesis class; the right answers come from a target concept, and the source is a distribution. The miss rate is the risk, or generalization error; ε is the accuracy and δ the confidence, and the model is probably approximately correct (PAC) learning. A learner that returns a rule fitting the sample is a consistent learner, or empirical risk minimization; the number of examples needed is the sample complexity, and (ln N + ln(1/δ))/ε is the bound for a finite class, by the union bound. Answering a set in every way is shattering it; the largest d is the Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension; the count of rows is the growth function, bounded by the Sauer–Shelah lemma. A learner that returns a rule outside the family is improper. The fit of least size is the minimum-norm interpolant, and the walk’s preference is its implicit bias. The second curve is double descent, the size of first exact fit the interpolation threshold, and exact fitting of noise without harm is benign overfitting.
