Why Does a Wire Turn a Compass?

Why does a wire turn a compass? In 1820 a lecturer in Copenhagen saw a compass needle swing when he switched on a current in a nearby wire. Electricity and magnetism, two separate mysteries, turned out to be one. A magnetic field pushes only on moving charges, and always sideways. This page writes that push with rung 27’s cross product, sends a charge round in a circle whose period does not depend on its speed, finds the field round a wire from rung 28’s circulation, finds the pull between two wires, and makes a voltage by turning a loop in a field.

A charge q moving with velocity v in a field B feels F = q v × B, at right angles to v, so it never changes the speed. Balanced against the pull a circle needs, r = mv/(qB), and the period 2πm/(qB) is the same at every speed. The circulation of B round a loop is μ₀ times the current through it; round a wire, B = μ₀I/(2πr). Two currents push each other with that field. A changing flux through a loop makes a voltage, the generator.

28phys21A Push That Is Always Sideways

From here on, on this page, a magnetic field B is an arrow at each point of space, measured in teslas. A charge q moving with velocity v through it feels a push F = q v × B, by 27k’s cross product. A charge at rest feels nothing.

F = q\,v \times B @@ the push
F \cdot v = q\,(v \times B) \cdot v = 0 @@ a cross product is at right angles to v (27k)
\tfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\,v \cdot v\right) = F \cdot v @@ the moving-dot technique (27k)
\htmlClass{new}{= 0:\ \text{the speed never changes}} @@ it turns the charge, never speeds it

Sideways technique: a magnetic field pushes a moving charge at right angles to its motion, so it bends the path without changing the speed.

28phys22Round in a Circle

A charge moving square to a steady field feels a push of size qvB, always toward one side: it goes round a circle, and that push supplies the pull v²/r the circle needs, as in 21·phys.

qvB = \dfrac{m v^2}{r} @@ the push is the circle’s pull
r = \dfrac{mv}{qB} @@ faster, a wider circle
\text{period} = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB} @@ v cancels
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a proton in } 1 \text{ T: } \tfrac{qB}{2\pi m} \approx 15 \text{ million turns a second}} @@ at any speed

Circle technique: a charge in a steady field goes round in a time 2πm/(qB) that does not depend on its speed. So a push in step with the turning, given every half turn, speeds it up while it spirals out, always in time: that is how early particle accelerators worked. The radius sorts particles by mass, which is how the masses of atoms are weighed.

28phys23The Field Round a Wire

A current makes a field. The rule found by measurement is about circulation, as in 28l: round any closed loop, the circulation of B is μ₀ times the current through the loop, with μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷.

\oint B \cdot d\ell = \mu_0 I @@ the circulation rule
\text{by symmetry, } B \text{ runs round circles about the wire, the same size on each} @@ nothing picks out a side
B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I @@ circulation round a circle of radius r
\htmlClass{new}{B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}} @@ falls off as 1/r

Wire technique: a long straight current I makes a field running round it, of size μ₀I/(2πr) at distance r. 10 amperes at 1 cm gives 2 × 10⁻⁴ tesla, several times the earth’s own field: enough to swing a compass, as in Copenhagen.

28phys24Two Currents Push Each Other

A second wire, a distance d from the first, carries current I₂. Its moving charges feel the first wire’s field.

B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi d} @@ at the second wire (28phys23)
\text{force on length } L:\ I_2 L B_1 @@ adding q v × B over the moving charges
\dfrac{F}{L} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} @@ divide by L
\htmlClass{new}{\text{same way: pull together; opposite: push apart}} @@ by the cross products’ directions

Currents in the same direction pull together, and opposite ones push apart, with a force per length μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd). Until 2019 the ampere itself was defined by this: two wires a metre apart, each with one ampere, pull with 2 × 10⁻⁷ newtons on each metre.

28phys25Turning a Loop Makes a Voltage

The flux of B through a loop is its size times the loop’s area across it, as in 28l. Faraday found by experiment that a changing flux drives a voltage round the loop, equal to the flux’s rate of change.

\Phi = N B A \cos(\omega t) @@ N turns of area A, turning at ω
\text{voltage} = -\Phi\prime = N B A\,\omega \sin(\omega t) @@ the slope of cos (21)
N = 100,\ B = 0.5,\ A = 0.01,\ \omega = 2\pi \cdot 50 @@ a small machine
\htmlClass{new}{\text{peak} = 100 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.01 \cdot 314 \approx 157 \text{ V}} @@ a swinging voltage, as 26·eng uses

Turning technique: a loop turned in a field makes a voltage that swings as sin(ωt), with height NBAω. Every power station turns such loops, which is why the grid swings. Run backward, a current in the loop in a field is pushed round: a motor.

28phys27A Loudspeaker’s Push

A current makes a field, and a field pushes a current sideways (28phys21). A loudspeaker hangs a coil in the gap of a strong magnet; the signal current through the coil pushes it, and the coil pushes the cone that pushes the air.

F = B\,I\,L @@ field times current times the wire’s length in the field
L = 5 \text{ m of wire},\ I = 2 \text{ A}:\ F = 1 \times 2 \times 5 = 10 \text{ N} @@ 
\text{the current swings, so the push swings with it} @@ the cone follows the signal
\htmlClass{new}{\text{run backwards, push the cone: the coil makes a voltage } BLv} @@ a microphone, 28phys25

Coil-push technique: a coil of length L of wire in a field B carrying current I is pushed with BIL, at right angles to both; the same coil moved at speed v makes a voltage BLv. Shown on a balance: a straight wire across a magnet sitting on a scale changes the reading by exactly BIL when the current is switched on, and reverses it when the current is reversed.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

F = q v × B is the Lorentz force. The circle’s period gives the cyclotron frequency, and the accelerator built on it is the cyclotron; sorting by radius is mass spectrometry. The circulation rule is Ampère’s law and μ₀ the magnetic constant; a changing flux making a voltage is Faraday’s law of induction, with its minus sign Lenz’s law. The pull between wires was the definition of the ampere until the SI changes of 2019.

Who said it first

Hans Christian Ørsted saw the wire swing a compass in 1820, and André-Marie Ampère worked out the forces between currents within months. Michael Faraday found induction in 1831. Ernest Lawrence built the first cyclotron in 1931.
