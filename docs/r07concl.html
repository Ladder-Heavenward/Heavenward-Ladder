7·∎ · conclusion

Every statement is true or false

Every statement is so or not so, with nothing between, even when nobody can find out which. On this ladder that is proved, not assumed: choosing forces it.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 7d the payoff: double negation and the withheld licences is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 7 promised to close the gap rung 1 kept open: from ¬¬P there was no tech to P. It promised that the ladder would not have to lay the principle down, because rung 6's choosing axiom already proves it. 7a stated the either-way principle. It showed that no instance can be refuted, that it is the same principle as not-not out, and that a world of stages honours rung 1's techs and breaks it. 7b showed that where the cases are finitely many and each can be checked, a look settles the question with no principle at all.

7c proved the principle from the choosing axiom, every line cited. 7d spent it, completing the foreclosures that rungs 1 to 6 had written down. This page puts the rung's claim together. The claim is not that anyone knows which side is true: it is that one of the two is so.

The claim, in full

Every statement is true or false, exactly one of the two, whether or not anyone can show which.

Let P be any statement.

Then P ∨ ¬P: P is so, or ¬P is so.

P and ¬P are not both so, so exactly one of them is.

From ¬¬P you may write P.

Where P is a some or an every over a set with n members, of a condition decidable at each member, P ∨ ¬P needs no axiom: a look settles it.

The proof

The first part is the choice-gives-either-way technique of 7c. Here are its lines again, shortened, so that the whole claim stands on one page. Each reason names the tech or the earlier result it rests on.

\text{let } P \text{ be any statement} @@ speak of anything
U = \{x \in 2 : x = 0 \lor P\},\ \ V = \{x \in 2 : x = 1 \lor P\} @@ Separation (2b), twice: each condition mentions P
0 \in U,\ \ 1 \in V @@ the self-sameness axiom; add an option; ︿ each sift
\text{call } c \text{ with } c(U) \in U \land c(V) \in V @@ the choosing axiom (6a): every member of {U, V} is non-empty
u = c(U),\ v = c(V):\ \ u = 0 \lor P,\ \ v = 1 \lor P @@ names for the two picks; ﹀ each sift at its pick
u = v \lor u \ne v @@ u and v are numbers (ℕ-is-transitive), so settled-order (4g) decides it, with no either-way
\textbf{case } u \ne v:\ \text{assume } P;\ \ U = V;\ \ u = v;\ \ \bot @@ with P both sifts hold all of 2: same-members (2a); the self-sameness axiom, then the swap axiom; clash with the case
\quad \htmlClass{new}{\neg P} @@ refute
\textbf{case } u = v:\ \ P,\ \text{or } u = 0 = v @@ cases on u = 0 ∨ P; in the second, the chain technique
\quad v = 1 \text{ gives } 0 = 1,\ \bot;\ \text{so } \htmlClass{new}{P} @@ cases on v = 1 ∨ P; 0 ≠ 1 (next-never-empty), clash, everything permitted; reason by cases
\htmlClass{new}{P \lor \neg P} \quad \blacksquare @@ add an option in each case; reason by cases on u = v ∨ u ≠ v; the call for c closes

The trick, in words. The two sifts are the same set exactly when P is true. A function must pick the same member from the same set. So its picks carry news of P out to where comparing 0 with 1 can read it, and the axiom never says what the picks are.

The or of the first part is 1c's generous or, which would allow both sides. Both is impossible, by rung 1's techs alone. So exactly one side is true.

\text{assume } P \land \neg P @@ aiming to refute
P,\ \ \neg P @@ keep a part, twice
\bot @@ clash
\htmlClass{new}{\neg(P \land \neg P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute

The third part is 1b's gap, closed: the not-not-out technique of 7d. 7a showed it is the same principle as the first part, when each is laid down for every statement.

\neg\neg P @@ given
P \lor \neg P @@ the first part, for this P
\htmlClass{new}{P} \quad \blacksquare @@ the remaining-option technique (1c): the side ¬P is refuted by the given

The last part says where the first part is news and where it is not. Over a set you can count, with a condition you can check at each member, a look settles the some and the every. 7b proved it by induction on the number of members, and no line of it cites either-way.

a \text{ has } n \text{ members};\ \ \varphi \text{ decidable on } a @@ given
\exists x \in a\,\varphi(x) \lor \neg\exists x \in a\,\varphi(x) @@ the finite-search technique (7b), the some: induction on n, one member led out at each step
\neg\varphi \text{ decidable on } a @@ the settled-join technique (7b), at each member
\htmlClass{new}{\forall x \in a\,\varphi(x) \lor \neg\forall x \in a\,\varphi(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the finite-search technique, the every: from the some for ¬φ, by counterexample and not-some (1e)

So the first part's whole weight falls on statements whose cases never end, such as the even-sum guess, and on conditions nobody can check. Rung 1's techs alone could never have reached it: 7a's world of two stages honours every one of them, and P ∨ ¬P fails at its first stage. On this ladder the gap closed by a proof from the choosing axiom, not by a new axiom.

That is the gap closed, and the price named: an or with no address. Logicians with a lifetime behind them still argue about this one line, and you have just begun to see why.
