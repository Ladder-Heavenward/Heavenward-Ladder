43j · technique

Which Primes Are Two Squares

5 = 1 + 4, 13 = 4 + 9, 29 = 4 + 25; but 3, 7 and 11 are not sums of two squares. Fermat claimed in 1640 that an odd prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is one more than a multiple of 4. The clock tells which primes cannot be: a square is 0 or 1 on the clock of 4, so two squares are never 3. For the others, the crowded-box technique (42i) finds the two squares. This step proves Fermat’s claim, multiplies sums of two squares, and says which whole numbers are sums of two squares.

If p ≡ 3 (mod 4), x² + y² ≡ 0, 1 or 2, never 3. If p ≡ 1 (mod 4), −1 is a square on the clock of p, say a² ≡ −1; the crowded box gives small x, y with x ≡ ay, so x² + y² is a multiple of p below 2p: it is p. And (a² + b²)(c² + d²) = (ac − bd)² + (ad + bc)².

43j1Minus One Is a Square

(-1)^{(p-1)/2} \equiv \Big(\dfrac{-1}{p}\Big) @@ the square test (43f2)
p \equiv 1 \pmod 4 \Rightarrow \tfrac{p - 1}{2} \text{ even} \Rightarrow -1 \text{ is a square} @@ a² ≡ −1 for some a
p \equiv 3 \pmod 4:\ x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0, 1, 2 \pmod 4 @@ never 3
\htmlClass{new}{13:\ 5^2 = 25 \equiv -1} @@ a = 5

On the clock of an odd prime p, −1 is a square exactly when p is one more than a multiple of 4; and a prime three more than a multiple of 4 is never two squares.

43j2Small Squares by Crowding

x - ay \text{ for } 0 \le x, y \le k:\ (k + 1)^2 > p \text{ values} @@ more pairs than hours
\text{two land on one hour; subtract: } x \equiv ay,\ |x|, |y| < \sqrt p,\ \text{not both } 0 @@ the crowded box (42i1)
x^2 + y^2 \equiv a^2y^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod p,\ \ 0 < x^2 + y^2 < 2p @@ a multiple of p, and small
\htmlClass{new}{x^2 + y^2 = p} @@ 13 = 3² + 2²

Two-squares technique: an odd prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is one more than a multiple of 4. The proof finds the squares by crowding, without searching.

43j3Products of Sums

(a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2 @@ multiply out; it is |z|²|w|² = |zw|² (26)
65 = 5 \cdot 13 = (1\cdot 3 - 2\cdot 2)^2 + (1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 3)^2 = 1 + 64 @@ and also 16 + 49
q \equiv 3 \pmod 4,\ q \mid x^2 + y^2 \Rightarrow q \mid x \text{ and } q \mid y @@ −1 is not a square on q’s clock
\htmlClass{new}{n \text{ is two squares} \iff \text{each prime } \equiv 3 \text{ divides } n \text{ an even number of times}} @@ 21 = 3 · 7 is not; 45 = 9 · 5 = 36 + 9 is

A whole number is a sum of two squares exactly when each prime three more than a multiple of 4 divides it an even number of times. Every whole number is a sum of four squares.

Accepted names

This is Fermat’s theorem on sums of two squares; the crowding is Thue’s lemma; the product rule is the Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity. Four squares for every number is Lagrange’s four-square theorem.

Who said it first

Pierre de Fermat stated the two-squares claim in a letter of 1640; Leonhard Euler proved it in 1749; Axel Thue’s crowding proof is from 1902. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved four squares in 1770.
