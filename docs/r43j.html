43j · technique

Polynomial division by hand

Division with remainder, done again and again, finds the greatest common factor of two polynomials, as rung four’s oldest recipe found it for two numbers. That is slow by hand and quick to check. There is a faster test, which never divides at all: write the coefficients of the two polynomials into one square grid, shifted along row by row, and take its determinant. It is zero exactly when the two share a factor. The same grid, built with a second letter riding along in the coefficients, throws that letter away and leaves a polynomial in the first letter alone, whose zeros are where two curves meet. Counting the degree of that polynomial gives a bound on how many times two curves can cross: a line meets a parabola at most twice, two conics at most four times, and two curves of degrees m and n at most m times n, unless they share a piece.

Repeated division finds the greatest common factor. A grid of shifted coefficients has determinant zero exactly when two polynomials share a factor. Built with a second letter in the coefficients, it eliminates that letter, and its degree bounds the meetings of two curves by the product of their degrees.

43j1The remainder chain

Remainder-chain technique: to find the greatest common factor of f and g over a field, divide f by g, then g by the remainder, then that remainder by the next, until a remainder is 0; the last remainder other than 0 is the greatest common factor, up to a constant. 4j’s split-keeps-divisors technique, with polynomials: f = gq + r and g share exactly the common factors of g and r.

f = x^4 - 1,\ \ g = x^3 - 2x^2 + 2x - 1 @@ over ℚ
x^4 - 1 = (x + 2)\,g + (2x^2 - 3x + 1) @@ divide (43c)
g = (\tfrac12 x - \tfrac14)(2x^2 - 3x + 1) + \tfrac34 (x - 1) @@ divide again
2x^2 - 3x + 1 = (x - 1)(2x - 1) + 0 @@ the chain stops
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the greatest common factor is } x - 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last remainder other than 0, top coefficient made 1

Run backwards, the chain writes x − 1 as uf + vg for polynomials u and v, as 4j’s plus-only combination did for numbers. That is the one-maker technique made a recipe: (f, g) = (x − 1). The fractions grow fast, which is why the chain is slow by hand.

43j2A grid that tests for a shared factor

Let f have degree m and g degree n, over a field. Their resultant Res(f, g) is the determinant of the grid with m + n rows: n rows of f’s coefficients, each shifted one place right of the last, then m rows of g’s.

Shared-factor-test technique: Res(f, g) = 0 exactly when f and g share a factor of degree at least 1.

\text{share } h \iff uf = vg \text{ for some } u \ne 0,\ \deg u < n,\ \deg v < m @@ if f = hf₁, g = hg₁, take u = g₁, v = f₁; the other way, one factoring (43d) puts all of f’s unsplittables in vg, so one of them divides g
uf - vg = 0 \text{ is a straight equation in the } m + n \text{ coefficients of } u, v @@ each coefficient of uf − vg is a mix of them
\text{the grid of that straight map is the resultant’s grid, turned on its side} @@ row by row, the shifted coefficients
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a nonzero answer} \iff \det = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the straight map loses more than 0 exactly when its determinant is 0 (27c, 27e)
  
\mathrm{Res}(x^2 - 3x + 2,\ x^2 - 1) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = 0 @@ they share x − 1
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{Res}(x^2 + 1,\ x - 2) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = 5} \quad \blacksquare @@ no shared factor; 5 = 2² + 1, the value of x² + 1 at the zero of x − 2

43j3Throwing a letter away

Two curves f(x, y) = 0 and g(x, y) = 0. Read each as a polynomial in y, with coefficients that are polynomials in x. Then Res_y(f, g) is a polynomial in x alone.

Eliminate-a-letter technique: if (x₀, y₀) is on both curves, then Res_y(f, g) is 0 at x₀; and if the top coefficients in y do not both vanish at x₀, every zero x₀ of Res_y comes from a shared point, perhaps with a complex y₀.

f = x^2 + y^2 - 5,\ \ g = xy - 2 @@ in y: f = y² + 0·y + (x² − 5), g = x·y − 2
\mathrm{Res}_y = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & x^2 - 5 \\ x & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & x & -2 \end{pmatrix} = x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 @@ the grid: one row of f, two of g
x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = (x - 1)(x + 1)(x - 2)(x + 2) @@ a quadratic in x²
\htmlClass{new}{(1, 2),\ (2, 1),\ (-1, -2),\ (-2, -1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ y = 2/x at each; each is on the circle

The parabola y = x² and the line y = x + 2: Res_y(y − x², y − x − 2) = x² − x − 2 = (x − 2)(x + 1), and the meetings are (2, 4) and (−1, 1). Elimination did what substitution does, and it works when nothing can be solved for a letter.

43j4How many times two curves meet

Meeting-count technique: two curves of degrees m and n with no shared piece meet in at most mn points. A piece here is a factor of degree at least 1 common to f and g.

\text{turn the plane so that no two meetings share an } x @@ finitely many directions are bad (20’s turns)
\text{in each grid entry, the coefficient of } y^{k} \text{ in } f \text{ has degree at most } m - k \text{ in } x @@ f has degree m
\text{each term of the determinant has degree at most } mn \text{ in } x @@ count: the entry in row i, column j has degree at most its place’s weight, and the weights add to mn
\mathrm{Res}_y \ne 0 @@ no shared piece (the shared-factor-test technique, over the fractions of polynomials in x)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at most } mn \text{ zeros, so at most } mn \text{ meetings}} \quad \blacksquare @@ at-most-n-zeros (9g); each meeting gives its own x

So a line meets a cubic at most 3 times, and two conics at most 4: the circle and the hyperbola above reach 4. Two circles x² + y² = 1 and x² + y² = 4 never meet: Res_y is the constant 9. The count is exactly mn only after counting complex meetings, meetings twice where curves touch, and meetings at the far points of 29m. Held in trust: that exact count is much higher up the ladder.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The remainder chain is the Euclidean algorithm for polynomials. The resultant’s grid is the Sylvester matrix, and the shared-factor-test technique is the resultant criterion. Res(f, f′) is, up to a factor, the discriminant. The meeting-count technique is the easy half of Bézout’s theorem; the exact count, with complex points, multiplicities and points at infinity, is Bézout’s theorem in the projective plane.

Who said it first

Étienne Bézout wrote on elimination and the count of meetings in 1779; Isaac Newton had guessed the count earlier. James Joseph Sylvester wrote the resultant’s grid in 1840. Leonhard Euler eliminated a letter from two equations in 1748.

A proof that is not allowed

“x² + 1 and x² + 4 have no real zeros, so Res(x² + 1, x² + 4) = 0.” Not allowed: the test is about shared factors, not about zeros on the line. The two share no factor, and their resultant is 9.
