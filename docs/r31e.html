Quadratic Forms

A two-slot reading fed the same vector twice gives a number that grows like a square: two times one place squared, plus the two places multiplied, plus another square. Such readings measure the energy of a stretched frame, the bend of a surface at a flat point, and the spread of a cloud of data in each direction. Read along the axes of the last step, every one of them is a plain sum of squares, each with its own stretch in front. Some of those stretches are above zero, some below and some exactly zero. A change of measuring directions can change the numbers, but it can never change how many are positive, how many negative and how many zero. That count decides everything: whether the reading is above zero for every vector but zero, whether a surface has a bottom, a top or a saddle. Rung twenty eight’s test for flat points used a determinant without saying why. Here is why.

A quadratic form is xᵀAx with A symmetric. Along A’s axes it is a sum of the stretches times squares. No change of measuring directions changes how many of those numbers are positive, negative or zero. A form is positive for every vector but zero exactly when every stretch is above zero, and 28l’s test for flat points is this count for the grid of second slopes.

31e1A Sum of Squares, Along the Axes

From here on, a quadratic form on lists of n real numbers is q(x) = xᵀAx, with A a symmetric grid: 27h2’s two-slot reading B(x, y) = xᵀAy, fed x in both slots. Any grid G gives the same q as the symmetric ½(G + Gᵀ), so nothing is lost by asking for A symmetric. Its academy name, quadratic, from the Latin quadratus, squared, is the one this lesson picks up.

Axes-form technique: if A = QDQᵀ as in 31d3 and y = Qᵀx, then q(x) = λ₁y₁² + ⋯ + λₙyₙ²: read along A’s axes, a quadratic form is a sum of squares with the stretches in front.

31e2The Signs Cannot Be Changed

There are other ways to write q as a sum of squares. Completing the square (8i) gives 2x₁² + 2x₁x₂ + 2x₂² = 2(x₁ + ½x₂)² + (3/2)x₂²: the numbers in front are 2 and 3/2, not 3 and 1. Any undoable change of directions x = Sz turns A into SᵀAS, and the numbers in front change with it. What cannot change is their signs.

Sign-count technique: however q is written as c₁z₁² + ⋯ + cₙzₙ² with z = Tx and T undoable, the number of cₖ above 0, the number below 0, and the number equal to 0 are always the same.

q = \textstyle\sum c_kz_k^2 = \sum d_kw_k^2,\ \ z = Tx,\ w = T'x @@ two such ways, T and T′ undoable
p = \#\{c_k > 0\},\ \ p' = \#\{d_k > 0\},\ \text{suppose } p > p' @@ to be refuted
U = \{x : z_k = 0 \text{ unless } c_k > 0\},\ \ \dim U = p @@ the vectors where q uses only its positive part; T is undoable
U' = \{x : w_k = 0 \text{ when } d_k > 0\},\ \ \dim U' = n - p' @@ lost plus reached (27c2): p′ samenesses, free
\dim U + \dim U' > n \Rightarrow \text{some } x \ne 0 \text{ in both} @@ a free list from each would be too long for the space (27b2)
q(x) > 0 \text{ and } q(x) \le 0 @@ in U, q is a sum of positive multiples of squares, not all 0; in U′ every term is at most 0

31e3Positive for Every Vector

From here on, q is positive definite when q(x) > 0 for every x ≠ 0. The dot’s form x₁² + ⋯ + xₙ² is one, and so is every product’s ⟨x, x⟩ on lists: a product is a positive definite form with its two-slot reading kept.

Form-test technique: q is positive definite exactly when every stretch of A is above 0. For n = 2, with A having rows (p, s) and (s, r), that is exactly when p > 0 and pr − s² > 0.

\text{all } \lambda_k > 0 \Rightarrow q(x) > 0;\ \ \lambda_j \le 0 \Rightarrow q(Qe_j) = \lambda_j \le 0 @@ both ways
p x_1^2 + 2s\,x_1x_2 + r x_2^2 = p\big(x_1 + \tfrac{s}{p}x_2\big)^2 + \tfrac{pr - s^2}{p}\,x_2^2 @@ completing the square (8i), for p ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{q \text{ positive definite} \iff p > 0,\ pr - s^2 > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sign-count technique: two positive numbers in front, in this way as in every way; p = 0 gives q(e₁) = 0

pr − s² is det A, the product of the two stretches (27l1). Both above 0 makes it positive, but so does both below 0. That is why the test asks for p too.

31e4The Test for Flat Points, Explained

28l2’s grid test classed a flat point p of f by A = fxx(p) and D = AC − B², and did not say where the test came from. 28l1 gives f(p + h) = f(p) + ½hᵀHh + E(h) at a flat point, with H the symmetric grid of second slopes and E(h)/|h|² heading to 0. The test is the sign count of H.

Hug-axes technique: at a flat point with continuous second slopes, if every stretch of H is above 0 the point is a bottom, if every one is below 0 a top, and if H has stretches of both signs a saddle; in two variables this is 28l2’s grid test.

The accepted names

A quadratic form is xᵀAx; the axes-form technique is its diagonalization by the principal axis theorem. The sign-count technique is Sylvester’s law of inertia, here an accepted name for a proved result, not a measured one, and the three counts are its inertia; the positive count minus the negative count is the signature. Positive definite, negative definite, indefinite and semidefinite name the sign patterns. The test with p > 0 and pr − s² > 0, carried to n variables with the corner determinants, is Sylvester’s criterion. H is the Hessian matrix, and the hug-axes technique is the second-derivative test in n variables.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss studied forms in two variables with whole-number coefficients in 1801. James Joseph Sylvester stated the law of inertia in 1852, and Carl Jacobi had proved it earlier without publishing; Sylvester also coined the word “matrix” in 1850. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the test for a bottom of a function of two variables in the 1760s, and Otto Hesse’s name went on the grid of second slopes in the 1840s.

A proof that is not allowed

“Along the x-axis f = x² rises and along the y-axis f = y² rises, so f = x² + 3xy + y² has a bottom at 0.” Not allowed: rising along two directions says nothing about the others. Along (1, −1) it falls, as its stretch −1 shows.
