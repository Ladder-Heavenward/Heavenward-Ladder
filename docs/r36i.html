36i · proof

Side by side is not mixed

Red and green mixed are yellow. Red beside green are red beside green, and that is a different object: a pair, not a mix, the way xy is a different thing from x ∧ y. This step says what the pair is, proves that the mix forgets it, and finds the place, in the eye and not in the mathematics, where side by side turns into mixed.

A pair of colours is 2g’s ordered pair. A picture is a function from places to colours, and keeps every pair. The mix is a function from pairs to colours that forgets which was which. The eye mixes what it cannot resolve, and where it starts to is a fact by cases.

36i1The pair

From here on, a pair of colours is 2g1’s ordered pair (c, d), and side by side is a picture with c at one place and d at the next: a function from two places to colours, 9a’s pairs of pairs. (c, d) ≠ (d, c) unless c = d, by 2g5’s equal pairs; the picture with red on the left is not the picture with red on the right.

This is juxtaposition. “xy” written side by side is two names in a row, and what it says depends on the matter at hand (a product at 4g, two names in two gaps at 0a5); “x ∧ y” is one statement built with 1a3’s joining operator and 1c1’s mark. Red beside green is the first; yellow is the second, with mixing for the joining word. The pair says nothing about a mix, and a mix says nothing about a pair.

Pair technique: the picture of (c, d) and the picture of (d, c) are the same function exactly when c = d. Proof: 9a3, same pairs, same function; 2g5, equal pairs have equal parts.

36i2The mix forgets

The mix is a function from pairs to colours: mix(c, d) = c + d. It is commutative, so mix(c, d) = mix(d, c), and so the mix of a pair does not determine the pair: from yellow you cannot recover whether red was on the left. Worse, many pairs have one mix, by 36c1: every pair of metamers of the parts.

Forgetting technique: the mix function is not one-to-one; the pair function is. Proof: mix(c, d) = mix(d, c) with (c, d) ≠ (d, c) for c ≠ d (pair technique); the picture is a function of its places and keeps them (9a).

So a picture holds more than its mix, and the difference is exactly what 2g1’s order carries. A picture of a thousand colours side by side is a function with a thousand pairs; its mix is one colour; and the thousand is not recoverable from the one. That is 36j7’s technique in the small: reducing the picture to its mix drops dimensions, and the picture remains true.

36i3Where side by side becomes mixed

Stand far enough from a picture and the eye can no longer tell two places apart: the pair becomes a mix, red beside green becomes yellow, and the painter who put dots of pure colour next to one another counted on it. The distance at which that happens is not a theorem; it is a fact by cases about the eye, 36g3’s kind, with a rate, and it depends on the eye, the light and the size of the dots. The accepted name for the painting is pointillism; the ladder’s name for the fact is resolution, and it is laid down as measured.

What is mathematics is the shape of the change: as the distance grows, the picture seen passes from the pair to the mix through every stage between, and each stage is 18a5’s dividing of the segment from the pair to the mix at a different point. From here on, the seen mix at a stage k in [0, 1] is k·mix(c, d)/2 + (1 − k)·(the pair), a gradient from the pair to the mix. At k = 0 the eye sees the pair; at k = 1 the mix; between, both, which is why a pointillist painting shimmers.

Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

The pair is a one-statement: this place is red, that place is green. The mix is a crowd-statement: the picture, as a whole, is yellow. Both are true, at their own sizes, and the bridge between them is a spread, the distance at which the eye stops resolving. The ladder says which size a sentence about a picture lives at.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Side by side is juxtaposition; mixing by the eye at a distance is optical mixing, or partitive mixing. The distance at which places run together is set by the eye’s resolution, its visual acuity, about one minute of arc for a sharp eye. The seen mix is a blend, or linear interpolation, between the picture and its average.

Who said it first

Michel Eugène Chevreul wrote on colours set side by side in 1839. Georges Seurat and Paul Signac painted with dots of pure colour from the middle 1880s, and the name pointillism was given to the painting by its critics. A screen’s red, green and blue dots, and a printer’s halftone dots, count on the same mixing by the eye.

A proof that is not allowed

“The flag with red over green mixes to yellow, and so does the flag with green over red. Things that mix to the same colour are the same. So the two flags are the same.” Not allowed: the second line reads the pair off the mix, which the forgetting technique rules out.
