13a · proof

The slope at a point

This step gives the rung's words their jobs: point, chord, slope, and a name for a change of input. Then it gives the slope of a function at an input as a limit of chord slopes, with a new mark for it. It proves that the new use of “slope” agrees with the old one for linear functions, and then computes the slopes of the square, the cube and one over the input from the definition, one move per line. Last, the tangent line, and how people talked before there were limits to talk with.

Fix the input, and change it by a small real other than 0. Divide the change in the output by the change in the input. That chord slope is a function of the change, with a hole at 0. Where it heads as the change heads to 0 is the slope of the function at the input.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 9e (the plane, the graph, the linear function with its step m, same-ratio, two-points-one-line), 11e (the rate of a linear function), 8h (displacement, |x|, unpack-the-bars), 5h (square-of-a-sum, the grid of cells, the displacement pattern), 5g (fractions-of-rationals), 10a (the limit line, defined near w, agreeing-near, line-limit, a hole), 10b (one-limit and the name), 10c (substitution), 8d (the rules for ℝ), 12a (open interval). 13b to 13f prove every rule from this definition; 13k works tangent lines by hand.

13a2Points, chords and slopes

Stands on 13a1, 9e (graphs, two-points-one-line) and 11e (rates).

Every word in the rung's title needs a job before it can be used. The first is the smallest.

From here on, a point of the graph of a function f is one of its pairs (w, f(w)): 9e's dot, in its usual word. “At a point” means at that pair's input: the slope of f at the point (w, f(w)) is the slope of f at w. No shape is meant; the points of geometry wait for rung 18.

A reuse, said out loud. “Point” has had three jobs: 5g's decimal point, a mark in a numeral; 11·chem's melting point and boiling point, which are temperatures; and 12·phys's balance point, a place along a bar. Rungs 10 and 11 also said “at a point” loosely for “at an input”, which is this step's sense. The old jobs keep their own words around them, and what the word is said of says which.

From here on, take a function f whose inputs and outputs are reals, and two different inputs s and t of f. The chord of f through s and t is the linear function through the points (s, f(s)) and (t, f(t)). It is the one that 9e's two-points-one-line gives. It is a function on ℝ, a set of pairs, like every linear function. “Chord” is a word for it, and nothing about shape is meant.

From here on, the slope of a linear function x ↦ mx + b is m. So the slope of the chord of f through s and t is two-points-one-line's step, (f(t) − f(s))/(t − s). “Slope” is the word 9e kept back for this rung. A chord is also called a secant line, and its slope a difference quotient.

Reuses, said out loud. The real m has been called the step (9e) and the rate (11e). Slope is a third name for it, and all three stay. The word “step” also names 4c's step of a definition by repeating and 11a's step function u; what it is said of says which. And “slope” gets a second job further down this step, the slope of a function at an input. The slope-of-a-line technique below shows that for a linear function the two jobs come to the same thing.

So the chord of x² through 1 and 4 has slope (16 − 1)/(4 − 1) = 5. Through 1 and 2 it is (4 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 3. Through 1 and 1.1 it is (1.21 − 1)/0.1 = 2.1. The chord of 1/x through 1 and 2 has slope (½ − 1)/(2 − 1) = −½. For a linear function every chord has the same slope, by same-ratio; for x² the slope depends on the chord. The rest of the step asks what the chords through w and a nearby input are doing.

13a3A change of input, and its name

Stands on 13a2 and 8h (displacement).

Keep one input a fixed and move the other. Two marks help, one old and one new.

From here on, Δ (the Greek letter delta, in its capital form) may be written before the letter of a quantity. It names the change of that quantity, final less starting: ΔT = T − T₀, 8h's displacement of T from T₀. It is also written before the name of a function, or before the letter that names its outputs. For an input a changed to w + h, Δf names f(w + h) − f(w), and so does Δy when y = f(x). Δx names (w + h) − w. Δf is one name, read “delta f”; the Δ is not a number multiplying f. Δ itself is an operator: written before a name, it makes the name of a change. The step always says at which a and which h it is taken. 10a's small δ is a different mark.

From here on, h names a real other than 0 that changes an input: w + h is the input a changed by h. It is just a number. It may be positive or negative, as near 0 or as far from it as you like. It is never 0, since a chord needs two different inputs. It is not “a bit” of anything, and there is no such thing as a bit of a bit: h · h is another real, the product of h with itself.

A reuse, said out loud: h has been a letter in several steps before. It was 5h's fixed amount in the displacement pattern (x − h)², the displacement in 9e's and 9f's f(x − h), 8f's step 1/n, the step of 10f's picture, and 12d's reciprocal. Those were names in their own steps. From here on, wherever a step writes a chord slope or says “a change h”, h has this job. A step that wants h for something else says so.

Now look at the two changes. The change of input is Δx = (w + h) − w = h, by adding-keeps-answers. The change of output is Δy = f(w + h) − f(w). Both are displacements, new less old: Δx is 8h's x − x₀ with x₀ = w, and Δy is the output f(w + h) displaced from f(w). This is the displacement pattern of 5h and 8h, once on the top and once on the bottom of one fraction. The chord of f through w and w + h has slope

Call it the chord slope of f at w with change h. Fix w, and suppose f is defined on some open interval (w − r, w + r), 12a's word. Then w is an input and f is defined near w. The chord slope is defined at every h with 0 < |h| < r. As a function of h it is defined near 0, and 0 is not one of its inputs. At h = 0 the fraction would be 0/0, which names nothing (5d's nothing-times-0-is-1). The chord slope has a hole at 0, in 10a's second meaning. And 10a's limit was built for exactly that: the value at the hole plays no part in where a function is headed.

13a4The slope at a point

Stands on 13a3, 10a (limits) and 10b (one limit).

The rung's first principle is a definition.

From here on, take a function f whose inputs and outputs are reals, and an input a of f with f defined on some open interval (w − r, w + r). f has a slope at a when the chord slopes (f(w + h) − f(w))/h have a limit as h → 0. That limit is the slope of f at w, written f′(w):

Read f′(w) as “f prime of w”, or “the slope of f at w”. The top is a displacement and the bottom is the change h. The letter h is bound by the mark h → 0, as 10a's x was by x → w. By 10b's one-limit at most one real is the limit, so f′(w), when it exists, names one real.

A reuse, said out loud: the mark ′, a prime, has been written after letters before, to make a fresh name near an old one. There were 2b's e′, 4h's q′ and r′, 5f's L′ and R′, 8d's α′, 12b's x′ and K′, 12d's u′ and c′, among others. There it meant nothing by itself. After the name of a function, from here on, it has a job: f′ is fixed by f. The prime is then an operator: carried out on f, it produces one object, the function f′. A prime after any other letter keeps its old use. These steps avoid that use from here on, so the two never meet.

From here on, f is differentiable at w when it has a slope at w. Take the function w ↦ f′(w), on the set of inputs at which f has a slope. It is named f′ and called the derivative of f, read “f prime”. Derivative is from the Latin derivare, “to draw off”: f′ is drawn from f. It is the first rate the ladder derives, and the first mark that, carried out on a function, produces another function. The value f′(w) is also called the derivative of f at w, and the instantaneous rate of change of f at w. f is differentiable on a set A when it is differentiable at every member of A. It is differentiable, with no set named, when it is differentiable at every one of its inputs. And when a function is given by a formula in x, the formula in brackets with a prime after it, as in (x²)′, names that function's derivative. (x²)′ = 2x says the derivative of x ↦ x² is x ↦ 2x.

Two small techniques make the definition easier to use. The first says the chord slope can be written with the second input named x instead of w + h.

Two-ways-to-write-a-limit technique: for F defined near w and a real L, lim_{h→0} F(w + h) = L exactly when lim_{x→w} F(x) = L.

\text{assume } \lim_{h \to 0} F(w + h) = L;\ \ \text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0;\ \ \text{call } \delta @@ 10a's line, for the function h ↦ F(w + h), defined near 0 with the same reach as F near w
\text{let } x \in \operatorname{dom} F \text{ with } 0 \lt |x - w| \lt \delta @@ speak of everything
h = \htmlClass{new}{x - w},\ \ \text{so } x = w + h @@ name the displacement; adding-keeps-answers read in ℝ
0 \lt |h| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; |F(w + h) - L| \lt \varepsilon @@ the promise, at this h
|F(x) - L| \lt \htmlClass{keep}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap axiom: a + h = x. Leftward, the same column read back, with x = a + h

So the slope is also the limit as x → w of (f(x) − f(w))/(x − w): the displacement of the output over the displacement of the input, both written out. The second says that only inputs near a matter.

Slopes-decided-near technique: if f and g agree on an open interval (w − r, w + r), then f has a slope at w exactly when g has one, and then f′(w) = g′(w).

0 \lt |h| \lt r \;\Rightarrow\; f(w + h) = g(w + h) \land f(w) = g(w) @@ both inputs lie in (w − r, w + r), where the two agree
\dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \dfrac{g(w + h) - g(w)}{h} @@ the swap axiom, twice: the chord slopes agree on a neighbourhood of 0 of reach r
\text{one heads to } L \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{the other does} \quad \blacksquare @@ agreeing-near (10a)

13a5A linear function’s slope is its slope

Stands on 13a4 and 9e (same-ratio).

Slope-of-a-line technique: x ↦ mx + b has a slope at every real w, and it is m.

\dfrac{f(w + h) - f(w)}{h} = \dfrac{(m(w + h) + b) - (ma + b)}{h} @@ f(x) = mx + b: substitute for the expression
= \dfrac{\htmlClass{new}{mh}}{h} @@ spread on ℝ, then ma and b cancel (regroup for plus on ℝ, negative on ℝ, plus-zero on ℝ of 8d)
= \htmlClass{new}{m} \quad (h \ne 0) @@ regroup for times on ℝ, reciprocal on ℝ, times-one on ℝ: h · h⁻¹ = 1, licensed because h ≠ 0
f'(w) = \lim_{h \to 0} m = \htmlClass{keep}{m} \quad \blacksquare @@ agreeing-near, and line-limit with step 0: a constant heads to itself

Every chord of a linear function has the slope m, by same-ratio; now the linear function has the slope m at every input too. The two jobs of “slope” agree wherever both apply. A constant function c is the linear function with m = 0, so its slope is 0 everywhere; and x ↦ x has slope 1 everywhere.

13a6x², one move per line

Stands on 13a5, 5h (square of a sum) and 5d (no dividing by 0).

\dfrac{f(3 + h) - f(3)}{h} = \dfrac{(3 + h)^2 - 3^2}{h} @@ f(x) = x², at a = 3: substitute for the expression
= \dfrac{\htmlClass{new}{9 + 6h + h^2} - 9}{h} @@ square-of-a-sum (5h), read in ℝ: (3 + h)² = 3² + 2 · 3 · h + h², and 3² = 9
= \dfrac{6h + h^2}{h} @@ the 9s cancel: regroup for plus on ℝ, negative on ℝ, plus-zero on ℝ
= \dfrac{(6 + h)\, h}{h} @@ spread on ℝ turned about: h is a common factor (5h's factoring)
= \htmlClass{new}{6 + h} \quad (h \ne 0) @@ reciprocal on ℝ, times-one on ℝ: licensed because h ≠ 0
f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0}\, (6 + h) @@ the definition, with agreeing-near: the chord slope agrees with 6 + h at every h ≠ 0
= \htmlClass{new}{6} @@ line-limit (10a): the linear function h ↦ h + 6 heads to its value at 0

Look at the h · h piece. In the top, h² sits beside 6h. Divided by h it is h, exactly, for every h ≠ 0. So the chord slope is 6 + h: not “about 6”, but 6 + h, which is not 6 for any h in this step. Then the limit sends h to 0, because line-limit says the linear function h ↦ 6 + h heads to 6 as h heads to 0. The h · h piece goes to 0 in the limit; it is not dropped for being small. Every chord slope is a little off 6. The slope is where the chord slopes head, which is a different thing from any of them.

The same column, with any input in place of 3:

\dfrac{(w + h)^2 - w^2}{h} = \dfrac{\htmlClass{new}{2ah + h^2}}{h} @@ square-of-w-sum; the w² cancel
= \htmlClass{new}{2w + h} \quad (h \ne 0) @@ spread on ℝ turned about; reciprocal on ℝ
f'(w) = \lim_{h \to 0}\,(2w + h) = \htmlClass{keep}{2w} \quad \blacksquare @@ agreeing-near, line-limit

Slope-of-x² technique: x² has a slope at every real w, and it is 2w: (x²)′ = 2x. At −1 the slope is −2: the graph falls there, two up for each step left. At 0 it is 0.

The tempting shortcut is to put h = 0 into the first line. The top is then 9 − 9 = 0 and the bottom 0, and 0/0 names nothing: 5d's nothing-times-0-is-1 proved that no real answers a division by 0. The limit is what licenses the work. Simplify for every h ≠ 0 first, and only then ask where the result is heading. The order matters.

13a8The tangent line

Stands on 13a7 and 9e (the climbing kind).

A slope at a point gives one linear function with a claim on the point.

From here on, take f differentiable at w. The tangent line of f at a is the linear function through the point (w, f(w)) with slope f′(w): the function x ↦ f(w) + f′(w)(x − w). With x₀ for a and y₀ for f(x₀), its pairs are exactly the (x, y) with y − y₀ = f′(x₀)(x − x₀). That is the displacement of the output against the displacement of the input, scaled by the slope. “Tangent line” is its accepted name, from the Latin tangere, to touch. Here it names this linear function, a set of pairs, and nothing about shape.

Reuses, said out loud. “Line” alone keeps its two jobs, 8a's ordered set ℝ and a line of a proof; “tangent line” is one name, two words long, for a linear function. x₀ is 8h's low-marked name for a fixed input, as in x − x₀, and y₀ is the same habit for a fixed output. Neither low mark is a power.

It is a linear function: f(w) + f′(w)(x − w) = f′(w)x + (f(w) − f′(w)w), with step f′(w). So its slope is f′(w) by slope-of-a-line, and at a its value is f(w).

x^2 \text{ at } 3:\ \ y_0 = 9,\ \ f'(3) = 6 @@ slope-of-x²
t(x) = 9 + 6(x - 3) = \htmlClass{new}{6x - 9} @@ the line for the tangent line; spread on ℝ
x^2 - t(x) = x^2 - 6x + 9 = \htmlClass{new}{(x - 3)^2} @@ square-of-a-difference (5h) turned about: the displacement pattern, squared
x^2 - t(x) \ge 0 @@ square-never-negative (8f): no pair of x²'s graph sits below its tangent line at 3, and only (3, 9) is on it
x^3 \text{ at } 1:\ \ t(x) = 1 + 3(x - 1) = 3x - 2 @@ slope-of-x³: slope 3
x^3 - (3x - 2) = (x - 1)^2 (x + 2) @@ factor (9g): 1 is a zero, twice. Below the tangent line for x < −2, and meeting it again at −2
\tfrac{1}{x} \text{ at } 2:\ \ t(x) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac14 (x - 2) = 1 - \tfrac{x}{4} @@ slope-of-one-over-x: slope −¼

So a tangent line need not stay on one side of the graph, and it may meet the graph again far away. The definition asks neither. It asks only for the pair at w and the slope there. 13k works tangent lines by hand.

13a9How people talked before limits

Stands on 13a1 to 13a8.

Two inventors each had a working method by the 1670s. One's fluxions were rates of flowing quantities. The other's d before a letter named an endlessly small difference of it. They became rivals, and their followers fought about who had been first for decades. Both methods gave right answers, and neither could say what the small quantities were. Two centuries later the books still taught it their way.

A popular calculus book of 1914 tells its reader that d means “a little bit of”, so that dx is a little bit of x. It lets x grow by dx, and squares. It meets (dx)², which it calls a little bit of a little bit. It strikes that out as small enough to neglect, before dividing by dx.

Measure that against the definition. Written that book's way for x² at 3, the work has (3 + dx)² − 9 = 6 · dx + (dx)². Then it has 6 · dx + (dx)² = 6 · dx, with the bit of a bit struck out, and then a division by dx. If dx is a real other than 0, then (dx)² is a real other than 0 (5d's zero-product, read in ℝ and turned about by turn-and-deny). Then the struck-out line is a false equation. If dx is 0, the division is 5d's forbidden one. And there is no third kind of real for it to be: 8e's everyday-counting-passes says no positive real is below every 1/n. A “bit” fails the test of what a differential would have to be: a number that is neither 0 nor any positive real. A well-known modern calculus book makes the same test of the inventors' small quantities. So the ladder never frames the slope with bits. h is a real, never 0. Nothing is struck out. The h · h piece, divided by h, is h, and it goes to 0 in the limit.

A philosopher put the objection first, a century and a half before that book was written. Of the small quantities he asked: “They are neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?”

A French mathematician, in his lectures of 1823, answered by defining the slope as the limit of the ratio of the two changes as the change of input heads to 0. That is this step's line. A Berlin teacher's every and some, 10a's game, made “heads to” provable. After them the ghosts are not needed. Every quantity in this step is a real, and the only thing that heads to 0 is a function's input, in the precise sense rung 10 gave it.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

Things don't jump between states. They pass through a middle that belongs to neither. A river does not go from flowing to frozen; it goes through slush, and 11·chem measured that slush. 12a proved that a continuous change passes through every value between its ends. The slope at a point is a middle of this kind. Every chord has two points, a before and an after. The slope at w is what the chords settle on as the two points close in. At h = 0 there is a hole, so the middle is never caught on its own, and the limit still measures it exactly.

Describe a change by its rates, not only by its before and after.

Rest is often two changes in balance rather than no change at all.

The hole at h = 0 is an old one. There the chord slope reads 0/0, the quotient 5d found fits every value, as a truth-teller box fits every mark (4·comp). 1/0 had no answer to give. This 0/0 has every answer, and the chords on the way in all point to one of them. What picks the one value the slope has?

At the top of a thrown ball's path, its height has stopped rising and has not started falling. What is the slope of its height there? Is the ball at rest?

13a11Running it backwards

Stands on 13a1 to 13a8.

A limit shaped like a chord slope is a slope, and the definition read right to left computes it. lim_{h→0} ((2 + h)³ − 8)/h is the slope of x³ at 2, since 8 = 2³: it is 12, by slope-of-x³. lim_{h→0} (1/(3 + h) − 1/3)/h is the slope of 1/x at 3: −1/9, by slope-of-one-over-x. lim_{x→4} (x² − 16)/(x − 4) is two-ways-to-write-a-limit's second form for x² at 4: 8, by slope-of-x². And from a chord slope to a function: if the chord slopes of f at w are exactly 4 + h, then f′(w) = 4. x² at 2 is one function that fits (2 · 2 + h). Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The two inventors are Isaac Newton (fluxions) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (the d-mark). The popular book is Silvanus P. Thompson’s Calculus Made Easy; the modern book is Michael Spivak’s Calculus. The philosopher is George Berkeley. The French mathematician is Augustin-Louis Cauchy, and the Berlin teacher Karl Weierstrass.

Thompson tells his reader that d means “a little bit of”, so that dx is a little bit of x. He lets x grow by dx, squares, meets (dx)², which he calls a little bit of a little bit, and strikes it out as small enough to neglect, before dividing by dx.

Thompson, Calculus Made Easy, 2nd ed. (1914), chs. I–III

Spivak observes that the small quantities of Leibniz's notation cannot be numbers: a number other than 0 is not infinitely small, and 0 cannot be divided by. He keeps the notation for its convenience and takes its meaning from the limit alone.

Spivak, Calculus, ch. 9

“They are neither finite Quantities nor Quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the Ghosts of departed Quantities?”

Berkeley, The Analyst (1734), §XXXV, as transcribed in D. R. Wilkins's edition (2002)

Accepted names: f′ is the derivative of f; a function with a slope at w is differentiable at w.
