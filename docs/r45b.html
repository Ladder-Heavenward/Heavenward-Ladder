45b · technique

Polynomials in Several Letters

Polynomials in one letter over a field have finitely made ideals. Add a letter: are the ideals of F[x, y] still finitely made? Hilbert proved in 1890 that adding a letter never breaks the condition. So any system of polynomial equations in finitely many letters, even endlessly many equations, says no more than finitely many of them. This step proves the adding-a-letter technique and reads off what it says about equations.

If every ideal of R is finitely made, so is every ideal of R[x]. Take the top coefficients of the members of an ideal I: they form an ideal of R, finitely made. Members with those tops, together with finitely many of each low degree, make I, by dividing away the tops. By induction, F[x₁, …, xₙ] has finitely made ideals.

45b1Top Coefficients Form an Ideal

L = \{\text{top coefficients of members of } I\} \cup \{0\} @@ top of a member
a, b \in L \Rightarrow a + b \in L @@ raise the lower member by a power of x, then add
r \in R,\ a \in L \Rightarrow ra \in L @@ multiply the member by r
\htmlClass{new}{L = (a_1, \ldots, a_k),\ a_j \text{ the top of } f_j \in I} @@ R’s ideals are finitely made

The top coefficients of the members of an ideal of R[x] form an ideal of R; so finitely many members of I already show every top.

45b2Dividing Away the Tops

f \in I,\ \deg f \ge N:\ f - \textstyle\sum r_j x^{\deg f - \deg f_j} f_j \text{ has lower degree} @@ match its top with the a_j
\text{repeat until the degree is below } N @@ each step lowers the degree (44c)
\text{members of degree} < N:\ \text{an } R\text{-module made by finitely many} @@ tops of each degree d < N: an ideal of R, finitely made
\htmlClass{new}{I = (f_1, \ldots, f_k,\ g_1, \ldots, g_m)} @@ finitely made

Adding-a-letter technique: if every ideal of R is finitely made, so is every ideal of R[x]; so F[x₁, …, xₙ] and ℤ[x₁, …, xₙ] have finitely made ideals.

45b3Endlessly Many Equations Say Finitely Much

\{f = 0 : f \in S\},\ S \text{ endless} @@ any set of equations
(S) = (f_1, \ldots, f_k),\ \text{each } f_j \text{ from } S @@ finitely made, makers chosen from S
\text{every } f \in S \text{ is } \textstyle\sum r_j f_j @@ so it is 0 wherever f₁, …, fₖ are
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the system says exactly what } f_1 = \cdots = f_k = 0 \text{ says}} @@ finitely many suffice

Any set of polynomial equations in finitely many letters has the same answers as finitely many of its equations. Hilbert’s proof showed that the makers exist without building them, which Gordan called theology, not mathematics.

Accepted names

This is the Hilbert basis theorem; the makers are a basis of the ideal in the old sense. Gordan’s remark, “Das ist nicht Mathematik, das ist Theologie,” is reported from 1888 or 1890.

Who said it first

David Hilbert proved it in 1890, settling the invariant problem of Paul Gordan, who had built makers by hand for small cases.
