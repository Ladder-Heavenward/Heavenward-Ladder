When Should You Buy Instead of Rent?

When should you buy instead of rent? Skis rent for $1 a day and sell for $B. If the number of days D were known, the choice would be easy: rent when D is under B, buy at once when it is not, and pay the smaller of D and B. But the season ends without warning, and each morning the choice is made again without knowing D. One technique is to rent until the rent paid would have bought the skis, then buy. It never pays as much as twice what hindsight would have paid, and no technique fixed in advance does better. A coin brings the factor from 2 down towards 1.58. Since 2018 the question has been what to do with a forecast of D that may be wrong, and how much trust in it costs.

Technique k: rent days 1 to k − 1, buy on day k. Cost D if D < k, else k − 1 + B. Hindsight pays min(D, B). k = B: cost ≤ (2 − 1/B)·min(D, B), and every fixed rule meets a season that costs it that. B = 2, buy on day 1 with chance 1/3: average cost ≤ 4/3 of hindsight. Forecast y, trust λ: buy on day ⌈λB⌉ if y ≥ B, else on day ⌈B/λ⌉; ratio < 1 + λ if y is on the right side of B, and < 1 + 1/λ always.

inf81.1Rent Until the Rent Would Have Bought

A day’s rent is $1 and the price is $B, a whole number, at least 2. The season lasts D days, and D is not known until it is over. Write best(D) for the smaller of D and B: what a buyer who knew D would pay. A technique fixed in advance is a day k: rent on days 1 to k − 1, and buy on the morning of day k if the season is still on. It pays D if D < k, and k − 1 + B if D ≥ k. The technique with k = B rents until the next day’s rent would bring the rent paid up to the price.

Rent-to-the-price technique: the technique k = B pays at most (2 − 1/B)·best(D), for every D.

D < B:\quad \text{pays } D = \text{best}(D) @@ it never buys
D \ge B:\quad \text{pays } (B - 1) + B = 2B - 1 @@ B − 1 days of rent, then the price
\text{best}(D) = B @@ D ≥ B
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{2B - 1}{B} = 2 - \tfrac{1}{B}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the worst of the two cases

With B = 10 the technique pays at most $19 where hindsight pays $10. The technique knows nothing about seasons. It promises a ratio, not a cost, and it keeps the promise for every D at once.

inf81.2No Fixed Technique Does Better

Could another buying day do better? Think of an opponent who is told the technique, and then picks the season.

Worst-season technique: every technique fixed in advance pays at least (2 − 1/B)·best(D) on some season D.

\text{technique } k,\ \text{season } D = k:\quad \text{pays } k - 1 + B @@ the season ends the day the skis are bought
k \le B:\quad \tfrac{k - 1 + B}{k} = 1 + \tfrac{B - 1}{k} \ge 1 + \tfrac{B - 1}{B} @@ best(k) = k; a larger k below gives less (8g)
k > B:\quad \tfrac{k - 1 + B}{B} \ge \tfrac{2B}{B} = 2 @@ best(k) = B, and k − 1 ≥ B
\text{never buys}:\quad \tfrac{D}{B} \text{ passes every number} @@ let D grow
\htmlClass{new}{\text{some season costs at least } \left(2 - \tfrac1B\right)\text{best}(D)} \quad \blacksquare @@ in every case

So the two proofs meet: 2 − 1/B can be promised, and nothing less can. The worst season for any technique is the one that ends on the day of buying. Buying early loses to a short season, and buying late loses to a long one.

inf81.3A Coin Does Better

Let the technique toss a coin, and let the opponent fix the season before the toss. Now the cost is an average. Take B = 2, where the best fixed technique promises 3/2. Buy on day 1 with chance p. Otherwise rent on day 1 and buy on day 2.

One-coin technique: with B = 2 and p = 1/3, the average cost is exactly 4/3 of best(D), for every D.

D = 1:\quad p \cdot 2 + (1 - p) \cdot 1 = 1 + p,\quad \text{best} = 1 @@ the average of the two costs (30g1)
D \ge 2:\quad p \cdot 2 + (1 - p) \cdot 3 = 3 - p,\quad \text{best} = 2 @@ buy at once, or rent a day and buy
1 + p = \tfrac{3 - p}{2} \iff p = \tfrac13 @@ make the two ratios equal
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1 + p}{1} = \tfrac{3 - p}{2} = \tfrac43 < \tfrac32} \quad \blacksquare @@ at p = 1/3

The opponent’s one weapon was to end the season on the day of buying, and no longer knows which day that is. For any B the same idea works: buy on day i, from 1 to B, with a chance proportional to (1 − 1/B)B − i, so that later days are likelier. Every season then has the same ratio, 1/(1 − (1 − 1/B)B). That is 4/3 for B = 2, about 1.535 for B = 10 and 1.577 for B = 100. By the small-step technique of 24i, (1 − 1/B)B heads to 1/e, so the ratio heads to e/(e − 1), about 1.582. As B grows, no technique with a coin holds its ratio below that number.

inf81.4A Forecast, Trusted in Part

Now suppose a forecast y of D is at hand, from a machine trained on past seasons, and it may be wrong. Trusting it fully is ruinous when it fails. Ignoring it throws away what it knows. Choose a trust λ, with 0 < λ ≤ 1. If the forecast says a long season, y ≥ B, buy early, on day ⌈λB⌉. If it says a short one, y < B, buy late, on day ⌈B/λ⌉. Here ⌈x⌉ is x rounded up to a whole number, so x ≤ ⌈x⌉ < x + 1.

Trusted-forecast technique: if y and D are on the same side of B, the technique pays less than (1 + λ)·best(D); whatever y is, it pays less than (1 + 1/λ)·best(D).

y \ge B,\ D \ge B:\quad \text{pays } \lceil \lambda B \rceil - 1 + B < (1 + \lambda)B @@ best = B; it buys, since ⌈λB⌉ ≤ B ≤ D
y < B,\ D < B:\quad \text{pays } D @@ ⌈B/λ⌉ ≥ B > D: it never buys
y \ge B,\ D < B:\quad \tfrac{k - 1 + B}{k} < 1 + \tfrac{B}{k} \le 1 + \tfrac1\lambda,\quad k = \lceil \lambda B \rceil @@ worst season D = k, as before; k ≥ λB
y < B,\ D \ge B:\quad \text{pays at most } \lceil B/\lambda \rceil - 1 + B < \left(\tfrac1\lambda + 1\right)B @@ best = B
\htmlClass{new}{\text{right side: } < (1 + \lambda)\,\text{best};\quad \text{always: } < \left(1 + \tfrac1\lambda\right)\text{best}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 + λ ≤ 1 + 1/λ, since λ ≤ 1

At λ = 1 nothing is trusted: the technique is rent-to-the-price, and both promises are 2. At λ = 1/2 a forecast on the right side costs under 1.5 of hindsight and a wrong one under 3. As λ shrinks the first promise heads to 1 and the second grows without limit. The technique never asks how good the forecast is. It asks how much the buyer is willing to lose if it is bad.

inf81.5The Same Choice Elsewhere

The skis are a costume. The same choice, pay a little each time or pay once and be done, is made by machines many times a second.

  • The question came from machines with several processors and a shared memory, in work printed in 1988. A processor holding a copy of a block must hear of every change to it, a small cost each time, or drop the copy, and pay to fetch it again if it is wanted.
  • In 1990 (in print 1994) the coin technique and its ratio e/(e − 1) were given.
  • A machine receiving packets on a network must say they arrived. It may answer each at once, or wait and answer several together, and each moment of waiting costs a little. In 1998 this was studied as the same game.
  • In 2018 the technique with a forecast and a trust λ was given, and in 2020 the first limits on such techniques were proved: for a technique with no coin, the trade of 1 + λ against 1 + 1/λ cannot be bettered.

What the last two entries changed is the opponent. The old proofs ask for a promise against every season. A forecast is knowledge of which seasons come, and the new techniques keep a promise against every season while paying less in the ones that were foretold.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞I.oq.1With a forecast and a coin together, what is the best trade between the cost when the forecast is right and the cost when it is wrong? Limits were proved in 2020; is the trade known exactly for every price B?
∞I.oq.2The proofs here charge a technique for which side of B the forecast fell on. For choices with more than two answers, which measure of a forecast’s miss should a technique be charged for, and can one way of trusting serve them all?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Buy or rent came out of a study of the caches of shared-memory machines, printed in 1988, which judged a technique by its worst ratio to hindsight.

The coin technique and the number e/(e − 1) came in 1990. The same number turned up in the waiting game of network replies in 2001.

In 2018 forecasts from learning machines were brought into the game, with the two promises: one if the forecast is good, one whatever it is. In 2020 the first limits on what both promises can be at once were proved.

The accepted names

The question is the ski rental problem, the plainest rent-or-buy problem. A technique that decides without knowing the future is an online algorithm; hindsight is the offline optimum. The worst ratio of the two is the competitive ratio, and the study is competitive analysis. Rent-to-the-price is the break-even algorithm. The opponent is the adversary; one who must fix the input before the coin is tossed is oblivious, and one who sees the tosses is adaptive. A technique with a forecast is a learning-augmented algorithm, or an algorithm with predictions; the promise when the forecast is right is its consistency, and the promise whatever the forecast is its robustness. The processors’ question is snoopy caching; the network one is dynamic TCP acknowledgment.
