24k · technique

Fixed points, stability and the start of chaos

A rule that gives the rate from the present state alone, and never looks at the clock, has resting places: states where the rate is zero, so that a quantity put there stays there. A cup at room temperature, a population at its ceiling, a ball at the bottom of a bowl. Some of these resting places hold. Nudge the quantity off, and it comes back. Others fall apart at the smallest nudge, like a ball balanced on the top of a hill. This step shows that one number decides which: the slope of the rule at the resting place. Then it turns a knob in the rule and watches resting places appear, merge and vanish. Last it goes back to the yearly logistic rule of the last step and turns its knob up. The single resting place splits into a swing between two values, then four, then eight, faster and faster, until the run never repeats at all. The doublings come at a pace that seems to be the same for every rule of this shape, a number found by computing, not by proof.

A resting place of a rule holds when the rule's slope there is negative and falls apart when it is positive. Turning a knob in the rule can make resting places appear in pairs, meet, and vanish. The yearly logistic rule, turned up, swings between two values, then four, then eight, and then never repeats, though every step is fixed. The doublings crowd together at a pace that looks the same for many rules.

What this stands on, and where it goes

24k1Resting places that hold

Stands on 24d1 (tame rules), 24d3 (the drift technique), 12a4 (the sign-kept-near-an-input technique), 14c (the mean value technique) and 24a3 (the own-rate technique).

From here on, a rule y′ = F(y) that does not involve x looks only at the state. A resting place of it is a y* with F(y*) = 0; the constant y = y* is then a solution, 24f3's resting line. It holds when every solution that starts near enough to y* stays near it and heads to it, and falls apart when solutions starting near it, but not at it, leave. Accepted name: an autonomous equation, an equilibrium, stable and unstable.

Sign-of-slope technique: let F have a continuous slope near a resting place y*. If F′(y*) < 0, the place holds: some window around y* and some m > 0 give |y(t) − y*| ≤ |y(0) − y*|·e^(−mt) for every solution starting in the window and every t ≥ 0. If F′(y*) > 0, the place falls apart.

F'(y^*) < 0:\ \ F' \le -m \text{ on } W = [y^* - \delta,\ y^* + \delta],\ \ m = -\tfrac12 F'(y^*) @@ the sign is kept near an input (12a4), for F′ + m
F(y) = F'(c)\,(y - y^*) \text{ for } y \in W @@ the mean value technique (14c), with F(y*) = 0
u = y - y^*:\ \ (u^2)' = 2u\,F(y) = 2F'(c)\,u^2 \le -2m\,u^2 \text{ while } y \in W @@ the chain rule (13e)
\big(u^2e^{2mt}\big)' = \big((u^2)' + 2m\,u^2\big)e^{2mt} \le 0 @@ the product rule, as in 24a3
u(t)^2 \le u(0)^2e^{-2mt}, \text{ so } y \text{ never leaves } W @@ a slope at most 0 never climbs (14c)
\htmlClass{new}{|y(t) - y^*| \le |y(0) - y^*|\,e^{-mt} \text{ for every } t \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ it stays bounded, so it never runs off (24e); with F′(y*) > 0 the same lines, turned, give |u| ≥ |u(0)|e^(mt) while y is in W

Near a holding place the departure shrinks roughly like e^(F′(y*)t), so 1/|F′(y*)| is its time scale. The logistic rule has F′(P) = r(1 − 2P/K): F′(0) = r > 0, so the empty state P = 0 falls apart, and F′(K) = −r < 0, so the ceiling holds, with time scale 1/r. Between two neighbouring resting places F keeps one sign (12a6), so a solution there climbs or falls the whole way to one of them. The resting places and the signs between them tell the whole story, without a formula.

When F′(y*) = 0 the technique is silent. y′ = −y³ holds at 0, y′ = y³ falls apart there, and y′ = y² does both: from below it climbs to 0, from above it runs off.

24k2Turning a knob

Stands on 24k1 and 14c (the mean value technique).

Most rules carry a number that can be turned: a growth rate, a catch, a temperature. Turn it, and the resting places move. Mostly they move smoothly, and keep holding or falling apart. But where a resting place's slope passes through 0, something new can happen.

Fold technique: for y′ = μ − y², when μ > 0 there are two resting places, √μ holding and −√μ falling apart; at μ = 0 they meet; and when μ < 0 there is none, and every solution falls past every floor.

\mu - y^2 = 0 \iff y^2 = \mu @@ the resting places
\mu > 0:\ y^* = \pm\sqrt \mu,\ \ F'(\pm\sqrt \mu) = \mp 2\sqrt \mu @@ the slope of μ − y² is −2y
\sqrt \mu \text{ holds},\ -\sqrt \mu \text{ falls apart} @@ the sign-of-slope technique (24k1)
\mu = 0:\ y^* = 0,\ F'(0) = 0 @@ the two have met; the technique is silent there
\mu < 0:\ y' = \mu - y^2 \le \mu < 0,\ \ y(t) \le y(0) + \mu t @@ the mean value technique (14c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{as } \mu \text{ falls through } 0, \text{ a holding place and a falling-apart place meet and vanish}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

A fish stock that grows by the logistic rule and loses a steady catch H a year obeys P′ = rP(1 − P/K) − H. Its resting places are the two roots of a quadratic, and they meet when H = rK/4. A catch a little below that leaves a stock that holds. A catch a little above leaves none, and the stock falls to nothing, however large it started. A small turn of the knob, a sudden change in what happens: 24·then reads this as a fishery.

Two other shapes come up again and again. In y′ = μy − y², the resting places 0 and μ cross at μ = 0 and swap which one holds. In y′ = μy − y³, the one resting place 0 holds for μ < 0; for μ > 0 it falls apart and two new holding places ±√μ appear beside it, like the prongs of a fork. A ruler pressed end to end does this: past a certain push, straight falls apart, and it bows to one side or the other.

24k3The yearly rule doubles

Stands on 24j4 (the logistic walk), 22h3 (the pull-together technique), 22h4 (where a rule does not settle) and 13e (the chain rule).

From here on g(x) = μx(1 − x), with 0 < μ ≤ 4, which sends [0, 1] into itself, since its largest value is μ/4. It is 24j4's yearly logistic rule, written in x. A point g leaves alone is a fixed point, and two points p ≠ q with g(p) = q and g(q) = p make a cycle of two. A cycle of two is a pair of fixed points of g∘g, so 22h4 decides whether it pulls: by the slope of g∘g there.

Two-cycle technique: for 1 < μ < 3 the fixed point 1 − 1/μ pulls nearby starts in; past μ = 3 it pushes, and a cycle of two appears, at p, q = ((μ + 1) ± √((μ + 1)(μ − 3)))/(2μ); the cycle pulls nearby starts in for 3 < μ < 1 + √6 ≈ 3.449, and pushes past it.

g(L) = L \iff L = 0 \text{ or } L = 1 - \tfrac1\mu;\ \ g'(1 - \tfrac1\mu) = \mu\big(1 - 2 + \tfrac2\mu\big) = 2 - \mu @@ g′(x) = μ(1 − 2x)
|2 - \mu| < 1 \iff 1 < \mu < 3 @@ 22h4: below 1 in size pulls, above 1 pushes
g(g(x)) - x = -x\,(\mu x - \mu + 1)\,\big(\mu^2x^2 - \mu(\mu + 1)x + (\mu + 1)\big) @@ multiply out; the fixed points are factors, since they are left alone by g∘g too
p + q = \tfrac{\mu + 1}{\mu},\ \ pq = \tfrac{\mu + 1}{\mu^2},\ \ p, q \text{ real and different} \iff (\mu + 1)(\mu - 3) > 0 @@ the quadratic formula (8i) on the last factor
(g \circ g)'(p) = g'(q)\,g'(p) = \mu^2\big(1 - 2(p + q) + 4pq\big) = -\mu^2 + 2\mu + 4 @@ the chain rule (13e); g(p) = q
\htmlClass{new}{|-\mu^2 + 2\mu + 4| < 1 \iff 3 < \mu < 1 + \sqrt6} \quad \blacksquare @@ the value is 1 at μ = 3 and falls to −1 where μ² − 2μ − 5 = 0

At μ = 3.2 this gives p = 0.7995 and q = 0.5130, the two threads 22h4 found by running the rule, and the slope of g∘g there is 0.16: the cycle pulls hard. The step where a fixed point stops pulling, with its slope passing −1, and a cycle of twice the length appears beside it, is a doubling. Past 1 + √6 the cycle of two does the same: it pushes, and a cycle of four appears.

24k4Doubling without end, and then chaos

Stands on 24k3, 21d4 (the double technique), 4g (induction), 12a6 (the intermediate value technique) and 24d3 (the drift technique).

Turn μ past 1 + √6 and the cycle of four appears, then eight at about 3.5441, sixteen at 3.5644, then 3.5688, 3.5697, each doubling closer to the last. They crowd at about 3.5699, and past it most runs never repeat. At μ = 4 the run can be written down, and it shows what never repeating means.

Angle-doubling technique: for g(x) = 4x(1 − x) and x₀ = sin²θ₀, the run is xₙ = sin²(2ⁿθ₀). Every x₀ in [0, 1] is sin²θ₀ for some θ₀ in [0, π/2], since sin² climbs from 0 to 1 there without a break.

4\sin^2\theta\,(1 - \sin^2\theta) = 4\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = (2\sin\theta\cos\theta)^2 @@ cos² + sin² = 1, the circle technique (21)
= \sin^2 2\theta @@ the double technique (21d4)
\htmlClass{new}{x_n = \sin^2(2^n\theta_0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction (4g): each step doubles the angle
\theta_0 \text{ and } \theta_0 + \varepsilon \text{ give angles } 2^n\varepsilon \text{ apart after } n \text{ steps} @@ the line above, twice

So the rule is fixed, and every run is fixed by its start, and still the run cannot be foreseen. Know x₀ to ten places, so θ₀ to about 10⁻¹⁰. After n steps the angle is known to about 2ⁿ·10⁻¹⁰, and 2³³ is about 8.6 × 10⁹: after some thirty-three steps the angle is not known at all. Starts at 0.3 and 0.3 + 10⁻¹⁰ are more than 0.1 apart by step 31. The map is tame, |g(x) − g(y)| ≤ 4|x − y|, so a gap can grow at most fourfold a step, as the drift technique bounds the drift of a tame rule; the angle shows the gap really does grow, about doubling each step. And the run hides cycles of every length: from sin²(π/7) it returns after three steps (0.1883, 0.6113, 0.9505, 0.1883), since eight times π/7 is a half turn more than π/7.

Now the number the doublings share. Write uₖ for the k-th doubling: 3, 3.4495, 3.5441, 3.5644, 3.5688, 3.5697. The ratios of neighbouring gaps, (uₖ − uₖ₋₁)/(uₖ₊₁ − uₖ), come out 4.751, 4.656, 4.668, 4.6687, 4.6691, and computed further they agree to more and more places, near 4.6692. Do the same for the rule x ↦ μ·sin(πx), or for any rule with one smooth hump, and the same number appears.

That this number is shared, and that the ratios settle at it, is not proved on this ladder. It is a guess, and its basis is stated: the doublings of a handful of rules, computed to many places, and one ratio that every one of them approaches. Many cases, one rule, and the variation from case to case is the kind a rule predicts: it is a guess by induction under the case with predictable variation. It is used, and it is labelled.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A resting place is an equilibrium, or steady state; holding is (asymptotic) stability, and the sign-of-slope technique is linear stability analysis in one dimension. The picture of resting places and arrows on a line is a phase line. A change in the resting places as a knob turns is a bifurcation: the fold is a saddle-node bifurcation, the swap a transcritical bifurcation, the fork a pitchfork bifurcation, and a doubling a period-doubling (flip) bifurcation. The ratio near 4.6692 is the Feigenbaum constant δ = 4.669201609…; the crowding point near 3.5699 is the onset of chaos. Losing the start's digits at a fixed rate is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and the rate (here ln 2 a step) is a Lyapunov exponent.

Who said it first

Henri Poincaré studied how solutions change as a knob turns in the 1880s, and gave bifurcation its name. Robert May urged in 1976 that the logistic map's wildness be taught to every student. Mitchell Feigenbaum, computing the doublings on a hand calculator in 1975, found the ratio, and then found the same number for the sine map; Pierre Coullet and Charles Tresser found it at the same time. Oscar Lanford proved in 1982, with a computer whose every step was boxed, that the shared constant exists. Edward Lorenz found in 1961 that rounding a weather model's start to three places changed its forecast completely. Pierre-Simon Laplace described the all-knowing intellect in his Essai philosophique sur les probabilités (1814).
