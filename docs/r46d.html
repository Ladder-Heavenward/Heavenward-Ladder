46d · proof

Subgroups and Middle Fields

Between the rationals and the field the roots make there are fields in the middle, and inside the group of the equation there are smaller groups. This step matches them exactly. Each smaller group leaves some members of the field alone, and those members make a middle field. Each middle field is left alone by some of the symmetries, and those make a smaller group. The two ways round undo each other, the bigger group goes with the smaller field, and the sizes match: how many symmetries a part-group has is how far the field climbs above its middle field. So a question about fields, which may be endless sets, becomes a question about a finite group, which can be listed. One more thing comes out. Some middle fields are themselves the fields of roots of an equation, and they are exactly the ones whose groups are normal, the kind of part-group rung 43 cut along.

A part-group H gives the field of what H leaves alone; a middle field E gives the group of symmetries that leave E alone. These undo each other, reverse containment, and the field climbs above its middle field by exactly as many steps as the part-group has members. A middle field is a splitting field exactly when its group is normal, and then its own group is the quotient.

46d1Fixed Fields and Fixing Groups

Let K be the splitting field of a polynomial over F inside ℂ, and G = Aut(K/F). For a part-group H of G, the fixed field K^H is the set of a in K with σ(a) = a for every σ in H. It is a field between F and K: if σ fixes a and b, it fixes a + b, ab, −a and 1/a. For a field E with F ⊆ E ⊆ K, a middle field, Aut(K/E) is the part-group of G that fixes every member of E.

\tau = \text{the flip of } \mathbb C \text{ on } K:\ \ \sqrt[3]2 \mapsto \sqrt[3]2,\ \ \omega \mapsto \omega^2 @@ 25a’s flip keeps sums and products, and K is closed under it
K^{\{e, \tau\}} \supseteq \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2) @@ τ fixes ∛2
\sigma:\ \sqrt[3]2 \mapsto \omega\sqrt[3]2,\ \ \omega \mapsto \omega,\ \ \text{the loop } (1\ 2\ 3) @@ a symmetry, by the count of 46c3
\htmlClass{new}{K^{\{e, \sigma, \sigma^2\}} \supseteq \mathbb Q(\omega),\ \ \mathrm{Aut}(K/\mathbb Q(\omega)) \supseteq \{e, \sigma, \sigma^2\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ σ fixes ω

The containments go both ways round: a bigger part-group fixes fewer members, and a bigger middle field is fixed by fewer symmetries. The next two footings turn the ⊇ above into =.

46d2A Part-Group Fixes a Field of Its Own Size

Fixed-size technique: for every part-group H of G, [K : K^H] = |H|.

H = \{h_1, \ldots, h_m\},\ \ h_1 = e;\ \ x_1, \ldots, x_{m+1} \in K @@ to show: any m + 1 members of K are tied over K^H, so [K : K^H] ≤ m
\textstyle\sum_j h_i(x_j)\,y_j = 0,\ \ i = 1, \ldots, m @@ m conditions on m + 1 unknowns in K
\text{some answer } y \ne 0;\ \text{take one with fewest } y_j \ne 0, \text{ and } y_1 = 1 @@ lost plus reached (27c): the map from K^(m+1) to K^m loses more than 0; renumber and scale
\textstyle\sum_j h_i(x_j)\,h(y_j) = 0 \text{ for } h \in H @@ apply h to the i-th condition: h hᵢ runs over H as hᵢ does (19b’s one-answer technique)
y - h(y) \text{ is an answer with } y_1 - h(y_1) = 0 @@ subtract; it has fewer entries that are not 0, so it is 0
h(y_j) = y_j \text{ for every } h, \text{ so every } y_j \in K^H @@ fewest
\textstyle\sum_j x_j y_j = 0 \text{ with } y_j \in K^H \text{ not all } 0 @@ the condition for h₁ = e
[K : K^H] \le m \le |\mathrm{Aut}(K/K^H)| = [K : K^H] @@ H ⊆ Aut(K/K^H), and K is the splitting field over K^H too, so 46c3 counts it
\htmlClass{new}{[K : K^H] = |H|,\ \ \mathrm{Aut}(K/K^H) = H} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sizes force equality

With H = G: K^G is a field E with F ⊆ E and [K : E] = |G| = [K : F]. Since degrees multiply up the tower F ⊆ E ⊆ K, [E : F] = 1 and E = F. So what every symmetry leaves alone is exactly F.

46d4Normal Middles

A middle field E is closed when every σ in G sends E onto E. Then each σ, cut down to E, is a symmetry of E over F.

Normal-middle technique: E = K^H is closed exactly when H is normal in G; then E is the splitting field of a polynomial over F, and Aut(E/F) matches the quotient G/H.

\sigma(K^H) = K^{\sigma H \sigma^{-1}} @@ a is fixed by H exactly when σ(a) is fixed by σHσ⁻¹
E \text{ closed} \iff \sigma H \sigma^{-1} = H \text{ for every } \sigma \iff H \text{ normal} @@ the middle-field technique: different part-groups, different fields
a \in E \text{ closed}:\ \ g_a(x) = \textstyle\prod (x - b) \text{ over the different } \sigma(a) @@ its roots are in E, and G shuffles them
g_a \text{ has coefficients in } K^G = F @@ symmetric in the σ(a), so fixed by every σ (46b), and 46d2
E \text{ is the splitting field of the product of } g_a \text{ over finitely many } a \text{ that build it} @@ [E : F] is finite
G \to \mathrm{Aut}(E/F),\ \ \sigma \mapsto \sigma \text{ on } E: \text{ keeps composing, loses exactly } H @@ σ fixes E exactly when σ is in Aut(K/E) = H
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{Aut}(E/F) \text{ matches } G/H} \quad \blacksquare @@ onto, since both have |G|/|H| members (46c3); the first isomorphism technique (43·cb19)

For x³ − 2: {e, σ, σ²} is normal in S₃, and ℚ(ω) is the splitting field of x² + x + 1, with group S₃/{e, σ, σ²} of size 2. {e, τ} is not normal, since σ τ σ⁻¹ = (1 3); and ℚ(∛2) is not closed, since σ sends ∛2 to ω∛2, outside it. Here τ (little tau) is a swap of two roots, and e is the shuffle that moves nothing, not 17e's number e.

The accepted names

The middle-field technique is the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, and the matching is the Galois correspondence. The fixed-size technique is Artin’s theorem, and its proof is Artin’s lemma on the independence of the symmetries, in the form of a fewest-terms argument. A closed middle field is a normal extension of F. The middle fields are intermediate fields.

Who said it first

Évariste Galois described, in his memoir of 1831, how adjoining a root of an auxiliary equation shrinks the group of an equation, and when the smaller group is what he called a proper decomposition, the normal case. The matching of part-groups with middle fields, stated as an exact correspondence, comes from Richard Dedekind and Heinrich Weber in the 1890s. Emil Artin gave the proof by linear algebra used here, in lectures at Notre Dame in 1938, printed in 1942.

A proof that is not allowed

“The middle fields of x³ − 2 are ℚ(∛2), ℚ(ω) and nothing else, since ∛2 and ω make K.” Not allowed: a middle field is any field between, and ℚ(ω∛2) and ℚ(ω²∛2) are two more, each of degree 3. Listing the generators misses them; listing the part-groups does not.
