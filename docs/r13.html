Rung 13 · theory

Measuring change in an instant

A linear function changes its output by the same amount for every step of its input: 9e called that amount its step, 11e its rate. Most functions are not linear functions. Between two inputs of the squaring function, the output changes by one ratio; between two others, by another. So what could it mean to say how fast squaring is changing at one input, where there is nothing to divide by? This rung's answer: take a second input, change it, and watch the ratio between the two. As the second input heads to the first, the ratios head somewhere, and that somewhere is the answer. Rung 10 made “heads to” something you can prove; rung 11 said when a function lands where it is headed. This rung puts both to work, and it is where the ladder turns into what is called the calculus. In this section we'll teach you what it means to say that the slope at a point is a limit of chord slopes, and what you can do because you know it.

You have watched a speedometer’s needle and known it meant “right now”. Saying what “right now” is takes everything from rungs 9 to 11. You have all of it. What you get for the work is not an answer to look up. It is a technique you will run thousands of times.

A function gives single values, one input at a time. A rate between functions gives a technique: the slope rule for squaring speaks for every input at once, and so does every technique this rung proves. Each answer is still an equality, so each is still a question about the series, settled by an equation.

Stand at an input, and step away by a real other than 0. Divide the change in the output by the step. Let the step head to 0: where those ratios head is the slope there. Then prove each rule from that one limit, once, and use the rule ever after.

What it means to say it

Take a function whose inputs and outputs are reals, and two different inputs of it. 9e's two-points-one-line gives exactly one linear function through the two pairs of its graph. 13a calls that linear function a chord of the function, and calls its step its slope: how much the output changes per step of the input, between those two inputs. A chord is a function, a set of pairs, like every linear function. Nothing about shape is meant.

For a linear function, every chord has the same slope: that is 9e's same-ratio. For x², the chord through 1 and 4 has slope (16 − 1)/(4 − 1) = 5, and the chord through 1 and 2 has slope (4 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 3. The ratio depends on which two inputs you take. Keep the first input fixed at w, and let the second be w changed by some real other than 0. Then the chord's slope is a function of the change: the change in the output divided by the change in the input. 13a gives the change in the output a mark.

That function of the change has no value when the change is 0, since a chord needs two different inputs. It has a hole there, in 10a's second meaning. And a hole is exactly what rung 10's limit was built for: the value at the hole plays no part in where the function is headed. So the claim of the rung's title can be said in full. The slope of a function at an input w is the limit of its chord slopes as the change heads to 0, when that limit exists. When it does not, the function has no slope at w. 13a lays this down as a definition, with a new mark for the slope. It also gives the word “point” its job. A point of a graph is one of its pairs, and the slope at a point is the slope at that pair's input.

For x² at 3 the chord slope with change h, any real other than 0, is exactly 6 + h. Not roughly: exactly, for every such h. As h heads to 0, 6 + h heads to 6. So the slope of x² at 3 is 6. Nothing was thrown away, and h was never 0.

Where it comes from

Two mathematicians found the subject at about the same time, and quarrelled about it for the rest of their lives. One, in England in the 1660s, thought of a quantity as flowing in time, and called its rate of flow its fluxion. The other, on the Continent in the 1670s, thought of a quantity as made of endlessly small differences. He wrote d before a letter for the difference of that letter. The second published first, in 1684. The first one's friends accused him of taking the idea. A report of the Royal Society in 1712, much of it written by the first himself, found for the first. Mathematicians on the Continent took the second's marks, and the English kept the first's, for most of a century.

Both methods worked, and neither inventor could say what its small quantities were. In 1734 a philosopher, a bishop in Ireland, asked in print: are they numbers or not? If they are numbers other than 0, the steps that throw them away at the end make false equations. If they are 0, the steps that divide by them divide by 0. Rung 5 proved that no number answers a division by 0, and rung 8 that no positive real is below every 1/n. A small quantity that is neither 0 nor any positive real has no place on this ladder.

The answer took another century. A French mathematician, in his lectures of 1823, defined the slope as the limit of the ratio of the two changes as the change of input heads to 0. A teacher in Berlin, from the 1850s, wrote “heads to” as rung 10's every and some. After that nothing small and mysterious is needed. The change is an ordinary real, never 0, and the limit says, with every and some, where the ratios head.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves six things.

First, the definition: the slope of f at w is the limit of the chord slopes from w, as the change of input heads to 0. The top of the chord slope is a displacement: the output's new value less its old one, 8h's displacement said of outputs. The bottom is the change itself. 13a computes the slopes of x², x³ and 1/x from it. It proves that a linear function's slope at every input is its step, so that the old and new uses of “slope” agree.

Second, a function with a slope at an input has no gap there: it is continuous at that input. The change in the output is the chord slope times the change of input. The first heads to the slope, the second to 0, so their product heads to 0. Turned about, a discontinuity means no slope. Not backwards: a function can be continuous at an input and have no slope there.

Third, the product rule. Write each function's new value as its old value plus its change, and multiply out by 5h's grid of cells. The change in the product is three of the four cells. Two of them, divided by the change of input, head to each function times the other's slope. The third is the product of the two changes. Divided by the change of input it is a chord slope times a change, which goes to 0 in the limit by the second principle. So the slope of a product is each function times the slope of the other, added.

Fourth, the power rule. A power is a statement that names the power before it times one more x (4f's step, read for reals on 8f). So the product rule climbs from each power to the next, and induction does the rest: the slope of x to a whole power is that power times x to one less.

Fifth, the chain rule. In a chain, name the inside r, as 9f did. The inside changes by its rate times the change of input. The outside changes by its own rate, taken where the inside stands, times the inside's change. Rates through a chain multiply. The obvious proof divides by the inside's change, which can be 0 when the change of input is not. The proof on 13e uses a helper function that never divides by it.

Sixth, a joint has no slope. At 0, |x| falls on the left and climbs on the right, and 11e proved it continuous there all the same. Its chord slopes from the left are all −1 and from the right all 1, and a limit cannot head two ways. So |x| has no slope at 0. Rung 11 asked what the difference in rates at a joint would cost: this is the cost.

What follows

The slope of a sum is the sum of the slopes, from 10c's technique for sums. The slope of a constant is 0, and a constant factor comes out. So every polynomial has a slope at every real, given by another polynomial. And that one has a slope too: the slope of the slope, which rung 14 reads. Adding a constant to a function leaves its slopes alone, and displacing its input displaces its slopes with it. The quotient rule follows from the product and chain rules together. The linear function through a pair of the graph with the slope there is the tangent line. The d-mark for the slope (Leibniz's) comes back, read as one mark and never as a fraction. And when a function and its undoing both have slopes, the two slopes multiply to 1.

What you can do because you know it

You can say exactly how fast a function is changing at one input, and prove it. You can find the slope of any polynomial, product, chain or fraction of them by rules, without taking a limit again. Each rule was proved once, from the definition, and is licensed ever after. You can read a limit of a certain shape backwards as a slope, and get it in one line. You can find where a function in pieces fails to have a slope, and choose its pieces so that it has one. And you can say which of these techniques needs what: none of them needs anything beyond rung 10's techs, and no new axiom.

That is the start of calculus, not the end of it. Someone who has taught it for forty years is still a beginner at slopes, and you have barely begun.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, 13a carries a green box: the middle of a change. A change is described by its rates, not only by its before and after.

And 13·nav asks how fast the tide is rising, reading rates from real tide tables as chords and from a fitted rise as a slope at an instant. It carries a box on the middle of a change.

What it costs

No axiom is laid down, and no choice is made. Every proof on the rung is a few moves of rung 10's limit techniques and rung 11's continuity, arranged with care. Some of those techniques leaned on rung 7's either-way principle: 10b's proof that a function heads to at most one value, and 11a's continuity unpacked. Where they did, this rung leans on it too, through them. The one place that needs real care is the chain rule, where the tempting proof divides by something that may be 0. 13e mends it with the helper rather than with a new principle. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say that the slope at a point is a limit of chord slopes, and what you can do because you know it.

Who said it first

The two who found the subject are Isaac Newton (fluxions, 1660s) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (the d-mark, published 1684). The bishop is George Berkeley, Bishop of Cloyne, in The Analyst (1734). The French mathematician is Augustin-Louis Cauchy, and the Berlin teacher Karl Weierstrass.

Accepted names: the slope at a point is the derivative; the product, power, chain and quotient techniques for slopes are the product rule, power rule, chain rule and quotient rule.
