30l · technique

Chance by the rules

A lottery ticket bought at a corner store in Vancouver, a card drawn from a shuffled deck, a test result handed over in a clinic in Eugene: each asks for a chance, and each chance has to be worked out by hand before anything can be done with it. The rung has already said what a chance is, a size on a space of outcomes, and it has laid down the rules a chance obeys. This step turns those rules into tools. Where every case is equally likely, a chance is a count, and the counting of choices from long ago does the work. Where events happen one after another, a tree lays them out, and the chance of each path is a product along its branches. Where a result has to be read backwards, from what was seen to what caused it, a chance given one thing is turned around into a chance given the other, and the easiest way to do that by hand is with odds. The worked runs show how often careful people get the backwards reading wrong.

When the cases are equally likely, a chance is the count of the good cases over the count of all of them. Along a tree, chances multiply on the branches of one path and add over separate paths. To read a result backwards, the odds before are multiplied by a ratio of two chances, and that gives the odds after.

30l1Counting equally likely cases

Stands on 30f1 (a chance space, with P(Ω) = 1), 30b3 (the countable-adding technique), 4j5 (the separate-piles technique), 4j6 (the stages-multiply technique) and 4j13 (the choosing-counts-parts technique).

4·stat and 5·stat took a chance to be a count of cases over a count of all cases, as a way of speaking. In a chance space (30f1) it is a technique, once the cases are given equal chances. Let Ω have finitely many outcomes, and let each single outcome be an event.

Equal-cases technique: if Ω has n outcomes, each one an event with the same chance, then each outcome has chance 1/n, and an event made of k outcomes has chance k/n.

\Omega = \{\omega_1\} \cup \cdots \cup \{\omega_n\},\ \ \text{separate pieces} @@ each outcome is its own event
1 = P(\Omega) = P(\{\omega_1\}) + \cdots + P(\{\omega_n\}) @@ P(Ω) = 1 (30f1); size adds over separate pieces (the countable-adding technique, 30b3)
1 = n\,c,\ \ c = \tfrac{1}{n} @@ every outcome has the same chance c
A = \{\omega_{i_1}\} \cup \cdots \cup \{\omega_{i_k}\} @@ an event of k outcomes, split into its outcomes
\htmlClass{new}{P(A) = k\cdot\tfrac{1}{n} = \tfrac{k}{n}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the countable-adding technique again
  
|\Omega| = \tbinom{49}{6} = 13\,983\,816 @@ a ticket picks 6 of the numbers 1 to 49, and so does the draw (the choosing-counts-parts technique, 4j13)
\text{every draw equally likely} @@ the guess the machine is built to make true
\text{jackpot: } \tfrac{1}{13\,983\,816} @@ the equal-cases technique, k = 1
\text{exactly 3 right: } \tbinom{6}{3}\tbinom{43}{3} = 20 \times 12\,341 = 246\,820 @@ choose 3 of the 6 winning numbers, then 3 of the 43 others (the stages-multiply technique, 4j6)
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{exactly 3 right}) = \tfrac{246\,820}{13\,983\,816} \approx 0.01765,\ \text{about 1 in 57}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-cases technique

Equal chances for the cases are a guess about the world, not a fact of counting. A lottery machine is built and tested to make the guess good; a die with a chipped corner is not. The technique says what follows once the guess is made. Whether to make it is checked against tallies, as 15·stat checked a density against a histogram.

30l2Trees, and the chance of at least one

Stands on 30l1, 30f2 (the chance of A given B, and the independent technique), 30f1 (the chance-rules technique), 30b3 (the countable-adding technique) and 4b (induction).

Many chances come from things done one after another: a card, then another card; a throw, then another throw. A tree draws them so. Each branch carries the chance of its step given the path that leads to it. A path from the root to a leaf is one way the whole run can go, and two different paths are separate events.

Branch-product technique: the chance of a path through a tree is the product of the given chances on its branches; and the chance of an event is the sum of the chances of the separate paths that make it up.

P(A_1 \text{ and } A_2) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1) @@ the chance of A₂ given A₁ (30f2), multiplied out, with P(A₁) > 0
P(A_1 \text{ and } \cdots \text{ and } A_n) = P(A_1 \text{ and } \cdots \text{ and } A_{n-1})\,P(A_n \mid A_1 \text{ and } \cdots \text{ and } A_{n-1}) @@ the same line, with the first n − 1 steps as one event
P(\text{path}) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1)\cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \text{ and } \cdots \text{ and } A_{n-1}) @@ induction on n (4b)
E = \text{path}_1 \cup \cdots \cup \text{path}_m,\ \ \text{separate} @@ different paths part at some branch
\htmlClass{new}{P(E) = P(\text{path}_1) + \cdots + P(\text{path}_m)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the countable-adding technique (30b3)

At-least-one technique: if A₁, …, Aₙ are independent, the chance that at least one of them happens is 1 − (1 − P(A₁))(1 − P(A₂))⋯(1 − P(Aₙ)).

P(\text{not } A \text{ and } B) = P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B) = (1 - P(A))\,P(B) @@ B splits into its part in A and its part out of A (30b3); this is the independent technique (30f2)
\text{not } A_1, \ldots, \text{not } A_n \text{ are independent} @@ the line above, one event at a time
P(\text{none happens}) = (1 - P(A_1))\cdots(1 - P(A_n)) @@ none happens = not A₁ and … and not Aₙ
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (1 - P(A_1))\cdots(1 - P(A_n))} \quad \blacksquare @@ the chance-rules technique (30f1): P(not E) = 1 − P(E)
  
P(\text{ace, ace}) = \tfrac{4}{52}\cdot\tfrac{3}{51} = \tfrac{1}{221} @@ the branch-product technique: after one ace, 3 are left among 51 cards
P(\text{exactly one ace}) = \tfrac{4}{52}\cdot\tfrac{48}{51} + \tfrac{48}{52}\cdot\tfrac{4}{51} = \tfrac{32}{221} @@ two separate paths, added
P(\text{at least one six in 4 throws}) = 1 - \big(\tfrac56\big)^4 = \tfrac{671}{1296} \approx 0.518 @@ the at-least-one technique: separate throws are independent
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{at least one double six in 24 throws of two dice}) = 1 - \big(\tfrac{35}{36}\big)^{24} \approx 0.491} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same technique: a little less than an even bet

The last two lines were a gambler’s question in Paris in 1654. Four throws for one six, and twenty four throws for a double six, keep the same ratio of throws to faces. The gambler had found by playing that the first bet won more often than half the time and the second did not. The technique shows why: chances of at least one do not grow in proportion to the throws.

30l3Turning a given chance around, by hand

Stands on 30l2 and 30f3 (the turn-around and whole-chance techniques).

A test result is seen. What is wanted is the chance of the cause given the result, and what is known is the chance of the result given the cause. The turn-around technique of 30f3 does this. By hand there are two good ways. The first is to count an imagined crowd. The second is to work with odds: the odds of A are P(A) / P(not A), so a chance of 1/4 is odds of 1/3, one to three.

Odds-update technique: if P(E) > 0 and P(E | not A) > 0, the odds of A given E are the odds of A times the ratio P(E | A) / P(E | not A).

P(A \mid E) = \tfrac{P(E \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(E)} @@ the turn-around technique (30f3)
P(\text{not } A \mid E) = \tfrac{P(E \mid \text{not } A)\,P(\text{not } A)}{P(E)} @@ the same, for not A
\tfrac{P(A \mid E)}{P(\text{not } A \mid E)} = \tfrac{P(E \mid A)}{P(E \mid \text{not } A)}\cdot\tfrac{P(A)}{P(\text{not } A)} @@ divide: P(E) cancels
\htmlClass{new}{\text{odds after} = \text{ratio} \times \text{odds before}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the odds of A given E, and the odds of A
  
P(A) = 0.01,\ \ P(E \mid A) = 0.9,\ \ P(E \mid \text{not } A) = 0.09 @@ 1 in 100 have the condition; the test finds 9 in 10 of them; it wrongly flags 9 in 100 of the rest
\text{crowd of } 10\,000:\ \ 100 \text{ have it, } 90 \text{ flagged};\ \ 9900 \text{ do not, } 891 \text{ flagged} @@ the branch-product technique, in counts
P(A \mid E) = \tfrac{90}{90 + 891} = \tfrac{90}{981} = \tfrac{10}{109} \approx 0.092 @@ the whole-chance technique (30f3) for P(E), then the turn-around technique
\text{odds: } \tfrac{1}{99}\times\tfrac{0.9}{0.09} = \tfrac{10}{99},\ \ \text{chance } \tfrac{10}{109} @@ the odds-update technique: the same answer, in one line
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a second test, independent given the condition: } \tfrac{10}{99}\times 10 = \tfrac{100}{99},\ \ \text{chance} \approx 0.50} \quad \blacksquare @@ the odds-update technique again, with the first answer as the odds before

A positive result on a test that is right nine times in ten leaves a chance of about one in eleven. The condition is rare, and the many people without it make most of the flags. The ratio 0.9 / 0.09 = 10 is all the test can say. What the result means also needs the odds before, and those come from outside the test.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The equal-cases technique is the classical definition of probability, read as a technique: outcomes that are equally likely, then favourable cases over all cases. A tree of chances is a probability tree (or tree diagram). The branch-product technique is the multiplication rule, or chain rule, of probability; adding over paths is the addition rule for disjoint events, with the law of total probability behind it. The at-least-one technique is the complement rule at work. Odds keep their name; the ratio P(E | A) / P(E | not A) is the likelihood ratio, or Bayes factor, and the odds-update technique is Bayes' rule in odds form. Counting an imagined crowd is the method of natural frequencies. For a test, P(E | A) is its sensitivity, P(not E | not A) its specificity, P(A) the prevalence or base rate, and P(A | E) the positive predictive value. Swapping P(E | A) for P(A | E) is the base-rate fallacy, called in a courtroom the prosecutor's fallacy. The belief that black is due after a run of red is the gambler's fallacy. The three doors are the Monty Hall problem; the shared birthdays, the birthday problem.

Who said it first

The gambler of 1654 was Antoine Gombaud, the Chevalier de Méré, and Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat answered him in letters that began the calculus of chance. Pierre-Simon Laplace set out the classical definition in his Analytic Theory of Probabilities (1812) and his Philosophical Essay (1814). Alan Turing and I. J. Good worked with odds and the logarithm of the likelihood ratio, which they called the weight of evidence, at Bletchley Park in the Second World War; Harold Jeffreys used the Bayes factor in his Theory of Probability (1939). Richard von Mises posed the birthday problem in 1939. Steve Selvin put the three doors in a letter to The American Statistician in 1975, and Marilyn vos Savant's answer in 1990 drew thousands of letters insisting she was wrong. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed in the 1970s how often people ignore the base rate; William Thompson and Edward Schumann named the prosecutor's fallacy in 1987. Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed in 1995 that natural frequencies make the turn-around easier. The robbery of 1964 is People v. Collins: the Supreme Court of California reversed the conviction in 1968, and its opinion worked out the chance, about 0.41, that another couple matched. Lotto 6/49 began in Canada in 1982.
