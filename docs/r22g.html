22g · proof

Signs that alternate, and sums that can be rearranged

Some sums take a step up, then a step down, then up again, each step smaller than the last. Such a sum settles even when the sizes of its steps alone would wander, because each step undoes part of the one before. But a sum that settles only because its signs take turns is fragile. Change the order of its pieces and it can be made to settle anywhere you like, or to wander. A sum that settles in size is safe: any order gives the same total.

When the signs take turns and the pieces shrink to nothing, the sum settles, and the running totals bracket the total. A sum that settles but not in size can be reordered to reach any total. A sum that settles in size gives the same total in every order.

22g1Steps that take turns

Bracket technique: if vₙ falls and heads to 0, then v₁ − v₂ + v₃ − v₄ + ⋯ settles, and its total lies between any two running totals in a row. The even running totals climb, the odd ones fall, and the gap between them is one piece.

s_{2m+2} = s_{2m} + (v_{2m+1} - v_{2m+2}) \ge s_{2m} @@ vₙ falls: the even totals climb
s_{2m+1} = s_{2m-1} - (v_{2m} - v_{2m+1}) \le s_{2m-1} @@ the odd totals fall
s_2 \le s_{2m} \le s_{2m+1} \le s_1 @@ s₂ₘ₊₁ − s₂ₘ = v₂ₘ₊₁ ≥ 0
s_{2m} \to L,\ s_{2m+1} \to L' @@ the climb and fall techniques (22a2)
L' - L = \lim v_{2m+1} = 0 @@ vₙ heads to 0
\htmlClass{new}{s_n \to L, \text{ and } L \text{ lies between } s_n \text{ and } s_{n+1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ both threads settle at L (22b1)

So 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + ⋯ settles, though 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ wanders (22d3). Its total is ln 2. The even running total s₂ₙ is 1 + ½ + ⋯ + 1/(2n), less twice ½ + ¼ + ⋯ + 1/(2n), which is H₂ₙ − Hₙ. By the harmonic gap (22f4), that is (H₂ₙ − ln 2n) − (Hₙ − ln n) + ln 2, which heads to γ − γ + ln 2. The bracket gives an error bound for free: stop after n pieces, and you are off by less than the next piece.

22g2Settling in size, or settling only by taking turns

A sum that settles in size settles (22e4). A sum can settle without settling in size, as 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ⋯ does. Call that settling only by taking turns.

u_n^+ = \max(u_n, 0),\ \ u_n^- = \max(-u_n, 0) @@ the gains and the losses
u_n = u_n^+ - u_n^-,\ \ |u_n| = u_n^+ + u_n^- @@ each piece is one or the other
\textstyle\sum |u_n| \text{ settles} \Rightarrow \sum u_n^+,\ \sum u_n^- \text{ settle} @@ comparison (22e1): each is at most |uₙ|
\textstyle\sum u_n \text{ settles},\ \sum |u_n| \text{ wanders} \Rightarrow \sum u_n^+,\ \sum u_n^- \text{ both wander} @@ if one settled, the other would be ∑uₙ⁺ − ∑uₙ (22d4), and ∑|uₙ| would settle
\htmlClass{new}{\text{settling only by taking turns: the gains and the losses each pass every ceiling}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines before

That last line is the whole trouble. Such a sum is a tug of war between two endless supplies, gains and losses, each big enough to pull anywhere. Only the order of the pieces keeps them balanced.

22g3Any total you like

Reorder technique: if Σ uₙ settles only by taking turns, then for any number T some reordering of its pieces settles at T. Every piece is used exactly once; only the order changes.

\text{take gains until the running total first passes } T @@ possible: the gains pass every ceiling (22g2)
\text{take losses until it first drops below } T @@ possible: so do the losses
\text{repeat, always taking the next unused gain or loss} @@ each pile is used in its own order, so no piece is missed
|\text{total} - T| \le \text{the last piece taken} @@ each switch overshoots by at most one piece
\text{the pieces head to } 0 @@ ∑uₙ settles (22d2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the reordered sum settles at } T} \quad \blacksquare @@ the overshoot heads to 0

Taking two gains for each loss in 1 − ½ + ⅓ − ¼ + ⋯ gives 1 + ⅓ − ½ + ⅕ + ⅐ − ¼ + ⋯, which settles at (3/2) ln 2. Same pieces, a total half again as big. And a sum that settles in size cannot be pulled like this: every reordering of it has the same total. A sum written down is a set of pieces and an order, and the order is part of the arithmetic.

22g.4.5seamOn this rung a total is where the running totals settle, and these take turns between 1 and 0. A monk who wrote this sum in 1703 gave it the total ½, and one of the inventors of the calculus agreed. In 1826 a young mathematician wrote that sums with no total are an invention of the devil, and a shame to base any proof on. Late in that century others took the averages of the running totals instead, and built rules that give the usual total to every sum that settles.

The accepted names

The bracket technique is the alternating series test (Leibniz test), with its remainder estimate. Settling in size is absolute convergence; settling only by taking turns is conditional convergence. The reorder technique is the Riemann rearrangement theorem; the other half, that a sum settling in size has one total in every order, is Dirichlet's theorem. 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ is Grandi's series.

Who said it first

Gottfried Leibniz stated the test in letters of 1713–1714. Guido Grandi wrote in 1703 that 1 − 1 + 1 − ⋯ was ½, and drew a theological lesson from it. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet showed in 1837 that order can matter, and Bernhard Riemann proved in 1854 (published 1868) that a conditionally convergent sum can be reordered to any total. In 22g.4.5, the monk is Grandi, the inventor of the calculus Leibniz (1713), and the young mathematician Niels Henrik Abel, in a letter of 1826; the averages of running totals are Cesàro summation (Ernesto Cesàro, 1890).
