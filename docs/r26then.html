How Can a Circuit Say True or False?

How can a circuit say true or false? Rung seven read a switch as a statement: closed is true, open is false. But a chip does not hold switches that are either closed or open. It holds billions of tiny valves whose current climbs smoothly with the voltage, and the voltage on a wire is never exactly anything. It wobbles with heat, with the motors in the next room, with the wire beside it. What makes the question hard is that every part is a smooth curve, and yet the chip must answer yes or no, billions of times a second, without ever being wrong. This page follows the curves: a valve whose current grows by equal factors for equal steps of voltage, a small wiggle made large by a tangent line, a huge and unruly gain tamed by feeding some of it back, and a chain of gates that pulls every wobbling voltage back to a clean true or false. Then it builds 4·comp’s adder out of these gates, makes a memory out of two of them in a ring, and finds the same circuit in a nerve cell.

A diode’s current grows tenfold for each extra sixty thousandths of a volt, so it is shut below about half a volt and wide open just above. A small wiggle at a transistor’s input comes out larger by the slope of its curve, which is its tangent line. A huge gain fed back through two resistors gives a gain fixed by the resistors alone. A gate whose curve is flat at the ends and steep in the middle reads a whole band of voltages as true and another as false, and a chain of such gates pulls every voltage outside the middle back to a clean value. Two gates in a ring hold one of two states, which is a bit of memory; three in a ring never settle, which is a clock. A nerve cell is a store of charge with a leak, and it fires when its voltage crosses a threshold.

26then1A One-Way Valve

A diode is a sandwich of two kinds of silicon. On one side the current is carried by electrons. On the other it is carried by holes, places where an electron is missing, and holes move the way 7·phys’s arrow for the current points. A measured law gives its current I for a voltage V across it: I = I_s(e^(V/V_T) − 1). Here I_s is tiny, about 10⁻¹² A for a small silicon diode, and V_T ≈ 0.0259 V at room temperature.

V = V_T\ln\big(\tfrac{I}{I_s} + 1\big) \approx V_T\ln\tfrac{I}{I_s} @@ undo the exponential (17); the 1 is tiny once I is far above I_s
V(10I) - V(I) \approx V_T\ln 10 \approx 0.0596 \text{ V} @@ the log of a product is the sum of the logs
0.1 \text{ mA}: 0.48 \text{ V},\ \ 1 \text{ mA}: 0.54 \text{ V},\ \ 10 \text{ mA}: 0.60 \text{ V} @@ I_s = 10⁻¹² A
V < 0:\ \ I \approx -I_s @@ backwards, almost nothing flows
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every } 60 \text{ mV more multiplies the current by } 10} \quad \blacksquare @@ so the voltage across a working diode hardly moves

Sixty-millivolt technique: each extra 60 thousandths of a volt across a diode multiplies its current tenfold, so a diode is shut below about half a volt and wide open just above. This is why 7·phys could treat its LED as taking a fixed 2 V. Its exponential law is so steep that any sensible current needs nearly the same voltage.

26then2Gain Is a Tangent Line

A transistor is two diodes back to back, with a thin middle. The voltage V on its input diode sets a current through the whole transistor by the same exponential law: I = I_s e^(V/V_T). Put a resistor R between a supply V_s and the transistor. The voltage left at the output is V_out = V_s − RI. A small wiggle on the input comes out as a wiggle on the output. How much larger?

V_{\text{out}}(V) = V_s - R\,I_s e^{V/V_T} @@ the exponential law, through the resistor (7·phys)
V_{\text{out}}'(V) = -\tfrac{R\,I_s e^{V/V_T}}{V_T} = -\tfrac{R\,I}{V_T} @@ exp is its own slope (17f), and the chain rule
\Delta V_{\text{out}} \approx -\tfrac{R\,I}{V_T}\,\Delta V @@ the tangent line (13k): its error shrinks faster than the wiggle
\htmlClass{new}{I = 1 \text{ mA},\ R = 5 \text{ k}\Omega:\ \ -\tfrac{5}{0.0259} \approx -193} \quad \blacksquare @@ a 1 mV wiggle comes out 193 mV, upside down

From here on, this slope is the gain. Gain technique: a transistor carrying current I through a resistor R makes a small wiggle −RI/V_T times larger, because that is the slope of its curve. The tangent line is only good for small wiggles. For a wiggle of 5 mV the exponential has already bent 10% away from it.

26then3Feedback Fixes the Gain

Stack many transistors and the gain becomes huge, A ≈ 200 000 for a cheap amplifier on one chip. But A is unruly: it changes with warmth, with age, from one chip to the next. The cure is to send a share β of the output back, taken off by two resistors, and subtract it from the input. The amplifier only sees the difference.

V_{\text{out}} = A\big(V_{\text{in}} - \beta V_{\text{out}}\big) @@ the amplifier multiplies the difference by A
V_{\text{out}}(1 + A\beta) = A\,V_{\text{in}} @@ gather the outputs
\tfrac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{in}}} = \tfrac{A}{1 + A\beta} = \tfrac{1}{\beta + 1/A} @@ divide by A
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{A}{1 + A\beta} \to \tfrac{1}{\beta} \text{ as } A \to \infty} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1/A heads to 0 (10)

Feedback technique: a huge gain A fed back through a share β gives a gain close to 1/β, fixed by two resistors and not by the unruly A. With A = 200 000 and β = 0.1 the gain is 9.9995. Halve A and it is 9.9990. The feedback trades a gain nobody can fix for one anybody can.

26then4Thresholds as True and False

A gate on a chip is made of two kinds of transistor used as switches. One kind closes when its input is high, the other when its input is low. Put one of each between the 3.3 V supply and the ground, and join them at the output. A high input connects the output to ground; a low input connects it to the supply. That is a NOT gate. Its output g(v) for an input v is flat near 0 and near 3.3 V, and falls steeply in the middle, with a gain far above 1.

Chips agree on bands, as 7·phys did. An input below 0.8 V is read as false. An input above 2.0 V is read as true. The strip between is forbidden. Every gate promises outputs below 0.4 V or above 2.4 V. A wire may pick up 0.4 V of noise, the margin, and still be read right.

v_{n+1} = g(v_n) @@ a chain of NOT gates, each feeding the next
G = g \circ g:\ \ G(0.4) \approx 0.005,\ \ G'(v) = g'(g(v))\,g'(v) @@ two gates in a row; the chain rule. Model: g(v) = 3.3/(1 + e^{4(v − 1.65)})
|G'| \le k < 1 \text{ on } [0, 0.8] \text{ and on } [2.0, 3.3] @@ at each end one factor is tiny: the curve is flat there
v_0 \in [0, 0.8] \Rightarrow v_{2n} \to \text{the low rest of } G @@ the pull-together technique (22h): G sends [0, 0.8] into itself, and shrinks gaps
\htmlClass{new}{G'(1.65) = g'(1.65)^2 > 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the middle rest does not hold (24k): a voltage there is pushed out to one side

Restoring technique: a chain of gates whose curve is flat at the ends and steep in the middle pulls every voltage outside the forbidden strip back toward 0 or the supply, so noise smaller than the margin dies out. A switch is never quite closed or open. A chain of gates makes it so.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, read as one value

No wire ever carries exactly 0 V or exactly 3.3 V. Each carries a cloud of voltages, smeared by heat and by its neighbours. The chip does not pretend otherwise. It reads a whole band as one value, and it promises an output band narrower than the band it reads, with a stated margin between them.

Read a whole band as one value, and keep a stated margin between the bands.

That margin is what makes a billion gates in a row add up to no errors at all. Every gate takes in a cloud and gives out a tighter one. The forbidden strip is where the chip declines to answer, and it is kept empty on purpose.

A long cable across a sawmill floor in Tacoma picks up half a volt of hum from the motors. Can it carry 3.3 V logic safely? What would you change, the bands or the cable?

26then5Gates, Sums and Memory

Put two switches of the first kind in a row to ground, and two of the second kind side by side to the supply. The output is pulled low exactly when both inputs are high. That is a NOT-AND gate, a NAND, made of four transistors. A NAND with its two inputs joined is a NOT. A NAND followed by a NOT is an AND. Four NANDs make an exclusive or.

A + B = 2\,(A \cdot B) + (A \oplus B) @@ the two-bit-adder technique (4·comp)
A \oplus B:\ 4 \text{ NANDs};\ \ A \cdot B:\ \text{its first NAND, then a NOT}:\ 1 \text{ more} @@ the exclusive or’s first gate is already NAND(A, B)
\text{two-bit adder} = 5 \text{ NANDs} = 20 \text{ transistors} @@ four each
\htmlClass{new}{\text{full adder} = 9 \text{ NANDs} = 36 \text{ transistors}} @@ two two-bit adders and an OR, sharing gates (4·comp)

Now join two NOT gates in a ring: the output of each is the input of the other. The ring rests where v = G(v), with G = g ∘ g. There are three such places.

G(v) = v \text{ near } 0,\ \text{ at } 1.65,\ \text{ near } 3.3 @@ the rests of two gates in a ring
|G'| < 1 \text{ near } 0 \text{ and } 3.3:\ \text{they hold} @@ the sign-of-slope technique (24k), on the steps of the ring
G'(1.65) > 1:\ \text{it does not hold} @@ the slightest noise pushes the ring to one side
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an even ring holds one of two states: one bit}} @@ a third NOT gate in the ring: g∘g∘g has only the middle rest, and it does not hold

Ring technique: two NOT gates in a ring rest in one of two states and keep it, which is one bit of memory; an odd ring has no rest that holds, so it runs round for ever, which is a clock. A memory cell on a chip is that ring of four transistors, with two more as doors to read and write it.

26then6A Nerve Cell as a Circuit

A nerve cell is wrapped in a thin fatty skin, its membrane. The membrane keeps charge apart, so it is a store of charge C. Small channels leak ions through it, so it is a resistor R. Pumps keep more potassium inside and more sodium outside, so it is a battery: at rest the inside sits about 70 mV below the outside, E = −70 mV. A current I flowing in from other cells charges it.

C\,V' = -\tfrac{V - E}{R} + I @@ the two circuit laws (7·phys): what flows in charges the store or leaks out
V - E = RI\big(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\big),\ \ \tau = RC @@ the target technique (24·then.2), from rest
V - E = d \iff e^{-t/\tau} = 1 - \tfrac{d}{RI} @@ the cell fires when it climbs d above rest; then it is reset to rest
\htmlClass{new}{T = \tau\ln\tfrac{RI}{RI - d}\ \text{ if } RI > d;\ \text{ never if } RI \le d} \quad \blacksquare @@ take logs (17); below d, the target is under the threshold

Fire technique: a cell whose drive RI is above its threshold gap d fires every τ ln(RI/(RI − d)), and a cell whose drive is below it never fires. A typical cell has τ ≈ 10 ms and d ≈ 15 mV. A drive of RI = 20 mV gives T ≈ 13.9 ms, about 72 spikes a second. Each spike is all or none: true or false again.

Where does −70 mV come from? Potassium leaks out until the charge left behind holds it back. A measured law gives that balance as (RT/F) ln(outside/inside) = 26.7 mV × ln(5/140) ≈ −89 mV at body warmth. The cell sits a little above it, at −70 mV, because some sodium leaks in too. In 1952 a model fitted to the giant nerve fibre of the squid, half a millimetre across, made the leak itself depend on the voltage, through sodium and potassium gates that open and shut at their own rates. Four rules on rates, stepped forward by hand (24i), made the spike by themselves: its height, its shape and its speed down the fibre.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The diode law is the Shockley diode equation, I_s the saturation current and V_T = kT/q the thermal voltage. The two kinds of silicon are n-type and p-type, and the sandwich is a p-n junction. The transistor here is a bipolar junction transistor, and its gain as a slope is its small-signal gain, g_m R with g_m = I/V_T the transconductance. The amplifier with huge gain is an operational amplifier, and β is the feedback fraction of negative feedback. The switches on a chip are n-channel and p-channel MOSFETs, and a gate built from both kinds is CMOS logic. 0.8 V and 2.0 V are the input logic levels of 3.3 V logic, and 0.4 V the noise margin. The two gates in a ring are a latch, and with two doors a six-transistor SRAM cell; an odd ring is a ring oscillator. The membrane model is the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron; −70 mV is the resting potential, and the potassium balance is the Nernst potential. The 1952 model is the Hodgkin–Huxley model, and a spike is an action potential.

Who said it first

John Bardeen and Walter Brattain made the first transistor at Bell Labs in 1947, and William Shockley gave the junction transistor and the diode law in 1949; he then moved to Mountain View, where his staff left to found Fairchild Semiconductor. Harold Black thought of negative feedback on the Lackawanna ferry in 1927. Frank Wanlass at Fairchild built the first CMOS gate in 1963. Henry Sheffer (1913), and Charles Sanders Peirce before him (1880), found that one gate is enough for all of logic. Louis Lapicque modelled a nerve as a leaky store of charge in 1907. Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley measured the squid’s giant fibre, which J. Z. Young had found in 1936, and Huxley stepped the four rules forward on a hand-cranked calculator. In 26·then.8.5, Luigi Galvani published animal electricity in 1791; Alessandro Volta answered with his pile of metals in 1800. Galvani’s nephew Giovanni Aldini went on to make muscles twitch with no metals at all.
