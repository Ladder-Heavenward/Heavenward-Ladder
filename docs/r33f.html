33f · proof

Boundary value problems

Every rule on this rung so far met its sine shapes by luck. The rod, the string and the round plate each turned out to have shapes that only fade or only swing, and those shapes turned out to be perpendicular, so the series of the rung before could be used. This step shows it was not luck. A rule of second slopes on a stretch, with its ends held at 0, is a map on functions, and moving one slope across by parts, twice, shows that the map is symmetric. Rung 31 proved that a symmetric map has perpendicular square axes. Among functions, the square axes are the shapes, and so the shapes of every such rule are perpendicular, whatever the rod is made of and however it varies along its length. Then the step answers a different question about the same rules: how does a string tied at both ends sag under a load? Load it at one point with one kick. The answer to that one kick, slid along and added up, answers every load. And the kick’s answer, written in shapes, gives a sum of rung 32 for free.

A second-slope rule with its ends at 0 is a symmetric map on functions, so its shapes are perpendicular and, when nothing pulls the other way, have positive numbers. The answer to a single kick at one point builds the answer to every load, and written in shapes it is each shape divided by its number.

33f1A map on functions that swaps by parts

Let p have a continuous slope and p > 0 on [α, β], and let q be continuous there. For y with continuous second slopes, write

\(Ly = -(p\,y^{\prime})^{\prime} + q\,y.\)

L takes functions to functions, and it is straight: L(y + z) = Ly + Lz and L(cy) = cLy. A rod whose stiffness p and loss q vary along it gives such an L; the rod of 33a is p = 1, q = 0, and Ly = −y″. Hold y and z at 0 at both ends, and use 31a’s product of two functions, ⟨y, z⟩ = ∫ y z.

Swap-by-parts technique: if y and z are 0 at both ends, then ⟨Ly, z⟩ = ⟨y, Lz⟩. So L is symmetric for the product.

\textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta -(p\,y^{\prime})^{\prime} z = \big[-p\,y^{\prime}z\big]_\alpha^\beta + \int_\alpha^\beta p\,y^{\prime}z^{\prime} @@ parts (16e)
\big[-p\,y^{\prime}z\big]_\alpha^\beta = 0 @@ z = 0 at α and at β
\langle Ly, z\rangle = \textstyle\int_\alpha^\beta \big(p\,y^{\prime}z^{\prime} + q\,yz\big) @@ add the q term
\text{the right side does not change when } y \text{ and } z \text{ swap} @@ each product is the same either way round
\htmlClass{new}{\langle Ly, z\rangle = \langle y, Lz\rangle} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines with y and z swapped

33f2The shapes are perpendicular

A shape of L is a function y, not always 0, with y = 0 at both ends and Ly = λy for some number λ, its number. This is 27f’s arrow that a map only stretches, among functions. Its accepted name is an eigenfunction.

Perpendicular-shapes technique: two shapes of L with different numbers are perpendicular. If q ≥ 0, every number is positive.

Ly = \lambda y,\ \ Lz = \mu z,\ \ \lambda \ne \mu @@ two shapes, both 0 at the ends
\lambda\langle y, z\rangle = \langle Ly, z\rangle = \langle y, Lz\rangle = \mu\langle y, z\rangle @@ the swap-by-parts technique
(\lambda - \mu)\langle y, z\rangle = 0,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{\langle y, z\rangle = 0} @@ λ − μ is not 0: the argument of 31d, among functions
\lambda\langle y, y\rangle = \langle Ly, y\rangle = \textstyle\int\big(p\,y^{\prime 2} + q\,y^2\big) @@ the third line of 33f1, with z = y
\htmlClass{new}{\lambda > 0 \text{ when } q \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ ⟨y, y⟩ > 0; and y′ is not always 0, else y would be constant, so 0

For Ly = −y″ on [0, L], the shapes are sin(nπx/L) with numbers (nπ/L)², as 33a2 found. That they are perpendicular was 32a’s computation; now it is forced by the symmetry, and it holds just as well for a rod of copper welded to a rod of steel, where no one can write the shapes down. And the numbers are positive, so every cooling shape cools, and none grows.

33f3The answer to a single kick

A string of tension 1 is tied at x = 0 and x = 1 and loaded with a force h(x) per metre. When it is still, the string’s rule of 33c1 with y_tt = 0 says −y″ = h. For 0 ≤ s ≤ 1, let

\(G(x, s) = x(1 - s) \text{ for } x \le s,\qquad G(x, s) = s(1 - x) \text{ for } x \ge s.\)

For a fixed s, G is a tent: two straight pieces from 0 up to the peak s(1 − s) at x = s and back to 0. It is the shape of the string pushed by one unit of force at the single point s.

Single-kick technique: for every continuous load h, the one answer of −y″ = h with y(0) = y(1) = 0 is y(x) = ∫₀¹ G(x, s) h(s) ds.

y(x) = (1 - x)\textstyle\int_0^x s\,h(s)\,ds + x\int_x^1 (1 - s)\,h(s)\,ds @@ split the total at s = x
y^{\prime}(x) = -\textstyle\int_0^x s\,h + (1 - x)x\,h(x) + \int_x^1 (1 - s)\,h - x(1 - x)\,h(x) @@ the product rule and the moving-end technique (16b)
y^{\prime}(x) = -\textstyle\int_0^x s\,h(s)\,ds + \int_x^1 (1 - s)\,h(s)\,ds @@ the two middle terms cancel
y^{\prime\prime}(x) = -x\,h(x) - (1 - x)\,h(x) = -h(x) @@ 16b again
y(0) = 0 + 0,\ \ y(1) = 0 + 0 @@ each piece has a factor or a total that is 0 there
\htmlClass{new}{y = \textstyle\int_0^1 G(\cdot, s)\,h(s)\,ds \text{ is the one answer}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two answers differ by a straight line that is 0 at both ends, which is 0 (14e)

So the response to every load is the sum of responses to kicks, each kick weighted by the load at its point. And G(x, s) = G(s, x): a kick at s seen at x is a kick at x seen at s. That is the swap-by-parts technique in another form. One example first. A clothesline 10 m long under a tension of 200 N has a 20 N jacket hung 3 m from one end. Scaled to this string, the sag under the jacket is 20 × 3 × 7 / (200 × 10) = 0.21 m.

33f4The kick, written in shapes

Kick-in-shapes technique: G(x, s) = Σ 2 sin(nπx) sin(nπs) / (n²π²), the sum over n = 1, 2, 3, …: each shape of −y″ on [0, 1], times its value at the kick, divided by its number.

b_n(s) = 2\textstyle\int_0^1 G(x, s)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx @@ the sine coefficients of the tent (32a, as on 33a3)
\textstyle\int_0^1 G\,(n\pi)^2\sin(n\pi x)\,dx = \big(G^{\prime}(s^-) - G^{\prime}(s^+)\big)\sin(n\pi s) = \sin(n\pi s) @@ (nπ)² sin = −(sin)″; parts twice on [0, s] and on [s, 1]: G″ = 0 on each, G and sin are 0 at the ends, and G′ is 1 − s left of s and −s right of it
b_n(s) = \tfrac{2\sin(n\pi s)}{n^2\pi^2} @@ divide by (nπ)²
\textstyle\sum |b_n(s)| \le \tfrac{2}{\pi^2}\sum \tfrac{1}{n^2} \text{ settles} @@ the p-sums (22f); so the series settles at once and is continuous (23d)
\htmlClass{new}{G(x, s) = \textstyle\sum_n \tfrac{2\sin(n\pi x)\sin(n\pi s)}{n^2\pi^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 32c returns G at every x ≠ s, where it has a slope; both sides are continuous, so they agree at s too

Read it as 31d would. To undo a symmetric map, undo it along each square axis: divide by the number. The kick is every shape at once, and the string answers each shape by dividing it by (nπ)². Now set x = s = ½. The left side is ¼. On the right, sin²(nπ/2) is 1 for odd n and 0 for even n. So

\(\tfrac14 = \tfrac{2}{\pi^2}\Big(1 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{25} + \tfrac1{49} + \cdots\Big),\qquad 1 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac1{25} + \cdots = \tfrac{\pi^2}{8}.\)

32b found 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯ = π²/6. Its even terms are a quarter of the whole, so its odd terms are three quarters: π²/8. The string, kicked at its middle, knew.

The accepted names

L is a Sturm–Liouville operator, and a shape with its number is an eigenfunction with its eigenvalue. The swap-by-parts technique says L is self-adjoint (symmetric) on functions vanishing at the ends. G is the Green’s function of −d²/dx² with Dirichlet conditions, and the single kick is a point load, written with the delta function δ(x − s), which is not a function. G(x, s) = G(s, x) is the reciprocity of Maxwell and Betti. The kick-in-shapes technique is the eigenfunction expansion of the Green’s function. The edge standing question is the Fredholm alternative.

Who said it first

Charles-François Sturm and Joseph Liouville worked out the theory of these rules in a series of papers of 1836 and 1837. George Green, a miller of Nottingham, used the kick answer for electricity in an essay he printed himself in 1828. James Clerk Maxwell (1864) and Enrico Betti (1872) found the reciprocity of loads in structures. Paul Dirac wrote the kick as δ in 1927, and Laurent Schwartz made it rigorous as a distribution in 1945.

A proof that is not allowed

“The kick is a load that is 0 except at s and totals 1. So y = ∫G h with that h gives y(x) = G(x, s) × 0 everywhere but s, and the total is 0.” Not allowed: no function is 0 except at one point and totals 1; a single point has size 0 (30a). The kick is the end of a list of loads, each spread over a smaller stretch about s, each totalling 1, and their answers head to G.
