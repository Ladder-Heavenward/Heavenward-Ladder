Why Did the Bridge Sway?

Why did the bridge sway? A footbridge of steel and aluminium opened across the Thames in London on 10 June 2000, and crowds walked over it from the first morning. Within minutes of the crowd stepping on, the deck began to swing from side to side, far enough to make walking hard. Two days later it was shut, and it stayed shut for almost two years while dampers were fitted. Nothing was wrong with its strength. The trouble was that a walker pushes sideways a little with every step, left foot then right, about once a second, and the bridge had a way of swinging sideways at about that pace. One walker barely moves it. A crowd at random barely moves it either, because their pushes mostly cancel. But a walker on a moving deck adjusts, and the adjusting can bring walkers into step with the deck, so their pushes stop cancelling, and the swing grows, and more walkers fall in. This page builds the deck as one way of shaking, the walker as a push at a pace, the falling into step, and the number of walkers at which the swing starts to feed itself, and then turns to a bridge in Washington State that twisted itself apart in a gale in 1940, for a reason that was told wrongly in textbooks for half a century. Whether the London crowd fell into step first, or the deck moved first and the stepping followed, is argued today.

A span moving as X sin(πx/L) is one mass M = mL/2 on a spring, with damping ratio ζ. N walkers pushing G sin θᵢ at random phases make a push of size about √N·G; in step, N·G. A walker whose pace is within CA of the deck’s locks, a quarter turn ahead, and feeds the swing. Balancing the energy fed against the energy lost gives a crowd size Nc = 4ζMΩ²/(πGC·P(Ω)) past which the swing grows: about 150 for the north span. A deck twisted by wind can feed itself with no pace to match at all, once the wind passes one speed.

sky43.1A Deck as One Way of Shaking

31·eng split a building’s motion into ways of shaking, each going on its own, and 31·eng.2 found that a push reaches a way only through its reading along it. A footbridge deck is a long strip, and the way that matters here is its first sideways one: the deck bowing to one side along the span, most in the middle, not at all at the piers. For a span of length L held at both ends, that shape is sin(πx/L), and the deck’s sideways move at x is X(t) sin(πx/L).

Modal-mass technique: a span of mass m per metre moving in the shape X sin(πx/L) has the energy of motion of one mass M = mL/2 moving at X′, and a sideways push F at the point x drives it as a push F sin(πx/L) on that one mass. So the whole deck, for this way, is 26·cb24’s spring with a brake: MX″ + BX′ + KX = push, with its own pace Ω = √(K/M) and damping ratio ζ = B/(2√(MK)).

\tfrac12\int_0^L m\,\big(X'\sin\tfrac{\pi x}{L}\big)^2dx = \tfrac12\,m X'^2\int_0^L \sin^2\tfrac{\pi x}{L}\,dx @@ the energy of motion, strip by strip
\int_0^L \sin^2\tfrac{\pi x}{L}\,dx = \int_0^L \tfrac{1 - \cos(2\pi x/L)}{2}\,dx = \tfrac L2 @@ the cosine runs one whole turn and totals 0 (21)
\tfrac12\,m X'^2\cdot\tfrac L2 = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{mL}{2}\big)X'^2 @@ the energy of one mass mL/2
\htmlClass{new}{M = \tfrac{mL}{2},\ \ \text{push at } x \text{ counts as } F\sin\tfrac{\pi x}{L}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shared-push technique (31·eng.2): the reading of a push at one point
  
M = 1.13\times10^5 \text{ kg},\ \ \Omega = 6.47 \text{ rad/s} @@ the north span’s first sideways way, as the 2005 study took it
f = \Omega/2\pi = 1.03 \text{ Hz},\ \ K = M\Omega^2 = 4.73\times10^6 \text{ N/m} @@ about one swing a second
\zeta = 0.75\% \Rightarrow B = 2\zeta\sqrt{MK} = 1.10\times10^4 \text{ N s/m} @@ a light brake: under one percent
\htmlClass{new}{m = 2M/L = 2790 \text{ kg per metre over } L = 81 \text{ m}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the modal-mass technique run backwards

A damping ratio of 0.75% means each swing, left alone, is about 95% of the one before, by 26·cb24’s light-brake technique: e^(−2π × 0.0075) = 0.954. A deck like this rings for many swings after a push. That is the soil the rest of the page grows in.

sky43.2A Walker Is a Push at a Pace

A walker’s weight moves over the left foot, then over the right. To do that the foot pushes the ground sideways, one way then the other, so one sideways cycle takes two steps. At about two steps a second that is about one push cycle a second, close to the north span’s 1.03 Hz. Model walker i as a sideways push G sin θᵢ with its own phase θᵢ, and take G = 30 N, the figure the 2005 study used.

MX'' + BX' + KX = G\sin\Omega t @@ one walker, at the deck’s own pace
X = A\sin(\Omega t - \tfrac\pi2),\ \ A = \tfrac{G}{B\Omega} @@ at its own pace the spring and the mass cancel; only the brake is left (32j2)
A = \tfrac{30}{1.10\times10^4 \times 6.47} @@ the north span
\htmlClass{new}{A = 0.42 \text{ mm}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one walker: nothing anyone would feel

Random-crowd technique: N pushes G sin(Ωt + θₖ) with phases θₖ independent and spread evenly round the turn add to one push G·|Σ e^(iθₖ)| sin(Ωt + Φ), and the average of |Σ e^(iθₖ)|² is N; so a crowd at random pushes about √N times as hard as one walker, and a crowd in step N times. Here Φ (capital phi) means the phase of the whole push.

\textstyle\sum_k G\sin(\Omega t + \theta_k) = G\,\operatorname{Im}\big(e^{i\Omega t}\sum_k e^{i\theta_k}\big) @@ each push is the height of a turning arrow (26a)
\big|\sum_k e^{i\theta_k}\big|^2 = N + \sum_{j \ne k} e^{i(\theta_j - \theta_k)} @@ multiply the sum by its mirror image: |z|² = z z̄
E\,e^{i(\theta_j - \theta_k)} = E\,e^{i\theta_j}\cdot E\,e^{-i\theta_k} = 0 @@ independent averages multiply (30g3); an even spread round the turn averages 0
\htmlClass{new}{E\big|\sum_k e^{i\theta_k}\big|^2 = N} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spreads of the N arrows add
  
\text{at random: } \sqrt{160}\times 0.42 = 5.3 \text{ mm} @@ the random-crowd technique
\text{in step: } 160\times 0.42 = 68 \text{ mm} @@ every push lined up
\htmlClass{new}{\text{falling into step multiplies the swing by } \sqrt{160} \approx 13} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same people, the same pushes

The swings on opening day were large enough to close the bridge. A random crowd moves the deck by millimetres; a crowd in step moves it by centimetres. Five millimetres is not nothing, though: it is enough to be felt underfoot, and that is where the next footing starts.

sky43.3Falling Into Step

37i4’s lock-in technique: two timekeepers whose gap obeys φ′ = Δω − K sin φ settle to one time exactly when |Δω| ≤ K. Here one timekeeper is the deck, so massive that the walkers barely change it, and the other is each walker. A walker on a deck swinging with size A is nudged, by an amount that grows with A, towards a phase a quarter turn ahead of the deck, where its push is in step with the deck’s speed: the lean that keeps a walker’s balance on a deck moving under it. With the deck X = A sin Ψ, Ψ′ = Ω, the 2005 model, in its simplest case, writes θᵢ′ = ωᵢ + CA cos(θᵢ − Ψ), with ωᵢ the walker’s own pace and C a sensitivity, fitted to the crowd tests at C ≈ 16 per metre per second.

\varphi_i = \theta_i - \Psi,\ \ \nu_i = \omega_i - \Omega @@ the walker’s phase measured from the deck’s
\varphi_i' = \nu_i + CA\cos\varphi_i @@ 37i4’s rule for the gap, turned a quarter turn
\text{rest: } \cos\varphi^* = -\nu_i/(CA),\ \text{an answer} \iff |\nu_i| \le CA @@ the lock-in technique
\tfrac{d}{d\varphi}(\nu_i + CA\cos\varphi) = -CA\sin\varphi < 0 \iff \sin\varphi^* > 0 @@ the rest that holds (24k1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a locked walker has } \sin\varphi^* = \sqrt{1 - \nu_i^2/(CA)^2} > 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ ahead of the deck: pushing with its speed

Locked-share technique: if the walkers’ paces have density P near Ω, nearly flat over the window |ν| ≤ CA, then the locked walkers among N give Σ sin φᵢ* ≈ N·P(Ω)·πCA/2. The sum is N times the area under P(Ω)√(1 − ν²/(CA)²) over the window, and that curve is a half disc squashed to height 1 and width 2CA, of area π·CA/2. Here ν (little nu) means a walker's pace less the deck's.

P(\Omega) = \tfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}},\ \ \sigma = 0.63 \text{ rad/s} @@ walkers’ paces as a bell (30j) of width 0.1 Hz round the deck’s
P(\Omega) = 0.633 \text{ s} @@ the height of the bell at its middle
CA = 16 \times 0.005 = 0.08 \text{ rad/s} @@ a swing of 5 mm, the random crowd’s
\htmlClass{new}{\text{share locked} \approx 2 \times 0.08 \times 0.633 = 10\%} \quad \blacksquare @@ the window times the density

One walker in ten locks to a swing of 5 mm, and each one locked pushes in step with the deck’s speed, so it feeds energy in. The walkers who do not lock drift past, sometimes ahead and sometimes behind; their pushes average close to 0 and are left out, held in trust in the 2005 analysis, which treats them exactly. A larger swing widens the window, and more lock.

sky43.4The Number That Starts It

Now balance the books. The deck’s energy is ½MΩ²A². The brake takes energy at BX′², and the walkers put it in at their push times the deck’s speed. Over one swing, with X = A sin Ψ and X′ = ΩA cos Ψ, each average is a whole turn of a cosine squared or a product, as in sky43.1.

Crowd-threshold technique: with the locked walkers feeding Σ sin φᵢ* ≈ N·P(Ω)·πCA/2 and the rest averaging 0, a small swing grows when N > Nc and dies when N < Nc, where Nc = 4ζMΩ²/(πGC·P(Ω)).

\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(\tfrac12M\Omega^2A^2\big) = \tfrac12 G\Omega A\textstyle\sum_i\sin\varphi_i - \tfrac12 B\Omega^2A^2 @@ average of sin(Ψ + φ) cos Ψ is ½ sin φ; of cos² Ψ is ½
A' = \tfrac{G}{2M\Omega}\textstyle\sum_i\sin\varphi_i - \zeta\Omega A @@ divide by MΩ²A; B/(2M) = ζΩ
A' = \Big(\tfrac{G}{2M\Omega}\cdot N P(\Omega)\tfrac{\pi C}{2} - \zeta\Omega\Big)A @@ the locked-share technique: the feed grows with A too
A' > 0 \iff N\,\pi G C P(\Omega) > 4\zeta M\Omega^2 @@ multiply by 4MΩ/A
\htmlClass{new}{N_c = \tfrac{4\zeta M\Omega^2}{\pi G C\,P(\Omega)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ below it every small swing dies; above it every small swing grows
  
4\zeta M\Omega^2 = 4\times0.0075\times1.13\times10^5\times6.47^2 = 1.419\times10^5 @@ the deck’s side: brake, mass, pace
\pi G C P(\Omega) = \pi\times30\times16\times0.633 = 954.9 @@ the crowd’s side, per walker
\htmlClass{new}{N_c = 148.6} \quad \blacksquare @@ the 2005 study’s supplement gives 148 with these numbers, a figure not revisited beyond it

The crowd tests on the north span in December 2000 added walkers in steps, and the swing took off at about 165, by the count reported in a 2006 study of the tests. The model gives 149. Every term reads like an engineering choice. The brake ζ is on top, and the 2002 repair raised it many times over with dampers. The deck’s mass and stiffness are on top too: a stiffer deck needs more walkers. Divided by Ω, the crowd’s side πGC·P(Ω)/Ω = 148 N s/m is each walker acting as a brake turned backwards. The fitted C sits on the bottom, and nobody measured it walker by walker.

sky43.5A Twist That Pushes Itself

A suspension bridge across the Tacoma Narrows in Washington State opened on 1 July 1940 and fell on 7 November 1940, in a wind of about 18 m/s, 40 miles an hour. It had bounced up and down in light winds since it opened. That morning the deck began to twist, one side up and the other down, about twelve times a minute, a period near 5 seconds, and in about an hour the twisting tore it apart. The textbook story became resonance: the wind’s gusts or swirls kept time with the twist. The trouble with that story is that a gale has no single pace, and resonance needs one.

Self-push technique: a twist θ with θ″ + (2ζω − εU)θ′ + ω²θ = 0, where the wind of speed U adds a push εUθ′ in step with the twist’s own speed, has every motion dying when U < Uc = 2ζω/ε and some motion growing without bound when U > Uc; no pace of the wind enters.

\beta = \tfrac12(2\zeta\omega - \varepsilon U),\ \ \beta^2 < \omega^2 @@ the brake left over, light
\theta = e^{-\beta t}\big(C_1\cos\omega_d t + C_2\sin\omega_d t\big),\ \ \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega^2 - \beta^2} @@ the decaying-turn technique (26·cb24.2)
U < U_c \iff \beta > 0 @@ the size e^{−βt} falls
\htmlClass{new}{U > U_c \iff \beta < 0:\ \text{the size } e^{|\beta|t} \text{ grows}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every start but rest itself
  
\omega = 2\pi/5 = 1.257 \text{ rad/s},\ \ \zeta = 0.5\%,\ \ U_c = 15 \text{ m/s} @@ the twist’s period from the film; ζ and Uc chosen for the example
\beta = \zeta\omega\,(1 - U/U_c) @@ since ε = 2ζω/Uc
U = 18:\ \ \beta = 0.00628\times(1 - 1.2) = -0.00126 \text{ per s} @@ a fifth past the speed that starts it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the twist doubles every } \ln2/0.00126 = 550 \text{ s, about 9 minutes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ six doublings in an hour: 64 times

Set this beside a push at a pace, the 1.03 Hz of a walker on the north span. A push at a pace gives a swing of size push/(brake × pace), largest when the paces match and smaller on either side of it. A self-push gives a growth, and it grows faster the harder the wind blows, at any wind above Uc. The deck’s shape, a shallow flat section with solid sides, is what made ε large: twisting turned the wind’s flow into a lift that led the twist. Engineers name this flutter. The page keeps to the example; the εU for the real deck was measured later on models in wind tunnels.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞43.oq.1How does a walker really answer a moving floor? The 2005 model’s rule, θ′ = ω + CA cos(θ − Ψ), was laid down as the plainest that nudges, and its C was fitted to the north span’s crowd tests. Later models have walkers shift where they put their feet rather than when, and each gives a different feed. Tests on treadmills and lab decks that sway disagree on how much walkers change their pace at all. A rule measured walker by walker, which then predicts a bridge it was not fitted on, would settle it; none has yet.
∞43.oq.2Did the crowd fall into step first, or did the deck move first? A 2021 study argued that walkers who never fall into step still act as a brake turned backwards, by how they place their feet to keep their balance, so the swing can start with no stepping together, and the stepping together is a result of the swing, not its cause. The 2005 account puts falling into step at the start. Both give a threshold count of about the right size for the north span. Which mechanism starts it, on which bridges, is argued.
∞43.oq.3Why did the Tacoma deck switch from bouncing to twisting on the last morning? For months it had bounced up and down, a motion locked to the swirls the wind shed off its sides. On 7 November it switched, after a cable band slipped, to twisting. Studies from 2017 to 2022 explain the twist as flutter, or as an instability of the hanging structure itself, and a 2022 study offers a test for when twisting takes over that its own authors say does not fully describe it. The switch is not fully explained.
∞43.oq.4How many walkers can a given footbridge carry before it sways? The advice is to keep sideways paces above about 1.3 Hz, or to add enough damping, and the crowd-threshold technique turns this into a count. But the count needs C and the spread of paces of a real crowd, which change with how dense the crowd is, how fast it moves and what it is there to do. A rule that a designer can use before the bridge is built, and that holds on bridges not yet tested, is still sought.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The bridge at Tacoma Narrows opened on 1 July 1940 with a long, slender deck that bounced up and down in moderate winds from the start. Four months later, on 7 November, in a wind of about 40 miles an hour, the bouncing gave way to a twisting that broke the centre span within about an hour. It was filmed, and the film, shown in classrooms ever since, is how most people know it.

For decades the collapse was taught as resonance, the wind pushing at the deck’s own pace, from newspapers of 1940 into the physics textbooks of the 1960s. Engineers in wind tunnels had meanwhile come to a different view: the twisting deck shaped the wind’s flow so that the wind pushed in step with the twist’s own speed, a brake turned backwards. A paper of 1991 in a journal for physics teachers set out the case that the collapse was not a resonance, and the correction has spread slowly; a 2015 article still described it as spreading. Fluid studies of 2017 to 2022 agree on the self-push for the twist but differ on what set it off, and on how much the structure itself, apart from the air, took part.

The footbridge across the Thames opened in June 2000 and was shut after two days of swaying. Its unusually shallow suspension was blamed at first; the engineers who built it showed that the same sway could arise on any bridge with a sideways pace below about 1.3 Hz carrying enough walkers, and that it had been seen on other bridges before, under crowds. Crowd tests on the deck, with walkers added in steps, found that the swing took off past a count, and measured how much each walker added. The fix, at a cost of about £5 million, added fluid dampers and tuned masses that raised the brake from under 1% to about 20%. It reopened in February 2002.

The engineers’ 2001 account was empirical: each walker on a swinging deck pushes in proportion to the deck’s speed, measured at about 300 N s/m, so a crowd acts as a brake turned backwards and the threshold follows. It did not say why walkers do that. In 2005 a team of mathematicians and an engineer gave a mechanism in a page of a leading science journal: the falling into step of sky43.3, with the walkers as timekeepers and the deck as the beat. It was widely taken as the explanation, a textbook example of falling into step.

The doubts came from engineers who studied walking. A walker on a moving floor adjusts where the next foot lands, to keep balance, and models built on that gave a backwards brake without any falling into step. A 2017 study showed that several such models produce the sway, and a 2021 study, with authors from both camps, argued that falling into step comes after the swing has started, as its consequence, and called the textbook account of crowd synchrony wildly inaccurate for this bridge. What is agreed is that a count exists, and that dampers work.

What is argued now is what a designer may rely on. The advice since 2000 has been to keep a footbridge’s sideways paces above about 1.3 Hz or to give it enough brake, but the numbers behind a check, the push per walker and how it grows with the deck’s motion, are fitted from a few bridges and lab tests. Both stories, the 1940 twist and the 2000 sway, are stories of a structure that feeds itself from a steady source, a gale or a crowd at its own pace; the lesson engineers drew from both is to measure the brake and to make it larger than anything that could turn it backwards.

The accepted names

The bridge is the London Millennium Bridge, and its sway is synchronous lateral excitation. The modal-mass technique gives the modal mass of the first mode of a simply supported span. The random-crowd technique is the incoherent sum of random phasors. The 2005 model is a Kuramoto model of pedestrians coupled through the bridge; the locked-share technique is the step in Kuramoto’s self-consistency argument that gives the critical coupling 2/(πg(0)), and Nc is the critical crowd size. The backwards brake is negative damping; the 2001 force-per-speed rule is the Arup (Dallard) negative-damping coefficient. The self-push technique describes single-degree-of-freedom torsional flutter, aeroelastic self-excitation, and Uc is the critical flutter speed. The 1940 bridge was nicknamed Galloping Gertie.

Who said it first

Pat Dallard and colleagues at Arup, the bridge’s engineers, published the diagnosis and the negative-damping rule in 2001. Steven Strogatz, Daniel Abrams, Allan McRobie, Bruno Eckhardt and Edward Ott published the crowd synchrony model in 2005, building on Yoshiki Kuramoto’s model of falling into step. Igor Belykh and colleagues published the foot-force study of 2017, and Igor Belykh, Mateusz Bocian, Alan Champneys, Allan McRobie and colleagues the study of 2021. Frederick Burt Farquharson measured the last motions of the 1940 bridge, and K. Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan published the 1991 paper.
