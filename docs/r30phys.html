How is energy shared among molecules?

How is energy shared among molecules? A litre of air in a room in Victoria holds about twenty-five thousand billion billion molecules, and a fixed amount of energy among them. Some race, some crawl, some spin, and a few shake along their bonds. No one can follow them. Yet the air has one temperature, one pressure, and a heat capacity that can be looked up in a table and is the same next year. The newsflash before this page found the spread of speeds by asking that no direction be special. But how is the energy shared out among all the ways a molecule can hold it: moving, turning, shaking, sitting high up in the air or low down? What makes the question hard is that every sharing out that adds up to the right total is allowed. Why should one of them be the one we see? The answer this page gives is a counting answer. The sharing we see is the one that can happen in the most ways, by an amount so large that the others never show. Rung twenty-eight found best points on a constraint, and rung twenty-two counted ways in bits. Together they find the sharing, and from it the thin air on a mountain top, the faint pull on protons in a hospital scanner, the heat a gas soaks up, and why the shaking of a nitrogen molecule takes no part at room temperature.

Hand out a fixed total of energy among a great many molecules, each of which can sit in any of its states. The number of ways to do it with given shares is largest, by far, at one set of shares, and those shares can be found as a best point on a constraint: the uphill arrow of the count lines up with the uphill arrows of the two totals that are held fixed. The answer is that a state’s share falls off as an exponential in its energy, at a pace set by the temperature. One sum over all the states then holds everything: the average energy is a slope of its log, and the spread of the energy is the next slope. A body cut off with an exact energy, a body in touch with a big room, and a body that can swap molecules with the room as well all give the same averages, because the spread is a tiny share of the whole. Every part of a molecule’s energy that goes as a square averages half of the same small amount, which counts the heat a gas or a solid soaks up. And a shake that comes in lumps too big for the temperature to pay for sits still, frozen out.

30phys1The most ways, under a fixed total

Each of N molecules sits in one of the states i = 1, 2, 3, …, with energy Eᵢ. Hand out the molecules with shares pᵢ. 22·comp counted the ways to choose a share p of N things as about 2^(N h(p)). The same count, with 22k’s size of n! and many states, gives the number of ways W for the shares pᵢ: ln W ≈ −N Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ. The total energy is fixed, N Σ pᵢEᵢ = U. Which shares have the most ways?

H(p) = -\textstyle\sum_i p_i\ln p_i,\ \ \sum_i p_i = 1,\ \ \sum_i p_iE_i = \varepsilon @@ the count to make greatest, and the two totals held fixed; ε = U/N
-\ln p_i - 1 = \lambda + \beta E_i @@ the lines-up technique (28g), run with many knobs and two constraints, one multiplier each
p_i = \tfrac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z},\ \ Z = \textstyle\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j} @@ the shares add to 1, which fixes λ
H(r) \le -\textstyle\sum_i r_i\ln p_i = \beta\varepsilon + \ln Z = H(p) @@ any shares r with the same ε: the log-bounds technique (17a), ln x ≤ x − 1 with x = pᵢ/rᵢ
\htmlClass{new}{p_i = \tfrac{1}{Z}e^{-E_i/kT}} \quad \blacksquare @@ S = Nk H: its slope along U is kβ (the slope of ln Z along β is −ε), and the second law makes it 1/T (22·comp, 28·then)

Energy-share technique: among all ways to share out a fixed total of energy, the shares with the most ways give each state a share in proportion to e^(−E/kT), where T is the temperature. The lines-up technique finds the shares. The log-bounds technique proves that they truly have the most ways, and not merely a level point. Its accepted name is the canonical distribution.

The most ways are the most by far. Any other set of shares r has a count smaller by a factor of about e^(−N(H(p) − H(r))). With N near 10²² and H(p) − H(r) even as small as 10⁻¹⁵, that factor is e^(−10 000 000): it never shows.

A molecule of air at height h has extra energy mgh. So the share of molecules at height h falls as e^(−mgh/kT), and drops by a factor of e every kT/(mg). For air at 15 °C, with m = 28.96 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, that is 8.4 km. At the summit of Mount Rainier, 4392 m up, the share is e^(−4392/8433) ≈ 0.59 of the share at sea level. 15·chem gave the summit’s pressure as about 0.60 atm. The air is colder up there, and that is most of the gap.

30phys2One sum holds everything

Write β = 1/kT. The sum Z = Σ e^(−βEᵢ) has an accepted name, the partition function. It looks like a mere total that makes the shares add to 1. It is much more.

\tfrac{dZ}{d\beta} = -\textstyle\sum E_ie^{-\beta E_i} \Rightarrow -\tfrac{d\ln Z}{d\beta} = \sum E_ip_i = \operatorname{E}E @@ the slope of each term (17); divide by Z
\tfrac{d^2Z}{d\beta^2} = \textstyle\sum E_i^2e^{-\beta E_i} \Rightarrow \tfrac{1}{Z}\tfrac{d^2Z}{d\beta^2} = \operatorname{E}[E^2] @@ once more
\tfrac{d^2\ln Z}{d\beta^2} = \tfrac{1}{Z}\tfrac{d^2Z}{d\beta^2} - \big(\tfrac{1}{Z}\tfrac{dZ}{d\beta}\big)^2 = \operatorname{E}[E^2] - (\operatorname{E}E)^2 @@ the slope of a quotient
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}E = -\tfrac{d\ln Z}{d\beta},\ \ \operatorname{Var}E = \tfrac{d^2\ln Z}{d\beta^2} = kT^2c} \quad \blacksquare @@ the spread (30g); c = d(E E)/dT, since dβ/dT = −1/(kT²)

Sum-over-states technique: the average energy is minus the slope of ln Z along β, and the spread of the energy is the next slope, which equals kT² times the heat capacity c. So the heat a body soaks up and the jitter of its energy are one number. The free energy of 28·then is F = −NkT ln Z, so every quantity of the body’s heat can be read from Z.

The simplest body has two states, 0 and Δ. Then Z = 1 + e^(−βΔ), and the share in the upper state is 1/(e^(βΔ) + 1). A proton in a magnet is such a body: its spin points with the field or against it. In a 1.5 T hospital scanner in Seattle the gap is Δ = h × 63.9 MHz = 4.2 × 10⁻²⁶ J, and at body heat, 310 K, βΔ ≈ 9.9 × 10⁻⁶. The two shares differ by about βΔ/2: five protons in a million more point with the field than against it. The picture of the inside of a body is made from those five.

30phys3Three ways to hold a body

A body can be held three ways. Sealed off, its energy is exact: drop the energy total from the lines-up technique, keep only Σpᵢ = 1 over the states of that energy, and every state gets the same share. Then H = ln W, and S = k ln W: 22·comp’s entropy. In touch with a large room, it trades energy, and only the average is fixed: the energy-share technique. Open to the room, it trades molecules too, and the average number of molecules is a third total held fixed.

\textstyle\sum p_i = 1,\ \ \sum p_iE_i = \varepsilon,\ \ \sum p_iN_i = \nu @@ three totals; Nᵢ the molecules in state i
-\ln p_i - 1 = \lambda + \beta E_i - \beta\mu N_i @@ the lines-up technique, one multiplier for each total; write the third as −βμ
\htmlClass{new}{p_i = \tfrac{1}{\mathcal Z}e^{-(E_i - \mu N_i)/kT}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the log-bounds technique again shows these have the most ways

Grand-share technique: a body that trades energy and molecules with a large room shares itself out in proportion to e^(−(E − μN)/kT), where μ is the room’s chemical potential. A site that holds one oxygen molecule, with energy −ε₀, or none, is such a body. Its share full is 1/(1 + e^(−(ε₀ + μ)/kT)). 28·then gave μ = μ° + kT ln(P/P°) for a gas, per molecule. Put it in, and the share full is P/(P + P½) for a fixed P½. Myoglobin, the oxygen store of muscle, follows this curve with P½ near 2.8 mmHg. In the muscle of an elephant seal diving off Año Nuevo, at 20 mmHg it is 88% full, and at 2 mmHg it has given up more than half its oxygen.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud

Why do three ways of holding a body give the same heat capacities and the same pressures? Because the energy of a body in touch with a room is a cloud, and the cloud is thin. By the sum-over-states technique its spread is NkT²c for N molecules. For air, c = (5/2)k and the average energy per molecule is (5/2)kT. So the spread width, as a share of the energy, is √(2/(5N)).

A quantity of heat is the middle of a cloud of energies, and is reported with its width, √(kT²C), however thin.

A litre of air at 20 °C and one atmosphere holds N = PV/kT = 2.5 × 10²² molecules. Its energy is uncertain by four parts in a million million. A sealed litre and a litre in touch with the room differ by less than any instrument can read. The averages of this page are anexact: they are the middle of a cloud. They are rigorous because the width of the cloud is computed, and it is far below the measurement.

A speck of dust in the air of Victoria holds about a billion molecules. Would its energy be thin enough a cloud to call its temperature exact? What width would you report?

30phys4Half of kT for every square

Many parts of a molecule’s energy go as a square of something that can take any value: ½mv_x² for its motion along x, ½Iω² for a turn, ½κx² for the stretch of a bond. Take one such part, a y² with y any real number.

p(y) \propto e^{-\beta \kappa y^2} @@ the energy-share technique, with the states running over every y
p \text{ is a bell with spread } \tfrac{1}{2\beta \kappa} @@ e^(−y²/(2σ²)) with σ² = 1/(2βκ): the bell (30j), stretched (30m’s stretched-density technique)
\operatorname{E}[\kappa y^2] = \kappa\cdot\tfrac{1}{2\beta \kappa} @@ the average square of a bell centred at 0 is its spread (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\operatorname{E}[\kappa y^2] = \tfrac12 kT,\ \text{ whatever } \kappa \text{ is}} \quad \blacksquare @@ β = 1/kT

Half-kT technique: every part of the energy that goes as a square averages ½kT, whatever its size, so a body’s heat capacity counts its squares: ½k for each. 30·then found ½kT along each direction from the gas law. Here it comes from the energy-share technique, and it covers turning and shaking as well. Its accepted name is equipartition.

Count squares, and multiply by ½R for a mole. Argon has three: (3/2)R = 12.5 J/(mol K), as measured. Nitrogen and oxygen have three for motion and two for turning: (5/2)R = 20.8, the 2.5R that 28·then used for air, as measured. A solid has three springs per atom, each with a square for motion and one for stretch: 3R = 24.9. Copper measures 24.4, and lead 26.4. Diamond measures 6.1. And nitrogen’s bond is a spring too, with two more squares, which should make it (7/2)R. It is not. The counting is right; something stops some squares from taking their share.

30phys5Molecular partition functions

A molecule’s energy is the sum of independent parts: moving, turning, shaking. Then Z splits.

E_{(i, j)} = E^{\text{move}}_i + E^{\text{shake}}_j @@ a state is a pair: one state of each part
Z = \textstyle\sum_{i, j}e^{-\beta E^{\text{move}}_i}e^{-\beta E^{\text{shake}}_j} @@ e to a sum is a product (17e)
\htmlClass{new}{Z = Z_{\text{move}}\,Z_{\text{turn}}\,Z_{\text{shake}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ sum one index at a time, either order (30e), and use the same for turning

States-multiply technique: when a molecule’s energy is a sum of independent parts, its sum over states is the product of each part’s, so ln Z adds and so do the average energies. Now the shake. A bond, measured, takes energy only in lumps hf, like 17·phys’s light: its states have energies 0, hf, 2hf, and so on.

Z_{\text{shake}} = \textstyle\sum_{n \ge 0}e^{-n\beta hf} = \tfrac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta hf}} @@ a geometric sum (22e), ratio below 1
\operatorname{E}E_{\text{shake}} = -\tfrac{d}{d\beta}\ln Z_{\text{shake}} = \tfrac{hf}{e^{\beta hf} - 1} @@ the sum-over-states technique
kT \gg hf:\ \ \tfrac{hf}{e^{\beta hf} - 1} \approx kT @@ e^x − 1 ≈ x for small x: the half-kT technique’s two squares
\htmlClass{new}{kT \ll hf:\ \ \operatorname{E}E_{\text{shake}} \approx hf\,e^{-hf/kT} \to 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the shake is frozen: its lump is too dear

Frozen-shake technique: a shake that takes energy in lumps hf holds about kT when kT is far above hf, and almost nothing when kT is far below it. For nitrogen, hf/k = 3390 K. At 293 K the share of molecules shaking at all is e^(−11.6) ≈ 9 in a million, and the shake adds 0.0013k to the heat capacity: nitrogen stays at (5/2)R. Iodine’s bond is loose, hf/k = 309 K, and at 293 K its shake is mostly awake: 0.91k more. Diamond’s springs are stiff, near 1320 K, and the same count gives 5.7 J/(mol K) against the 6.1 measured. The squares were all there. The lumps froze them.

The accepted names

The energy-share technique gives the Boltzmann distribution (the canonical distribution), and e^(−E/kT) the Boltzmann factor. H = −Σ p ln p is the Gibbs entropy (per k), and finding shares as the most H under fixed totals is the maximum entropy method. Z is the partition function (Zustandssumme, the sum over states). The three ways of holding a body are the microcanonical, canonical and grand canonical ensembles; 𝒵 is the grand partition function and μ the chemical potential. The site’s curve P/(P + P½) is the Langmuir isotherm, and myoglobin’s P½ is its P50. The half-kT technique is the equipartition theorem; 3R for solids is the law of Dulong and Petit. The frozen-shake technique is the Einstein model of a vibration, hf/k the vibrational temperature, and the factorisation of Z the molecular partition function written as translational, rotational and vibrational parts. The thin cloud of 30·phys.3 is the equivalence of ensembles in the thermodynamic limit.

Who said it first

Ludwig Boltzmann gave the e^(−E/kT) shares in 1868 and the count of ways in 1877; Max Planck wrote S = k ln W in that form about 1900. Josiah Willard Gibbs set out the ensembles and the sum over states in Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics (1902). Edwin Jaynes argued in 1957 that the shares are the most H under what is known, a reading of statistical mechanics as inference. Pierre Louis Dulong and Alexis Thérèse Petit found the 3R rule in 1819, and Heinrich Weber measured diamond’s low heat capacity in 1875. Maxwell called the failure of equipartition for gases the greatest difficulty of the molecular theory (1875). Albert Einstein froze the springs of a solid with Planck’s lumps in 1907, and Peter Debye improved the count in 1912. Irving Langmuir gave the site curve in 1916. In 30·phys.7.5, Josef Loschmidt raised the reversed paths against Boltzmann in 1876, and Ernst Zermelo the return of a sealed box in 1896, from a result of Henri Poincaré (1890); Boltzmann answered both, and the answer still argued over is that the world began in very few ways.
