23c · proof

How far a power sum reaches

Let the matching polynomial go on forever and you get an endless sum of powers. Put in a number and it may settle or wander. This step finds that the answer has a simple shape. Around the centre there is a distance, the reach, inside which the sum settles, and settles firmly, while outside it the sum wanders. Only at the two edges can anything happen. The reach can be found from the coefficients alone, by the same comparison with a geometric sum that the last rung used. And it can be smaller than you expect: the sum for one over one plus x squared stops reaching at one, though that function is smooth along the whole line.

An endless power sum settles inside a distance from its centre and wanders outside it. That distance, the reach, comes from the coefficients alone. At the edges anything can happen.

23c1Power sums

Stands on 23a2 and 22d1 (running totals).

From here on, a power sum about a is Σₖ cₖ(x − a)ᵏ, with real coefficients c₀, c₁, c₂, …. For each x it is an endless sum of reals, which settles or wanders. Accepted name: a power series.

The endless matching polynomial of a smooth f is the power sum with cₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)/k!. 23b showed it settles at f(x) for eˣ, sin and cos at every x, and for ln(1 + x) on [0, 1]. Other power sums need not come from any function you know; the sum then defines one, wherever it settles.

Reach technique: if a power sum about a settles at x₀, it settles in size at every x nearer to a than x₀ is.

c_k(x_0 - a)^k \to 0 @@ the sum settles at x₀: the shrink test (22d2)
|c_k(x_0 - a)^k| \le B \text{ for every } k @@ a list that settles has a floor and a ceiling (22b)
|c_k(x - a)^k| = |c_k(x_0 - a)^k| \cdot \big|\tfrac{x-a}{x_0-a}\big|^k \le B\,r^k,\ \ r = \big|\tfrac{x-a}{x_0-a}\big| < 1 @@ |x − a| < |x₀ − a|
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum |c_k(x-a)^k| \text{ settles}} \quad \blacksquare @@ comparison with the geometric sum ΣBrᵏ (22e1)

23c2The reach

Stands on 23c1 and 8c (the least upper bound).

Reach-of-a-sum technique: every power sum about a has a reach R, a real ≥ 0 or no ceiling at all, such that it settles in size where |x − a| < R and wanders where |x − a| > R.

S = \{|x - a| : \text{the sum settles at } x\} @@ it holds 0: at x = a every piece after c₀ is 0
R = \sup S,\ \text{or no ceiling if } S \text{ has none} @@ 8c
|x - a| < R \Rightarrow \text{some } x_0 \text{ with } |x - a| < |x_0 - a| \text{ settles} @@ R is the least ceiling of S
|x - a| < R \Rightarrow \text{settles in size} @@ the reach technique (23c1)
|x - a| > R \Rightarrow |x - a| \notin S @@ R is a ceiling of S
\htmlClass{new}{\text{inside } R \text{ the sum settles in size; outside it wanders}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

From here on, R is the reach of the power sum. Accepted name: the radius of convergence. Where there is no ceiling, write R = ∞ and say the sum reaches everywhere.

23c3Finding the reach from the coefficients

Stands on 23c2 and 22e2 (the ratio test).

Reach-by-ratio technique: if |cₖ₊₁/cₖ| heads to L, the reach is 1/L; if it heads to 0, the sum reaches everywhere; if it passes every ceiling, the reach is 0. Run the ratio test on the sizes of the pieces.

\Big|\tfrac{c_{k+1}(x-a)^{k+1}}{c_k(x-a)^k}\Big| = \Big|\tfrac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}\Big|\,|x-a| \to L|x-a| @@ for x ≠ a
L|x - a| < 1 \Rightarrow \text{settles in size} @@ the ratio test (22e2)
L|x - a| > 1 \Rightarrow \text{the pieces grow: wanders} @@ the shrink test (22d2)
\htmlClass{new}{R = 1/L} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines meet at |x − a| = 1/L
  
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{x^k}{k!}:\ \tfrac{k!}{(k+1)!} = \tfrac{1}{k+1} \to 0,\ R = \infty @@ the sum for eˣ reaches everywhere
\textstyle\sum x^k:\ 1 \to 1,\ R = 1 @@ the geometric sum (15i)
\textstyle\sum (-1)^{k+1}\tfrac{x^k}{k}:\ \tfrac{k}{k+1} \to 1,\ R = 1 @@ the sum for ln(1 + x)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum k!\,x^k:\ (k+1) \text{ passes every ceiling},\ R = 0} @@ it settles at x = 0 and nowhere else

23c4Anything at the edge

Stands on 23c2, 22d3 (the harmonic sum), 22g1 (the bracket) and 22f2 (the p-sums).

\textstyle\sum x^k:\ \text{wanders at } 1 \text{ and at } -1 @@ the pieces do not head to 0
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{x^k}{k}:\ \text{wanders at } 1,\ \text{settles at } -1 @@ the harmonic sum (22d3); the bracket (22g1)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum \tfrac{x^k}{k^2}:\ \text{settles at both}} \quad \blacksquare @@ in size it is the p-sum with p = 2 (22f2)

Now a puzzle. 1/(1 + x²) is smooth along the whole line. Its power sum about 0 is 1 − x² + x⁴ − ⋯, the geometric sum with ratio −x². Its reach is 1. At x = 2 the function is a fifth, and the sum wanders. Nothing on the real line explains why the sum stops at 1. The reason lives off the line, in numbers that turn: 1 + x² is 0 at x = i, one unit from the centre. Rung 25 builds the place where that makes sense.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A power sum is a power series; its reach is the radius of convergence, and the stretch (a − R, a + R) with whichever edges it settles at is the interval of convergence. The reach technique is often called Abel's lemma. The reach also has a formula from roots, R = 1/limsup |cₖ|^(1/k), the Cauchy–Hadamard formula.

Who said it first

Niels Henrik Abel proved the reach technique in 1826. Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave the root formula in 1821, and Jacques Hadamard rediscovered and proved it in 1888. Cauchy also explained why 1/(1 + x²) stops at 1, by looking off the line.
