44h · proof

Only One Complete Ordered Field

Rung eight built the line from cuts of the fractions and proved it was a regular number system with no holes. It mentioned, and left, a further claim: that any two such systems are copies of each other. Rung thirty seven asked for the outline of that claim, and found it needs the least upper bound said of every set. This step proves it in full, and first builds the line a second way, to show what the claim is worth. The second way uses lists of fractions that crowd together, from rung twenty two, and divides them by the lists that head to nothing: a ring divided by a largest ideal, so a field. Cuts and crowding lists look nothing alike. The claim says they are the same field, matched member for member, and that no other matching is possible. So the line is fixed by its rules. How it is built is a choice of scaffolding, and what it is does not depend on the choice.

Crowding lists of fractions form a ring, the lists heading to 0 form a largest ideal, and the quotient is a field: the line again. Inside any regular number system with no holes sit the fractions, and counting passes every member. Sending each member to the least upper bound of the fractions below it matches any two such systems, keeping sums, products and order, and the only map of the line to itself that keeps plus and times is doing nothing.

44h1The Line Built a Second Way

Let C be the set of lists of fractions that crowd together (22c), added and multiplied term by term. Let Z be the lists that head to 0.

Crowding-quotient technique: C is a ring that turns about, Z is a largest ideal of C, and so C/Z is a field.

a, b \in C \Rightarrow |a_n| \le A,\ |b_n| \le B @@ a crowding list is bounded (22c)
a_n b_n - a_m b_m = a_n(b_n - b_m) + (a_n - a_m)b_m @@ add and take away aₙbₘ
|a_n b_n - a_m b_m| \le A|b_n - b_m| + B|a_n - a_m| @@ so ab crowds: C is a ring
z \in Z,\ a \in C \Rightarrow |a_n z_n| \le A|z_n| \to 0 @@ Z swallows multiplying; it holds sums by 10c
a \notin Z \Rightarrow |a_n| \ge \varepsilon > 0 \text{ for } n \ge N @@ a crowds and does not head to 0
c_n = 1/a_n \ (n \ge N),\ \ c \in C,\ \ ac - 1 \in Z @@ |1/aₙ − 1/aₘ| ≤ |aₘ − aₙ|/ε²
\htmlClass{new}{(a + Z)(c + Z) = 1 + Z:\ C/Z \text{ is a field}} \quad \blacksquare @@ every class other than Z has a reciprocal; by 44b’s largest-ideal technique, Z is largest

Call a + Z positive when aₙ ≥ ε > 0 for all large n. With that, C/Z is a regular number system, and it has no holes: a bounded set of classes gets a least upper bound by 12e’s halving, with the halving points as a crowding list. The fraction r sits inside as the class of the list r, r, r, …. The class of 1, 3/2, 17/12, 577/408, …, 13m’s tangent steps toward √2, squares to the class of 2.

44h2Fractions Inside Any Line

Let L be any regular number system with no holes. In L, 1 is positive (a square, 25b1), so 1 + 1 + ⋯ + 1 is positive for any count of 1s and never 0. So the sums of 1 are a copy of the counting numbers, their differences of ℤ, and their quotients of ℚ: m/n in L is (m · 1)(n · 1)⁻¹.

Fractions-sit-inside technique: every regular number system L holds a copy of ℚ, keeping sums, products and order; and if L has no holes, counting passes every member, and between any two members of L lies a fraction.

\text{suppose every } n \cdot 1 \le u \text{ in } L @@ counting bounded
s = \sup\{n \cdot 1\} @@ L has no holes
s - 1 < n \cdot 1 \text{ for some } n @@ s − 1 is no upper bound
\bot:\ (n + 1) \cdot 1 > s @@ so counting passes every member
w < b \Rightarrow \tfrac{1}{n} < b - w,\ \ \tfrac{m}{n} \in (w, b) \text{ for the least } m \text{ with } \tfrac{m}{n} > w @@ 8e’s fraction-between, read in L
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the fractions are packed into } L \text{ everywhere}} \quad \blacksquare @@ each line used only the twelve techniques and no holes

That is 8e’s argument, and the point is only that it never looked at cuts. A regular number system with holes may fail it: 38f’s worlds of the line with endlessly small members have members above every count of 1s.

44h3Any Two Lines Are One

Only-line technique: any two regular number systems with no holes, L and L′, are matched by exactly one map that keeps sums, products and order.

h(w) = \sup\nolimits_{L'}\{r \in \mathbb Q : r < w\} @@ the fractions below w in L, read in L′; bounded, so the sup exists (no holes in L′)
r \in \mathbb Q \Rightarrow h(r) = r @@ the fractions below r have r as their sup (fraction-between)
w < b \Rightarrow \text{fractions } r < s \text{ with } w < r < s < b \Rightarrow h(w) \le r < s \le h(b) @@ order is kept, strictly: h is one-to-one
u' \in L' \Rightarrow u' = h(\sup\nolimits_L\{r < u'\}) @@ the same move from L′ back: h is onto
h(w + b) = h(w) + h(b) @@ fractions below w + b are sums of fractions below w and below b, and the other way
h(wb) = h(w)h(b) \text{ for } w, b > 0 @@ the same, with products of positive fractions; signs by 8d’s signs-come-out
\htmlClass{new}{h \text{ is a matching keeping } +, \cdot, <} \quad \blacksquare @@ any such map fixes ℚ and keeps order, so it is this h

Applied to cuts and to C/Z: the cut α goes to the class of any list of fractions that climbs to α. 38e’s countable world of the line, with a bounded set lacking a least upper bound, is not a regular number system with no holes, and the technique says nothing about them. That is the price 38l described: “no holes” is said of every set, and only then does it fix the line.

44h4The Line Has No Other Self

Rigid-line technique: the only map from ℝ to ℝ that keeps sums and products and sends 1 to 1 is doing nothing. It is not told to keep order. It keeps it anyway.

f(w) = f(\sqrt w)^2 \ge 0 \text{ for } w \ge 0 @@ every w ≥ 0 is a square (8f), and f keeps products; squares are not negative (25b1)
w < b \Rightarrow f(b) - f(w) = f(b - w) \ge 0 @@ f keeps sums; and f(b − w) ≠ 0, since f(b − w)f(1/(b − w)) = f(1) = 1
f(r) = r \text{ for every fraction } r @@ f(1) = 1, sums of 1, differences, quotients
r < w < s \Rightarrow r < f(w) < s @@ order kept, fractions fixed
\htmlClass{new}{f(w) = w} \quad \blacksquare @@ a fraction lies between any two different reals (8e)

ℚ(√2) has a second self: a + b√2 ↦ a − b√2 keeps sums and products and moves √2. It breaks order, sending √2 > 0 to −√2. ℂ has a second self too, the flip z ↦ z̄ of 25e. The line has none. 43 makes the selves of a field its study.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

Crowding lists are Cauchy sequences, and C/Z is Cantor’s construction of the reals by Cauchy sequences, a completion. Counting passing every member is the Archimedean property. The only-line technique is the uniqueness of the complete ordered field up to isomorphism, its categoricity; the rigid-line technique says the real field has no automorphism but the identity. ℚ(t) with the order given is a non-Archimedean ordered field.

Who said it first

Georg Cantor and Charles Méray built the reals from crowding lists in 1872, the year of Richard Dedekind’s cuts; Eduard Heine wrote up Cantor’s version the same year. Edward Huntington proved in 1903 that any two complete ordered fields are matched, and Oswald Veblen made such uniqueness a theme in 1904, calling a system with one model up to matching categorical.

A proof that is not allowed

“A map of ℝ to itself that keeps sums must be w ↦ cw, since it fixes the fractions up to c.” Not allowed: keeping sums fixes f(r) = rf(1) for fractions r only. Without continuity or order there are maps of ℝ that keep sums and are not w ↦ cw; building one spends choice (6), through a basis of ℝ over ℚ.
