44h · proof

Subgroups of every prime-power size

Rung 19 proved that the size of a part-group divides the size of the group. The converse is false: the even shuffles of four have twelve members and no part-group of six. This step proves the converse for prime powers. If a prime power divides the size of a finite group, the group has a part-group of exactly that size. For the largest such power, the part-groups of that size are all relabellings of one another, and how many there are leaves one on the clock of the prime and divides what is left of the size. These facts make a group’s size say a great deal about its shape: a group of fifteen must be a clock, and every group whose size is a power of a prime comes apart, which 44e left open and 44i will need.

The size of a group is the size of its centre plus the sizes of its other conjugate families. In a group of prime-power size the centre is not trivial, so such a group has normal part-groups of every smaller prime-power size, and is solvable. Every finite group has a part-group of the largest power of each prime dividing its size; all such are conjugate, and their number leaves one on the clock of the prime and divides the rest of the size.

44h1The count by families

The centre Z(G) is the set of members that commute with every member. Each z in Z(G) is a family of one by itself. Every other family has |G|/|C(x)| members, a divisor of |G| above 1.

Centre technique: if |G| = pᵏ with p prime and k ≥ 1, then Z(G) has more than one member.

|G| = |Z(G)| + \textstyle\sum |G|/|C(x_i)| @@ one xᵢ from each family of more than one member; the families cut G
|G|/|C(x_i)| \text{ divides } p^k \text{ and is above } 1 @@ so it is a power of p above 1
p \text{ divides } |G| \text{ and every term of the sum} @@ k ≥ 1
\htmlClass{new}{p \text{ divides } |Z(G)| \ge 1,\ \text{so } |Z(G)| \ge p} \quad \blacksquare @@ e is in Z(G)

The square’s group of 8 (19f) has centre {e, half turn}: two members, a power of 2 above 1, as the technique says.

44h2Groups of prime-power size come apart

44h3Part-groups of the largest prime power

Write |G| = pᵃm with p not dividing m. A part-group of size pᵃ is a largest p-part of G.

Largest-part technique: every finite group has a largest p-part for every prime p; so it has a part-group of every prime-power size that divides its size.

\text{induction on } |G|;\ a \ge 1 @@ if a = 0, {e} is one
p \mid |Z(G)|:\ z \in Z(G) \text{ of order } p,\ N = \{e, z, \ldots\} \lhd G @@ the prime-member technique
G/N \text{ has a largest } p\text{-part } \bar P,\ |\bar P| = p^{a-1} @@ |G/N| = p^(a−1)m < |G|
P = \text{its members' preimage},\ |P| = p^{a-1}\cdot p = p^a @@ 42·cb19
p \nmid |Z(G)|:\ \text{some family size } |G|/|C(x)| \text{ is not divisible by } p @@ the count by families: p divides |G|
p^a \text{ divides } |C(x)| < |G| @@ |G| = |C(x)| · (a number not divisible by p)
\htmlClass{new}{C(x) \text{ has a part-group of size } p^a,\ \text{a part-group of } G} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction; then the prime-power-chain technique gives every pⁱ, i ≤ a

44h4How many, and how alike

Count-of-parts technique: any two largest p-parts are conjugate; and their number n_p leaves 1 on the clock of p and divides m.

Q \text{ fixed by all of } P \Rightarrow PQ \text{ is a part-group of size } |P||Q|/|P \cap Q| @@ xQx⁻¹ = Q for x in P
|PQ| = p^{2a}/|P \cap Q| \text{ is a power of } p,\ \text{so } P = Q @@ a part-group of G of prime-power size has at most pᵃ members, so |P ∩ Q| = pᵃ

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A largest p-part is a Sylow p-subgroup, and the largest-part and count-of-parts techniques are the Sylow theorems. The count by families is the class equation; Z(G) is the centre, and N(P) the normalizer of P. A group of prime-power size is a p-group, and the prime-power-chain technique shows that p-groups are nilpotent, hence solvable. The proof of 44h.6.4 is Helmut Wielandt’s (1959).

Who said it first

Ludwig Sylow proved the existence and the count of largest p-parts in 1872, in a paper of ten pages, as shuffle groups. Augustin-Louis Cauchy had proved the case of a single prime in 1845. Georg Frobenius gave the proof by induction on the centre in 1887, and proved in 1895 that the part-groups of each prime-power size number 1 on the clock of p as well.

A proof that is not allowed

“A group of 15 has n₃ = 1 and n₅ = 1, so it is the product of a clock of 3 and a clock of 5, and that product has a member of order 15 because 15 = 3 · 5.” Not allowed as it stands: the product of two clocks needs their members to commute. They do here because both parts are normal and meet only in e, so xyx⁻¹y⁻¹ lies in both; that is the line that makes it a clock.
