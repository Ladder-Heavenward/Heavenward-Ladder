Learning to Choose

Rung 3 made a choice about the endless: an inductive set exists, and nothing beneath it could prove so. This rung makes a second choice, and it is the most argued-over statement in the foundations of mathematics. Suppose you have a collection of sets, each with something in it. It says there is one object holding a member of every one of them at once. That is true even when there are endlessly many and nothing tells you which member to take. It sounds too plain to need saying. It turns out that it cannot be proved from the principles already laid down. It cannot be refuted from them either. And it is the same principle as two others that look nothing like it.

You have done this as well. Handed a row of boxes, each with something in it, you reach in and take one from each, with no rule for which. Nobody asks you to justify the reach. This rung asks whether the ladder may do the same, and owns up that it is a choice.

One from each, all at once, with no recipe. For finitely many sets you can pick by hand, one at a time. With a rule you can pick from endlessly many. With endlessly many and no rule, only an axiom will do.

What it means to say you can pick without a rule

Look back at the tools of rung 2. Each of them said exactly what the members of its result are. A sift keeps the members that pass a condition. A gathering holds the things named and nothing else. A union holds whatever is in any member. The set of all the subsets holds every part. Rung 3's axiom was different in one way: it said some inductive set exists without saying which. Rung 4 then sifted from it, and the answer did not depend on which set had been handed over.

There is one place where the ladder has picked already. 2b's tech for using a some says: from “there is a thing with this property”, write “call such a thing b”, and go on with b. That is a pick. It names one member of a set that has a member, and it says nothing about which one. But it lives inside a proof. The name b is good only between the opening of the call and its close, and one call names one thing.

Now suppose you have many sets at once, each with a member. You want one object that holds a pick from every one of them: a function that takes each set to a member of it. With two sets, call a member of the first something, call a member of the second something else, and pair each set with its pick. With any finite number of sets, do the same, one call per set; 6c turns this into a technique, by induction on the number of sets. With endlessly many sets, a column of lines runs out of calls: a proof is finitely many lines long. Then you need a single statement that names every pick at once. Either a rule does it, or nothing on the ladder so far does. The principle of this rung says that the function exists anyway. That is the choosing axiom, and 6b states it in words and then in marks.

Shoes and socks

An old picture makes the point. A rich man owns endlessly many pairs of boots and endlessly many pairs of socks. To pick one boot from every pair is easy: take the left one, every time. One condition, “is a left boot”, does all the picking, and it is a sift, so rung 2's Separation already says the function is a set. To pick one sock from every pair, there is no such condition. The two socks of a pair are made alike; whatever you can say of one you can say of the other. With a few pairs on the bed you can still pick, one call per pair. With endlessly many, there is no rule to write and no column long enough. Only the axiom says there is a set holding one sock from each pair.

The ladder already owns a rule of the boot kind. 4h proved that a settled set of numbers with a member has a least member, by a search up from 0. So from any collection of such sets, “take the least” picks from every one at once, and no axiom is needed. 6b writes this rule out. It also shows where the rule stops: at sets of numbers that are not settled, where 4h had only the double negative, and at the fractions, where nothing positive is least.

Where it comes from

The principle was first stated outright in 1904. It was stated to prove something an earlier founder of set theory had wanted: that every set can be laid out in an order in which every non-empty part has a first member. The proof caused a storm. Several of the leading analysts in France objected that a choice nobody can describe is no choice at all. Others pointed out that the objectors had made such choices in their own work without noticing. One of the inventors of the ladder's marks had already warned, years before, against applying an arbitrary rule endlessly many times. The answer was to write down axioms for sets, with choice among them, so that everyone could see which proofs used it. The ladder's rung 2 is built on the list begun then.

Why it has to be chosen

The quarrel was settled in the strangest way possible: neither side could win. 3e showed what “cannot be proved” means. Build a world in which every principle laid down so far is true and the statement in question is false. Every proof, from those principles, ends in a line that is true in that world, so no proof ends in the statement. 3e built such a world for infinity. For choice it took some of the great work of the last century, and it took two worlds, one for each direction.

In 1938 a world of sets was built inside set theory in which every set is made by a description from sets made earlier. In that world every set can be picked out by its earliest description. So “take the member with the earliest description” is a rule of the boot kind, and choice is true. So choice cannot be refuted from the other principles. In 1963 a way was found to build a world in which every other principle is true and choice is false, so choice cannot be proved from them. The second had already been done in 1922 for a world with things that are not sets in it, and that world is the socks made exact. 6d tells all three worlds in words and works the heart of the 1922 argument line by line.

So choice is independent, in 3e's word. Like infinity, it is chosen, not discovered. Almost all of mathematics chooses yes, and so does this ladder.

What you can do because you know it

Choice in its plain form is rarely what mathematicians quote. They quote two other statements, which given the other principles say the same thing. The first is well-ordering: every set can be put in an order in which every non-empty part has a least member. The counting numbers in their own order are the model. The second is the maximal-member principle. Suppose things are partly ordered, so that some pairs compare and some do not. Suppose every run of things that do compare has something above it all. Then there is something with nothing above it. Much of later algebra stands on the second form, much of the theory of the endless on the first.

6e builds the words: an order that may leave pairs uncompared, a chain, an upper bound, a maximal member. Then it proves what the ladder can prove. Well-ordering gives choice at once: take the least member of each set. The maximal-member principle gives choice too, by finding a largest partial picking. Here the ladder's care shows. The column stops one line short, at a double negative. It is the same gap 1b left open and 4h met in the least-member principle. The other direction, from choice to an order, means choosing from what is left again and again, past every counting number. The recursion that runs that far is not built on this ladder. 6e says so, sketches it in words, and proves the case it can: a finite set, which 4k's counting already puts in order.

That care runs through the whole rung. No proof on these pages splits a statement into “so or not so” unless the split has been earned, by a settled set, a finite set, or a proved or. Where a proof needs more, the page says which line, and that rung 7 is the rung that takes up the gap rung 1 left.

The rung's science page is about something else that cannot be done. You tune a bass by ear. Every musician who has tuned an instrument has met a puzzle that took two thousand years to state cleanly: tune every fifth pure and the octaves come out wrong. 6·phys shows the puzzle has no solution, and the reason is not acoustics or the ear. It is rung 4's technique that a number has only one splitting into primes.

At the end of the rung you know what a choosing function is, and when you can build one by hand or by rule. You know why the endless case without a rule needs an axiom, and why that axiom can be neither proved nor refuted. And you know how it turns into the two forms the rest of mathematics uses.

This is the first look at what choosing can do. The ladder expects to hear about the rest from you.

Who said it first

The boots and socks are Bertrand Russell’s. The principle was stated outright by Ernst Zermelo in 1904, to prove what Georg Cantor had wanted, that every set can be well-ordered. The French objectors were Émile Borel, René Baire and Henri Lebesgue; Jacques Hadamard took Zermelo’s side. The earlier warning against endless arbitrary picks was Giuseppe Peano’s. Zermelo’s answer was his list of axioms for sets (1908). The world in which choice is true is Kurt Gödel’s (1938), the world in which it is false is Paul Cohen’s (1963), and the 1922 world with things that are not sets is Abraham Fraenkel’s.

Russell's point, in his own example: among boots a rule of selection is ready to hand, since every pair has a left and a right; among socks no such principle suggests itself, and without assuming the axiom we cannot be sure there is any collection containing one sock from each pair.

Russell, Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy (1919), ch. XII

Zermelo called the principle a logical one that cannot be reduced to anything simpler, yet is applied everywhere in mathematical reasoning without hesitation. Four years later, answering his critics, he pointed out that several of them had used it in their own work.

Zermelo, Mathematische Annalen 59 (1904) and 65 (1908)

Accepted names

The maximal-member principle has the accepted name Zorn’s lemma. Well-ordering is called the well-ordering theorem or principle.
