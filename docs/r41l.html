41l · technique

Best from best pieces

Rung 39 solved hard questions by remembering the answers to smaller ones and building up, and aligned two strings of letters that way. On a graph that idea has a plain form: every piece of a shortest route is itself a shortest route between its ends. So the distances can be built from the distances of fewer links, or of fewer stops, one layer at a time. This step does it three ways. On one-way links with no cycle, put the dots in an order where every link goes forward, and one pass along the order gives every best route, shortest or longest. When some lengths are negative, the nearest first of the last step fails, but rounds of improvement, each allowing one more link, still work, and one extra round tells whether a cycle of negative total exists, which in money is a way to get rich by trading in a circle. And allowing the stops one at a time gives the distance between every two dots at once. Last, rung 39’s alignment of two strings turns out to be a best route through a grid, and its table is the forward pass.

A piece of a best route is a best route between its ends. With one-way links and no cycle, an order with every link forward exists, and one pass along it finds every best route. With negative lengths, n − 1 rounds of improvement find every distance, and one more round finds a cycle of negative total if there is one. Allowing the stops one at a time finds every distance at once. An alignment is a best route through a grid.

41l1Pieces of a best route

Links are one-way here, each (u, w) with a length ℓ(u, w), which may be negative. A path’s length is the sum along it.

Best-pieces technique: if a path from r to w is shortest, each of its pieces, from a dot u on it to a later dot x on it, is a shortest path from u to x. A shorter path from u to x, put in place of the piece, would give a shorter walk from r to w; if it met the rest of the path, cutting out the loop would shorten it again, as long as no cycle has negative total.

A one-way graph with no cycle can be put in forward order: a list of its dots in which every link goes from an earlier dot to a later one. Forward-order technique: every finite one-way graph with no cycle has a forward order. Some dot has no link coming in, since walking backwards along links from any dot must stop, or it would repeat a dot and close a cycle. Put that dot first, take it away, and go on by induction.

\text{dist}(r) = 0;\ \text{for each later } w \text{ in order: } \text{dist}(w) = \min_{(u, w)} \big(\text{dist}(u) + \ell(u, w)\big) @@ every u with a link into w comes earlier
\text{each } \text{dist}(u) \text{ used is already final} @@ induction along the order
\text{a shortest path to } w \text{ ends with a link } (u, w) \text{ after a shortest path to } u @@ the best-pieces technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every distance from } r \text{, each link looked at once}} \quad \blacksquare @@ and longest paths the same way, with max for min

41l2Rounds of improvement

With cycles and negative lengths, let dₖ(w) be the least length of a walk from r to w with at most k links. Then d₀(r) = 0, and d₀ has no value elsewhere.

Rounds technique: dₖ(w) is the least of dₖ₋₁(w) and dₖ₋₁(u) + ℓ(u, w) over the links (u, w); if no cycle has negative total, dₙ₋₁ is the distance; and some cycle has negative total exactly when a further round still lowers some value.

\text{a best walk of at most } k \text{ links has at most } k - 1 \text{, or ends with a link } (u, w) @@ the best-pieces technique on its last link
\text{no negative cycle: a shortest walk is a path, of at most } n - 1 \text{ links} @@ cutting a loop does not lengthen it
d_{n-1} = d_n = d_{n+1} = \cdots @@ no more links help
\text{a negative cycle: walking round it again and again lowers some } d_k \text{ for ever} @@ so round n lowers something

41l3Every pair at once

Name the dots 1 to n. Let Dₖ(i, j) be the least length of a path from i to j whose stops, the dots strictly between its ends, are all among 1 to k. D₀(i, j) is ℓ(i, j) if there is a link, 0 if i = j, and no value otherwise.

Stops-allowed technique: Dₖ(i, j) is the least of Dₖ₋₁(i, j) and Dₖ₋₁(i, k) + Dₖ₋₁(k, j); so with no negative cycle, Dₙ gives every distance after n³ steps. A best path with stops among 1 to k either avoids k, or passes it once, and its two pieces, before and after k, have stops among 1 to k − 1 (the best-pieces technique).

\ell(1,2) = 4,\ \ell(2,3) = -2,\ \ell(1,3) = 5,\ \ell(3,1) = 1 @@ one-way links
D_2(1, 3) = \min(D_1(1, 3),\ D_1(1, 2) + D_1(2, 3)) = \min(5,\ 4 - 2) = 2 @@ stop 2 allowed
D_3(2, 1) = \min(D_2(2, 1),\ D_2(2, 3) + D_2(3, 1)) = \min(\text{none},\ -2 + 1) = -1 @@ stop 3 allowed

41l4An alignment is a route

39o aligned two strings by a table: an entry for each pair of starting pieces, built from three neighbours. Read the table as a graph. Its dots are the places (i, j), i letters of the first string used and j of the second. A one-way link goes right (a letter of the second against a gap), down (a letter of the first against a gap), or down and right (two letters against each other), with the score of that step as its length. Every link goes forward in the order row by row, and an alignment is a path from (0, 0) to the far corner.

Alignment-route technique: the best alignment of two strings is a longest route through their grid, and 39o’s table is the forward pass of the forward-order technique.

\text{score: } +1 \text{ a match},\ -1 \text{ a mismatch},\ -1 \text{ a gap} @@ the scores of 39o
\text{grid } 5 \times 4 \text{ places; three kinds of link} @@ (i, j) with i ≤ 4, j ≤ 3
\text{route: } \mathrm{A/A},\ \mathrm{C}/{-},\ \mathrm{G/G},\ \mathrm{T/T} @@ down-right, down, down-right, down-right
\htmlClass{new}{1 - 1 + 1 + 1 = 2,\ \text{the longest route}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the forward pass gives 2 at the corner

So alignment is not a new idea but a graph in disguise, and every trick for routes applies: the layers of 41k to bound how far off the diagonal a good route strays, the cost of n times m for the pass. 41·bio aligns proteins this way before it builds a tree.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Building best answers from best pieces is dynamic programming, and the best-pieces technique is Bellman's principle of optimality, or optimal substructure. A one-way graph with no cycle is a directed acyclic graph, and a forward order is a topological sort. The rounds technique is the Bellman–Ford algorithm; the stops-allowed technique is the Floyd–Warshall algorithm. The best alignment is the Needleman–Wunsch alignment (1970), and the longest chain of tasks in 41l.6.1 is the critical path.

Who said it first

Richard Bellman named dynamic programming in the early 1950s, choosing a name, he wrote, that a congressman could not object to. Alfonso Shimbel (1955), Lester Ford (1956), Edward Moore (1957) and Bellman (1958) found the rounds. Bernard Roy (1959), Robert Floyd (1962) and Stephen Warshall (1962) found the stops-allowed recipe. Saul Needleman and Christian Wunsch aligned proteins this way in 1970. The critical path method was developed at DuPont in 1957.

A proof that is not allowed

“A shortest path to w ends with a link (u, w). The path to u is shortest, by the best-pieces technique. So take the nearest u, and the distance to w is that u’s distance plus ℓ(u, w).” Not allowed: the best u is the one with the least dist(u) + ℓ(u, w), not the least dist(u). A far u with a short link can beat a near u with a long one.
