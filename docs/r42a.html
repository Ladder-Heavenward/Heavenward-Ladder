42a · proof

Plus and times alone

Rung five proved twelve techniques for the fractions, and rung eight proved them again for the line. Nine of them are about adding and multiplying and nothing else. Since then the same nine, or most of them, have turned up again and again in places that are not numbers at all: the clock of rung forty one, the polynomials of rung nine, the grids of rung twenty seven, the tables of true and false from the first rungs. Each time the ladder checked the rules one by one, as if it were news. This step stops checking and starts from the rules. Whatever obeys them is one kind of thing, and whatever is proved from the rules alone is proved for all of them at once. The first surprise is how much depends on two rules that are easy to forget: that the order of a product does not matter, and that a product of two things that are not nothing is not nothing. Drop either, and cancelling goes. Keep both, and a finite collection gets reciprocals for free.

Take the rules of adding and multiplying alone, with no numbers behind them. The whole numbers, the clocks, the polynomials, the grids and the tables of true and false all obey them. When two members other than 0 can multiply to 0, cancelling fails. When they cannot, and the collection is finite, every member but 0 has a reciprocal.

42a1The rules, taken alone

Take a set R with two ways of combining, + and ·, and two members, 0 and 1. Ask only that seven of 5d1’s techniques hold, for every w, b, c in R: regroup for plus, plus-zero, negative, turn-about for plus, regroup for times, times-one, and spread, on both sides, w(b + c) = wb + wc and (b + c)w = bw + cw. Its accepted name is a ring, from the German word for a ring of numbers closed on itself, and the ladder uses that name from here on.

Two more of 5d1’s rules are left out on purpose. When turn-about for times holds as well, the ring turns about. When it turns about, 1 is not 0, and every member but 0 has a reciprocal, the ring is a field: 9·cb8’s nine rules. Everything 5e proved from the rules alone, such as anything-times-zero and the two negatives, holds in every ring where its proof used only these seven. Most of 5e’s proofs did, and those that turned a product about hold in every ring that turns about.

One-member technique: if 1 = 0 in a ring, the ring has only one member.

w = w \cdot 1 @@ times-one
\phantom{w} = w \cdot 0 @@ the given 1 = 0, by the swap axiom
\phantom{w} = 0 @@ anything-times-zero (5e), whose proof uses spread and negative only
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every } w \text{ is } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ w was any member

So a field must ask for 1 ≠ 0, or the one-member set {0} would be a field, with 0 its own reciprocal. That is the one place where the rules alone cannot keep 0 and 1 apart.

42a2Five rings

The whole numbers ℤ are a ring that turns about, and not a field: 2 has no whole reciprocal. 41a’s clock ℤ/n is a ring that turns about. The grids of 27d with n rows and n columns are a ring that does not turn about once n ≥ 2: the product is doing one map after another, and order matters (27d).

For any ring R, the polynomials R[x] are the lists of coefficients from R with only finitely many not 0, added place by place and multiplied by gathering: the coefficient of xᵏ in pq is the sum of pᵢqⱼ with i + j = k. 9g did this with real coefficients. The difference here is that the coefficients come from R, and the letter x is not a number of R to be put in: it only marks the place.

The parts of a set S make a ring of true and false. Plus is either-but-not-both, X + Y = the members in exactly one of X and Y. Times is both, X · Y = X ∩ Y. 0 is ∅ and 1 is S. On a set with one member this is 38a’s table, with either-but-not-both as the sum by 2 and and as the product.

Gathered-product technique: the polynomials with coefficients in a ring form a ring, and they turn about when the coefficients do.

((pq)r)_k = \textstyle\sum_{l + m = k} \big(\sum_{i + j = l} p_i q_j\big) r_m @@ the gathered product, twice
\phantom{((pq)r)_k} = \textstyle\sum_{i + j + m = k} (p_i q_j) r_m @@ spread in R, finitely many terms
\phantom{((pq)r)_k} = \textstyle\sum_{i + j + m = k} p_i (q_j r_m) = (p(qr))_k @@ regroup for times in R; gather the other way
\htmlClass{new}{(pq)r = p(qr) \text{ in } R[x]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other rules go place by place, the same way

One example, in ℤ[x]: (1 + 2x)(3 + x) = 3 + 7x + 2x². And in the ring of parts, X · X = X ∩ X = X for every X: every member is its own square. 42l comes back to that.

42a3When cancelling fails

On ℤ/6, 2 · 3 = 0, and neither factor is 0. A grid can square to the zero grid without being zero: the grid with one 1 above the diagonal, and 0 elsewhere, does it. So 5e’s zero-product technique, which says a product is 0 only when a factor is, does not come from the rules alone. In a ring it can fail.

A ring that turns about, has 1 ≠ 0, and in which ab = 0 only when a = 0 or b = 0, is a cancelling ring. ℤ, every field and ℚ[x] are cancelling rings. ℤ/6 is not.

Cancel-exactly-when technique: in a ring that turns about, ab = ac with a ≠ 0 gives b = c for every a, b, c exactly when the ring has no two members other than 0 whose product is 0.

ab = ac \iff a(b - c) = 0 @@ spread and negative: a(b − c) = ab − ac
\text{no zero products:}\ a \ne 0 \Rightarrow b - c = 0 \Rightarrow b = c @@ the given, on a(b − c)
\text{cancelling:}\ ab = 0 = a \cdot 0,\ a \ne 0 \Rightarrow b = 0 @@ anything-times-zero, then cancel a
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cancelling} \iff \text{no two members other than } 0 \text{ multiply to } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two directions

On ℤ/6, 2 · 1 = 2 · 4, both 2, and 1 ≠ 4. 5a’s parts under ∪ were worse: there a member that swallows everything ruined cancelling at once.

42a4Finite and cancelling gives reciprocals

A member with a reciprocal in the ring is undoable. The undoable members of a ring form a group under times (19a): a product of undoables is undone by the undoes in reverse order, as 19c undressed. In ℤ they are 1 and −1. In ℤ/6 they are 1 and 5. In the grids they are 27d’s undoable grids.

Finite-cancelling technique: a cancelling ring with finitely many members is a field.

a \ne 0,\ \ m_a(b) = ab \text{ on } R @@ multiplying by a, a function from R to R
ab = ab' \Rightarrow b = b' @@ the cancel-exactly-when technique
m_a \text{ is one-to-one from a finite set to itself, so it is onto} @@ a one-to-one map of a finite set to itself misses nothing (9c, counted as in 4k)
\htmlClass{new}{ab = 1 \text{ for some } b} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 is reached; b turns about with a

On ℤ/7 with a = 3, the multiples are 0, 3, 6, 2, 5, 1, 4, all seven, and 3 · 5 = 1. 41b found the reciprocals of a prime clock by 4j’s divisors. This is a second road, with no division at all: a prime clock has no zero products, so it is a field because it is finite. An endless cancelling ring need not be one: ℤ is cancelling, and 2 has no reciprocal.

The accepted names

A ring here is a ring with identity; one that turns about is a commutative ring. A cancelling ring is an integral domain, or simply a domain. Undoable members are units, and they form the group of units R×. Members a, b other than 0 with ab = 0 are zero divisors. The polynomials R[x] are the polynomial ring, and the parts of a set with symmetric difference and intersection are a Boolean ring. The finite-cancelling technique is usually stated as: every finite integral domain is a field. A ring in which every member but 0 has a reciprocal but which need not turn about is a division ring or skew field.

Who said it first

Richard Dedekind worked with rings of algebraic numbers from 1871 without the word. David Hilbert wrote Zahlring in his report on number fields of 1897. Abraham Fraenkel wrote rules close to these in 1914, and Emmy Noether set rings as the subject of a theory in papers of 1921 and after. William Rowan Hamilton found the four-part numbers in 1843, cutting the rule into a bridge in Dublin. Joseph Wedderburn proved in 1905 that every finite ring with every member but 0 undoable turns about.

A proof that is not allowed

“On ℤ/12, 3x = 3 · 5, so x = 5.” Not allowed: 3 · 1 = 3 and 3 · 5 = 15 = 3 on the clock of 12, and 3 · 9 = 27 = 3 too. 3 times 4 is 0, so 3 cannot be cancelled. The answers of 3x = 3 are 1, 5 and 9.
