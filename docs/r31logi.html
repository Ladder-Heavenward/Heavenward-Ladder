31·logi · logistics

Should every counter have its own line?

Should every counter have its own line? A post office in Spokane has two clerks. At noon people come in at random, about 54 an hour, and each clerk serves about 30 an hour, so the clerks are busy nine tenths of the time. The office can paint two lines on the floor, one to each counter, or rope off a single line that feeds whichever clerk is free. The clerks are the same either way, and so are the people. What makes the question hard is that the averages are the same in both plans: 27 people an hour to each clerk, on average, either way. Yet one plan makes people wait twice as long as the other. 30o found the steady line at one counter, with chance arrivals and chance service. This page reads from it how fast the wait grows as a counter nears full use, then finds the steady line at two counters sharing one line, and shows why the shared line wins, by exactly how much, and why the gain is largest when the office is busiest.

At one counter busy a share ρ of the time, a person waits on average ρ/(1 − ρ) service times before being served, which grows without bound as ρ nears 1. Two counters sharing one line, each busy a share ρ, cut that wait by the factor ρ/(1 + ρ), nearly half when the office is busy, because a clerk is never idle while someone waits.

31logi1One counter, nearly full

At one counter, people arrive as a rare-arrival stream of rate λ, and each takes a chance time to serve, exponential with rate μ, apart from everything else. 30o’s steady-line technique, with ρ = λ/μ below 1, gives the steady chance (1 − ρ)ρⁿ of n people at the counter, being served or waiting. This page holds in trust one more fact, true for rare-arrival streams: a person arriving finds n there with the steady chance.

Near-full technique: at one counter with ρ = λ/μ below 1, a person waits on average ρ/(1 − ρ) service times, ρ/(μ − λ), before being served; so the wait grows like 1/(1 − ρ) as the counter nears full use.

\text{finds } n \text{ there: waits for } n \text{ services} @@ the one being served has 1/μ left, by the forgets-the-wait technique (30m)
\operatorname{E}(\text{wait}\mid n) = n/\mu @@ averages add (30g)
\operatorname{E}(\text{wait}) = \textstyle\sum_{n \ge 0}(1 - \rho)\rho^n\,\tfrac n\mu @@ the steady chances, held in trust for arrivals
\textstyle\sum_{n \ge 0} n\rho^n = \tfrac{\rho}{(1 - \rho)^2} @@ the slope of the geometric sum (22e)

From 0.9 to 0.99 the clerk does ten percent more work, and the wait grows elevenfold. A counter run near full is not efficient; it is a line that has stopped being steady in any useful sense. Each step closer to full costs more than the one before, because 1/(1 − ρ) has slope 1/(1 − ρ)².

31logi2Two counters, one line

Now two clerks, each serving at rate μ, share one line, and people arrive at rate λ, with ρ = λ/(2μ) below 1. With n people there, service ends at rate μ if n = 1 and at rate 2μ if n is 2 or more, by 30o’s first-of-two technique: two independent exponential times end first at the sum of their rates. In the steady line, the flow up from n to n + 1 balances the flow down, as in 30o.

\lambda p_0 = \mu p_1,\ \ \lambda p_n = 2\mu p_{n+1}\ (n \ge 1) @@ balance across each step
p_1 = 2\rho\,p_0,\ \ p_n = 2\rho^n p_0\ (n \ge 1) @@ λ/μ = 2ρ, λ/2μ = ρ
p_0\big(1 + \tfrac{2\rho}{1 - \rho}\big) = 1 \Rightarrow p_0 = \tfrac{1 - \rho}{1 + \rho} @@ the geometric sum (22e)
\text{finds } n \ge 2: \text{waits for } n - 1 \text{ ends, each at rate } 2\mu @@ the first-of-two technique
\operatorname{E}(\text{wait}) = \textstyle\sum_{n \ge 2} 2\rho^n p_0\,\tfrac{n - 1}{2\mu} = \tfrac{p_0}{\mu}\cdot\tfrac{\rho^2}{(1 - \rho)^2} @@ Σ (n − 1)ρⁿ from n = 2 is ρ²/(1 − ρ)²

Shared-line technique: two counters each busy a share ρ, sharing one line, give an average wait of ρ²/(μ(1 − ρ²)); two separate lines with the arrivals split evenly give ρ/(μ(1 − ρ)); the shared wait is the separate one times ρ/(1 + ρ), below a half for every ρ below 1. Each separate line is a single counter with arrivals at λ/2, so the near-full technique gives its wait, and the ratio is ρ²(1 − ρ)/(ρ(1 − ρ²)) = ρ/(1 + ρ).

\lambda = 54,\ \mu = 30 \text{ an hour},\ \ \rho = 0.9 @@ each clerk busy nine tenths of the time

\text{one line: } \tfrac{0.81}{30 \times 0.19} \approx 0.142 \text{ h} \approx 8.5 \text{ min} @@ the shared-line technique

31logi3Where the time goes

With two lines, a clerk can stand idle while the other has people waiting. The lines are independent, each a single counter with ρ = 0.9. Clerk A is idle with chance 1 − ρ, and clerk B has someone waiting, two or more there, with chance ρ². So one clerk idles while someone waits at the other with chance 2(1 − ρ)ρ² = 2 × 0.1 × 0.81 ≈ 0.16. For about a sixth of the noon hour, a clerk is free and a person is waiting for no reason but which line they chose.

Idle-waste technique: with separate lines, the chance that a clerk idles while someone waits is 2(1 − ρ)ρ²; with one shared line it is 0, since a free clerk takes the next person. The first part is the steady-line technique at each counter, with the two counters independent; the second is how the line is built.

The shared line is better for the average wait and worse for nothing but the length it looks. It is also fairer: people are served in the order they came, while with two lines a person can be stuck behind one slow service and watch later arrivals go first. Its cost is floor space for one long line, and a short walk to the free counter. Supermarkets often keep a line per till anyway, because people who see one long line leave, and banks and airports mostly rope off one.

A third plan is one clerk twice as fast. Its total time in the office, waiting and served, is 1/(2μ − λ), 10 minutes at noon, less than the shared line’s 10.5, but its wait before service is 9 minutes, more than 8.5. Which plan is best depends on which time is counted, and that is not a question the arithmetic answers.

The accepted names

A single counter with rare arrivals and exponential service is the M/M/1 queue, and two counters sharing one line the M/M/2 queue; c counters give the M/M/c queue, whose chance of waiting is the Erlang C formula. ρ is the utilisation or traffic intensity. That arrivals in a rare-arrival stream see the steady chances is PASTA, Poisson arrivals see time averages. That the average number equals λ times the average time is Little’s law. Pooling the lines is resource pooling; one line feeding many counters is a single-queue multi-server or serpentine line, and switching between lines is jockeying.

Who said it first

Agner Krarup Erlang worked out the waits at telephone exchanges in Copenhagen from 1909 to 1917, including many lines sharing one wait. David Kendall gave the M/M/c names in 1953. John Little proved his law in 1961. Ronald Wolff proved in 1982 that rare-arrival streams see the steady chances.
