27e · proof

The Determinant

A square grid moves the unit square to a slanted parallelogram, and the unit cube to a slanted box. By how much does it scale area, or volume? This step asks for a number with three plain properties, and finds there is exactly one. It multiplies when maps are done one after another, and it is zero exactly when the map flattens space and cannot be undone.

Exactly one number per square grid is straight in each column, is zero when two columns agree, and is one for doing nothing. It multiplies along products, and it is zero exactly when the grid cannot be undone.

27e1Three Rules for a Volume Scale

Look for a number D(A) for each n × n grid A, with three rules. Straight: D is straight in each column when the other columns are held fixed. Flat: D(A) = 0 when two columns of A are equal. Unit: D(I) = 1. These are what a signed volume should do: stretching one edge stretches the volume, a box with two equal edges is flat, and the unit cube has volume 1.

Column-slide technique: if D is straight and flat, then swapping two columns changes the sign of D, adding a stretch of one column to another leaves D unchanged, and a column of zeros gives 0.

0 = D(\ldots, u+v, \ldots, u+v, \ldots) @@ flat
= D(.., u, .., u, ..) + D(.., u, .., v, ..) + D(.., v, .., u, ..) + D(.., v, .., v, ..) @@ straight, in each of the two columns
\htmlClass{new}{D(.., u, .., v, ..) = -D(.., v, .., u, ..)} @@ the first and last are 0, by flat
D(.., u + tv, .., v, ..) = D(.., u, .., v, ..) + t\,D(.., v, .., v, ..) @@ straight in the first column
\htmlClass{new}{D(.., u + tv, .., v, ..) = D(.., u, .., v, ..)} \quad \blacksquare @@ flat; a zero column is 0 times itself, so gives 0

27e2Exactly One Such Number

Three-rules technique: exactly one D obeys straight, flat and unit: D(A) = Σ over the shuffles σ of 1..n of sign(σ) a_{σ(1)1} a_{σ(2)2} ⋯ a_{σ(n)n}, where sign(σ) is +1 for an even shuffle and −1 for an odd one. Any D that is only straight and flat equals D(I) times this.

\text{column } j = \textstyle\sum_i a_{ij}e_i @@ the columns technique (27d1)
D(A) = \textstyle\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n} a_{i_1 1}\cdots a_{i_n n}\,D(e_{i_1}, \ldots, e_{i_n}) @@ straight, in every column in turn
\text{terms with a repeated } e_i \text{ are } 0 @@ flat
D(e_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, e_{\sigma(n)}) = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\,D(I) @@ undo σ by swaps (19e); each swap changes the sign (27e1)
\text{the formula is straight, and } 1 \text{ at } I @@ each term holds one entry from each column
\text{two equal columns: terms pair off by one swap, with opposite signs} @@ a swap changes parity (19e)
\htmlClass{new}{D(A) = D(I)\textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\prod_j a_{\sigma(j)j}} \quad \blacksquare

For a 2 × 2 grid that number is ad − bc. It is the signed area of the parallelogram on the two columns, as 15e signed it: positive when the second column is a turn of less than a half turn anticlockwise from the first. Accepted name: the determinant, det A.

27e3Volume Scales Multiply

Det-product technique: det(AB) = det A · det B for all n × n grids.

D(B) = \det(AB),\ A \text{ held fixed} @@ a number for each grid B
\text{column } j \text{ of } AB = A(\text{column } j \text{ of } B) @@ the product-is-doing technique (27d2)
D \text{ is straight and flat} @@ A keeps mixes; two equal columns of B give two equal columns of AB
D(B) = D(I)\det B = \det A\,\det B @@ the three-rules technique (27e2), last sentence
\htmlClass{new}{\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B} \quad \blacksquare

27e4Zero Exactly When It Flattens

Nonzero-det technique: a square grid can be undone exactly when its determinant is not 0.

AA^{-1} = I \Rightarrow \det A\cdot\det A^{-1} = 1 @@ the det-product technique (27e3), and unit
\Rightarrow \det A \ne 0 @@ a product of 1 has no zero factor
A \text{ not undoable} \Rightarrow Av = 0,\ v \ne 0 @@ the undoable-group technique (27d3): it loses more than 0
\Rightarrow \text{the columns are not free} \Rightarrow \text{one is a mix of the others} @@ Av is the mix of the columns with the numbers of v; the spare-vector technique (27b1)
\text{subtract that mix from it: a zero column, same det} @@ the column-slide technique (27e1)
\htmlClass{new}{\det A = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ a zero column gives 0

27e5Moves to Watch Out For

\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> det is straight in each column alone, not in the whole grid: det(I + I) = 2ⁿ
\det(tA) = t\det A\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> each of the n columns is stretched: det(tA) = tⁿ det A
\text{swapping two rows leaves det alone}\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> a row swap also changes the sign: 27g proves det A equals the det of A turned on its side
\det A = 0 \Rightarrow \text{a column is } 0\,? @@ <span class="vd vd-p">outside the plan</span> a column need only be a mix of the others

The accepted names

The number is the determinant, det A or |A|. The three rules say det is multilinear, alternating and normalised; the formula over shuffles is the Leibniz formula. The det-product technique is the multiplicativity of the determinant. A grid with det A = 0 is singular.

Who said it first

Seki Takakazu in 1683 and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 1693 each used determinants to decide when equations can be solved. Gabriel Cramer gave his rule in 1750. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved det(AB) = det A det B in 1812, and gave the word its present meaning. Karl Weierstrass defined the determinant by the three rules in lectures published in 1903.
