24l · technique

Solutions as Power Series

The last rung rebuilt a smooth function from one point as a power sum without end, and showed that such a sum may be taken apart term by term. That gives one more way to solve a rule on rates. Guess that the solution is a power sum with unknown coefficients, put the guess into the rule piece by piece, and ask each power to balance. The rule turns into a recipe for the coefficients, each from the ones before. If the sum the recipe builds reaches anywhere at all, it is a solution. This works even when the solution has no formula, and that is when it matters most. Two rules found this way have solutions so useful that they carry names: one describes the pull of a planet that is not quite round, the other the shape of a beaten drum. The second has a point where the rule itself breaks down, and there a plain power sum is not enough, though a power of x times one is.

Guess a power sum, put it into the rule term by term, and balance each power. The rule becomes a recipe for the coefficients, and the sum it builds is a solution wherever it reaches. For the rule of a planet's shape the recipe can stop, and the solution is a polynomial. At a point where the rule breaks down, try a power of x times a power sum, and the lowest power says which power to try.

24l1A Power Sum That Obeys the Rule

Sum-solves technique: put y = Σ uₖ(x − w)ᵏ into a rule, take its slopes term by term, and ask each power of (x − w) to balance. If the coefficients obey the recipe that results, and the sum has reach R > 0, then the sum solves the rule on |x − w| < R. Two power sums with the same total near w have the same coefficients, by the smooth-sum technique, so balancing each power is not only enough but needed.

y = \textstyle\sum u_k x^k,\ \ y' = \sum (k + 1)\,u_{k+1}x^k @@ the term-by-term technique (23e3), inside the reach
1 + 2xy = 1 + \textstyle\sum_{k \ge 1} 2u_{k-1}x^k @@ multiply each piece by 2x and shift the count
u_0 = 0,\ \ u_1 = 1,\ \ (k + 1)\,u_{k+1} = 2u_{k-1} \ (k \ge 1) @@ the start; then balance the power x⁰, and each xᵏ
u_{2m} = 0,\ \ u_{2m+1} = \tfrac{2^m}{1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots(2m + 1)} @@ induction (4h): u₂ = 0, u₃ = 2/3, u₅ = 4/15, u₇ = 8/105
\Big|\tfrac{u_{2m+3}\,x^{2m+3}}{u_{2m+1}\,x^{2m+1}}\Big| = \tfrac{2x^2}{2m + 3} \to 0 @@ the ratio of neighbouring pieces, for every x
\htmlClass{new}{y = x + \tfrac23x^3 + \tfrac4{15}x^5 + \tfrac8{105}x^7 + \cdots \text{ solves the rule on the whole line}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the reach has no ceiling (23c3); the rule is tame on every bounded strip, so it is the only solution (24d3)

The integrating factor of 24g solves the same rule as y = e^(x²)·∫₀ˣ e^(−t²) dt. That is correct, and it is not a formula you can work out: the area under e^(−t²) has no antiderivative made from the usual functions. The sum works it out. At x = 1, four pieces give 2.0095 and enough pieces give 2.0301. The area under e^(−t²), a bell-shaped curve, is one that the chances of this floor will need, and this is one way it is computed.

24l2The Rule of the Planets' Shapes

A planet that spins bulges at its middle, and the pull it exerts is not quite that of a ball. Split that pull into parts by latitude, and each part obeys the rule (1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n + 1)y = 0, with x the sine of the latitude and n a whole number. Its coefficients are not constant, so 24h's trial of e^(rx) fails. A power sum does not.

Stopping-sum technique: for (1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n + 1)y = 0, power sums about 0 obey uₖ₊₂ = (k − n)(k + n + 1)uₖ/((k + 2)(k + 1)). When n is a whole number, the sum of powers with the same evenness as n stops at xⁿ and is a polynomial; a sum of the other evenness that does not stop has reach 1.

y'' = \textstyle\sum (k + 2)(k + 1)\,u_{k+2}x^k,\ \ x^2y'' = \sum k(k - 1)\,u_kx^k,\ \ xy' = \sum k\,u_kx^k @@ term by term (23e3), each sum written by its power xᵏ
(k + 2)(k + 1)\,u_{k+2} - \big(k(k - 1) + 2k - n(n + 1)\big)u_k = 0 @@ balance xᵏ (24l1)
k(k + 1) - n(n + 1) = (k - n)(k + n + 1) @@ multiply out both sides
u_{k+2} = \tfrac{(k - n)(k + n + 1)}{(k + 2)(k + 1)}\,u_k @@ the recipe: u₀ fixes the even powers, u₁ the odd ones
k = n:\ u_{n+2} = 0, \text{ so } u_{n+4} = u_{n+6} = \cdots = 0 @@ the top factor vanishes once, and every later coefficient is a multiple of it
\htmlClass{new}{\text{for whole } n \text{ one solution is a polynomial of degree } n;\ \ \big|\tfrac{u_{k+2}x^{k+2}}{u_kx^k}\big| \to x^2 \text{ for the other}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ratio of neighbouring pieces; the reach-by-ratio technique (23c3) in x²

n = 2, u₀ = 1: u₂ = (0 − 2)(0 + 3)/2 = −3, and then nothing. So y = 1 − 3x² solves the rule; scaled to be 1 at x = 1 it is (3x² − 1)/2. That polynomial is the shape of the earth's bulge in its pull: the earth's gravity, measured from orbit, is mostly a ball's, plus a small multiple of (3x² − 1)/2. The sum of the other evenness reaches only to x = ±1, the poles, where the rule's first coefficient 1 − x² is 0. That is not a coincidence, and the next footing looks at such points.

Every solution is a mix A·(even sum) + B·(odd sum), with A and B fixed by y(0) and y′(0). That a rule of the second order is fixed by those two numbers is 24d carried over to pairs of functions, and this page holds it in trust.

24l3A Point Where the Rule Breaks Down, and the Drum

Write a second-order rule as y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0. Where P and Q are power sums about w, the sums of 24l1 work. Where P or Q passes every bound, as 1/x does at 0, the point is a bad point of the rule, and solutions may have no power sum there at all. When the badness is mild, a power of x times a power sum does.

Starting-power technique: for x²y″ + x·p(x)y′ + q(x)y = 0 with p and q power sums about 0, try y = xˢ Σ uₖxᵏ with u₀ ≠ 0. The lowest power, xˢ, balances only if s(s − 1) + p(0)s + q(0) = 0.

y = \textstyle\sum u_kx^{k+s},\ \ xy' = \sum (k + s)\,u_kx^{k+s},\ \ x^2y'' = \sum (k + s)(k + s - 1)\,u_kx^{k+s} @@ the slope of a real power (17), term by term, for x > 0
\text{lowest power } x^s:\ \ 4s(s - 1) + 4s - 1 = 4s^2 - 1 = 0 @@ only u₀ reaches it; u₀ ≠ 0; here p(0) = 1, q(0) = −1/4
s = \tfrac12 \text{ or } s = -\tfrac12 @@ two starting powers
\big(4(k + s)^2 - 1\big)u_k + 4u_{k-2} = 0 @@ balance x^{k+s}; take u₁ = 0
s = \tfrac12:\ u_k = -\tfrac{u_{k-2}}{k(k + 1)} \Rightarrow y = x^{1/2}\big(1 - \tfrac{x^2}{3!} + \tfrac{x^4}{5!} - \cdots\big) @@ 4(k + ½)² − 1 = 4k(k + 1)
\htmlClass{new}{y = \tfrac{\sin x}{\sqrt x} \text{ and, from } s = -\tfrac12,\ y = \tfrac{\cos x}{\sqrt x}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sums of sin and cos (23a3), divided by √x

Neither solution has a power sum about 0. One heads to 0 there like √x, and the other passes every bound. The rule is the drum's rule in disguise. A round drumhead struck in its middle rings in shapes y(r), with r the distance from the centre, and they obey x²y″ + xy′ + x²y = 0 with x proportional to r. There p(0) = 1 and q(0) = 0, so s² = 0: only one starting power, and only one power sum: y = 1 − x²/4 + x⁴/64 − x⁶/2304 + ⋯, with uₖ = −uₖ₋₂/k². The second solution needs ln x beside its sum, and passes every bound at the centre, so a drum, which is finite at its centre, rings only in the first.

The rim cannot move, so the rim must sit where the sum is 0. The sum is first 0 at x = 2.405, then at 5.520 and 8.654. The pitches of the drum go as those numbers, and 5.520/2.405 = 2.295 is not a whole number. A string's overtones are 2, 3, 4 times its lowest pitch, which is why it sounds like a note. A drum's are not, which is why it thuds.

The accepted names

Solving by a guessed power sum is the power series method; a point where the coefficients are power sums is an ordinary point, and a bad point is a singular point, regular when the badness is as mild as this page allows. The starting-power technique is the method of Frobenius, and the starting-power equation is the indicial equation. The rule of 24l1 has the solution e^(x²)·∫₀ˣ e^(−t²) dt, a relative of Dawson's function and of the error function. The rule of the planets' shapes is Legendre's equation, and its polynomials, scaled to be 1 at x = 1, are the Legendre polynomials P₀, P₁, P₂ = (3x² − 1)/2, …; the earth's bulge term in its gravity is called J₂. The rule of the drum is Bessel's equation of order 0, and its finite solution is the Bessel function J₀; 4x²y″ + 4xy′ + (4x² − 1)y = 0 is Bessel's equation of order ½. The functions of the sky and of the drum are called special functions.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton solved rules on rates by power sums in his Method of Fluxions (1671). Adrien-Marie Legendre found his polynomials in 1782, studying the pull of a planet that is not a ball. Daniel Bernoulli met the drum's sum in 1732, in a hanging chain, and Leonhard Euler met it in the drum in 1764; Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel studied it in full in 1824, in the motion of the planets. Georg Frobenius gave the starting-power method in 1873.
