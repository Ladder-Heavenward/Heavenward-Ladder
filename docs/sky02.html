How Do Qubits Keep From Falling Apart?

How do qubits keep from falling apart? The last step counted ten billion gates for one factoring run, and the physics annex of rung thirty nine found that a qubit keeps its state for a few thousand. Everything between those two numbers is this page. It builds how a qubit is worked at all, by a pulse at its own frequency that swings it from one answer to the other; how a stray field turns it a little at random, so that each run stays clean while the average fades; why the ordinary cure, copying and voting, is closed by a short proof; and how three qubits, and then a square grid of them, guard a state without ever reading it. Below a certain error rate a larger code does better, and above it worse: that rate, the threshold, decides whether a large machine can exist. In 2024 a chip was reported below it. This page works out what that showed, what it did not, and what the claims around it are worth.

Driven at its own frequency, a qubit swings: the chance of 1 is sin²(Ωt/2), and off by Δ it never passes Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²). Random turnings of ±ε fade the average as (cos ε)ᴺ. No gate copies every state. u|000⟩ + v|111⟩ and two parity readings mend one flip, failing with chance 3p² − 2p³, and a repetition code of distance 2t − 1 fails with chance at most ½(4p)ᵗ. The grid code needs 2d² − 1 qubits and divides its failure by Λ for each step of 2 in d; the 2024 chip measured Λ = 2.14. Here Ω (capital omega) means the rate at which the drive swings the qubit, not 3a's looped set, and Δ (capital delta) how far the drive is off the qubit's own frequency, not 13a's mark for a change.

sky2.1Driven at Its Own Frequency, It Swings

41·phys.5 said that a microwave pulse at the gap’s frequency turns a qubit from |0⟩ toward |1⟩ at a steady rate. Here is why, and what happens when the drive is a little off. A qubit with gap ħω₀, driven by a field of frequency ω, obeys 35·phys.2’s law iħψ′ = Hψ with a grid H of two rows. Seen from a frame that turns with the drive, and dropping the parts that turn at about 2ω₀ and average out, the rule for ψ = u|0⟩ + v|1⟩ becomes iψ′ = ½[[−Δ, Ω], [Ω, Δ]]ψ. Here Δ = ω − ω₀ is the miss and Ω the strength of the drive. The dropping is anexact, good to about Ω/ω₀: 0.002 for a 10 MHz drive on a 5 GHz qubit.

Drive-swing technique: on frequency, Δ = 0, the state from |0⟩ is cos(Ωt/2)|0⟩ − i sin(Ωt/2)|1⟩, so the chance of 1 is sin²(Ωt/2); off by Δ, the chance is (Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²)) sin²(√(Ω² + Δ²) t/2), and never passes Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²).

iu' = \tfrac{\Omega}{2}v,\ \ iv' = \tfrac{\Omega}{2}u @@ the rule at Δ = 0
u = \cos\tfrac{\Omega t}{2},\ \ v = -i\sin\tfrac{\Omega t}{2}:\ \ iu' = -\tfrac{i\Omega}{2}\sin\tfrac{\Omega t}{2} = \tfrac{\Omega}{2}v @@ slopes of cos and sin (21g)
iv' = i(-i)\tfrac{\Omega}{2}\cos\tfrac{\Omega t}{2} = \tfrac{\Omega}{2}u @@ the second equation holds too
u(0) = 1,\ \ v(0) = 0 @@ the start; the only solution, by 24d’s tame rules
\htmlClass{new}{|v|^2 = \sin^2(\Omega t/2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swing
  
\Omega/2\pi = 10\ \text{MHz}:\ \ \Omega t = \pi \text{ at } t = 50\ \text{ns} @@ a full flip, |0⟩ to |1⟩
\Omega t = \pi/2 \text{ at } t = 25\ \text{ns} @@ an even spread, H up to turnings
\Delta = \Omega:\ \text{top } \tfrac12;\ \ \Delta = 0.1\,\Omega:\ \text{top } 0.990 @@ Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a miss of } 1\ \text{MHz in } 5\ \text{GHz costs } 1\% \text{ of the flip}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the frequency must be known to one part in 5000

The form off frequency is held in trust: it is the same work with the whole grid, whose stretches are ±½√(Ω² + Δ²) (27f). A swing that never reaches 1 is how a qubit’s frequency is found: sweep the drive and watch where the swing is tallest.

sky2.2Noise as a Small Random Turning

A stray field moves the gap a little, at random. Over each short step the turning between the two parts moves by +ε or −ε, a coin toss each time, so after N steps it has walked as 30n’s walks do. A state (|0⟩ + e^(iθ)|1⟩)/√2, read after H, gives 0 with chance (1 + cos θ)/2 by sky1.1’s two-roads technique.

Fading-turn technique: if the turning between the parts takes N independent steps of +ε or −ε, each with chance ½, the average of e^(iθ) is (cos ε)ᴺ, about e^(−Nε²/2); so the average chance of reading 0 after H falls from 1 toward ½ as ½(1 + (cos ε)ᴺ).

e^{i\theta} = \textstyle\prod_{j=1}^{N} e^{\pm i\varepsilon} @@ angles add, turnings multiply (26b)
E\,e^{\pm i\varepsilon} = \tfrac12\big(e^{i\varepsilon} + e^{-i\varepsilon}\big) = \cos\varepsilon @@ one step
E\,e^{i\theta} = (\cos\varepsilon)^N @@ the independent-averages-multiply technique (30g)
(\cos\varepsilon)^N \approx \big(1 - \tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\big)^N \to e^{-N\varepsilon^2/2} @@ the many-small-chances technique (22j1)
\htmlClass{new}{E\,P(0) = \tfrac12\big(1 + (\cos\varepsilon)^N\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ averages add (30g); E cos θ is the real part of E e^(iθ)

With ε = 0.01 and N = 10 000, (cos 0.01)ᴺ = 0.607, and the chance of 0 is 0.803 where it was 1. It falls to 1/e at N = 2/ε² = 20 000 steps. Each run is a clean state turned by some θ; it is the average over runs that fades, and a computation is an average over runs. Flips from |0⟩ to |1⟩, the heat of 41·phys.5’s cold-qubit technique, are a second kind of error. Every error of one qubit is a mix of flips and turnings, held in trust, so a code that mends both mends everything.

sky2.3No Copying

Ordinary codes copy: send a bit three times and take the majority, 44·comp1’s code of distance 3. A qubit is another matter.

No-copy technique: no gate U sends ψ ⊗ |0⟩ to ψ ⊗ ψ for every state ψ of a qubit; a gate can copy two states of size one only if they are equal or at right angles.

U(\psi \otimes |0\rangle) = \psi \otimes \psi,\ \ U(\varphi \otimes |0\rangle) = \varphi \otimes \varphi @@ suppose U copies both
\langle \psi \otimes |0\rangle,\ \varphi \otimes |0\rangle\rangle = \langle \psi, \varphi\rangle\,\langle 0, 0\rangle = \langle \psi, \varphi\rangle @@ the product on ℂ² ⊗ ℂ² in the basis eᵢ ⊗ fⱼ (47h1), multiplied out
\langle \psi \otimes \psi,\ \varphi \otimes \varphi\rangle = \langle \psi, \varphi\rangle^2 @@ the same rule
\langle \psi, \varphi\rangle = \langle \psi, \varphi\rangle^2 @@ U keeps every product (31d4)
\htmlClass{new}{\langle \psi, \varphi\rangle = 0 \text{ or } 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ at right angles, or equal, since sizes are one

Take |0⟩ and H|0⟩: their product is 1/√2, and its square ½ is not 1/√2. The copy gate of 41·phys.5 copies |0⟩ and |1⟩, which are at right angles. Fed H|0⟩ ⊗ |0⟩, it makes Φ, which 41·phys.2’s no-split technique says is no H|0⟩ ⊗ H|0⟩. So a quantum code cannot vote on copies. It must spread a state over several qubits and check something about them that says nothing of the state.

sky2.4Three Qubits Against One Flip

Spread u|0⟩ + v|1⟩ to u|000⟩ + v|111⟩ by two copy gates from the first qubit to two fresh ones. That is no copy: three copies of the state would have eight parts, and this has two. It is 44·comp1’s code of distance 3, laid on the basis.

Parity-read technique: reading whether qubits 1 and 2 agree, and whether 2 and 3 agree, gives the same answers for |000⟩ and |111⟩ under the same flip; so the two readings name which qubit flipped, if one did, leave u and v untouched, and flipping that qubit back mends the state.

u|100\rangle + v|011\rangle @@ the first qubit flipped
\text{1 and 2 differ, 2 and 3 agree, in both parts} @@ each part gives the same two answers
\text{the reading leaves } u|100\rangle + v|011\rangle @@ both parts fit it, so the square-chance law removes neither (41·phys.1)
\text{flip qubit 1 again:}\ \ \htmlClass{new}{u|000\rangle + v|111\rangle} \quad \blacksquare @@ mended, with u and v never read

The four answers name the four cases: agree and agree, no flip; differ and agree, qubit 1; differ and differ, qubit 2; agree and differ, qubit 3. If each qubit flips independently with chance p, the mending fails when two or three flip: 3p²(1 − p) + p³ = 3p² − 2p³. At p = 0.01 that is 2.98 × 10⁻⁴, 34 times better, and it helps whenever p < ½. But a turning of one part’s sign on any qubit sends u|000⟩ + v|111⟩ to u|000⟩ − v|111⟩, which every parity reading accepts, and with three qubits there are three places for it: turnings now get through with chance about 3p. Guarding both at once took a code of nine qubits.

sky2.5A Threshold

The repetition code of distance d = 2t − 1 spreads over d qubits and mends up to t − 1 flips by majority, by 44·comp1’s fix-radius technique.

Threshold-bound technique: if each of d = 2t − 1 qubits flips independently with chance p, the majority is wrong with chance at most ½(4p)ᵗ; so when p < ¼ the failure can be made as small as wished by making d larger.

P(\text{fail}) = \textstyle\sum_{k \ge t} \binom{d}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{d - k} @@ at least t of the d qubits flip
\le p^t \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{d} \binom{d}{k} = 2^d p^t @@ pᵏ ≤ pᵗ and (1 − p)^(d−k) ≤ 1; one count for each pattern of flips
2^d = 2^{2t - 1} = \tfrac12\, 4^t @@ d = 2t − 1
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{fail}) \le \tfrac12 (4p)^t} \quad \blacksquare @@ falls as t grows, when 4p < 1
  
d = 3, 5, 7, 9:\ \ 3.0 \times 10^{-4},\ 9.9 \times 10^{-6},\ 3.4 \times 10^{-7},\ 1.2 \times 10^{-8} @@ the exact sums
\text{the bound: } 8 \times 10^{-4},\ 3.2 \times 10^{-5},\ 1.3 \times 10^{-6},\ 5.1 \times 10^{-8} @@ ½(0.04)ᵗ, t = 2, 3, 4, 5
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each step of 2 divides the failure by about } 30} \quad \blacksquare @@ below the threshold, size buys reliability

Every threshold technique has this shape: count the ways to fail, and each way costs a power of p that grows with the code. The real case is harder. The checks are themselves noisy gates, the code must guard turnings too, and an error in a check can spread. With all of it counted, the threshold drops from ¼ to about 1% for the grid code of the next footing, a figure found by simulation and held in trust.

sky2.6The Grid Code

Lay d × d data qubits on a square and d² − 1 check qubits between them: 2d² − 1 in all, 17 for d = 3, 49 for d = 5, 97 for d = 7. Each check reads the parity of its four neighbours, or two at an edge. Half the checks read flips, as in sky2.4; half read turnings, by the same reading done after H on each neighbour. A round of checks takes about a microsecond, and a decoder, an ordinary machine, reads which checks changed and finds the likeliest errors. A failure needs a chain of at least (d + 1)/2 errors from one edge to the other.

Grid-code technique: if every gate and reading errs with chance p below a threshold p* near 1%, the grid code of distance d fails per round with chance about C(p/p*)^((d+1)/2); so each step from d to d + 2 divides the failure by Λ = p*/p. Held in trust: it is sky2.5’s count on chains in a square, with constants found by simulation and measured on chips.

d = 7:\ \ 1.43 \times 10^{-3} \text{ per round},\ \ \Lambda = 2.14 @@ measured on a chip in 2024
\text{to } 10^{-6}:\ \ \tfrac{\ln 1430}{\ln 2.14} \approx 9.5 \to d = 27,\ \ 2 \cdot 27^2 - 1 = 1457 @@ ten more steps of 2
\text{to } 10^{-12}:\ \ \approx 27.7 \to d = 63,\ \ 7937 \text{ qubits} @@ for one reliable qubit
\Lambda = 4:\ d = 39,\ 3041;\ \ \Lambda = 10:\ d = 27,\ 1457 @@ the same 10⁻¹², with better qubits
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 2d^2 \text{ qubits for each reliable one}} \quad \blacksquare @@ why ∞1’s estimates run to 10⁵ and 10⁶

So Λ matters more than count: at Λ = 2 one reliable qubit costs thousands of noisy ones, and better qubits shrink d. Codes whose checks reach across the chip, proposed in 2024, aim to need about ten times fewer, at the price of long links that a flat square does not need.

sky2.7What 2024 and 2025 Showed

Measured on a chip of 105 qubits, published in December 2024: grid codes of distance 3, 5 and 7, with Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02; at d = 7, failure of 0.143% per round; the reliable qubit lasted 291 µs against 119 µs for the best of its parts, 2.4 times longer; the decoder kept pace with the chip at d = 5. A repetition code to d = 29 on the same chip met a floor near 10⁻¹⁰ per round, set by rare bursts that strike many qubits at once.

Kept-runs technique: a code that only finds errors, and throws away every run in which one is found, reports the errors of the runs it keeps; that can be far below p, while the share of runs thrown away grows with the length of the computation.

4 \text{ qubits},\ p = 0.003:\ \ \text{thrown away} \approx 4p = 1.2\% @@ one error, found
\text{kept and wrong} \le \tbinom42 p^2 = 5.4 \times 10^{-5} @@ two errors can hide each other
p / (5.4 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 56 @@ “56 times better than one qubit”
\htmlClass{new}{\text{over } 10^6 \text{ rounds, kept with chance } 0.988^{10^6} \approx e^{-12\,000}} \quad \blacksquare @@ almost every run thrown away

Several results of 2023 to 2025, on atoms and ions, reported dozens of protected qubits, or errors hundreds of times below their parts, in part on runs kept this way. They showed real checks working; they did not show a long computation held up by them. The 2024 chip showed what throwing away cannot: errors falling as the code grows, round after round, with nothing discarded. What none had shown by the end of 2025: a reliable qubit at 10⁻⁶ or below, gates between many reliable qubits below threshold through a long run, or decoding at speed for large d.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞2.oq.1Does the noise of real chips allow a threshold at all? The threshold techniques assume errors that are independent, or nearly so. Bursts that strike many qubits at once already set a floor near 10⁻¹⁰ on one chip. A small school of critics argues that noise in large quantum systems is correlated in ways no code can beat. The question is about nature, and only building can settle it.
∞2.oq.2How few noisy qubits can carry one reliable qubit? On a flat square with checks among neighbours, there are proved limits on how good a code can be for its size. Codes with long links beat those limits on paper. Whether the long links can be built well enough to keep the gain is open.
∞2.oq.3Can the decoder keep pace at large distance? Finding the likeliest errors is a matching of changed checks, as on the graphs of rung 42, on a pattern that grows every microsecond. A decoder that falls behind lets the backlog grow without end. Fast decoders exist that are not the best; best decoders exist that are not fast.
∞2.oq.4Do the particles that would make a qubit guarded by its shape exist? A chip built on them was announced in February 2025. The paper published with it said its measurements did not by themselves establish the particles, and many physicists doubt that they have been seen.
∞2.oq.5How cheaply can the one turning the grid code cannot do directly, a turn by an eighth of a full turn, be made reliable? It is made by preparing many noisy copies of a special state and refining them, and that refining dominates the cost of the factoring estimates of ∞1. New ways to grow such states were proposed in 2024, and how far they go is argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In 1995 the strongest argument against quantum computers was this page’s first three footings: errors come in a continuum of small turnings, a state cannot be copied, and reading it destroys it. Several physicists wrote that no machine could therefore run long. That same year a code of nine qubits was found at Bell Labs that mended any one error, and in 1996 codes of seven qubits followed from Oxford and from Bell Labs. The trick was the parity reading: learn the error, never the state. The question changed from whether to how well.

Between 1996 and 1999 several groups, in Jerusalem, at Los Alamos, in Moscow and at Caltech, proved threshold techniques: below some error rate per gate, codes inside codes hold a computation of any length. The first thresholds were tiny, one error in a million gates or less. A code laid on a doughnut of qubits, from Moscow in 1997, and its flat form the next year, raised the figure to about 1% in simulations by the early 2000s, and made the grid code the plan of most builders. A mathematician in Jerusalem has argued since about 2005 that the assumptions about independent noise will fail at scale, and the argument has not been refuted, only tested.

For twenty years the measurements lagged. Superconducting gates passed one error in a hundred around 2014. In 2021 a Google chip ran repetition codes of growing size and found its errors falling, and also found bursts, traced to cosmic rays and stray radiation, that struck the whole chip at once. In 2023 the same group found a grid code of distance 5 slightly better than one of distance 3, 2.914% against 3.028% per round: below threshold, barely. Groups at Harvard with atoms and at Quantinuum and Microsoft with ions announced dozens of protected qubits and errors hundreds of times lower, and critics pointed out how much rested on runs thrown away when an error was found.

In December 2024 Google published its 105-qubit chip with Λ = 2.14, and the field broadly accepted it as the first clear grid code below threshold. Critics noted it was a memory, one reliable qubit sitting still, at 10⁻³ per round, a billion times short of a factoring run. In February 2025 Microsoft announced a chip built on particles that would guard a qubit by their shape; the journal paper beside the announcement said its data did not establish them, and a public presentation in March 2025 left many physicists unconvinced. The same month Amazon showed a chip that guards flips in the physics of each qubit, leaving the code to guard turnings.

Money shapes how each result is told. Companies announce in press releases before papers, share prices move on the word logical, and roadmaps promise hundreds of reliable qubits before 2030. Public funds follow the roadmaps. The ethics is argued both ways: the codes are what would let a machine read today’s secrets (∞1), and the claims that outrun the data spend the trust that public money needs.

What is argued now: whether Λ can be pushed from about 2 to 10, which would shrink every estimate; whether bursts and leaks of qubits out of their two states set a floor that no distance beats; whether atoms, ions, superconducting circuits or photons will carry it; and whether codes with long links, proposed by IBM in 2024, can replace the flat square. In 2025 a Harvard group reported codes on 448 atoms working below threshold; that result, and later claims for ions, have not been revisited on this page.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The swing is Rabi oscillation, Ω the Rabi frequency and Δ the detuning; the turning frame is the rotating frame, and dropping the fast parts is the rotating-wave approximation. A turning error is dephasing, its fading time T₂, and the H, wait, H run is a Ramsey experiment. Flips and turnings are the Pauli errors X and Z, and every one-qubit error is a combination of I, X, Z and XZ. The no-copy technique is the no-cloning theorem. The three-qubit code is the bit-flip code, and the parity readings its syndrome. The grid code is the surface code, its parent the toric code; p* is its threshold and Λ the error suppression factor. Throwing runs away is post-selection, and a code that only finds errors does error detection. Codes with long links are quantum low-density parity-check codes; IBM’s is the bivariate bicycle or gross code. The special states of oq.5 are magic states, refined by magic state distillation, and grown by magic state cultivation (2024). The particles of oq.4 are Majorana zero modes, and the qubits topological qubits.

Who said it first

Isidor Rabi described the swing for nuclear spins in 1937. William Wootters and Wojciech Zurek, and independently Dennis Dieks, proved no-cloning in 1982. Peter Shor gave the nine-qubit code in 1995; Andrew Steane the seven-qubit code in 1996; Robert Calderbank and Shor the general construction the same year. Threshold theorems were proved by Dorit Aharonov and Michael Ben-Or; Emanuel Knill, Raymond Laflamme and Wojciech Zurek; Alexei Kitaev; and John Preskill with others, 1996 to 1998. Kitaev gave the toric code in 1997, Sergey Bravyi and Kitaev the planar form in 1998, and Eric Dennis, Kitaev, Andrew Landahl and Preskill its threshold in 2002. Gil Kalai is the best-known mathematical critic. Google Quantum AI published “Quantum error correction below the surface code threshold” (Nature, online December 2024). Sergey Bravyi and colleagues at IBM published the gross code in 2024. Craig Gidney, Noah Shutty and Cody Jones proposed magic state cultivation in 2024.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each check reads a parity, a reading destroys the state, so the code destroys what it guards.” Not allowed: a reading changes a state only by removing the parts that do not fit it (41·phys.1). Both parts of u|000⟩ + v|111⟩, flipped or not, fit the same parity reading, so nothing is removed, and the reading learns the error without learning u or v.

A threshold, in 3b’s marks

sky2.5’s “as small as wished below the threshold” is 3b2’s □ with a width: necessity inside a stated range of p, nothing promised outside it.
