33j · technique

Potentials by hand

A still plate with nothing inside it is the average of its neighbours. Put something inside, a heater in the plate, a mass in space, a pump at the end of a pipe, and the average is tipped: each point sits a little above or below the average of the points round it, by an amount set by what is there. The rule becomes the sum of second slopes equals the source. This step solves it by hand where the shape is round, so that the round rules of 33g turn it into a rule of one input. The pull of gravity outside a round planet comes out as if the whole planet sat at its centre. Inside, the pull falls in proportion to the distance from the centre, and a stone dropped down a tunnel through a uniform earth would swing back and forth in a fixed time. A single point source in space has a potential that is one over the distance, the kick of space. And water pushed down a pipe moves in a parabola across it, with the flow growing as the fourth power of the pipe’s width.

With sources, the sum of second slopes equals the source. The books on a sphere give a round body’s pull outside as if all its mass were at the centre, and inside a uniform ball the pull grows with distance from the centre. A point source has potential minus one over four pi times the distance. Flow down a pipe is a parabola across it, and the flow goes as the fourth power of the radius.

33j1A round body’s pull

Gravity is a field g pointing toward mass. A measured law, held in trust: a point mass M pulls with strength GM/r² at distance r, and the pulls of many masses add. Write g = −∇φ, so that φ is the field’s potential (28n). The flux of the pull of a point mass inward through a sphere about it is GM/r² × 4πr² = 4πGM, whatever r is. The spread technique (28j) then turns the books on every small box into one rule:

\(\Delta\varphi = 4\pi G\rho,\)

where ρ is the density of mass. Where there is no mass, φ is balanced. The source tips the balance.

Shell-flux technique: if a body’s density depends only on the distance from its centre, its pull at distance r points to the centre with strength GM(r)/r², where M(r) is the mass within r.

g = -g(r)\,\tfrac{x}{r} @@ the body looks the same after any turn about its centre, so its pull points along the reach and depends only on r
\text{flux of } g \text{ inward through the sphere of radius } r = g(r)\cdot 4\pi r^2 @@ g is the same at every point of the sphere, and crosses it straight
\text{flux of } g \text{ inward} = \textstyle\iiint_{\text{ball}} 4\pi G\rho = 4\pi G\,M(r) @@ the spread technique (28j), with −Δφ = −4πGρ the spread of g
\htmlClass{new}{g(r) = \tfrac{G\,M(r)}{r^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by 4πr²

Outside the body, M(r) is the whole mass. So the earth pulls a stone at its surface as a point of mass M at its centre would. With GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² and R = 6371 km, g = GM/R² ≈ 9.82 m/s².

33j2Inside a ball

Inside a ball of even density ρ and radius R, the mass within r is M(r) = M r³/R³.

Inside-pull technique: inside a ball of even density, the pull at distance r from the centre is g_R r/R, where g_R is the pull at the surface. So a stone falling along a straight tunnel through the centre swings like 24h’s steady swing, with period 2π√(R/g_R).

g(r) = \tfrac{G\,M r^3/R^3}{r^2} = \tfrac{GM}{R^2}\cdot\tfrac{r}{R} @@ the shell-flux technique, with M(r) = Mr³/R³
\htmlClass{new}{g(r) = g_R\,\tfrac{r}{R}} @@ g_R = GM/R²
x^{\prime\prime} = -\tfrac{g_R}{R}\,x @@ along the tunnel, x the signed distance from the centre: the pull is toward 0 and grows with |x|
\htmlClass{new}{\text{period } 2\pi\sqrt{R/g_R}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the steady-swing technique (24h), with n² = g_R/R

For the earth taken as even, 2π√(6.371 × 10⁶ / 9.82) ≈ 5060 s, or 84 minutes: 42 minutes to fall from Seattle to the far side. A satellite skimming the ground would take the same 84 minutes for one turn. The real earth is denser in its middle, so its inside pull does not fall off in proportion, and the trip is shorter.

33j3The kick of space

Point-source technique: Φ(x) = −1/(4π|x|) is balanced everywhere in space but 0, and the flux of ∇Φ out of every sphere about 0 is 1. So Φ is the potential of one unit of source at one point: the kick of 33f3, now in space.

r\,\Phi = -\tfrac{1}{4\pi} @@ Φ depends only on r
\Delta\Phi = \tfrac1r\big(r\Phi\big)^{\prime\prime} = 0 \text{ for } r > 0 @@ the round-ball technique (33g4): a constant has second slope 0
\nabla\Phi = \tfrac{1}{4\pi r^2}\,\tfrac{x}{r} @@ the slope of −1/(4πr) along r is 1/(4πr²), pointing outward
\htmlClass{new}{\text{flux out of the sphere of radius } r = \tfrac{1}{4\pi r^2}\cdot 4\pi r^2 = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for every r

Kicks add, as on 33f3. So a spread of sources ρ, each piece a kick weighted by its size, has the potential −∫ ρ(y) / (4π|x − y|) dy, and that potential obeys Δφ = ρ. For gravity, multiply by 4πG. The pull of a mountain, an ocean, or a whole galaxy is a total of kicks. That the total has the right second slopes is held in trust here: the total passes every bound near each point of y, and taking slopes under it needs more care than 30d gives.

33j4Water down a pipe

Water is pushed along a straight round pipe of radius R by a pressure that falls by G per metre. A measured law, held in trust: in slow, steady flow, the water’s speed w along the pipe obeys Δw = −G/μ across the pipe, where μ is the water’s stickiness, and w = 0 at the wall. The flow depends only on the distance r from the pipe’s middle.

Pipe-flow technique: the speed across the pipe is w = G(R² − r²)/(4μ), a parabola, and the water passing each second is Q = πGR⁴/(8μ).

w^{\prime\prime} + \tfrac1r w^{\prime} = \tfrac1r\big(r\,w^{\prime}\big)^{\prime} = -\tfrac{G}{\mu} @@ the round-rate technique (33g1) with no turn; the product rule backward
r\,w^{\prime} = -\tfrac{G r^2}{2\mu} @@ same slopes (14e); the constant is 0, since rw′ is 0 at r = 0
w = \tfrac{G}{4\mu}\big(R^2 - r^2\big) @@ same slopes again, with w(R) = 0 at the wall
\htmlClass{new}{Q = \textstyle\int_0^R w\cdot 2\pi r\,dr = \tfrac{\pi G R^4}{8\mu}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the water through each thin ring, totalled (21o)

Fastest in the middle, still at the wall. One example first. A drip line 4 mm across, water at 20 °C with μ = 1.0 × 10⁻³ Pa s, and a pressure drop of 500 Pa a metre, carries π × 500 × (0.002)⁴ / (8 × 0.001) ≈ 3.1 millilitres a second, 0.5 m/s in the middle. Halve the width and the flow drops sixteen times. A narrowed artery that keeps half its width passes a sixteenth of the blood at the same push.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Δφ = ρ is Poisson’s equation, and Δφ = 4πGρ its form for gravity; φ is the gravitational potential. The shell-flux technique is Gauss’s law for gravity, with Newton’s shell theorem as its outside half. The tunnel is the gravity train. Φ = −1/(4π|x|) is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian in three dimensions, and the total of kicks is the Newtonian potential. The pipe-flow technique is Poiseuille’s law (Hagen–Poiseuille flow), and μ is the dynamic viscosity.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton proved in the Principia (1687) that a round body pulls outside as if its mass were at the centre, by adding up thin shells. Siméon Denis Poisson added the source term to Laplace’s rule in 1813. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the flux form in 1813 and 1840. Robert Hooke wrote to Newton about a stone falling through the earth in 1679. Gotthilf Hagen (1839) and Jean Léonard Marie Poiseuille (1840) measured flow in narrow tubes, the second to understand blood. George Stokes derived the parabola from the rule of a sticky fluid in 1845.
