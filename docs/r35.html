Infinities Come in Sizes

Rung 3 laid down an axiom so that the endless run of counting could be one finished set, and rung 4 counted finite sets by matching them with numbers. This rung asks what matching says about endless sets. Two sets are the same size when they can be matched, member for member, whatever else is true of them. With that one definition, the counting numbers, the even numbers, the pairs of counting numbers, the fractions and the roots of every polynomial with whole coefficients all turn out to have one size: each can be listed. Then the line cannot be. Whatever list of reals is written, a real built to differ from each entry in one digit is missing from it. So there are reals that are roots of no such polynomial, and most reals have no name at all. The same move shows that every set is smaller than the set of its parts, so sizes climb for ever and there is no largest. Two one-way matchings make a matching, so sizes are ordered; sizes add and multiply in a strange arithmetic; and the line has exactly the size of the parts of the counting numbers. Last comes a question the rung can ask and cannot answer: is there a size between the counting numbers and the line? It is answered yes in one world where every axiom is true and no in another.

Matching is size. The fractions and the algebraic reals can be listed and the line cannot; every set is smaller than its parts, so there is no largest size; two one-way matchings make a matching; and whether a size lies between the counting numbers and the line is left open by the axioms.

What it means to say it

To say that infinities come in sizes is to say that matching, the one technique rung 4 counted by, keeps working past the finite and gives different answers for different endless sets. Two sets have the same size when a matching joins them. One is smaller when it goes one-to-one into the other and no matching joins them. Nothing about order, length or nearness enters.

Two sets are the same size when they can be matched; the counting numbers cannot be matched with the line, and every set is smaller than its set of parts. The rung proves it, and finds where the axioms stop.

Where it comes from

In 1638 it was noticed that the squares can be paired with the numbers, and the conclusion drawn was that endless sets cannot be compared. In 1851 the pairing was taken seriously, and in 1888 it became the definition of the endless. In 1874 the algebraic numbers were listed and the line was shown to have no list, in one short paper; in 1877 the line was matched with the plane, in 1878 the continuum question was asked, and in 1891 came the diagonal and the result on parts. The work was fiercely opposed and as fiercely defended. In 1938 the continuum question was shown not to be refutable, and in 1963 not to be provable. Curves that fill a square were drawn in 1890 and 1891, and in 1918 sets were given dimensions that are not whole numbers. The names are in the cellars of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves five things, and states a sixth.

First, a set is countable when it can be listed. The pairs of counting numbers, the fractions and the algebraic reals are countable, and each is matched with the counting numbers.

Second, the line cannot be listed: every list of reals misses the diagonal real. So transcendental reals exist, and since names can be listed, most reals have none.

Third, no function from a set reaches every one of its parts, so every set is smaller than its set of parts, and there is no largest size and no set of everything.

Fourth, two one-way matchings make a matching, with nothing picked; and with choice, any two sets compare.

Fifth, sizes add, multiply and raise to powers by matching; the counting numbers’ size plus itself and times itself is itself; the line has the size of the parts of the counting numbers, and so does the plane.

Sixth, stated with its proofs beyond the rung: whether some set of reals lies between the counting numbers and the line in size is true in one world where every axiom is true and false in another, so the axioms do not decide it.

Anexact, yet rigorous. One box on this rung, and one in its annexes. 35h carries two sizes of truth: the dust has no length and as many points as the line, so length and size are two claims about one set, each exact, and neither gives the other. 35·phys carries laws with a known exception rate: the wave law says exactly how often an electron lands in each place, and nothing about where the next one lands.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 35g lists a set by a height, or finds a copy of the endless strings of zeros and ones inside it, and sorts sets by those two moves. 35h builds a dust with no length and the size of the line, and measures how much room such sets take by a dimension that need not be a whole number. 35i draws a curve, continuous and unbroken, that passes through every point of a square, and shows that it must pass some point twice. The annexes take the rung’s neighbours in the sciences further: whether an electron is a dot or a flow, how molecules turn and shake in the light they take in, and how many points there are on a line, by the strongest axioms proposed so far.

What you can do because you know it

You can decide whether a set can be listed, and prove it either way. You can compare two endless sets, build the matching that two one-way maps promise, and do the arithmetic of sizes. You can say why most reals have no name, why the parts of any set outrun it, and why a curve can fill a square without being one-to-one.

And you can say exactly where the axioms stop: at the size of the line.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 6’s choice twice, to compare any two sizes and to pick lists for countably many sets, and says so where it does. It spends rung 8’s line without holes, rung 9’s functions and matchings, rung 29’s points of the run and its Replace, and rung 34’s same-shape techniques. Two things are held in trust on 35f: the existence of the first point of the run that cannot be listed, with the size of its parts, and the two results of 1938 and 1963, named and not proved. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say infinities come in sizes, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
