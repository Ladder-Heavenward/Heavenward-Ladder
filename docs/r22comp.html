22·comp · computing

Does forgetting cost energy?

Does forgetting cost energy? A computer gets warm. Some of that heat comes from wires that are not perfect, and better wires could cut it. But is there a part that no engineering can remove? A memory that is wiped clean has lost something. Before, it could have held either of two things; after, it holds one. Physicists had long tied heat to the number of ways the molecules of a body can sit. What makes it hard is that a bit is information and heat is energy, and for a long time nobody could see why one should cost the other. This page shows, with counting from rung 4, logs from rung 17 and 22k’s size of the factorials, how to count the ways a thing can sit in bits, why a gas that fills its room is a matter of counting, and why wiping a bit sends a least amount of heat into the room. Then it looks back at the gates of 4·comp, and finds which of them forget.

The log of the number of ways a thing can sit adds when two things sit side by side, so it can be counted in bits, and a measured law ties heat to the same count. Choosing a share of many things can be done in a number of ways whose log is the count of things times a fixed fraction, by the size of the factorials. Squeezing a gas into half its room at a steady temperature turns a fixed amount of work into heat, and that heat is exactly what the lost ways are worth. A memory wiped clean loses ways in the same manner, so it must send heat into the room. A gate that sends two different inputs to the same output forgets, and pays. A program’s steps are counted by what it repeats, and halving a sorted list finds a name in about log₂ n of them.

22comp1Counting ways in bits

17·crypt counted a password’s choices in bits. Physics counts the ways the molecules of a body can sit. If one thing can sit in W₁ ways and another in W₂, the two side by side can sit in W₁W₂ ways. A count that multiplies is awkward. Its log adds.

W = W_1 W_2 @@ stages-multiply (4k): each way of the first with each way of the second
\ln W = \ln W_1 + \ln W_2 @@ log-of-a-product (17c)
\text{bits} = \log_2 W = \tfrac{\ln W}{\ln 2} @@ change of base (17h); n switches have W = 2ⁿ, so n bits
\htmlClass{new}{S = k\ln W = (k\ln 2)\cdot \text{bits}} @@ the measured law; k ln 2 ≈ 9.57 × 10⁻²⁴ J/K for each bit

A measured law ties the count to heat: a body that can sit in W equally likely ways has S = k ln W, with k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K, 17·phys’s fixed number. Its accepted name is the body’s entropy. Ways-to-bits technique: the log of the count of ways adds when things sit side by side, so the ways can be counted in bits, and each bit is worth k ln 2 of entropy. A byte has 256 ways, which is 8 bits.

22comp2The size of n! as the bridge

N switches with exactly k of them on can sit in N choose k ways (4k). Those factorials are far too large to write out. 22k gives their size: ln n! = n ln n − n + rₙ, where rₙ lies between 0 and ln n + 1. Write p = k/N for the share that is on.

\ln\binom{N}{k} = \ln N! - \ln k! - \ln(N - k)! @@ choosing-counts-parts (4k)
= N\ln N - k\ln k - (N - k)\ln(N - k) + r @@ the size of n! (22k); the −n parts cancel; |r| ≤ 2(ln N + 1)
= -k\ln\tfrac{k}{N} - (N - k)\ln\tfrac{N - k}{N} + r @@ N ln N = k ln N + (N − k) ln N
\htmlClass{new}{\log_2\binom{N}{k} = N\,h(p) + \tfrac{r}{\ln 2},\ \ h(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1 - p)\log_2(1 - p)} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by ln 2 (17h)

Share-count technique: a share p of N things can be chosen in about ways, and the most ways sit at the half, where h is 1. The error r is no bigger than a few times ln N, so for large N it hardly counts. For N = 1000 and k = 100 the exact count is 464.4 bits, and N h(0.1) is 469.0.

The count says how far a file can be squeezed. A file of a million bits, one in ten of them a 1, is one of about 2 to the 469 000 such files. There are fewer than 2ᵐ files shorter than m bits, so a squeeze that gives each such file its own shorter file needs about 469 000 bits. The file can be squeezed to about 47% of its length, and no further.

22comp3A gas that fills its room

N molecules of gas sit in a box at temperature T. The gas law of 15·chem, written for molecules, is pV = NkT. Squeeze it slowly into half its room, letting heat leave so the temperature stays fixed. A measured law, the second law of heat, says two things. Heat Q passed into a room at temperature T raises the room’s entropy by Q/T. And in anything that happens, the total entropy of a body and its room does not fall.

\text{work} = \int_{V/2}^{V}\tfrac{NkT}{v}\,dv = NkT\big(\ln V - \ln\tfrac V2\big) = NkT\ln 2 @@ work is the area under the pressure (15·chem); ln as an area (17a)
\text{heat out} = NkT\ln 2 @@ at a fixed temperature the gas keeps its energy, so the work leaves as heat
\text{the room gains } \tfrac{NkT\ln 2}{T} = Nk\ln 2 @@ the second law
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the gas loses } k\ln 2^N = Nk\ln 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ each molecule’s choice of half is gone: W falls by 2ᴺ

Halved-room technique: halving a gas’s room at a fixed temperature turns NkT ln 2 of work into heat, and that heat over T is exactly the entropy the gas lost. The squeeze lost N bits of ways, and the room took them as heat. A litre of air at 300 K and one atmosphere has pV ≈ 101.3 J, so halving it gives off about 70.2 J. It holds about 2.45 × 10²² molecules, so that is 2.45 × 10²² bits.

Anexact, yet rigorous · counting, by the trillion trillion

4k set beside counting a principle: random things in a crowd make smooth, steady patterns. One molecule can be anywhere in its box. A litre of air spreads evenly, and nothing pushes it to. Of the 2ᴺ ways to place N molecules in two halves, nearly all are close to even.

One molecule can be anywhere; a crowd of them sits where the ways are most, and the ways are the law.

The second law is that counting, done for you. It does not say the gas cannot crowd into one half. It says how rarely: for 100 molecules, all in the left half at one glance has chance 2⁻¹⁰⁰, about 8 × 10⁻³¹. For a litre of air the chance, written as a decimal, starts with about 7 × 10²¹ zeros.

A box holds 50 molecules. What share of the ways to place them in two halves puts exactly 25 in each? Is an even split the rule, or only the middle of a pile?

22comp4Wiping a bit

Build a memory out of one molecule in a box, with a wall across the middle. The molecule on the left is 0; on the right, 1. To wipe the memory is to set it to 0, whatever it held, without looking.

\text{take out the wall; push a piston from the right end to the middle} @@ whatever the bit was, the molecule is now on the left
\text{work} = kT\ln 2 @@ the halved-room technique with N = 1
\text{two ways become one: the memory loses } k\ln 2 @@ W falls from 2 to 1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{heat into the room} \ge kT\ln 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second law: the room must gain at least k ln 2

Erasure technique: wiping a bit clean, whatever it held, sends at least kT ln 2 of heat into the room. The molecule is only a picture. Any memory whose two ways become one loses k ln 2, and the second law sends the bill to the room. At 300 K that is about 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J a bit, so one watt could wipe at most about 3.5 × 10²⁰ bits a second. Today’s chips spend thousands of times more than that on each bit.

22comp5Gates that forget

A gate of 4·comp takes two bits in and gives one out. Feed it inputs whose four rows are equally likely. When an output comes from m rows, the gate has forgotten which of the m it was: log₂ m bits. On average that is the expected value of log₂ m.

\text{AND}:\ 1 \text{ from } 1 \text{ row},\ 0 \text{ from } 3:\ \ \tfrac14\log_2 1 + \tfrac34\log_2 3 \approx 1.19 \text{ bits} @@ the table (4·comp); expected value (5·stat)
\text{XOR}:\ \text{each output from } 2 \text{ rows}:\ \ \log_2 2 = 1 \text{ bit} @@ it keeps whether they differ, and forgets which
\text{half adder}:\ (S, C) = (1, 0) \text{ from } 2 \text{ rows, the others from } 1:\ \ \tfrac12 \text{ bit} @@ it forgets which input was the 1
\text{NOT}:\ \text{one row each}:\ 0 \text{ bits} @@ it can be run backwards
\htmlClass{new}{\text{heat} \ge (\text{bits forgotten})\cdot kT\ln 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the erasure technique, for each bit forgotten

Gate-loss technique: a gate that sends two input rows to one output forgets, and pays at least kT ln 2 of heat for each bit it forgets. A gate whose rows can be run backwards forgets nothing. The gate (A, B, C) ↦ (A, B, C ⊕ A · B) sends its 8 rows to 8 different outputs, so it forgets nothing. With C held at 0, its third wire gives A · B, and A and B ride along beside it.

22comp7Counting a program’s steps

Every gate use above can cost heat, so how many uses does a job take? Count a program’s steps by what it repeats. Two sign-up sheets hold n names each: which names are on both? Program one takes each name of the first sheet and compares it with every name of the second: n comparisons for each of n names, n² in all. Program two first sorts the second sheet by merge sort, in fewer than n log₂ n + n comparisons by the halvings technique (22l3). Then it finds each name of the first sheet by halving, as 12e halved for a root: compare with the middle name of what is left, and keep the half that could hold it.

Halve-to-nothing technique: a halving search of a sorted list of n items makes at most ⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1 comparisons; so program two makes fewer than 2n log₂ n + 2n, against program one’s n².

H(m) \le 1 + H(\lfloor m/2 \rfloor),\ \allowbreak \ H(0) = 0 @@ one comparison with the middle leaves at most ⌊m/2⌋ items
2^k \le m \lt 2^{k+1} \Rightarrow 2^{k-1} \le \lfloor m/2 \rfloor \lt 2^k \text{ for } k \ge 1 @@ halving lowers k by 1
H(m) \le k + 1 = \lfloor \log_2 m \rfloor + 1 @@ induction on k (4c), from H(1) ≤ 1 + H(0) = 1
\text{program }\allowbreak \text{two} \lt (n\log_2 n + n) + n(\log_2 n + 1) = 2n\log_2 n + 2n @@ one sort, then n searches
\htmlClass{new}{n = 10^6:\ \ 10^{12} \text{ against }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{fewer }}\allowbreak \htmlClass{new}{ \text{than } 4.2 \times 10^7} \quad \blacksquare @@ the ranking technique (22k2): n log n over n² heads to 0

At a billion comparisons a second, program one takes 1000 s, about 17 minutes, and program two 0.04 s. Both counts grow like a power of n, or less, and such jobs are counted as reasonable. A program that tried every way of picking some of the names would take 2ⁿ steps, and at n = 60, at the same pace, that is 36 years; 22·logi meets the same wall in routes. The count, not the machine, decides which jobs can be done. And if each step wipes even one bit, the count times kT ln 2 is a floor on the heat (22·comp.4). The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

S = k ln W is Boltzmann’s entropy formula, and W the number of microstates. The second law of heat is the second law of thermodynamics, and Q/T the Clausius entropy. h(p) is the binary entropy function, and the bits of a choice are its information entropy, or Shannon entropy; the share-count technique bounds lossless compression. The slow squeeze at fixed temperature is an isothermal compression. The erasure technique is Landauer’s principle, the one-molecule memory is the Szilard engine, and a gate whose rows can be run backwards is a reversible gate; the one on this page is the Toffoli gate, or controlled-controlled-not. Counting a program’s steps as n grows is finding its running time, or time complexity, and n² and n log n are orders of growth; program one is a nested loop, and halving search is binary search. Counts that grow like a power of n are polynomial time, called reasonable, and 2ⁿ is exponential time.

Who said it first

Rudolf Clausius named entropy in 1865, from heat over temperature. Ludwig Boltzmann tied it to counting the ways in the 1870s; Max Planck wrote it as S = k log W in 1900, and the formula is carved on Boltzmann’s grave in Vienna. Claude Shannon (1948) counted information in bits with the same h. Leo Szilard (1929) built the one-molecule engine on paper. Rolf Landauer, at IBM, argued in 1961 that wiping, not computing, is what must cost heat, and Charles Bennett (1973) showed that any computation can be done by gates that forget nothing. Tommaso Toffoli gave his gate in 1980. In 2012 a team in Lyon wiped a one-bit memory made of a glass bead held by laser light, and measured heat approaching kT ln 2. John Mauchly described binary search in 1946, and Derrick Lehmer gave a form for lists of every length in 1960. Donald Knuth set out the counting of a program’s steps in The Art of Computer Programming, from 1968.

In 22·comp.8.5, the small being is Maxwell’s demon (James Clerk Maxwell, 1867 in a letter, 1871 in print). Szilard (1929) and Léon Brillouin (1951) placed its cost in looking at the molecules. Bennett (1982) placed it in wiping the demon’s memory, after Landauer. John Earman and John Norton (1998 and 1999) argued that the wiping answer assumes the second law it is meant to save.
