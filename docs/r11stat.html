Which Came First, the Exposure or the Illness?

Which came first, the exposure or the illness? To learn whether a workplace solvent raises the risk of a rare illness, researchers can follow a large group of workers forward for years and count who falls ill. Or they can start from the people who already have the illness, find well people to compare, and look back at who worked with the solvent. The second way is far quicker and cheaper, but it cannot count risks. It can count odds. This page finds the risk ratio from a group followed forward, the odds ratio from a group started from the sick, shows with 11’s continuity that for a rare illness the two close in on each other, and lists what neither ratio can say.

Following forward, the risk ratio is the share of exposed who fall ill over the share of unexposed who do. Starting from the sick, compare the odds of exposure among the ill with the odds among the well. For a rare illness the odds ratio is close to the risk ratio, because odds and risks of a rare event are nearly the same.

11stat1Following a Group Forward: the Risk Ratio

A company has 20,000 workers: 10,000 handle a solvent and 10,000 never touch it. Researchers follow all of them for ten years and count who develops a rare liver illness.

handled the solvent309,97010,000

From here on, on this page, the risk of falling ill is the share of a group who fall ill, and the risk ratio is the risk in the exposed over the risk in the unexposed.

\text{risk, exposed: } \tfrac{30}{10\,000} = 0.003 @@ 
\text{risk, unexposed: } \tfrac{3}{10\,000} = 0.0003 @@

11stat2Starting From the Sick

Following 20,000 people for ten years to find 33 cases is slow and costly. Instead, find the 33 people who have the illness, pick 66 people from the same workforce who don’t, and ask each about the solvent.

handled the solventnever touched ittotal
ill (all of them)30333
well (a sample)333366

Now the researchers chose how many ill and how many well to study: 33 and 66. So the share of ill in this table, a third, is their choice, not a risk. Dividing down the solvent column, 30 of 63, gives a meaningless number. Risks cannot be read from this table.

11stat3Odds, Not Risks

What this table can give is the odds of exposure: exposed over unexposed, within each row. A row’s odds don’t depend on how many people were chosen for that row, only on the mix inside it.

\text{odds of exposure, ill: } \tfrac{30}{3} = 10 @@ within the ill row
\text{odds of exposure, well: } \tfrac{33}{33} = 1 @@ within the well row
\htmlClass{new}{\text{odds ratio} = \tfrac{10}{1} = 10} @@ the same as the risk ratio here

Odds-ratio technique: starting from the sick and a sample of the well, the odds of exposure among the ill over the odds among the well gives the odds ratio, which does not depend on how many of each were chosen. The odds ratio turns out to be the same read either way: the odds of illness among the exposed over the odds among the unexposed.

11stat4For a Rare Illness, the Odds Ratio Closes In on the Risk Ratio

Go back to the forward count. The odds of falling ill are ill over well: 30 to 9,970 among the exposed, 3 to 9,997 among the rest. When so few fall ill, “over well” is nearly “over everyone”.

\text{odds} = \dfrac{p}{1 - p},\ \ p \text{ the risk} @@ ill over well
\text{odds ratio} = \dfrac{p_1/(1 - p_1)}{p_0/(1 - p_0)} = \text{risk ratio} \cdot \dfrac{1 - p_0}{1 - p_1} @@ 
\dfrac{1 - p_0}{1 - p_1} \text{ is continuous in } p_0, p_1 \text{ and equals } 1 \text{ at } 0, 0 @@ quotients of continuous functions (11b)
\htmlClass{new}{p_0, p_1 \to 0:\ \ \text{odds ratio} \to \text{risk ratio}} @@ where the factor is headed is where it is (11)

Here the factor is 0.9997/0.997 ≈ 1.003: the odds ratio is 10.03 against a risk ratio of 10. For an illness rare in both groups, the odds ratio from a study of the sick is close to the risk ratio a ten-year study would have found. For a common illness the two drift apart, and the odds ratio exaggerates.

11stat5What a Ratio Cannot Say

An odds ratio of 10 says the illness goes with the solvent. It does not by itself say the solvent causes it.

what could also make the ratio bigexample
something that goes with bothsolvent handlers also work night shifts, and the night shift is what matters
the ill remember differentlypeople who are ill search their memory harder for what they handled
the well sample is not like the workforcewell people picked from the office, where nobody handles solvent

A ratio measures how strongly exposure and illness go together in the people studied; a cause needs more: that exposure came first, that the link survives accounting for other differences, that more exposure gives more illness, and that it holds in other studies. A study starting from the sick also cannot give the risk itself, only the ratio. An epidemiologist reading such a study checks how the well sample was chosen before believing the ratio.

Accepted names

Following a group forward is a cohort study; starting from the sick is a case-control study, with cases and controls. The odds ratio closing in on the risk ratio is the rare disease assumption. Something that goes with both exposure and illness is a confounder, and the ill remembering differently is recall bias.

Who said it first

Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill used a study starting from the sick to link smoking with lung cancer in 1950, and followed British doctors forward from 1951. Jerome Cornfield showed in 1951 that such a study’s odds ratio approximates the risk ratio for a rare illness.
