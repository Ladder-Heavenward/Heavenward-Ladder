13l · technique

Implicit slopes and the inverse dodge

Some sets of pairs are given by an equation between the across and up entries, not by a formula for the up entry. The pairs whose up entry squared is the across entry are not a function: at 4 they hold both 2 and negative 2. Yet near the pair 4 across, 2 up, they agree with a function, the square root, and that function has a slope. This step names such a function a branch, and takes its slope without writing it out. Take slopes of both sides of the equation, let the chain rule handle every up entry, and solve. Then the inverse dodge: when a function is easier to run backwards, take the backward function's slope and turn it upside down. 13e's undoing's-slope said what the two slopes multiply to if both exist; this step proves that the backward one exists.

Give a name to a branch’s outputs. Take slopes of both sides of the equation, each output the inside of a chain. Solve for the slope. To run a function backwards, take its slope at the matching point and turn it upside down.

13l1A set of pairs that is not a function

Stands on 5g (solution sets of pairs), 8g (unknowns in ℝ) and 9a (functions).

5g wrote the solution set of conditions on two unknowns as a set of pairs, and 8g let unknowns range over ℝ. So the solution set of the equation y · y = x is {(x, y) ∈ ℝ × ℝ : y · y = x}, a part of 9e's plane. It holds (4, 2) and (4, −2), so its column at 4 holds two pairs, and by 9e's column-test it is not a function of x. Near (4, 2), though, every pair of it has y = √x, and near (4, −2) every pair has y = −√x. Those are functions, and they have slopes.

From here on, take an equation in x and y, and a pair (x₀, y₀) in its solution set. A branch of the solution set through (x₀, y₀) is a function φ defined on an open interval around x₀, with φ(x₀) = y₀, whose every pair (x, φ(x)) is in the solution set. When φ has a slope at x₀, that slope is called a slope of the solution set at (x₀, y₀). Such a φ is called an implicit function, given by the equation rather than by a formula. Finding its slope from the equation is called implicit differentiation.

A reuse, said out loud. φ has been 2d's name for any condition, a statement with a gap, and 13e kept it away from its helper for that reason. In this step, and only here, φ names a branch: a function. The step always calls it a branch, so the two jobs never meet; elsewhere 2d's job stands.

For y · y = x: √, on (0, ∞), is a branch through (4, 2), and −√ is a branch through (4, −2). Through (0, 0) there is no branch at all. A function on an open interval around 0 would need an input x < 0 with φ(x) · φ(x) = x, and a square is never negative (square-never-negative).

13l2Slopes of both sides

Stands on 13l1, 13c (product for slopes) and 13e (chain for slopes).

Here is the licence, said in words first. On a branch, write y for φ(x). Then the equation, with φ(x) in y's place, is true at every input of an open interval: the two sides are two functions of x that agree there. By 13a's slopes-decided-near, functions that agree on an open interval around x₀ have the same slope at x₀, when one of them has a slope. So take the slope of each side; wherever y appears, it is the inside of a chain, and chain for slopes multiplies by y′.

y \cdot y = x @@ with y = φ(x) on a branch: true at every x of an open interval around x₀
(y \cdot y)' = (x)' @@ slopes-decided-near: the two sides agree on an open interval, so their slopes agree
\htmlClass{new}{2y \cdot y'} = 1 @@ chain for slopes with the inside r = y = φ(x), outside r²: 2r · r′. slope-of-a-line on the right
y' = \dfrac{1}{2y} \quad (y \ne 0) @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by (2y)⁻¹
\text{at } (4, 2):\ \ y' = \htmlClass{keep}{\tfrac14};\quad \text{at } (4, -2):\ \ y' = \htmlClass{keep}{-\tfrac14} @@ substitute the pair

Check the long way: on the branch √, slope-of-√ gives 1/(2√4) = ¼. The formula 1/(2y) did more than the long way: it gave the other branch's slope too, −¼, without your writing −√x. And it spoke only where it should: at (0, 0) its bottom is 0, and there is no branch there.

There is one caution, and it is 13i's. The column took the slope of y · y = φ(x)², which needs φ to have a slope first. For y · y = x the branches are ±√, and slope-of-√ supplies it. Taking slopes of both sides recomputes a slope known to exist; it never proves that one exists. In this step every branch is built from functions whose slopes the ladder has already proved. When a branch cannot be written with them, that it has a slope at all needs a technique the ladder has not built, so this step stays with branches it can write.

13l3Products, and an equation with cubes

Stands on 13l2 and 13h (root slopes).

x \cdot y = 1 @@ the solution set is the graph of 1/x on x ≠ 0: one branch through each pair, with a slope (slope-of-one-over-x)
1 \cdot y + x \cdot y' = 0 @@ product for slopes on the left: the slope of x is 1. constant-has-slope-0 on the right
y' = \htmlClass{new}{-\dfrac{y}{x}} \quad (x \ne 0) @@ adding-keeps-answers, then multiplying-keeps-answers by x⁻¹
\text{at } (2, \tfrac12):\ \ y' = -\tfrac14 @@ slope-of-one-over-x agrees: −1/2² = −¼

Now x³ + y³ = 9. Since cubing keeps order on all of ℝ (9e's odd-powers-keep-order), each x has exactly one y: φ(x) = ∛(9 − x³). It uses 8i's odd roots for the inputs past ∛9, where 9 − x³ < 0. So the solution set is a function after all, one branch everywhere, though writing it is less pleasant than not writing it. Its pairs include (1, 2) and (2, 1): 1 + 8 = 9 and 8 + 1 = 9.

x^3 + y^3 = 9 @@ y = φ(x) = ∛(9 − x³): a slope wherever 9 − x³ ≠ 0, by chain for slopes with 13h's root slopes
3x^2 + \htmlClass{new}{3y^2 \cdot y'} = 0 @@ slopes of both sides: power for slopes; chain for slopes with the inside y
y' = \htmlClass{new}{-\dfrac{x^2}{y^2}} \quad (y \ne 0) @@ adding-keeps-answers, multiplying-keeps-answers
\text{at } (1, 2):\ \ y' = -\tfrac14;\quad \text{at } (2, 1):\ \ y' = -4 @@ substitute each pair

Where y = 0, at x = ∛9, the formula's bottom is 0, and the formula is right to stop: φ has no slope there. Suppose it had one. Then slopes of both sides would give 3x² + 3 · 0 · φ′ = 0 at x = ∛9, so 3x² = 0, and x = ∛9 is not 0. Refuted, as 13e's undoing's-slope refuted a slope for ∛ at 0.

Look at the two slopes, −¼ and −4. The equation x³ + y³ = 9 does not change when x and y swap places. So its solution set is the same set as its swapped pairs (9d's swapped-pairs): φ undoes itself, φ(φ(x)) = x. And the two slopes, at the pair (1, 2) and at its swapped pair (2, 1), multiply to 1. The inverse dodge below says why.

Rung 18 takes slopes of both sides in the same way, for equations whose sets of pairs are its shapes.

13l5The inverse dodge

Stands on 13l4, 13e (undoing’s-slope) and 12 (in-between-value).

One popular calculus book gives this advice. When a function is easier to take the slope of when run backwards, you may do so. The reciprocal of the backward slope gives the forward one.

13e's undoing's-slope proved half of this: if f and its undoing g both have slopes, at matching inputs, then g′(y₀) · f′(x₀) = 1. It assumed g had a slope, and said that when it has one is a harder question. Here is the answer. It needs a word for functions that never turn back.

From here on, f keeps order on a set A of its inputs when x < x′ ⇒ f(x) < f(x′) for all x, x′ ∈ A. It turns order about on A when x < x′ ⇒ f(x′) < f(x). Keeping order is called increasing, and turning it about decreasing. A function that keeps or turns order about on A is one-to-one there, by three-way-order on ℝ. 8f's powers-keep-order and 9e's odd-powers-keep-order said that powers keep order: on [0, ∞), and, for odd powers, on all of ℝ. This line names what they said. How the sign of a slope bears on keeping order across a whole interval is rung 14's question.

Inverse-is-continuous technique: let f be continuous on an open interval I and keep order there, x₀ ∈ I and y₀ = f(x₀). Then the outputs of f on I include an open interval around y₀, and the function g that runs f kept to I backwards (9d) is continuous at y₀.

\text{let } \varepsilon \gt 0 \text{ with } [x_0 - \varepsilon, x_0 + \varepsilon] \subseteq I @@ I is open around x₀: some reach r; any ε < r will do (a smaller ε only makes the promise stronger)
f(x_0 - \varepsilon) \lt y_0 \lt f(x_0 + \varepsilon) @@ f keeps order
\delta = \min\big(y_0 - f(x_0 - \varepsilon),\ f(x_0 + \varepsilon) - y_0\big) \gt 0 @@ 8b's min; lesser-below-both
|y - y_0| \lt \delta \;\Rightarrow\; f(x_0 - \varepsilon) \lt y \lt f(x_0 + \varepsilon) @@ unpack-the-bars
\text{so } y = f(x) \text{ for some } x \text{ with } |x - x_0| \lt \varepsilon @@ in-between-value, general form (12a) on [x₀ − ε, x₀ + ε], where f is continuous: y is an output
g(y) = x,\ \ |g(y) - g(y_0)| \lt \htmlClass{new}{\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ f is one-to-one on I, so g(y) is that x (there-and-back); g(y₀) = x₀. continuity-unpacked (11a)

13l6The inverse’s slope

Stands on 13l5 and 13e (the helper).

Inverse-slope technique: let f be continuous on an open interval I, keep order there or turn it about, and have a slope f′(x₀) ≠ 0 at x₀ ∈ I. Then g, which runs f kept to I backwards, has a slope at y₀ = f(x₀), and g′(y₀) = 1/f′(x₀). This is the rule for the derivative of an inverse function.

g \text{ is defined on an open interval around } y_0 @@ inverse-is-continuous
0 \lt |k| \lt \delta:\ \ H = g(y_0 + k) - x_0,\ \ H \ne 0 @@ g is one-to-one (9d's inverse-is-a-bijection), and y₀ + k ≠ y₀
f(x_0 + H) = y_0 + k @@ there-and-back: f(g(y₀ + k)) = y₀ + k; substitute for the expression g(y₀ + k) = x₀ + H
k = f(x_0 + H) - f(x_0) = \htmlClass{new}{w(H) \cdot H} @@ helper (ii) (13e): w is f's helper at x₀. Nothing is divided by H
w(H) \ne 0 @@ k ≠ 0, so neither factor is 0 (anything-times-zero)
\dfrac{g(y_0 + k) - g(y_0)}{k} = \dfrac{H}{w(H) \cdot H} = \htmlClass{new}{\dfrac{1}{w(H)}} @@ the chord slope of g at y₀; cancel H (fractions-of-rationals i)
H \to 0 \text{ as } k \to 0 @@ inverse-is-continuous: g is continuous at y₀, and g(y₀) = x₀
w(H) \to w(0) = f'(x_0) @@ limit-passes-inside (11c), with helper (i): w is continuous at 0
g'(y_0) = \htmlClass{keep}{\dfrac{1}{f'(x_0)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ reciprocal-limit (10c): f′(x₀) ≠ 0

In d-marks: dx/dy · dy/dx = 1, with dx/dy taken at y₀ and dy/dx at x₀. 13e wrote the same line under the hypothesis that dx/dy exists; inverse-slope removes the hypothesis. And it is the swapped pairs at work (9d's swapped-pairs). A change h of input with change Δy of output on f is a change Δy of input with change h of output on g. So every chord slope of g is the reciprocal of one of f's, at the swapped pair.

Where it must fail: x³ keeps order on ℝ and has slope 0 at 0, so inverse-slope says nothing there, and 13e's undoing's-slope already proved ∛ has no slope at 0. The hypothesis f′(x₀) ≠ 0 is not decoration.

13l8Matching the inputs

Stands on 13l1 to 13l7.

The care is all in the word matching. The slope of the inverse at 2 is not 1/f′(2) = 1/13: that is the reciprocal of f's slope at the wrong input. The swapped pairs send (1, 2) on f to (2, 1) on the inverse, and the slopes that multiply to 1 are taken at those two pairs.

History, in words. In 1638 one mathematician, quarrelling by letter with another about how to find tangent lines, set him an equation as a test: x³ + y³ = 3kxy, now called a leaf. The other found its tangent lines without trouble. Its branches cannot be written with the functions this ladder has, so the leaf waits; the basement says for what.

The technique that shows such a branch has a slope, without writing the branch, is built on 28f. The leaf itself comes back on 18·cb13.

Who said it first

The popular book is Silvanus P. Thompson’s Calculus Made Easy. The quarrel of 1638 was between René Descartes, who set the test, and Pierre de Fermat, who passed it; the leaf is the folium of Descartes.

Thompson advises that when a function is easier to take the slope of when run backwards, you may do so, and the reciprocal of the backward slope gives the forward one.
