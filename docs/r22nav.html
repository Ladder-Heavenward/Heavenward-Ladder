Can a Flat Map Tell the Truth?

Can a flat map tell the truth? Every map of the world is drawn on a flat sheet, and the world is a ball. A sailor wants a map on which a steady compass heading is a straight line, so that a ruler can lay the course. A school wants a map on which every country is its true size. What makes it hard is that the skin of a ball cannot be laid flat without stretching, as an orange peel tears when it is pressed onto a table. So every flat map stretches something, and the only questions are what, and by how much. This page shows, with rung twenty-one’s turns, rung fifteen’s thin strips and rung twenty-two’s endless sums, how much a map must stretch to keep a compass course straight, why the poles fall off the top of such a map, and why no flat map keeps both sizes and shapes.

On a map whose lines of latitude run straight across, each of them is drawn as long as the equator, so the far north is stretched east and west. To keep every heading true, the map must stretch north and south by the same amount, strip by thin strip. The height of each latitude on the map is then the total of endlessly many thin strips, and the strips near the pole add up past every bound, as the sum of one, a half, a third and so on does. A map that keeps sizes must squash where the other stretches, so it cannot keep headings too. And no flat map at all keeps every distance.

22nav1Every Straight Grid Stretches the Far North

From here on, on this page, a straight-grid map draws every line of longitude as an upright straight line, evenly spaced, and every line of latitude as a straight line across. It draws the equator at true scale: a globe of radius R gives a map 2πR wide.

A line of latitude is a circle round the earth’s axis. On the globe it shrinks toward the pole. On a straight-grid map it is drawn as wide as the equator.

\text{latitude } \varphi:\ \text{a circle of radius } R\cos\varphi @@ two turns (21·cb18.1): its reach from the axis
\text{its length on the globe: } 2\pi R\cos\varphi @@ arcs on any circle (21j3)
\text{its length on the map: } 2\pi R @@ every line across is as wide as the equator
\htmlClass{new}{\text{east-west stretch at } \varphi = \tfrac{2\pi R}{2\pi R\cos\varphi} = \sec\varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tangent’s family (21c)

East-stretch technique: a straight-grid map stretches every east-west length at latitude φ by sec φ. At Eureka, 40.8° N, the stretch is 1.32. At Vancouver, 49.3° N, it is 1.53. At 60° N it is 2. At the pole sec is not defined: a single point would have to be drawn as wide as the equator.

22nav2Keep Every Heading: An Endless Sum of Thin Strips

A short step of length s on heading β goes s cos β north and s sin β east (21·nav.4). On the map the east part is stretched by sec φ. Let the north part be stretched by k, which the mapmaker is free to choose. The drawn step then leans at a heading β′ from the map’s upright lines.

\tan\beta' = \tfrac{\sec\varphi \cdot s\sin\beta}{k \cdot s\cos\beta} = \tfrac{\sec\varphi}{k}\tan\beta @@ east part over north part, as drawn
\beta' = \beta \text{ for every } \beta \Rightarrow \tfrac{\sec\varphi}{k} = 1 @@ take β = 45°, where tan β = 1
\htmlClass{new}{k = \sec\varphi} \quad \blacksquare @@ and k = sec φ keeps every β

Let y(φ) be the drawn height of latitude φ above the equator. A step R h north, from φ to φ + h, is drawn y(φ + h) − y(φ) tall. So the north stretch at φ is the slope y′(φ) divided by R.

\tfrac{y(\varphi + h) - y(\varphi)}{R\,h} \to \tfrac{y'(\varphi)}{R} = \sec\varphi @@ the north stretch is the east stretch
y(\varphi) = R\int_0^{\varphi}\sec t\,dt @@ the drawn height is the area under its slope (16c); y(0) = 0
\tfrac{d}{d\varphi}\ln(\sec\varphi + \tan\varphi) = \tfrac{\sec\varphi\tan\varphi + \sec^2\varphi}{\sec\varphi + \tan\varphi} = \sec\varphi @@ slope of ln (17b), the chain (13), slopes of sec and tan (21g)
\htmlClass{new}{y(\varphi) = R\ln(\sec\varphi + \tan\varphi)} \quad \blacksquare @@ both are 0 at the equator (16c)

Strip technique: a straight-grid map keeps every heading exactly when it draws latitude φ at height R ln(sec φ + tan φ), the total of its thin strips. A steady compass heading crosses every line of longitude at one angle, so on this map it is a straight line, and a ruler lays the course.

Before anyone had the formula, the heights were found by adding strips. Each strip of width h is fenced between R h sec at its lower edge and R h sec at its upper edge, since sec rises from the equator to the pole (15h).

6 \text{ strips of } 10^\circ:\ \ 1.2384\,R \le y(60^\circ) \le 1.4129\,R @@ lower and upper edges of each strip
60 \text{ strips of } 1^\circ:\ \ 1.3083\,R \le y(60^\circ) \le 1.3258\,R @@ the fences close in (15h)
\htmlClass{new}{y(60^\circ) = R\ln(2 + \sqrt3) \approx 1.3170\,R} @@ sec 60° = 2, tan 60° = √3

22nav3The Pole Falls off the Top

Near the pole sec φ is huge, so the strips there are drawn very tall. Measure from the pole instead: let c = 90° − φ, in radians. Then sec φ = 1 / sin c, and sin c ≤ c (21f), so sec φ ≥ 1 / c.

\text{cut from } c = h \text{ to } c = nh \text{ into strips of width } h @@ strip k runs from c = (k − 1)h to c = kh, for k = 2, …, n
\text{strip } k \ge R\,h \cdot \tfrac{1}{kh} = \tfrac{R}{k} @@ sec φ ≥ 1/c ≥ 1/(kh) across the strip
y(90^\circ - h) - y(90^\circ - nh) \ge R\,(\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots + \tfrac1n) @@ add the strips
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the drawn height passes every bound as } \varphi \to 90^\circ} \quad \blacksquare @@ fix nh; as n grows, 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ passes every ceiling (22d’s grouping technique)

Pole technique: on a map that keeps every heading, the drawn height of latitude φ passes every bound as φ nears the pole, just as 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ does. The pole is never on the map. Every such map is cut off at a top edge somewhere short of it.

y(89^\circ) \approx 4.74\,R,\ \ y(89.9^\circ) \approx 7.04\,R,\ \ y(89.99^\circ) \approx 9.35\,R @@ the strip technique
\sec\varphi + \tan\varphi = \tfrac{1 + \cos c}{\sin c} \approx \tfrac{2}{c} @@ c small
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each step ten times closer adds about } R\ln 10 \approx 2.30\,R} @@ log-of-a-quotient (17c)

22nav4Sizes or Headings, Not Both

A small patch of ground at latitude φ, a wide and b tall, is drawn a sec φ wide and b k tall. So its area is stretched by k sec φ (15e).

\text{area stretch} = k\sec\varphi @@ areas scale (15e)
\text{headings kept} \Rightarrow k = \sec\varphi @@ 22·nav.2
\text{sizes kept} \Rightarrow k\sec\varphi = 1 \Rightarrow k = \cos\varphi,\ \ y = R\sin\varphi @@ the slope of sin is cos (21g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both} \Rightarrow \sec^2\varphi = 1 \Rightarrow \varphi = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ only along the equator

Shape-or-size technique: a straight-grid map can keep every heading or every size, but it keeps both only along the equator. The heading map stretches every area by sec² φ.

\text{Greenland}:\ 2.17 \text{ million km}^2 \text{ near } 72^\circ \text{ N};\ \ \sec^2 72^\circ \approx 10.5 @@ the shape-or-size technique
\text{drawn as if } 2.17 \cdot 10.5 \approx 22.7 \text{ million km}^2 @@ at the equator’s scale
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Africa}:\ 30.4 \text{ million km}^2,\ \text{astride the equator, drawn near true size}} @@ fourteen times Greenland, drawn not much bigger

Maps that are not straight grids can bend their lines. Can one of them keep every distance? Take the north pole N, and on the equator A at longitude 0°, M at 45° and B at 90°. Along the surface, A, M and B are each a quarter turn from N, so NA = NM = NB = πR/2. Along the equator, AM = MB = πR/4 and AB = πR/2 (21·cb18.2).

A' = (0, 0),\ \ B' = (s, 0),\ \ s = \tfrac{\pi R}{2} @@ suppose a flat map keeps every distance; slide and turn it (20)
|A'M'| = |B'M'| = \tfrac{s}{2} \Rightarrow M' = (\tfrac{s}{2},\ 0) @@ subtract the two distance equations: x = s/2, then y = 0 (18a)
|A'N'| = |B'N'| = s \Rightarrow N' = (\tfrac{s}{2},\ \pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}s) @@ the same way
\htmlClass{new}{|N'M'| = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}s \ne s = NM} \quad \blacksquare @@ the map breaks one distance, so no flat map keeps them all

Keeping every heading and every size at once would make every short length come out true, and then every distance would be kept. That step, for maps that bend their lines, is held in trust for much higher up the ladder.

The accepted names

A straight-grid map is a cylindrical projection. The map that keeps headings is the Mercator projection, and a steady compass heading is a rhumb line, or loxodrome. The map that keeps sizes is Lambert’s cylindrical equal-area projection; stretched upright by a fixed factor it becomes the Gall–Peters projection. The east and north stretches are scale factors. A map that keeps every angle is conformal, and one that keeps every area is equal-area. The integral ∫₀φ sec t dt is the inverse Gudermannian function, and the drawn height on the heading map is often called the meridional part. That keeping angles and areas at once would keep lengths, and that no flat map keeps lengths, are the content of Gauss’s Theorema Egregium: the ball is curved, and the sheet is not.

Who said it first

Gerardus Mercator drew the heading map in 1569, spacing the lines of latitude by hand, without a formula. Edward Wright (Certaine Errors in Navigation, 1599) worked out its heights by adding the secants of every minute of latitude, this page’s strip sums. Henry Bond noticed about 1645 that Wright’s table matched a table of log tangents, the guess staged in 22·nav.6.5. Nicholas Mercator (no relation) offered a wager in the 1660s on whether it could be proved; James Gregory proved it in 1668 and Isaac Barrow gave the partial-fraction proof of 22·nav.6.4 in 1670. Johann Heinrich Lambert drew the size map in 1772. Leonhard Euler proved in 1777 that no flat map of a ball keeps every distance, and Carl Friedrich Gauss explained why in 1827.
