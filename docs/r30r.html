30r · technique

The Most Likely Parameter

Rung fourteen’s statistics page took one coin and one record of heads, and asked which chance would make that record most likely. The answer was the share seen. Now the same question is asked of every kind of record: counts of earthquakes in a month, the waits between birds at a feeder, the depths at which a disc vanishes in a lake. Each kind of record comes from a family of chance laws with a knob, and the record is fixed while the knob turns. The best guess is the knob setting that makes the record most likely. A record is many readings, and their chances multiply. The logarithm turns the product into a sum, so the slopes are easy, and the best guesses come out as familiar averages. Then the shape of the top answers the second question rung fourteen asked: how far behind do the other guesses fall, and so how sure is the best one.

Fix the record and turn the knob: the chance of the record, as a function of the knob, is the likelihood. Its logarithm has the same best setting and is a sum. For rare counts the best guess is the average count; for waits it is one over the average wait; for the bell it is the average and the spread with the count in the bottom. The sharper the top, the surer the guess.

30r1The Likelihood of a Sample, and Its Logarithm

A family of chance laws has a knob θ: the coin’s chance p, a rate λ, an average μ. Readings x₁, …, xₙ are independent, and f(x; θ) is the chance of a reading x when the knob is at θ. For readings that take any real value, f is the density of 30m1, and the chance of landing within a small h of x is close to f(x; θ) h; the h does not move with θ, so f stands in for the chance.

The likelihood is L(θ) = f(x₁; θ) ⋯ f(xₙ; θ): the chance of the record, by independence (30f2), read as a function of the knob. The best guess θ̂ is a setting where L is greatest. As on 14·stat, L(θ) is not the chance that θ is right.

Log-likelihood technique: where L is above 0, L is greatest at exactly the same settings as ℓ(θ) = ln f(x₁; θ) + ⋯ + ln f(xₙ; θ).

\ln L(\theta) = \textstyle\sum_i \ln f(x_i; \theta) = \ell(\theta) @@ the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms (17c)
L(\theta_1) < L(\theta_2) \iff \ell(\theta_1) < \ell(\theta_2) @@ ln is rising (17b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the tops of } L \text{ are the tops of } \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ a top is a setting no other beats
  
\ell(p) = \ln\tbinom nk + k\ln p + (n - k)\ln(1 - p) @@ the chance-of-a-record technique (14·stat), logged
\ell'(p) = \tfrac kp - \tfrac{n - k}{1 - p} = \tfrac{k - np}{p(1 - p)} @@ the slope of ln (17b), term by term
\htmlClass{new}{\hat p = k/n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sign of k − np, as on 14·stat: the seen-share technique again, in one line

The best guess does not care how the knob is labelled. Same-best technique: if g is one-to-one on the settings, the best guess of g(θ) is g(θ̂). L as a function of φ = g(θ) is L(g⁻¹(φ)), and it is greatest where g⁻¹(φ) = θ̂. So the best guess of the spread width σ is the root of the best guess of σ². Here φ (little phi) means the setting written another way, g(θ), not 2e's condition.

30r2Counts and Waits

A count of rare events in a fixed stretch, a month or a page, is k with chance e^(−λ)λᵏ/k! (the rare-count technique, 22·stat). A wait between events that arrive at rate λ has density λe^(−λx) for x ≥ 0 (the first-gap technique, 30o1).

Rare-guess technique: for counts k₁, …, kₙ of rare events with k̄ > 0, the best guess of λ is the average count k̄.

\ell(\lambda) = -n\lambda + \big(\textstyle\sum_i k_i\big)\ln\lambda - \sum_i \ln k_i! @@ the log-likelihood technique (30r1) on e^(−λ)λ^k/k!
\ell'(\lambda) = -n + \tfrac{n\bar k}{\lambda} = \tfrac{n(\bar k - \lambda)}{\lambda} @@ Σ kᵢ = n k̄
\ell' > 0 \text{ for } \lambda < \bar k,\ \ \ell' < 0 \text{ for } \lambda > \bar k @@ the sign of k̄ − λ
\htmlClass{new}{\hat\lambda = \bar k} \quad \blacksquare @@ the first-test and one-critical-point techniques (14f) on (0, ∞)

Gap-guess technique: for waits x₁, …, xₙ of rate λ, the best guess of λ is 1/x̄, and of the average wait it is x̄.

\ell(\lambda) = n\ln\lambda - \lambda\textstyle\sum_i x_i = n\ln\lambda - n\bar x\lambda @@ the log-likelihood technique on λe^(−λx)
\ell'(\lambda) = \tfrac n\lambda - n\bar x @@ positive for λ below 1/x̄, negative above
\htmlClass{new}{\hat\lambda = 1/\bar x,\ \text{ average wait } 1/\hat\lambda = \bar x} \quad \blacksquare @@ 14f; then the same-best technique with g(λ) = 1/λ
  
2,\ 0,\ 1,\ 3,\ 1,\ 2,\ 0,\ 1,\ 4,\ 2,\ 1,\ 1 @@ a year of monthly counts of felt earthquakes in one county of Washington
\hat\lambda = 18/12 = 1.5 \text{ a month} @@ the rare-guess technique
3.1,\ 0.8,\ 5.2,\ 1.9,\ 2.4,\ 0.6,\ 4.0,\ 2.0 @@ minutes between visits of hummingbirds to a feeder in Victoria
\htmlClass{new}{\hat\lambda = 8/20 = 0.4 \text{ a minute},\ \text{average wait } 2.5\ \text{min}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gap-guess technique

When every count is 0, ℓ(λ) = −nλ falls all the way, and the best guess sits at the end, λ̂ = 0, where the slope is −n: 14·stat’s end-guess technique, for counts.

30r3The Bell, With Two Knobs

The bell with average μ and spread v = σ² has density e^(−(x − μ)²/(2v)) / √(2πv), the standard bell of 30m3 stretched by σ and moved by μ (the stretched-density technique). Two knobs turn together.

Bell-guess technique: for bell-shaped readings x₁, …, xₙ, not all equal, the best guess of μ is x̄, and of σ² it is v̂ = ((x₁ − x̄)² + ⋯ + (xₙ − x̄)²)/n.

\ell(\mu, v) = -\tfrac n2\ln(2\pi v) - \tfrac{1}{2v}\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 @@ the log-likelihood technique on the bell
\textstyle\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 = D + n(\bar x - \mu)^2,\ \ D = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 > 0 @@ the squared-misses technique (8·stat)
\ell(\mu, v) \le \ell(\bar x, v) \text{ for every } v,\ \text{equal only at } \mu = \bar x @@ the second term is least at μ = x̄
\tfrac{d}{dv}\,\ell(\bar x, v) = -\tfrac{n}{2v} + \tfrac{D}{2v^2} = \tfrac{D - nv}{2v^2} @@ one knob at a time (28a): μ held at x̄
\htmlClass{new}{\hat\mu = \bar x,\ \ \hat v = D/n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the slope is positive below D/n and negative above: one-critical-point (14f)

The best guess of the spread has n in the bottom, and 30q2 proved that its average is (n − 1)σ²/n, a little short. The most likely guess and the guess that is right on average are different guesses, and both are honest about what they are.

30r4How Sharp the Top Is

14·stat measured how far the other guesses fall behind by the ratio L(p)/L(p̂). In logarithms that is ℓ(p) − ℓ(p̂), and near the top it is close to ½ ℓ″(p̂)(p − p̂)² (23a). A sharp top, with −ℓ″(θ̂) large, leaves few guesses near the best one.

Top-bend technique: for a share k/n with 0 < k < n, and for rare counts with k̄ > 0, the number 1/(−ℓ″(θ̂)) is the spread of θ̂ given by the sample-average technique, with θ̂ in place of θ.

\ell''(p) = -\tfrac{k}{p^2} - \tfrac{n - k}{(1 - p)^2} @@ the slope of 30r1’s ℓ′
-\ell''(\hat p) = \tfrac{n}{\hat p} + \tfrac{n}{1 - \hat p} = \tfrac{n}{\hat p(1 - \hat p)} @@ k = n p̂
\mathrm{Var}\,\hat p = \tfrac{p(1 - p)}{n} @@ the sample-average technique (30q1): one 0-or-1 reading has spread p(1 − p)
\ell''(\lambda) = -\tfrac{n\bar k}{\lambda^2},\ \ -\ell''(\hat\lambda) = \tfrac{n}{\bar k} @@ 30r2’s ℓ′, sloped again
\mathrm{Var}\,\hat\lambda = \tfrac{\lambda}{n} @@ one rare count has spread λ (the mean-and-spread technique, 22·stat)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{-\ell''(\hat\theta)} = \text{the spread, with } \hat\theta \text{ for } \theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines match, line for line

Held in trust: for large samples from a family whose ℓ is smoothly sloped, θ̂ is close to bell-shaped about the true θ, with spread close to 1/(−ℓ″(θ̂)). Its proof waits much higher up the ladder. What it gives: the interval θ̂ ± 1.96/√(−ℓ″(θ̂)), at about 95%, for any such family. For the share and for rare counts it is the bell-cover interval of 30q3.

-\ell''(0.7) = 10/0.21 \approx 47.6,\ \ 1/\sqrt{47.6} \approx 0.145 @@ the top-bend technique
0.7 \pm 1.96 \times 0.145 = 0.42 \text{ to } 0.98 @@ about 95%; 14·stat’s tenth-ratio stretch was 0.37 to 0.93
-\ell''(1.5) = 12/1.5 = 8,\ \ 1/\sqrt 8 \approx 0.354 @@ twelve months, k̄ = 1.5
\htmlClass{new}{1.5 \pm 0.69:\ \ 0.81 \text{ to } 2.19 \text{ a month}} \quad \blacksquare @@ about 95%, by the trusted bell shape

For ten tosses the bell is a rough fit: the interval runs nearly to 1, and a lopsided hump is squeezed into a shape that is even on both sides. For ten thousand tosses the two stretches agree closely.

The accepted names

The best guess is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE), and the method is maximum likelihood estimation. The knob is the parameter, and the family is a parametric model. ℓ is the log-likelihood. The same-best technique is the invariance (or equivariance) property of the MLE. −ℓ″(θ̂) is the observed information; its average is the Fisher information. The statement held in trust is the asymptotic normality of the MLE, and the interval it gives is a Wald interval. Matching averages is the method of moments. The density of the build standing question is the Cauchy distribution, whose likelihood can have several peaks. The edge standing question is the German tank problem’s model, where the MLE is the sample maximum.

Who said it first

Daniel Bernoulli proposed choosing the most probable value for astronomical observations in 1778, and Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1809 derived the normal law by asking that the mean be the most likely value. Ronald Fisher made maximum likelihood a general method between 1912 and 1922, named the likelihood, and showed the large-sample spread 1/I(θ). Karl Pearson’s method of moments (1894) is what he argued against. Harold Cramér and C. R. Rao found in 1945 that no unbiased estimator has a spread below 1/I(θ). Allied statisticians used serial numbers to estimate German tank production during the Second World War.
