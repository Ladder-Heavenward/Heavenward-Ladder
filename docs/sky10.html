Who Eats Whom, and Does It Last?

Who eats whom, and does it last? On the rocky shore of Mukkaw Bay, at the northwest tip of Washington, one kind of sea star eats mussels, barnacles, limpets and snails. In 1963 an ecologist began prying the sea stars off one stretch of shore and throwing them into deep water, and left the next stretch alone. Within a few years mussels had crowded out most of the cleared stretch, and the number of kinds living there had fallen by about half. Take away one link, and the whole web changes. In 1972 a count came from the other side: a web drawn at random, with more kinds and more links, is less likely to hold together, not more. That ran against what most ecologists then believed, and the argument it began has not ended. This page draws a web as a graph with arrows, reads the hares and lynx of rung twenty eight as a small grid, proves when a web held back by each kind’s own crowding must hold, counts what happens when the links are random, and finds which kind of link the count is kind to.

A food web is a graph with an arrow from each eaten kind to its eater; a kind’s level is one more than the average level of what it eats. Near a rest every web is x′ ≈ Ax, and it holds when every stretch of A has real part below 0. A pair of opposite signs holds at any strength; a pair of one sign only while pq < d². If each kind’s own crowding outweighs the total size of its other links, the web holds. With S kinds linked with chance C and spread width σ, a random web holds while σ√(SC) < 1, and eater-and-eaten pairs stretch that to about 2.75.

sky10.1A Web Is a Graph With Arrows

Draw a dot for each kind of living thing in a place, and an arrow from each kind to every kind that eats it: the links of 42a, each with a direction. A kind with no arrow coming in is a producer, making its food from light. A path along the arrows is a food chain. With S kinds and L arrows, the share of the S² possible arrows that are there, C = L/S², is the web’s connectance.

Food-level technique: give each producer level 1 and every other kind one more than the average level of what it eats; then the levels t solve (I − Q)t = 1, where row i of Q gives each food of kind i the share 1/kᵢ, kᵢ its count of foods; when no chain of eating comes back to where it began, the levels come out one kind at a time, from the producers up. With a loop of eating, the same samenesses are solved together by 27j’s elimination.

\text{plankton, kelp: } 1;\ \ \text{mussels (plankton), urchins (kelp): } 2 @@ producers, then one step up
\text{snails (mussels): } 3 @@ 1 + 2
\text{sea stars (mussels, urchins, snails): } 1 + \tfrac{2 + 2 + 3}{3} = \tfrac{10}{3} @@ one more than the average
\text{gulls (mussels, urchins, sea stars): } 1 + \tfrac{2 + 2 + 10/3}{3} = \tfrac{31}{9} @@ about 3.44
\htmlClass{new}{S = 7,\ L = 9,\ C = 9/49 \approx 0.18} \quad \blacksquare @@ count the arrows

Levels are rarely whole numbers, because most eaters eat at several levels. The longest chain here, plankton to mussel to snail to sea star to gull, has 4 links. Webs drawn from real places mostly have connectance between about 0.03 and 0.3, and few chains longer than five links. Who eats whom says nothing yet about whether the counts last. For that the arrows need numbers.

sky10.2A Pair, Read as a Grid

28·bio settled the hares and lynx of the North Cascades with a kept quantity, and found that a little crowding turns its loops into spirals. Read the same rules at their rest as a grid. For x′ = x(0.8 − 0.2y), y′ = y(−0.6 + 0.03x), resting at (20, 4), 27·cb24.4’s tangent grid has entry i, j the rate at which kind j moves kind i’s rate: rows (0, −4) and (0.12, 0). The lynx lower the hares, the hares raise the lynx. Its stretches are ±0.693i, on the line where the grid decides nothing, which is why 28·bio needed its kept quantity. With 28·bio.6’s crowding the rows are (−0.08, −4) and (0.108, 0), and the stretches −0.04 ± 0.656i spiral in.

Sign-pair technique: for the grid with −d on the diagonal, d > 0, and p, q off it, the stretches are −d ± √(pq); so when p and q have opposite signs the rest holds at every strength, and when they have one sign it holds exactly while pq < d².

\det\begin{pmatrix} -d - \lambda & p \\ q & -d - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (\lambda + d)^2 - pq @@ the stretch-test technique (27f)
pq \lt 0:\ \ \lambda = -d \pm i\sqrt{|pq|} @@ real part −d, however strong the pair
pq \gt 0:\ \ \lambda = -d \pm \sqrt{pq} @@ the larger is below 0 exactly while √(pq) < d
\htmlClass{new}{\text{opposite signs hold; one sign holds only while weak}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tangent-grid statement (27·cb24.4)

With d = 1: eater and eaten with p = −4, q = 0.12 give −1 ± 0.693i and hold. Two competitors for one food lower each other, p = −2, q = −0.3, and give −0.23 and −1.77: they hold. Make them compete harder, pq = 1.6, and one stretch is +0.26: one kind drives the other out. Opposite signs turn; one sign pushes.

sky10.3Strong Crowding Holds Any Web

With S kinds, each count obeys xᵢ′ = xᵢ fᵢ, where fᵢ depends on all S counts, and at a rest where every kind is present, the tangent grid A has entry Aᵢⱼ equal to xᵢ times the slope of fᵢ in xⱼ, both at the rest. The rest holds when every stretch of A has real part below 0 (27·cb24.4). Finding the stretches of a grid with a hundred rows is a machine’s work. A test by hand exists for webs whose kinds are held back mostly by their own crowding.

Own-crowding technique: if in every row of A the diagonal entry is below 0 and larger in size than the sum of the sizes of the other entries of that row, then every stretch of A has real part below 0, and the rest holds.

Av = \lambda v,\ v \ne 0;\ \ |v_i| \text{ the largest of } |v_1|, \ldots, |v_S| @@ a stretched arrow, and its largest entry
(\lambda - A_{ii})\,v_i = \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} A_{ij} v_j @@ row i of Av = λv
|\lambda - A_{ii}| \le \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} |A_{ij}|\,\tfrac{|v_j|}{|v_i|} \le \sum_{j \ne i} |A_{ij}| \lt -A_{ii} @@ divide by |vᵢ| ≠ 0; each |vⱼ| ≤ |vᵢ|
\text{Re}\,\lambda - A_{ii} \lt -A_{ii} @@ the real part of a gap is at most its size
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Re}\,\lambda \lt 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ for every stretch λ

Kelp, urchins and otters: rows (−1, −0.5, 0), (0.3, −0.8, −0.4) and (0, 0.2, −0.5) clear the test by 0.5, 0.1 and 0.3, so the rest holds; its stretches are −0.83 ± 0.42i and −0.64. The test asks a lot. In a web of 100 kinds, each linked to a tenth of the others by links from the bell curve of spread width 0.3, a row’s other entries add to about 2.4 in size, and crowding of 1 fails the test. Yet of 20 such webs drawn by machine, 19 held. The signs cancel, and the next footing counts how.

sky10.4A Web Drawn at Random

Take S kinds, each with crowding −1, and let each other entry Aᵢⱼ be 0, or, with chance C, drawn from the bell curve with average 0 and spread width σ, all independently. Write B for the grid with its diagonal taken out, so A = B − I, and every stretch of A is a stretch of B moved left by 1.

Random-stretch technique: for every arrow v, the average of |Bv|² over the draws is (S − 1)Cσ²|v|²; so B stretches a typical arrow by about σ√(SC).

(Bv)_i = \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} B_{ij} v_j @@ row i
B_{ij}:\ \text{average } 0,\ \text{spread } C\sigma^2 @@ 0 with chance 1 − C; spread σ² with chance C
\text{average of } (Bv)_i^2 = C\sigma^2 \textstyle\sum_{j \ne i} v_j^2 @@ the independent-spreads-add technique (30g)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{average of } |Bv|^2 = (S - 1)\,C\sigma^2\,|v|^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ add over i: each vⱼ² comes S − 1 times

That is a size, not yet a stretch. Held in trust, by the circle law proved in stages from 1965 to 2010: for large S the stretches of B fill the disc of radius σ√(SC) round 0, nearly evenly. So the random web holds while σ√(SC) < 1, and fails past it. For S = 100, C = 0.1, σ = 0.3 the radius is 0.95, and 19 of 20 machine draws held. With 200 kinds it is 1.34, and none of 20 held. Make any random web large enough and linked enough, and it fails.

sky10.5Eater and Eaten Beat Chance

The random web gave Aᵢⱼ and Aⱼᵢ independent signs. Real links come in pairs: if the lynx lower the hares’ rate, the hares raise the lynx’s. Draw the web that way. Each pair of kinds is linked with chance C; then one entry is +|X| and the other −|Y|, with X and Y from the bell curve of spread width σ, and a fair coin picks which kind eats. Each entry still has average 0 and spread Cσ², so the random-stretch technique gives the same size. But the stretches move.

\text{average of } A_{ij}A_{ji} = -C\,(\text{average } |X|)^2 = -C\sigma^2 \cdot \tfrac2\pi @@ one plus, one minus; average |X| = 2σ ∫₀^∞ x e^(−x²/2) dx/√(2π) = σ√(2/π)
\rho = \tfrac{\text{average of } A_{ij}A_{ji}}{C\sigma^2} = -\tfrac2\pi \approx -0.64 @@ how tightly the pair is tied
\text{the stretches fill an ellipse of half-width } \sigma\sqrt{SC}\,(1 + \rho) \text{ along the real line} @@ held in trust, 1988; ρ = 0 gives the disc
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the web holds while } \sigma\sqrt{SC} \lt 1/(1 - 2/\pi) \approx 2.75} \quad \blacksquare @@ the half-width below 1

This is footing 2’s pair in bulk: opposite signs turn instead of push. With S = 400, C = 0.1 and σ = 0.3, σ√(SC) = 1.90, and every random web of that size fails. With eater-and-eaten pairs the half-width is 0.69, and 20 of 20 machine draws held. Pairs of one sign, helpers and competitors in equal measure, give ρ = +2/π and a half-width of 3.1: they fail far sooner. The count of pairs came in 2012. Whether real shores hold because of their pairs, their weak links, or something no grid holds, is still argued.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞10.oq.1Why do large real webs hold? A lake or a shore has hundreds of kinds, and footing 4 says random webs of that size fail. Candidates: eater-and-eaten pairs, mostly weak links, webs split into nearly separate parts, or only that webs which fail are no longer there to be drawn. The arrows of a web are often known; the strengths Aᵢⱼ almost never are. Which structure does the work, and what measurement could tell?
∞10.oq.2Does diversity steady an ecosystem? In grassland plots in Minnesota, more kinds made the total crop steadier from year to year while each single kind swung more. Steady totals, steady kinds, a quick return after a fire, a web that newcomers cannot break into: these are different steadinesses, and they need not go together. Which one is meant, and is the grassland answer general?
∞10.oq.3Did wolves bring back the aspen of Yellowstone? Elk fell after wolves returned in 1995, and willow regrew along some streams. Papers disagree on how much the aspen recovered, and on whether fear of wolves, hunting by people, bears, cougars or drought did the work. What evidence can settle a cascade in a place that no one can repeat, with no stretch of shore left alone to compare?
∞10.oq.4Can anyone tell in advance which kind’s loss topples a web? When the sunflower stars of the Pacific coast died of a wasting disease from 2013, urchins boomed and the kelp went. Was the sunflower star’s place visible in the web’s arrows, or only in strengths nobody had measured? Is there a test, from a web as it is drawn, that picks out the kinds it cannot do without?
∞10.oq.5Why are food chains short? About a tenth of the energy at one level reaches the next, which would cap chains by the light a place receives. But chain length tracks the size of lakes better than how much they grow in some studies, and models find long chains harder to hold. Which limit binds, and does it differ between a pond and an ocean?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

1920s to 1934. The two-kind rules of 27·then were written in the United States in 1925 and in Italy in 1926, the second to explain why the fish markets of the northern Adriatic sold more sharks and rays during the First World War, when fishing stopped. The rules predicted swings that never die. In 1927 a British ecologist drew food chains and the pyramid of numbers: many small kinds at the bottom, a few large eaters on top. In 1934 a biologist in Moscow put a predator protozoan in a dish with its prey and got no swings at all: the predator ate every prey and starved, unless the dish had a place to hide or he kept adding prey. The rules and the dish disagreed from the start.

1955 to 1966: complexity was thought to help. In 1955 a young ecologist argued that the more paths food has through a web, the less the loss of any one matters, so more links should mean steadier counts. In 1958 the British ecologist of 1927 gathered the evidence: outbreaks of pests strike fields of one crop and islands with few kinds harder than mixed forests. It became the received view, and a reason given for keeping kinds alive. The experiment at Mukkaw Bay, reported in 1966, seemed to fit: take away the top eater, and the cleared shore fell from fifteen kinds to eight. In 1969 the same ecologist named such a kind a keystone.

1972: the count. A physicist who had turned to ecology published the count of footing 4: random webs fail once σ√(SC) passes 1, so more kinds and more links make a web less likely to hold. Ecologists objected that real webs are not random, that real counts do not sit at a rest, and that the count asks about small nudges, not floods or fires. Its defenders replied that it states exactly what any claim that complexity helps must explain. Through the 1980s the argument turned on drawings of webs, which seemed to show connectance falling as kinds rose, just fast enough to stay inside the bound; better-drawn webs of the 1990s, with a hundred kinds and more, suggested that the pattern came from drawing webs coarsely.

The shore. Sea otters, hunted almost to the end by 1911, were found in 1974 to keep urchins down and kelp standing in the Aleutians: islands with otters had forests, islands without had bare rock. Otters moved from Alaska to the outer coast of Vancouver Island between 1969 and 1972 have since spread, and the kelp has come back where they are, at a cost to the shellfish catch that fishers and coastal First Nations have weighed in different ways. From 2013 a wasting disease killed sea stars from Alaska to Mexico, the sunflower star almost entirely; purple urchins boomed, and the north coast of California lost about nine tenths of its kelp canopy within a few years. A bacterium was reported as a cause in 2025.

The theory’s replies. In 1998 a study argued that webs of mostly weak links with a few strong ones damp their own swings. In 2012 the pairs of footing 5 were counted: eater-and-eaten pairs widen the bound, and helpers and competitors narrow it. Grassland plots in Minnesota, sown from 1994 with one to sixteen kinds, gave a split answer in 2006: more kinds made the total crop steadier from year to year, and each single kind less steady. Both sides of 1972 could claim it, and both did.

Now. Wolves were brought back to Yellowstone in 1995 and 1996, and the elk fell. Papers from 2001 on reported willow and aspen returning along the streams, and told it as a cascade of fear: elk stopped browsing where wolves could catch them. Other teams measured aspen and found less recovery, or laid the fall in elk on hunting by people, bears, cougars and drought, and the dispute over how young trees were sampled was still running in 2026. What is argued now is less whether complexity helps than which complexity: which links, of what strength, in what pattern, and whether a rest and its grid are even the right question for counts that never sit still.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The graph is a food web, a producer a basal species, and connectance is directed connectance L/S². The food-level technique defines the trophic level, or trophic position, of each species. The two-kind rules are the Lotka–Volterra equations, and the tangent grid of a web at a rest is its community matrix, a Jacobian; holding near a rest is local asymptotic stability. A pair of opposite signs is a predator–prey interaction, both minus is competition, and both plus mutualism. The own-crowding technique is Gershgorin’s circle theorem, read for diagonally dominant matrices. The random-stretch technique is a second-moment bound; the disc is the circular law, and σ√(SC) < 1 is May’s stability criterion. The ellipse is the elliptic law, and its use for predator–prey webs the Allesina–Tang criterion. A kind whose loss changes the web out of all proportion to its numbers is a keystone species, and a chain of effects down the levels a trophic cascade.

Who said it first

Alfred Lotka wrote the rules in Elements of Physical Biology, 1925, and Vito Volterra in 1926, from fish data gathered by Umberto D’Ancona. Charles Elton gave food chains and the pyramid of numbers in Animal Ecology, 1927, and the evidence from pests in The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants, 1958. Georgii Gause did the dish experiments in The Struggle for Existence, 1934. Robert MacArthur gave the argument from paths in 1955. Robert Paine ran the Mukkaw Bay removal from 1963, reported it in 1966, and named keystones in 1969. Richard Levins named the community matrix in 1968. Robert May published “Will a large complex system be stable?” in Nature in 1972. James Estes and John Palmisano reported the otters and kelp in 1974. Semyon Gershgorin gave his circles in 1931; Jean Ginibre found the disc for complex grids of bell-curve entries in 1965, and Terence Tao and Van Vu proved it in general in 2010. Hans-Jürgen Sommers, Andrea Crisanti, Haim Sompolinsky and Yaakov Stein gave the ellipse in 1988. Kevin McCann, Alan Hastings and Gary Huxel argued for weak links in 1998, and Stefano Allesina and Si Tang counted the pairs in 2012. David Tilman, Peter Reich and Johannes Knops reported the Minnesota plots in 2006. William Ripple and Robert Beschta led the papers on the Yellowstone cascade.

A proof that is not allowed

“Each kind, crowded by itself, holds alone, so a web of such kinds holds.” Not allowed: two competitors each with crowding 1 and pq = 1.6 have a stretch of +0.26, and one drives the other out. Each kind holding alone is the diagonal; the web holding is the whole grid.
