21j · technique

Radians

Rung 18 measured an angle by halving the quarter turn, and counted its size in quarter turns and in degrees. This rung measured it a second way: the length of its arc, counted in radii. This step shows the two ways measure one size, written in different units. It also shows why mathematicians keep to the radius: in any other unit, the slopes of the circle’s coordinates come with an awkward extra number.

An angle measured in radii, in degrees or in turns is one angle in three units. Any measure that keeps 18f’s rules for sizes is the arc in another unit, so there was only ever one way to measure angles. The radius is the unit the circle chooses for itself.

21j1Arcs keep the rules of degrees

For an angle BAC, slide A to the origin and take the points where its two rays cross the circle of radius 1 about A. Its arc is the length of the shorter arc between them. Arc-rules technique: the arc of an angle obeys every rule 18f’s size technique proved for sizes, with π in place of 180°.

\text{equal angles} \iff \text{equal arcs} @@ a move carries one onto the other, and moves keep path length (21d1)
\text{one ray: } 0;\ \ \text{opposite rays: } \pi @@ no arc; half the circle (21a)
0 < \theta < \pi:\ \text{exactly one ray on each side} @@ arc-picks-a-point (21a3), and its flip for the other side
D \text{ between: } \theta_{BAD} + \theta_{DAC} = \theta_{BAC} @@ the arc from B to C passes through D's point, and lengths of arcs end to end add
\htmlClass{new}{m\angle BAC = \tfrac{180}{\pi}\,\theta_{BAC} \text{ obeys the rules of sizes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ multiply each line by 180/π

So halving the quarter turn and walking the rim give measures with the same rules. The next footing shows they are one measure.

21j2Only one measure keeps the rules

Sizes-are-arcs technique: any measure that keeps the rules of 18f’s sizes, with a half turn measuring 180, is 180/π times the arc. 18f’s degrees keep those rules, so an angle of x° has arc πx/180, and its size in quarter turns is 2/π times its arc. Halving i and walking the rim measure one thing.

f(\theta) = m\angle \text{ of an angle with arc } \theta @@ well defined: equal arcs, equal angles, equal measures
f(s + t) = f(s) + f(t),\ \ f(\pi) = 180 @@ angle addition; opposite rays
f(\tfrac{k}{n}\pi) = \tfrac{k}{n} \cdot 180 @@ n copies of π/n make π; then k copies, by induction
s < t \Rightarrow f(s) < f(t) @@ f(t) = f(s) + f(t − s), and only one ray has measure 0
\tfrac{k}{n}\pi \le \theta < \tfrac{k + 1}{n}\pi \Rightarrow \big|f(\theta) - \tfrac{180}{\pi}\theta\big| < \tfrac{180}{n} @@ both lie between k/n · 180 and (k + 1)/n · 180
\htmlClass{new}{f(\theta) = \tfrac{180}{\pi}\,\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ true within 180/n for every n

From here on, the arc of an angle is its size in radians, and “°” means “times π/180”: 180° = π, so 1° = π/180 and 1 radian = 180°/π.

21j3Arcs on any circle

Arc-on-any-circle technique: on a circle of radius r, an angle of θ radians at the centre cuts an arc of length rθ. The circle of radius r is the circle of radius 1 pulled by r, and a pull by r multiplies every path length by r.

P \mapsto rP @@ a pull by r about the centre (20h3) sends the circle of radius 1 to the circle of radius r
\text{lengths} \times r @@ every chord is multiplied by r, so every sum of chords is
\htmlClass{new}{s = r\theta} \quad \blacksquare @@ angles are kept, since rays through the centre are sent to themselves
\theta \text{ turns per second} \Rightarrow r\theta \text{ per second at the rim} @@ a wheel’s rim moves r times as fast as its angle

Three units name one angle: θ radians is θ/2π turns and 180θ/π degrees.

21j4Why the radius

Degree-slope technique: in degrees, the slope of the sine is π/180 times the cosine; only in radians is it the cosine itself.

g(x) = \sin(x°) = \sin\!\big(\tfrac{\pi}{180}x\big) @@ “°” means times π/180
g'(x) = \tfrac{\pi}{180}\cos\!\big(\tfrac{\pi}{180}x\big) @@ the chain rule with the slope of sin (21g)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x°) = \tfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(x°)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the extra number never goes away
\tfrac{\sin(x°)}{x} \to \tfrac{\pi}{180} @@ and sin h ≈ h holds only for h in radians (21f)

Every slope, every swing and every small-angle guess would carry π/180 in degrees. The radius is the unit in which the circle’s coordinates are their own slopes, up to sign.

The accepted names

The arc measure is the radian measure; the unit is the radian, written rad or nothing at all. A measure with 400 to the turn is the gradian (or gon). Some writers prefer the whole turn, 2π, as the unit and call it τ (tau).

Who said it first

Roger Cotes measured angles by the radius in 1714. The word “radian” was first printed in 1873, by James Thomson, brother of Lord Kelvin. In 21j.4.5, the revolution is the French Revolution’s decimal reform of the 1790s; the 360 of degrees goes back to Babylonian counting in sixties.
