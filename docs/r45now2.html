Is All of Mathematics One Theory?

Is all of mathematics one theory? Take one curve, the answers of an equation in two unknowns, a square on one side and a cube on the other, and count its points on the clock of each prime: on the clock of 2 there are 4, on the clock of 3 there are 4, on the clock of 5 there are 4, on the clock of 7 there are 9. The counts look like noise. Now take an endless product, built from the powers of one letter and from every eleventh power, and multiply it out. The count at each prime is that prime, less the number the product puts at that prime, for every prime anyone has ever checked, and the match has been proved. A curve, which is a question about whole numbers, and a series with a symmetry, which is a question about functions, know the same numbers. In 1967 a young mathematician wrote a letter guessing that this is no accident: that every question of this kind about numbers is matched with a question about symmetric functions, one for one. The guess grew into a programme, and the programme into a large part of today’s mathematics. Its first case is rung forty one’s reciprocity; one of its middle cases proved Fermat’s statement in 1995; and its form for curves over a field, where numbers are replaced by functions, was proved in 2024 as a matching of two categories.

The points of a curve on the clock of a prime are counted by the square test, and for one curve the count is the prime less a number from an endless product with a symmetry. The programme of 1967 guesses that every such count from numbers is matched with a symmetric function. Its smallest case is quadratic reciprocity. Its form over curves, proved in 2024, says two categories built from a curve are matched.

45·now2.1Counting Points on a Clock

On the clock of a prime p (42), count the pairs (x, y) of hours with y² + y = x³ − x². Call the count A_p, and write b_p = p − A_p. The square test of 42f says, for an hour c, whether some y has y² = c: χ(c) = 1 when c is a square and not 0, −1 when it is not a square, and χ(0) = 0.

Clock-count technique: on the clock of an odd prime p, the number of answers of y² + y = f(x) is the sum, over the p hours x, of 1 + χ(4f(x) + 1).

y^2 + y = f(x) \iff 4y^2 + 4y + 1 = 4f(x) + 1 \iff (2y + 1)^2 = 4f(x) + 1 @@ multiply by 4 and add 1; 4 has a reciprocal on an odd clock
y \mapsto 2y + 1 \text{ is a matching of the hours} @@ undone by z ↦ (z − 1)/2
\#\{z : z^2 = c\} = 1 + \chi(c) @@ none, one (c = 0) or two (z and −z), by the square test
\htmlClass{new}{A_p = \textstyle\sum_x \bigl(1 + \chi(4f(x) + 1)\bigr)} \quad \blacksquare @@ one count for each hour x
  
x = 0, 1:\ \ 4f + 1 = 1,\ \chi = 1 @@ two answers each
x = 2:\ \ f = 4,\ \ 4f + 1 = 17 = 2 \text{ on the clock of } 5,\ \ \chi(2) = -1 @@ the squares on the clock of 5 are 1 and 4
x = 3, 4:\ \ f = 18, 48 \to 3,\ \ 4f + 1 = 13, 13 \to 3,\ \ \chi(3) = -1 @@ none
\htmlClass{new}{A_5 = 2 + 2 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 4,\ \ b_5 = 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the clock-count technique

Worked the same way, A₃ = 4, A₇ = 9, A₁₃ = 9, A₁₇ = 19, and on the clock of 2, by trying all four pairs, A₂ = 4. So b₂ = −2, b₃ = −1, b₅ = 1, b₇ = −2, b₁₃ = 4, b₁₇ = −2.

45·now2.2An Endless Product That Knows the Counts

Multiply out q (1 − q)²(1 − q¹¹)² (1 − q²)²(1 − q²²)² (1 − q³)² ⋯, one pair of factors for each power, as 23j stored a list in one function. Write c_n for the number in front of qⁿ. Only finitely many factors touch each qⁿ, so each c_n is a finite sum.

c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_{13} = 1, -2, -1, 2, 1, 2, -2, 0, -2, -2, 1, -2, 4 @@ multiplied out, by machine or by hand, to q¹³
c_2 = -2 = b_2,\ \ c_3 = -1 = b_3,\ \ c_5 = 1 = b_5 @@ against 45·now2.1
c_7 = -2 = b_7,\ \ c_{13} = 4 = b_{13},\ \ c_{17} = -2 = b_{17} @@ and on, to every prime checked
\htmlClass{new}{c_p = p - A_p \text{ for every prime } p} @@ proved in 1954 for this curve; held in trust here

The product has a symmetry the counts do not show: as a function of q = e^(2πiz), with z in the upper half of the plane of 25, it changes in a fixed way when z is moved by the maps z ↦ (pz + q)/(rz + s) of 25f with whole numbers p, q, r, s, ps − qr = 1 and r a multiple of 11. Counting points on a curve is arithmetic. The symmetry under 25f’s maps is analysis. The match says they are one list of numbers.

Rung 42 has already met the smallest case. Whether 5 is a square on the clock of p, χ_p(5), depends on p only through its hour on the clock of 5: yes for p at hour 1 or 4, no at hour 2 or 3. So χ_p(5) is 1 for 11 and 19 and −1 for 7 and 13. That is quadratic reciprocity (42f): a question about every prime answered by a function on one clock, which is the simplest kind of symmetric function. The 1967 letter guessed the same for every count of this kind.

45·now2.3Symmetry on Both Sides

Where do the counts come from? On the clock of p, the map x ↦ xᵖ leaves every hour where it is, by 42d’s little technique. On the larger fields of 43g built over that clock, it moves members, and it belongs to the group of the equation (44c) of every polynomial over the clock. The points of the curve over all those fields are moved by it too, and A_p counts the points it leaves where they are. A group acting and a count of what it fixes: the number side is a symmetry.

The function side is a symmetry under 25f’s maps. The programme guesses a matching between the two kinds of symmetry, for every group of the right kind, in such a way that the counts are kept. Proved cases: one-dimensional symmetries, the theory of the 1920s that holds reciprocity; the curves of this page and every curve of its kind over the fractions, from 1995 to 2001, whose first part proved that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no answer in counting numbers above 0 for n above 2; and many more. Open: the general case over the fractions.

\text{number side: } p \mapsto b_p = p - A_p @@ from the symmetry x ↦ xᵖ on the curve’s points
\text{function side: } p \mapsto c_p @@ from the product, symmetric under 25f’s maps with 11 | r
\htmlClass{new}{b_p = c_p \text{ for every prime}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one case of the programme, proved

45·now2.4The Geometric Form, Proved

Replace the fractions by the functions on a curve over a field, the way rung 9 made functions into things to add and multiply. The number side becomes, for a group G, a category built from the ways to twist G-symmetry round the loops of the curve (34g). The function side becomes a category built from symmetric objects on the space of ways to spread G over the curve. In the geometric form the guess is no longer a matching of lists of numbers. It is that the two categories are matched: maps of categories F and F′ between them with F′ ∘ F and F ∘ F′ naturally matched (45c4) with the maps that do nothing.

That is this rung’s kind of statement. It does not say the two sides have the same things. It says their arrows match, naturally, so that anything said with arrows on one side is said on the other. In 2024 a group of nine mathematicians posted the proof of the central case, over curves over the complex numbers, in five papers of nearly a thousand pages in all.

F : \mathbf{A} \to \mathbf{B},\ \ F' : \mathbf{B} \to \mathbf{A} @@ maps of categories between the two sides
F' \circ F \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf A},\ \ F \circ F' \cong \mathrm{id}_{\mathbf B} @@ naturally matched (45c4)
\htmlClass{new}{\mathbf A \text{ and } \mathbf B \text{ matched as categories}} \quad \blacksquare @@ held in trust: the categories themselves are built much higher up the ladder

Is all of mathematics one theory? Not by this. But the guess of 1967 says two of its largest parts are one, seen through their arrows, and in the geometric form that has now been proved.

The accepted names

The curve is the elliptic curve 11a3 (y² + y = x³ − x², conductor 11), and A_p + 1 is its number of points over the field with p members, one point being at infinity; b_p is the trace of Frobenius. The product is the modular form η(z)²η(11z)² of weight 2 and level 11. That they match is the modularity of this curve, proved by Martin Eichler in 1954; the bound |b_p| ≤ 2√p is Helmut Hasse’s theorem of 1933. The programme is the Langlands programme; its one-dimensional case is class field theory, Teiji Takagi and Emil Artin in the 1920s; the modularity theorem for elliptic curves over ℚ is Andrew Wiles with Richard Taylor (1995) for the semistable case, which proved Fermat’s Last Theorem, completed by Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond and Taylor (2001). The geometric form is the geometric Langlands conjecture; the 2024 proof is of its unramified global form in the de Rham setting, an equivalence between a category of D-modules on Bun_G and a category of ind-coherent sheaves on the stack of local systems for the dual group.

Who said it first

Robert Langlands wrote to André Weil in January 1967. Vladimir Drinfeld began the geometric form in the 1980s with Gérard Laumon and Alexander Beilinson. The 2024 proof is by Dmitry Arinkin, Dario Beraldo, Justin Campbell, Lin Chen, Joakim Færgeman, Dennis Gaitsgory, Kevin Lin, Sam Raskin and Nick Rozenblyum, in five papers building on decades of work led by Gaitsgory. Laurent Lafforgue proved the case of function fields over finite fields for GL_n (2002), and Vincent Lafforgue a form for all groups (2018).

A proof that is not allowed

“The match b_p = c_p holds for every prime up to a billion, so it holds for all primes.” Not allowed as a proof: it is induction under the large case, strong evidence and the right kind for a guess, and the 1967 letter was built on such evidence. The proof for this curve is a deduction over the arrangement, through the symmetry of the product, and it is what turned the guess into a technique.
