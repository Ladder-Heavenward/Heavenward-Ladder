19f · technique

Symmetries by hand

A square card, put back in its outline, can sit eight ways. A triangle, six. A rectangle that is not a square, four. This step counts the moves that leave a figure looking the same, by hand: label the corners, ask where one corner can go, and see that the rest is forced. The moves are a group, and everything this rung proved about groups can be checked on them.

A symmetry of a polygon, with its corners numbered in order round it, is a shuffle of the corners that keeps neighbours neighbours. The first corner can go to any corner, and its neighbour then to one of two; the rest is forced. So a regular polygon has twice as many symmetries as corners.

19f1Shuffles that keep neighbours

Stands on 19a (shuffles, groups) and 19d1 (the part-group test).

From here on, on this step, label the corners of a polygon 1, …, n in order round it; 1 and n are neighbours. A symmetry of the polygon is a shuffle of its corners that sends every two neighbours to two neighbours. Its accepted name is a symmetry of the polygon, as a figure of points; rung 20 builds that version.

Symmetries-form-a-group technique: the symmetries of a polygon form a part-group of the shuffles of its corners. e keeps everything. Two neighbour-keepers in a row keep neighbours. And a neighbour-keeper matches the n neighbour pairs with themselves, one-to-one, so it is onto them too (4k), and its undo keeps neighbours.

19f2Count by where one corner goes

Stands on 19f1 and 4k (stages multiply).

Place-then-count technique: a regular polygon with n corners, n ≥ 3, has exactly 2n symmetries. Corner 1 can be sent to any of the n corners. Corner 2, its neighbour, must go to one of the two neighbours of wherever 1 went. Then corner 3 must go to the other neighbour of corner 2’s place, and so on round: nothing is left to choose.

1 \mapsto k:\ \ n \text{ choices} @@ any corner
2 \mapsto k \pm 1:\ \ 2 \text{ choices} @@ a neighbour of k, since 1 and 2 are neighbours
i + 1 \mapsto \text{the neighbour of } i\text{'s place not yet used} @@ forced, by induction round the polygon (4f)
\htmlClass{new}{n \cdot 2 = 2n \text{ symmetries}} \quad \blacksquare @@ stages multiply (4k); each choice does give a symmetry

A triangle has 6: every shuffle of three corners, since in a triangle every two corners are neighbours. A square has 8 of the 24 shuffles of its corners.

19f3Turning a card over

Stands on 19f2 and 18a7 (points in space).

Cut a square of card and draw its outline on the table. Four of its symmetries are turns of the card on the table: a quarter turn, a half turn, three quarters, and doing nothing. The other four need the card lifted. Pick it up, turn it over by half a turn about a line through its middle, and lay it back in the outline. Every symmetry of the square is a turn: four on the table, and four half turns in space.

e,\ \ [2\ 3\ 4\ 1],\ \ [3\ 4\ 1\ 2],\ \ [4\ 1\ 2\ 3] @@ turns on the table: none, a quarter, a half, three quarters
[2\ 1\ 4\ 3],\ \ [4\ 3\ 2\ 1] @@ turned over about a line through the middles of two opposite sides
[1\ 4\ 3\ 2],\ \ [3\ 2\ 1\ 4] @@ turned over about a line through two opposite corners: those two stay

19f5Fewer symmetries

Stands on 19f2.

A figure that is not regular has fewer. A rectangle that is not a square keeps its long sides long, so corner 1 can go anywhere, but its neighbour along the long side must stay along a long side: 4 × 1 = 4. A pinwheel with four bent blades can be turned on the table but not turned over, since its blades would then bend the other way: 4. The letter S has 2: do nothing, and a half turn on the table.

The fewer moves a figure has, the less of it looks alike. A figure with only e looks different from every side.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The symmetries of a regular n-gon form the dihedral group, written D_n or D_{2n}. The count by where one corner goes is the orbit-stabiliser theorem: the number of places corner 1 can reach, times the number of moves that keep it fixed. The repeating patterns of a flat wall fall into 17 kinds, the wallpaper groups.

Who said it first

In 19f.4.5, the palace is the Alhambra in Granada. Branko Grünbaum, “What symmetry groups are present in the Alhambra?” (2006), counted strictly and found fewer than all 17 kinds; counts that read the patterns differently, by Rafael Pérez-Gómez and José María Montesinos among others, found all 17.
