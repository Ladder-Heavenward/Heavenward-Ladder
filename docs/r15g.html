15g · technique

Sums and Σ by hand

Every lower and upper sum of this rung is a sum of many terms, and 15d could finish its squeeze only because 4k’s doubled-sum adds every number up to a given one in one line. The area under the square needs the squares added, and the area under the cube the cubes. This step gives the tools. There are the rules that let such sums be taken apart and shifted. There is one trick that adds many sums at once by making almost every term cancel. And there are the three formulas for the sums of whole numbers, of their squares and of their cubes.

A sum comes apart over adding and lets constants out. Its counting can be shifted. A sum of differences of a list collapses to its last term less its first. And that collapse, run on the gaps between neighbouring cubes and between neighbouring fourth powers, hands over the sums of squares and of cubes.

15g1Taking a sum apart

From here on, for whole p ≤ q, Σᵢ₌ₚ^q t(i) is t(p) + t(p + 1) + ⋯ + t(q), the Σ over the i with p ≤ i ≤ q. When p > q it is the empty sum, 0. The letter i is a dummy, like x in ∫.

Sums-are-linear technique: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (s(i) + t(i)) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ s(i) + Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ t(i), and Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ c · t(i) = c Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ t(i), for any number c. In particular Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ c = nc. Each is proved by induction on n with sum-one-more. The base n = 0 is 0 = 0 + 0 and 0 = c · 0. The step adds s(n + 1) + t(n + 1) to both sides and regroups (regroup for plus on ℝ, turn-about for plus on ℝ). Or it adds c · t(n + 1) and spreads (spread on ℝ).

Shifting the index changes the names, not the sum: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ t(i − 1) = Σⱼ₌₀ⁿ⁻¹ t(j), with j = i − 1 term for term. And a sum splits where you like: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ + Σᵢ₌ₖ₊₁ⁿ, so a sum that starts late is a difference of two that start at 1.

15g2The collapse

Collapsing-sum technique: for any list u₀, u₁, …, uₙ, Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (uᵢ − uᵢ₋₁) = uₙ − u₀. Its accepted name is a telescoping sum.

n = 0:\ \ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{0} = 0 = u_0 - u_0 @@ sum-of-none: the empty sum
\text{suppose } \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} (u_i - u_{i-1}) = u_n - u_0 @@ the hypothesis
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} (u_i - u_{i-1}) = (u_n - u_0) + (u_{n+1} - u_n) @@ sum-one-more
\htmlClass{new}{= u_{n+1} - u_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uₙ cancel (negative-of-a-sum, regroup for plus on ℝ); induction (4h)

15a's widths adding to xₙ − x₀ was collapsing-sum with uᵢ = xᵢ. So was 15a.4.4's gap for an increasing function. The trick is to see a term as a difference: then the sum costs nothing.

\tfrac{1}{i(i + 1)} = \tfrac1i - \tfrac{1}{i + 1} @@ two-factors (9g): two different factors
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{i(i+1)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big(\tfrac1i - \tfrac{1}{i+1}\Big) @@ the same terms
= 1 - \tfrac{1}{n + 1} @@ collapsing-sum with uᵢ = −1/(i + 1), read backwards
\tfrac12 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac{1}{12} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{9900} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{99}{100}} @@ n = 99

15g3Odd numbers, squares and cubes

Odd-numbers-make-squares technique: the first n odd numbers add to n²: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (2i − 1) = n². Each odd number is a difference of squares: i² − (i − 1)² = 2i − 1, by square-of-a-difference (5i). So collapsing-sum with uᵢ = i² gives n² − 0² = n². ∎ So 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16.

Sum-of-squares technique: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ i² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6. 15d.4.1 held this sum in trust. The column below proves it, and that flag is closed.

(i + 1)^3 - i^3 = 3i^2 + 3i + 1 @@ two-term-power (4k), or square-of-a-sum twice
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big((i + 1)^3 - i^3\big) = (n + 1)^3 - 1 @@ collapsing-sum with uᵢ = (i + 1)³
(n + 1)^3 - 1 = 3\textstyle\sum i^2 + 3 \cdot \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} + n @@ sums-are-linear; doubled-sum
3\textstyle\sum i^2 = (n + 1)^3 - (n + 1) - \tfrac{3n(n+1)}{2} = (n + 1)\big(n^2 + 2n - \tfrac{3n}{2}\big) @@ collect; take out n + 1 (spread on ℝ)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 = \tfrac{n(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6}} \quad \blacksquare @@ n² + n/2 = n(2n + 1)/2; divide by 3

Sum-of-cubes technique: Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ i³ = (n(n + 1)/2)², the square of doubled-sum's sum. The same collapse works with (i + 1)⁴ − i⁴ = 4i³ + 6i² + 4i + 1. It gives (n + 1)⁴ − 1 = 4Σi³ + 6Σi² + 4Σi + n, and the algebra leaves n²(n + 1)²/4 (15g.4.4).

\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{20} (3i^2 - 2i + 1) = 3 \cdot 2870 - 2 \cdot 210 + 20 @@ sums-are-linear; sum-of-squares (20 · 21 · 41/6 = 2870); doubled-sum
= \htmlClass{new}{8210} @@ 8610 − 420 + 20
\textstyle\sum_{i=5}^{12} i = \sum_{i=1}^{12} i - \sum_{i=1}^{4} i = 78 - 10 = 68 @@ split, then doubled-sum twice
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{10} i^3 = 55^2 = 3025 @@ sum-of-cubes

One early mathematician found that the ratio came out one third plus a little for the squares at one, two, three and more terms. He wrote that he would take it as established by induction, by which he meant by trying cases, and went on to the cubes and beyond.

Who said it first

Wallis, having found that the ratio came out one third plus a little for the squares at one, two, three and more terms, wrote that he would take it as established by induction, by which he meant by trying cases, and went on to the cubes and beyond.

Wallis, Arithmetica infinitorum (1656), Propositions 19 to 21; paraphrase

In the closer, the formulas by cases are John Wallis’s (1656); the demand for proofs, and the numbers 2^(2ⁿ) + 1, are Pierre de Fermat’s; the factor 641 is Leonhard Euler’s (1732).
