How Does a Small Push Lift a Heavy Weight?

How does a small push lift a heavy weight? A seesaw, a crowbar, a wheelbarrow, your own forearm, the gears of a clock: each is a stiff thing turning on a fixed support, and each obeys one law, proved about 2250 years ago. The law is a ratio: weights balance at distances in the reverse ratio of the weights. This page sets up the physics words from nothing, and states the law. It puts the law to work three ways: to balance a bar, to multiply a force, and to change how fast something turns. Along the way rung 12 earns its keep. “Is there a place where this bar balances?” is exactly a question the intermediate value technique answers. And the way to find the place without any formula is 12e's halving.

Weight times signed distance from the support is a turning effect. A bar balances when the turning effects add to 0. The total slides continuously from one sign to the other as the support slides along the bar. So a balance point exists, and halving finds it. Every lever and gear trades force for distance at a fixed ratio.

12phys1The Words

Physics needs a handful of words this ladder has not built. Each gets its line, in plain words, and none of them needs any shape.

From here on, on this page, a force is a push or a pull on a thing. The newton, written N, is the unit of force: 1 N = 1 kg · m/s². It is the force that, acting alone, changes the speed of a 1 kg mass by 1 m/s every second (5j's acceleration). A thing's weight is a force: the pull of the Earth on it. Near the ground a thing of mass m has weight m · g, with g ≈ 9.8 m/s², so about 9.8 N for each kilogram.

From here on, on this page, a lever is a stiff bar, one that does not bend, resting on a support called its pivot, or fulcrum. A place along the bar is named by its position: a real, in metres (5j's length), measured from the bar's left end, as a place on 8a's line. The distance between two places is 8h's distance |x − y|, a length in metres; nothing about shape is meant.

From here on, on this page, “turns” is a physical word. A bar turns on its pivot when one side goes down and the other side up. A wheel turns on its shaft, the rod it is fixed to, when its edge moves past a mark that stays still. One turn of a wheel is the motion that brings every place on its edge back where it started for the first time. Turns are counted like any quantity, as 3 turns or 1/12 of a turn. A bar balances on its pivot when, let go at rest, it does not begin to turn either way.

Page letters, and reuses, said out loud. On this page, as is usual, slanted W names a weight (not 7·phys's upright W for watt). m names a mass, and g the 9.8 m/s² above (not a function). x names a position and p the pivot's position. L names a bar's length (not 11·chem's latent heat, nor a limit). T names the net turning effect below (not 11·chem's temperature), and F a force; d names a distance from the pivot. Upright N is the newton, not a threshold. And the word balance, 5j's instrument that compares masses, is used here also as a verb, for a bar at rest on its pivot. The instrument is a lever that balances, so the two uses agree.

Mass and weight are kept apart. 5j's mass, in kilograms, is how much stuff a thing has; its weight, in newtons, is how hard the Earth pulls it. A 20 kg child has weight about 196 N.

12phys2The Law of the Lever: A Ratio

The oldest book on the lever that has come down whole is a chain of proofs, laid out like the oldest geometry book: postulates first, then propositions proved from them. Its first postulate is something nobody would argue with. Equal weights at equal distances from the support balance. Equal weights at unequal distances do not: the bar goes down on the side of the weight that is farther out.

From that and a few postulates like it, the book proves its law. Two weights balance at distances from the support reciprocally proportional to the weights. It proves this first when the two have a common measure, then when they do not. “Reciprocally proportional” is 5j's inversely proportional: if one weight is three times the other, it sits at a third of the distance. With weights W₁ and W₂ on opposite sides of the pivot at distances d₁ and d₂, the law says W₁/W₂ = d₂/d₁. Take weights with a common measure, such as 3 kg and 2 kg. The proof cuts both into equal pieces and sets the pieces out along the bar. Each piece on one side is matched by a piece at the same distance on the other side. Then the first postulate does all the work. For weights with no common measure, such as two whose ratio is √2, the book squeezes, in the spirit rung 8 made exact.

\tfrac{W_1}{W_2} = \tfrac{d_2}{d_1} @@ the lever's inverse ratio
W_1 d_1 = W_2 d_2 @@ ratios-cross-multiply (5j): cross-multiply; both distances are positive
20 \cdot 2.4 = 80 \cdot d @@ a 20 kg child 2.4 m out; where does an 80 kg adult sit?
d = \htmlClass{new}{0.6} \text{ m} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers by 1/80 (5g): four times the weight, a quarter of the distance

Weight times distance from the pivot measures how hard a weight turns the bar. In newtons, the child's is 20 · 9.8 · 2.4 ≈ 470 N · m. But the same 9.8 multiplies both sides of the balance, and multiplying-keeps-answers lets it go, so balancing in kilograms is safe.

12phys3Many Weights: The Turning Effect

With several weights, sign the distances. Put the pivot at position p and a weight W at position x. Then x − p is 8h's displacement of x from p, positive on the right of the pivot and negative on the left.

From here on, on this page, the turning effect about p of a weight W at position x is W · (x − p). It is positive when it turns the bar right side down, negative when left side down. The net turning effect of weights W₁, ⋯, W_n at x₁, ⋯, x_n is T(p) = Σ_{i≤n} W_i(x_i − p), 9g's Σ with real terms. Its accepted names are moment and torque. In newtons it is measured in N · m; in kilograms, in kg · m.

The law, for many weights, as physics: the bar balances on a pivot at p exactly when T(p) = 0. If T(p) > 0 it turns right side down, and if T(p) < 0 left side down. For two weights on opposite sides this is W₁d₁ = W₂d₂ again. And a bar's own weight counts. A bar that is the same all along (uniform) can be cut into pairs of equal pieces at equal distances on either side of its middle. So by the first postulate its own weight turns it as if all of it sat at its middle.

\begin{aligned}&\text{2 kg at } 0,\ \ 5 \text{ kg at } 1,\ \ 3 \text{ kg at } 4,\\ &\quad \ \ \text{the bar's own 2 kg at its middle } 2\end{aligned} @@ a bar 4 m long
T(p) = 2(0 - p) + 5(1 - p) + 3(4 - p) + 2(2 - p) @@ the turning effects, signed by the displacement x − p
T(p) = \htmlClass{new}{21 - 12p}\ \text{kg} \cdot \text{m} @@ 5i's collecting: 0 + 5 + 12 + 4 = 21, and 2 + 5 + 3 + 2 = 12

12phys4Why a Balance Point Must Exist

From here on, on this page, a balance point of a loaded bar is a position p on it at which a pivot leaves the bar balanced: T(p) = 0. “Point” here names a place along the bar, nothing more.

Balance-point technique: a loaded bar has a place where it balances. Precisely: take weights at positions on a bar from 0 to L, with some weight not at 0 and some weight not at L. Then there is a balance point p with 0 < p < L. Before computing anything, rung 12 says yes, and the argument is worth more than the number.

T(0) = \sum W_i x_i \gt 0 @@ pivot at the left end: every x_i ≥ 0, and some weight is out on the bar
T(L) = \sum W_i (x_i - L) \lt 0 @@ pivot at the right end: every x_i − L ≤ 0, and some weight is short of L
T(p) = \Big(\sum W_i x_i\Big) - \Big(\sum W_i\Big) p @@ termwise, factor-out (9g): a linear function in p
T \text{ continuous on } [0, L] @@ lines-are-continuous (11a)
\text{some } p,\ 0 \lt p \lt L,\ T(p) = 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ in-between-value (12a)

Notice what the argument did not use: the particular weights, how many there are, or even that they hang at single places. Replace them with a sack of sand slumped along the bar, a tapering oar, a bat. The physics premise, the one this page takes from experience, is that sliding the pivot a little changes the net turning effect a little. T is continuous in p for any stiff loaded bar. Then the same lines go through. At the left end everything turns the bar one way, at the right end the other, and in-between-value gives a balance point. No-holes, rung 8's, is what makes it a place on the bar and not a hole.

12phys5Finding It by Halving

Existence is not location. To locate it, do what 12e did for √2: test the middle of a trap, see which way the bar turns, keep the half where the sign still changes. Physically the test is cheap: set the bar on a knife-edge at the middle and watch which way it goes. The class's bar, pretending not to know the formula, looking only at signs:

[0, 4]:\ T(0) = 21 \gt 0,\ \ T(4) = -27 \lt 0 @@ a trap for T
p = 2:\ T(2) = \htmlClass{new}{-3} \lt 0 @@ turns left side down: keep [0, 2]
p = 1:\ T(1) = \htmlClass{new}{9} \gt 0 @@ right side down: keep [1, 2]
p = 1.5:\ T(1.5) = 3 \gt 0 @@ right side down: keep [1.5, 2]
p = 1.75:\ T(1.75) = \htmlClass{new}{0} @@ balance, on the fourth test (middle-of-a-trap: the middle is a zero)

By 12e's halvings-width, n tests on a bar of length L trap the balance point in a stretch of length L/2ⁿ. Ten tests on a 4 m bar pin it within 4/1024 m, about 4 mm. The method never needed the weights, their places, or the formula: only the sign. That is why it works on the sack of sand.

For weights at single places you can also solve directly, because T(p) = 0 is a linear function set to 0.

\sum W_i (x_i - p) = 0 @@ balance
\sum W_i x_i - p \sum W_i = 0 @@ termwise, factor-out, middle-swap
p = \htmlClass{new}{\frac{\sum W_i x_i}{\sum W_i}} @@ adding-keeps-answers, then multiplying-keeps-answers by the reciprocal of the total weight, which is positive
p = \tfrac{0 + 5 + 12 + 4}{12} = \tfrac{21}{12} = 1.75 \text{ m} @@ the class's bar: agrees with the halving

Balance-formula technique, just proved: the balance point is the weighted average of the positions, each counted as heavily as its weight. Two tempting moves are outside the plan. It is not the plain average of the positions. Here the plain average (0 + 1 + 4 + 2)/4 = 1.75 happens to agree. But move the 5 kg to 0.5 m, and the plain average says 1.625 while the balance point is 18.5/12 = 37/24 ≈ 1.54 m. And it is not the middle between the outermost weights (2 m here), which ignores everything in between. Its accepted names are the centre of gravity and the centre of mass.

12phys6The Steelyard: The Law Run Backwards

In Roman times goods were weighed with a steelyard: a bar with a hook close to the pivot and one sliding weight, the poise, on the long arm. Hang the load, slide the poise until the bar balances, and read the load off marks on the bar. It is the law of the lever solved for the unknown weight.

m \cdot 5 = 1 \cdot d @@ a load of m kg on the hook 5 cm from the pivot, poise 1 kg at d cm (the bar's own weight ignored)
d = 5m @@ the poise moves 5 cm for each kilogram of load
d = 30 \text{ cm} \;\Rightarrow\; m = \htmlClass{new}{6} \text{ kg} @@ undo: multiply by ⅕

d = 5m is directly proportional (5j), so the marks are evenly spaced. Doubling the poise halves the spacing and doubles what the arm can weigh.

12phys7Multiplying a Force

Read the law the other way: a small weight far out balances a large weight close in. Put your hand where the small weight was and you have a machine that multiplies force.

From here on, on this page, the effort is the force you supply to a lever, and the load is the force it works against. The mechanical advantage is the ratio load : effort, read as the fraction load/effort. For a lever in balance it equals the ratio of the effort's distance from the pivot to the load's.

F_{\text{effort}} \cdot 1.2 = F_{\text{load}} \cdot 0.1 @@ hands 1.2 m from the pivot, the stone 0.1 m: the law of the lever
\tfrac{F_{\text{load}}}{F_{\text{effort}}} = \tfrac{1.2}{0.1} = \htmlClass{new}{12} @@ multiplying-keeps-answers; the mechanical advantage is the ratio of the arms
F_{\text{effort}} = 50 \text{ N} \;\Rightarrow\; F_{\text{load}} = 600 \text{ N} @@ a 50 N push holds the weight of about 61 kg (600/9.8)

An old story tells that the lever's first prover boasted he could move the Earth, if he had a place to stand. With a long enough arm, the arithmetic agrees. Levers come in three arrangements. A seesaw or crowbar has the pivot between effort and load. A wheelbarrow or nutcracker has the load between pivot and effort, so the effort's arm is always the longer and the advantage always more than 1. Your forearm has the effort between. The biceps, the muscle at the front of the upper arm, pulls about 4 cm from the elbow, the pivot. A ball in your hand is about 35 cm out. Holding 5 kg (49 N) takes about 49 · 35/4 ≈ 430 N of muscle: a mechanical advantage of 4/35, about 1/9. The body is not being foolish; it is buying distance.

12phys8The Price Is Distance

The physics premise, from watching any seesaw: when a stiff bar turns a little on its pivot, each place on it moves in proportion to its distance from the pivot. The two sides move opposite ways. (The reason is geometry, which this ladder builds on rung 18; here it is taken from measurement, as 9·phys took its premise.) So when the crowbar's stone rises 1 cm, your hands move down 12 cm.

From here on, on this page, the work done by a force of F newtons that moves its thing d metres along the force is F · d joules. It is 7·phys's energy, since 1 N · m = 1 kg · m²/s² = 1 J. Lifting a mass m by a height h is the work m · g · h, 7·phys's line.

50 \text{ N} \cdot 0.12 \text{ m} = \htmlClass{new}{6} \text{ J} @@ your end: a small force a long way
600 \text{ N} \cdot 0.01 \text{ m} = \htmlClass{new}{6} \text{ J} @@ the stone's end: a big force a short way. The same

An ideal lever passes energy through unchanged: you may have a big force or a long distance, not both for free. Turning effects and work share the unit N · m, but they are different quantities, and physicists keep the name joule for work. Your forearm takes the bargain the other way round: the muscle pulls hard over a short distance and the hand moves about nine times as far.

12phys9Gear Trains: Ratios Compose

From here on, on this page, a gear is a wheel with teeth along its edge, fixed on a shaft. Two gears mesh when their teeth interlock, so that one turning makes the other turn; meshed gears turn opposite ways. An idler is a gear placed between two others only to pass the turning on. Gears fixed on one shaft turn together, the same number of turns.

Meshed teeth pass one for one. A driving gear with n₁ teeth, turned once, pushes n₁ teeth past the meeting place. That is n₁/n₂ of a turn of a driven gear with n₂ teeth. Count turns one way as positive and the other way as negative. Then a mesh takes t turns in to −(n₁/n₂)t turns out, a linear function with start 0 (9e). And a train of meshes is a chain of such functions, composed (9b).

Gear-train technique: a train of meshes, each taking t to m_k t, takes t to (m₁ m₂ ⋯ m_k)t: the ratios multiply. By induction on the number of meshes: m₂(m₁t) = (m₂m₁)t by regroup for times on ℝ, and turn-about for times on ℝ orders the factors. ∎

10 \text{ teeth} \to 30:\ \ \times\big(-\tfrac{10}{30}\big) @@ the minute shaft's gear drives a 30: a third of a turn, the other way
\text{8 teeth on that shaft} \to 32:\ \ \times\big(-\tfrac{8}{32}\big) @@ a gear fixed on the 30's shaft turns with it; the second mesh
\big(-\tfrac{10}{30}\big)\big(-\tfrac{8}{32}\big) = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{12}} @@ gear-train: multiply. two-negatives: two reversals make the same way

One turn of the minute hand is 1/12 of a turn of the hour hand. No single mesh of sensible gears gives 1/12, so clockmakers compound. Two moves outside the plan. Adding the ratios, 1/3 + 1/4 = 7/12, is wrong. The second mesh acts on what the first produced, so it scales it, and scales compose by multiplying. And counting an idler: put a 30-tooth gear between a 12 and a 36. The train gives (−12/30)(−30/36) = 12/36 = 1/3: the idler's teeth cancel, whatever their number (reciprocal on ℚ). It changes only which way the output turns, and where it sits. A bicycle chain is a mesh without the reversal. A 42-tooth front ring and a 14-tooth rear gear turn the wheel 42/14 = 3 times for each turn of the pedals. Say a road wheel carries the bike about 2.1 m each turn (measured by rolling it once). Then it carries you about 6.3 m per pedal turn.

The turning effect a gear passes on goes the other way from its turns. A gear turning three times slower turns three times harder. So turning effect times turns, which measures the work, again comes through unchanged.

Who said it first

The law of the lever is Archimedes’, proved in On the Equilibrium of Planes, laid out like Euclid’s Elements. The story of moving the Earth is told by Pappus of Alexandria, some five centuries later. The physicist of question 12·phys.12.4 is Ernst Mach, in The Science of Mechanics (1883).

Archimedes postulates that equal weights at equal distances from the support balance, and that equal weights at unequal distances do not, the bar going down on the side of the weight that is farther out.

Archimedes, On the Equilibrium of Planes I, Postulate 1, after Heath, The Works of Archimedes (1897)

From that and a few postulates like it, he proves that two magnitudes balance at distances from the support reciprocally proportional to the magnitudes, first when the two have a common measure, then when they do not.

Archimedes, On the Equilibrium of Planes I, Propositions 6 and 7, after Heath (1897)
