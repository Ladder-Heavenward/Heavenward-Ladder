Can a Loom Follow a Program?

Can a loom follow a program? Two centuries ago, weavers of patterned silk needed a helper perched on the loom, lifting the right threads by hand for every row. Then a chain of punched cards took the helper’s place: a hole meant lift, no hole meant stay. The loom wove whatever the cards said, and could weave it again. This page reads each card as a row of 1s and 0s (39a), the chain of cards as a list a machine follows (39j), repeats as 40b’s loops, traces the cards into the first computing machines, and finds the one thing a loom cannot do: decide what to weave from what it has woven.

Each card is one row of the cloth: a hole for each warp thread to lift, none for each to stay. The chain of cards is a program with no choices: do row 1, then row 2, and so on. A repeat is a loop: join the chain into a ring. The cards passed from looms to census machines and on to computers. But the loom never reads its own cloth, so it cannot branch, and it can compute nothing.

40eng21One Card per Row: Holes Lift Threads

Zoe weaves patterned cloth on a card loom. Above the loom, a row of hooks holds the warp threads, one hook per thread. Before each pass of the weft, a card is pressed against the hooks: where it has a hole, a hook goes through and lifts its thread; where it has none, the hook is pushed aside and its thread stays down.

\text{hole} = 1 = \text{lift},\ \ \text{no hole} = 0 = \text{stay} @@ true and false as 1 and 0 (39a)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one card} = \text{one row of the weave grid}} @@ as in 20·eng’s grids of 1s and 0s

From here on, on this page, a card is one row of 1s and 0s, as long as the number of warp threads, and a chain is the cards laced together in order.

40eng22The Chain of Cards Is a List a Machine Follows

After each pass, the loom turns the chain on by one card. The cloth grows row by row exactly as the chain lists.

\text{for each card in order: lift by its holes, pass the weft, beat it in} @@ a machine following a list (39j)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the weaver never decides a row; the chain does}} @@

Card-row technique: write the weave as a grid of 1s and 0s, cut one card per row, and lace the cards in order; the loom then weaves the grid exactly, as often as asked, with no one choosing threads. Zoe punches a 20-card chain for a twill border, runs it, and the cloth comes off with every crossing where the grid put it.

40eng23Repeats as Loops

Most cloth repeats. A 2/2 twill repeats every 4 rows; a damask flower might repeat every 400. Rather than punch the same cards again and again, join the end of the chain back to its start.

\text{straight chain: } 1\,000 \text{ cards} @@ 
\text{ring of } 4 \text{ cards, run } 250 \text{ times} @@ a loop (40b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a loop: a jump back to the start, taken every time}} @@ no condition

The loom’s ring of cards is a loop that always jumps back: it never asks whether to stop. The weaver stops it by hand.

40eng24From Loom Cards to Punched Cards

The card loom showed that a long, exact sequence of choices could be stored in holes and run by a machine. The idea travelled.

whenwhat the cards did
early 1800scard looms weave patterned silk
1830sa design for a calculating engine takes its instructions from loom-style cards
1890punched cards hold each person’s answers in a national census, sorted and counted by machine
1950s to 1970scomputer programs and data are typed onto punched cards

The punched card carried the loom’s idea, a sequence of choices stored as holes and read by a machine, from weaving into counting and then into computing.

40eng25What a Loom Can’t Do: Branch on What It Has Woven

A computer’s program can test a value and jump one way or the other (40b): if the count is done, stop; if not, go on. The loom has no such test. It never reads the cloth it has made, so its next row never depends on what came before.

\text{loom: next card} = \text{the card after this one, always} @@ 
\text{computer: next step may depend on what is stored} @@ a jump taken only if (40b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no test, no branch: a loom can store a pattern but compute nothing}} @@

A loom follows its list exactly but cannot choose; a machine that computes must also read what it has done and jump on it. That one addition, a test that picks the next step, is what turned stored patterns into programs, and the reason the calculating engine’s designer added it to the cards he borrowed.

Accepted names

The card loom is the Jacquard loom and its chain the card chain; the calculating engine was Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine; the census machines were Herman Hollerith’s tabulators. A test that picks the next step is a conditional branch.

Who said it first

Joseph Marie Jacquard demonstrated his card loom in the early 1800s; Babbage designed the Analytical Engine with cards in the 1830s, and Ada Lovelace wrote that it “weaves algebraic patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves”; Hollerith’s tabulators counted the 1890 United States census (not yet revisited for the dates).
