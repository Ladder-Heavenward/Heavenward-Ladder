7·logi · logistics

Can these meetings fit in three rooms?

Can these meetings fit in three rooms? Anyone who has run a library, a school, a hospital's operating theatres or an airport's gates has stared at a list of bookings and asked it. Each booking holds a room from when it starts until it ends. The question looks like a puzzle of shuffling: try this meeting here, that one there, and start again when something clashes. With a handful of meetings the shuffling works. With thirty it would take longer than anyone has, since every meeting could go in any room. What makes it easy, once you see it, is a single number: the most meetings going on at any one moment. If that number is four, no shuffling can save three rooms, and a short argument by refutation says why. If it is three, a plain rule that hands out rooms in order of starting time always gets by with three. The page proves both, and finds that the hard search was settled all along.

Write each meeting as the stretch of time from its start up to its end. At each start, count the meetings going on. The largest of those counts is the most going on at any moment. No schedule uses fewer rooms than that, since meetings going on together need rooms of their own. To use exactly that many, take the meetings in order of start, and give each the lowest numbered room that is free when it starts.

From here on, on this page, a time is written as hours and minutes since midnight, 09:30, and read as that many minutes, 570. A meeting from s to e is going on at a time t when s ≤ t < e: it holds its room from its start up to, but not including, its end.

So a meeting ending at 11:00 and one starting at 11:00 can share a room. A room holds one meeting at a time: two meetings in one room are never going on at the same time. That is the whole of the rule.

The most at once, found at a start

At 10:15, A, B, C and D are all going on. Four at once. There are endless moments in a day, since a time can be any fraction of a minute. Which ones need checking? Only the starts. Busiest-start technique: the most meetings going on at any moment is reached at the start of one of them.

\text{at } t,\ \text{the meetings } M_1, \ldots, M_w \text{ are going on} @@ any moment, and the meetings going on then
s = \text{the latest of their starts} @@ greatest-member (4k): a finite set of numbers with a member
s_i \le s \le t \lt e_i \text{ for each } i @@ s is the greatest start; s is one of the starts, each at most t
\htmlClass{new}{\text{all } w \text{ are going on at } s} \quad \blacksquare @@ each started by s and ends after it

So a day of n meetings has n moments worth checking. At each start, count the meetings going on. That is 7b's finite-search: a look through finitely many cases, each settled by comparing numbers (4h's any-two-compare). For Monday the starts give these counts.

Four at once will not fit in three

Room-count technique: if w meetings are going on at one moment, no schedule fits them into fewer than w rooms. The statement wanted is a denial, so 7e's refutation does it.

\text{suppose a schedule puts them in fewer than } w \text{ rooms} @@ to be refuted
\text{each of the } w \text{ has a room} @@ a schedule gives every meeting a room
\text{no two of them share a room} @@ they are going on together, and a room holds one at a time
\text{a matching of the } w \text{ with a part of the rooms} @@ different meetings, different rooms (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a collision with no-room}} \quad \blacksquare @@ no-room (6·logi): w members cannot match a part of fewer

On Monday, A, B, C and D go on together at 10:15. So Monday's bookings do not fit in three rooms, however they are shuffled. The interview has to move. Notice what kind of proof that was. 7e's test: the statement wanted is a denial, and the supposition was refuted, so no rung 7 was used. The question had a settled answer all along.

A rule that uses exactly that many

Move the interview to 13:00 to 13:30. Now the counts at the starts are at most 3. Does that mean three rooms will do? A count is not a schedule. Here is a rule that makes one. First-free-room rule: take the meetings in order of start; give each the lowest numbered room that is free at its start, opening a new room only when every open room is busy.

A\ 09{:}00 \to 1,\ \ B\ 09{:}30 \to 2,\ \ C\ 10{:}00 \to 3 @@ each finds every open room busy
E\ 11{:}00 \to 1,\ \ F\ 11{:}30 \to 2 @@ A ended 10:30; B ended 11:00
G\ 12{:}00 \to 3,\ \ H\ 12{:}30 \to 1,\ \ D\ 13{:}00 \to 2 @@ C ended 12:00; E ended 12:30; F ended 13:00
\htmlClass{new}{3 \text{ rooms}} @@ the most going on at once is 3

First-free-room technique: the first-free-room rule never opens more rooms than the most meetings going on at once. With the room-count technique, it uses exactly that many.

\text{the rule opens room } r \text{ for a meeting } M \text{ starting at } s @@ any room it ever opens
\text{rooms } 1, \ldots, r - 1 \text{ are all busy at } s @@ that is when the rule opens a new room
\text{each holds a meeting that started by } s \text{ and ends after } s @@ meetings are taken in order of start
r \text{ meetings going on at } s @@ those r − 1, and M
\htmlClass{new}{r \le w} \quad \blacksquare @@ w is the most going on at any moment

It is 6·logi's picking in turn, with an order that makes it work. There, picking down the board could strand a job. Here, picking in order of start never strands a meeting, because a meeting that finds every room busy proves the rooms were needed.

The search nobody needs to run

Could a computer just try every schedule? Give each of n meetings one of 3 rooms: by counted-functions (4k), there are 3ⁿ ways. For Monday's 8 meetings that is 6561. For a conference centre's 30 bookings it is 205 891 132 094 649, which at a billion tries a second is more than two days. The answer is settled either way, by 7b's finite-search, so the either-way principle is never needed. But nobody wants to wait for it. The busiest-start technique checks 30 starts, and the first-free-room rule writes the schedule in one pass. The most meetings going on at once is the number of rooms needed, no more and no fewer.

7e's test says more. In the first-free-room proof the supposition, if you write it as one, is used once, at the end. So the proof is direct underneath: every room the rule opens comes with a moment when that many meetings are going on. The rule hands you the crowded moment as well as the schedule. Gates at Sea-Tac, beds on a hospital ward and trucks at a loading dock are scheduled by rules of this shape every day.

Who said it first

Meetings as stretches of time, and the graph that joins two when they clash, were studied by György Hajós (1957) and in a paper by Paul Gilmore and Alan Hoffman (1964). That the rooms needed equal the most going on at once is part of the theory of perfect graphs that Claude Berge began around 1960. The first-free-room rule, in order of start, is a greedy rule from the textbooks of the 1970s. The last instant of a stretch with no last instant was argued over by Aristotle in his Physics, and by the schoolmen of the fourteenth century, Walter Burley and William of Ockham among them, as the problem of first and last instants.

Accepted names

A stretch of time from s up to, but not including, e is a half-open interval. The problem is interval scheduling, or interval partitioning. The most going on at once is the depth of the list. The graph that joins clashing meetings is an interval graph, and giving rooms is colouring it. The first-free-room rule is a greedy algorithm. That depth is enough says interval graphs are perfect. That every two clashing stretches all share a moment is the simplest case of Helly's theorem (Eduard Helly, 1913).

\text{at no start are more than } 3 \text{ going on} @@ fine: checked at each start
\text{so give each meeting room } 1, 2 \text{ or } 3 \text{ by its place in the list, in turns} @@ a rule that ignores the times
\text{so } 3 \text{ rooms hold them} @@ not allowed: a count is not a schedule; turns by list place can put two that clash in one room
