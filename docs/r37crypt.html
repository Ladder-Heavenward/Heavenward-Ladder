37·crypt · cryptography

Can a voice be hidden?

Can a voice be hidden? A voice on a wire can be overheard, and 9·crypt locked letters but not sounds. This page turns a voice upside down in pitch, which hides it from no one with the same box, and then follows the telephone that carried leaders’ voices in 1943 under a key no one could read. It hides a message in the quietest digit of a song, where no ear will find it and any program will. And it ends with the case of the voices people hear when records are played backwards, which say much more about the listeners than about the records.

Flipping a voice’s band upside down is its own undoing, and hides nothing from anyone with the flip. A voice taken apart into levels can be locked level by level with a fresh random key, read by remainders. A message written into the last bit of each sample is about 90 dB under the music. A voice played backwards keeps its recipe and loses its words, and listeners told what to hear will hear it.

37crypt1A voice upside down

A phone line carries about 300 to 3300 Hz. The oldest scrambler sent each turning at f out at 3600 − f instead: the band is turned upside down, low for high. Every vowel’s hills (37f3) land somewhere else, and the voice comes out as a quacking no one can follow.

Flip-band technique: the flip f → 3600 − f keeps the power of every turning, moves it to a new frequency, and is its own undoing. Proof: it sends each turning to one turning of the same size; and 3600 − (3600 − f) = f, so flipping twice is doing nothing, as with 9·crypt1’s key that undoes itself.

\text{hills near } 730,\ 1090,\ 2440 \text{ Hz} @@ 37f3’s hills, measured for “ah”
\to\ 2870,\ 2510,\ 1160 \text{ Hz} @@ the flip-band technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no vowel at all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hills in an order no tube makes

So the flip hides a voice from an ear but not from a flipper. It has one key, the number 3600, and an eavesdropper who guesses the trick needs only build the same box. 9·crypt3 said what that costs: with fewer keys than messages, the cipher rules messages out, and here it rules out all but one.

37crypt2A voice locked level by level

By 37f2 a voice is a buzz times a tube, so it can be sent as a few numbers, many times a second: the buzz’s pitch, and the level of the voice in each of a handful of bands. The telephone used for the most secret calls of 1943 rounded each level to one of six steps, 0 to 5, fifty times a second, and locked each with a key step drawn at random from a record that had one copy at each end.

Level-key technique: add to each level a fresh random key step, spread evenly over 0 to 5, and keep the remainder by 6; the step sent is then spread evenly over 0 to 5 whatever the voice, and the receiver gets the level back by adding 6 minus the key and keeping the remainder. Proof: for each level and each step sent, exactly one key step gives it, as with 9·crypt2’s one key for each pair, so each step sent has chance 1/6; and by 4·crypt1 adding k and then 6 − k is adding the remainder of 6 by 6, which is 0.

\text{level } 4,\ \text{key } 3:\ 4 + 3 = 7,\ \text{remainder } 1 @@ sent: 1
\text{receiver}:\ 1 + (6 - 3) = 4 @@ the level-key technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the line carries } 1,\ \text{as likely as any step}} \quad \blacksquare @@ an eavesdropper learns nothing

The price is the price of 9·crypt’s one-time key: the records had to be made in pairs, carried by hand to each end, and used once. A record played twice would give, by 9·crypt4, the difference of two voices’ levels, and the key would be gone.

37crypt3A message in the quietest digit

Hiding is not locking: a locked voice is seen and not read, a hidden one is not seen at all. A CD sample is 16 bits. Replace the last bit of each sample with a bit of a message, and the sound moves by at most one step.

Last-bit technique: writing random message bits into the last bit of every 16-bit sample adds a hiss about 90 dB below the loudest turning, and carries 44 100 bits a second in each channel. Proof: each sample moves by −q, 0 or q, and if the old and new last bits are each 0 or 1 by even chance, the average square of the move is q²/2; the loudest turning’s is (2¹⁵q)²/2 (37·comp1), and 10 log₁₀ 2³⁰ ≈ 90.3.

180 \times 44\,100 \times 2 \approx 15.9 \text{ million bits} @@ both channels
15.9 \times 10^6 / 8 \approx 2 \text{ million letters} @@ a long novel, in the hiss
\htmlClass{new}{\text{unheard, and gone after coding}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the hidden-hiss technique drops exactly such bits

No ear finds the novel, but any program that reads the last bits does, so a careful hider locks the message first. And it does not survive the trip to a phone: 37·comp4’s coder, keeping only the bits the ear needs, throws the last bit away first.

37crypt4Voices backwards

Now and then someone plays a record backwards and hears a voice. Played backwards, a sound s(t) becomes s(−t). Each turning a cos(2πft + φ) becomes a cos(2πft − φ), since cos(−x) = cos x: the same frequency, the same size, the turn reversed.

Backwards-recipe technique: a sound played backwards has, over the whole stretch, the same recipe as forwards, with only the turns reversed; but every change inside it runs the other way. Proof: the turnings line above, and the turn-free technique (37d4): the turns change no power at any frequency. The changes run backwards because s(−t) takes at t the value s took at −t.

\text{over the second: the same recipe} @@ the backwards-recipe technique: it still sounds like a voice
\text{inside it: a rising “yes?” falls; a “t” is a hiss, then a click, then a hush} @@ 37f5’s attacks become swells
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a voice’s colour, with none of its words}} \quad \blacksquare @@ which leaves room for guessing

Laid down, measured on listeners, with its rate: a listener who is told a phrase before hearing a backwards recording reports that phrase far more often than one who is not, and few untold listeners report any words at all. The ear (37c4) and the brain behind it fill a voice’s colour with the words they expect. So the backwards voice is mostly in the listener. Some bands, enjoying the fuss, put real backwards messages on records on purpose; one says, in reverse, that the music is reversible but time is not.

The accepted names

The flipped band is speech inversion; the box that takes a voice apart into levels is a vocoder; hiding a message in the last bit is least-significant-bit steganography. Playing sound backwards to hear or plant a message is backmasking, and hearing words in noise because you expect them is pareidolia, here primed listening.

Who said it first

Homer Dudley built the vocoder at Bell Labs in the 1930s. The locked telephone of 1943 was SIGSALY, linking Washington and London, with keys on pairs of records. The Beatles put a reversed vocal at the end of “Rain” in 1966. The Electric Light Orchestra’s “Fire on High” (1975) carries the reversed line about music and time, and “Weird Al” Yankovic’s “Nature Trail to Hell” (1984) a reversed joke about cheese. A committee of the California State Assembly heard claims of hidden backwards messages in rock records in 1982, and John Vokey and Don Read tested listeners in 1985 and found that the messages lived in what the listeners were told.

A proof that is not allowed

“A backwards recording has the same recipe as forwards, so it says the same thing.” Not allowed: the recipe over the whole stretch is the same, but words are in the changes, which the backwards-recipe technique says run the other way; the recipe of a whole second tells the voice, not the words.
