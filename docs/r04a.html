4a · proof

Prove It

Since 1a you have worked lines; since 2a, rows; since 3a, boxes. Every row came with a reason (2a3). Those arguments convinced, and they were right. But they used a thing the ladder had never built: the proof itself. Counting, induction and lists are built in the steps after this one, so here they are held in trust, each flagged where it opens and closed where it is built. That is the trade: the proof comes first, and every step after it can be written as one. This step starts with questions, and each one picks up your last answer. Work them in order. By the end you will have built the thing the ladder has been holding in trust, and then the step gives it a syntax.

A row’s reason has been a given, an axiom or a tech all along (1a5, 1a6, 2a3). One thing is new here: what a proof proves is always “from these givens, this foot”. Change the givens and you have a different proof. A given nothing uses is harmless.

Anything else is a wish: a line written with no given, axiom or tech behind it, such as “obvious” or “everyone knows” said in the middle of an argument. A wish stays a wish however true its statement is. It has two repairs: earn it from lines before it, or move it to the start as a given. And a reason points back, always, so that tracing a line through its reasons ends at givens and axioms and never goes round.

Argue something from scratch. Then look at what your argument is made of: lines, what each stands under, and an order. Collect every line any argument could write, and see what kind of set that is. Then write it down the short way.

4a8What You Built

The syntax · footings 4a8 to 4a20; read them once the chain is done

Look back at the chain. In 4a2 every line turned out to carry two things: the statement, and the collection of suppositions it stands under. That collection may be empty, and the last line's is.

A line of a proof is a pair: the suppositions it stands under, and the statement it says.

Example: in the fish argument, the line “the fish is a salmon” is the line ({the fish is a smolt}, the fish is a salmon). The last line is (∅, if the fish is a smolt, the fish swims).

Write the pair as (S, R), with S the collection of suppositions and R the statement. The pair is 2h's pair, so (S, R) and (R, S) are different things. Two lines are the same line exactly when both parts match.

In 4a3 the argument became a set of pairs; in 4a5 you wrote the rule for leaning; in 4a6 and 4a7 you found a set the rules cannot get out of, whose lines standing under nothing are the statements the givens force.

That set is what the ladder has been holding in trust since 1a. Footings 4a9 to 4a20 state it once and name it.

4a9A Proof Is a List

A list of n lines is a function from n to lines. 3c showed that the number n is the set of the numbers before it: 4 is {0, 1, 2, 3}. So a list of four lines is a function L from {0, 1, 2, 3}, and L(2) is line 2.

Which lines come before line k? Exactly the numbers that are members of k, because k is the set of the numbers before it. The order of the lines costs nothing new.

Now the rule. Fix a collection G of given statements. A list L of n lines is a proof from G when every line L(k) = (S, R) is one of these:

  • Given. R is a member of G, or an axiom, or a technique already earned. S may be anything.
  • Supposed. R is a member of S. You may suppose anything, as long as it is kept in S.
  • Moved. R comes by one move from given statements and from lines numbered before k, and every one of those lines stands under suppositions that are a part of S. Repeating a line above, unchanged, counts as a move that does nothing; its accepted name is reiteration.
  • Dropped. R comes by a tech that ends a supposition. It leans on earlier lines that stand under S with one supposition added, and on lines under S itself if it needs them, and the added supposition leaves. These are 3a’s outputs and 2a’s cases, written as pairs. Prove an if (3a3) takes (S with P added, Q) to “if P then Q”. Refute (3a4) takes (S with P added, ⊥) to “not P”. Cases (2a8) takes (S, P or Q), (S with P added, R) and (S with Q added, R) to R. Use a some (2b) takes (S, some x is P) and (S with “t is P” added, R) to R, for a name t used nowhere else.

The proof reaches R when its last line is (∅, R): the statement, under no supposition at all.

A proof is a statement that names a list of lines, each one given, supposed, moved from lines above it, or dropped, and its foot stands under no supposition.

Example: with no givens at all, take L(0) = ({P}, P) and L(1) = (∅, if P then P). Line 0 is supposed: P is in its S. Line 1 is dropped: prove an if takes line 0 to it, and P leaves S. The foot stands under nothing, so this two-line list reaches “if P then P”.

4a10The Old Citing Rule, as a Part

The third bullet of 4a9 holds 3a2’s rule for leaning, now said about parts.

Citing rule: a line may lean on a line before it only if that line's suppositions are a part of its own.

∅ is a part of every collection, so a line inside a box may lean on lines outside it; a line outside never leans on one inside, which carries a supposition it lacks. 3a2 laid that down as a rule. 4a14 earns it.

A box inside a box (3a6) is a line whose S has two members, and nothing more. An empty row (3a5) is a line whose S is ∅.

4a11The Identity of a Statement, Earned

The one-line argument, a given and nothing else, is the identity of a statement, as the self-sameness axiom is the identity of a name. Until now that could only be said. Here it is earned.

Plan: write a list with one line, and check that the line is given.

Proof. Let P be any statement, and let G be {P}, the collection whose only member is P. Let L be the list with one line, L(0) = (∅, P). That one line is given, since P is a member of G. So L is a proof from G. Its last line is (∅, P), so L reaches P. ∎

Identity technique: every statement is reached by a proof from itself.

The proof reaches P exactly as P was given: no line was moved, so nothing was added and nothing was lost. The foot is as so, or as not so, as the given was.

Could the identity technique be made an axiom, now that it is earned? Some ways of writing proofs do lay it down as an axiom, the first one every other proof starts from. On the ladder it cannot be one any more, and it does not need to be. An axiom is laid down with nothing beneath it. The identity technique now has something beneath it: the definition of a proof, built from lists, which were built from the numbers, which were built from sets. What is earned is a technique, and it is cited by name like any other. The self-sameness axiom stays an axiom: nothing on the ladder lies beneath it.

4a12The Column, at Last

Writing every line as a pair is slow. So the ladder writes a proof as a column: 2a’s rows, one line to a row, the statement on the left and the reason on the right, with 3a’s boxes drawn as indents.

The indent is S, drawn. Each box still open pushes a row one step to the right; a row “assume P” is where P joins S, and the indent closes where P leaves S. Read from the left, the indents of a column are its suppositions, nested.

Example: here is the fish argument from 4a1 as a column.

\qquad \text{assume the fish is a smolt} @@ supposed: S is {the fish is a smolt}
\qquad \text{the fish is a salmon} @@ every smolt is a young salmon, a given; same S
\qquad \text{the fish is a fish} @@ every salmon is a fish, a given; same S
\qquad \text{the fish swims} @@ every fish swims, a given; same S
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\text{if the fish is a smolt, the fish swims}} \quad\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ reported: prove an if; S is empty again

Read it back to pairs and you get the list you wrote in 4a3. The column is a way of writing; the list of pairs is the object. Every column from here on, and every column 2a and 3a drew, is one of these lists.

4a13Everything the Givens Let In

Which statements can G reach? One answer: those that are the foot of some proof from G. There is a second answer, and it has the shape of 4a6’s smallest closed set.

Call a collection of lines closed when it holds every given line and every supposed line, and holds every line that is moved or dropped from lines it holds.

Example: with G = {P}, the collection holding only (∅, P) is not closed. It leaves out the supposed lines, such as ({Q}, Q).

Closed collections exist. The lines that appear in proofs from G form one: to move from lines of two different proofs, write the proofs one after the other and add the new line at the foot. Sift the lines that belong to every closed collection. As 4a6 holds in trust until 4d, the result is closed, and it is a part of every closed collection. It is the smallest one.

Two-pictures technique: the lines some proof reaches are exactly the lines in the smallest closed collection.

Plan: show each collection is a part of the other, then close with double inclusion.

First, every line of every proof is in every closed collection. Fix a closed collection C and a proof. Prove by induction, held in trust from 4a6 until 4c, that for every k all the lines numbered before k are in C. For 0 there are none. Suppose all the lines before k are in C. Line k is given, supposed, moved from givens and those lines, or dropped from some of them. Each way, C holds it, since C is closed. So all the lines before k + 1 are in C.

Second, the lines reached by proofs form a closed collection, as above, so the smallest one is a part of them. Each is a part of the other, so by 2d's double inclusion they are the same.

So there are two pictures of a proof. One is a list you can write down and check line by line. The other is the smallest collection the rules cannot get out of. They pick out the same statements. The first is how you work. The second is how you prove things about every proof at once, as the next part does.

4a14A Supposition Never Hurts

One fact the lines used all along, without saying so: anything earned outside a supposition stays earned inside it. Here it is earned.

More-suppositions technique: if a proof from G holds the line (S, R), then for any statement P some proof from G holds the line (S with P added, R).

Example: the given line (∅, Q) becomes ({P}, Q), and it is still given, since a given line may stand under anything.

Plan: add P to every line of the proof, then check each line keeps its kind, by induction on line numbers.

Proof. Take the proof, and add P to the suppositions of every line. A given line is still given, since S may be anything. A supposed line is still supposed, since its statement is still in its S. A moved line still leans on lines before it whose suppositions are a part of its own, because P was added to both sides.

A dropped line still drops. The lines it came from gain P too, so (S with Q added, R) becomes (S with Q and P added, R), and the same Q is dropped. (If P happens to mention the fresh name of a use-a-some, rename that name first.) Every line keeps its reason, so the new list is a proof. ∎

That is why a box may cite the lines outside it. It is no longer a rule to follow. It is a technique, earned: the more-suppositions technique.

4a15The Tables, Again

1e checked each tech one row at a time, and said plainly that this covered only the argument in front of you. Now a proof is an object, and the same check covers every proof at once.

Plan: fix a row, or a world, where the givens are true. Then prove by induction on line numbers (held in trust from 4a6) that every line (S, R) of every proof from G has this property: wherever all of S is true, R is true.

A given line is true, by the choice of row. A supposed line has R in S. A moved line is true because each tech keeps what is true, which is 1e's table.

A dropped line is true too, one way of dropping at a time. Prove an if: wherever S is true, either P is false, and then “if P then Q” is true, or P is true, and then Q is true by the line it came from. Refute: wherever S is true, P cannot be true, since then ⊥ would be, so “not P” is true. Cases: wherever S is true, P or Q is true, and either way R is true. Use a some: wherever S is true, something is P; call it t, and R is true, since R does not mention t.

So the foot, which stands under nothing, is true wherever the givens are true. ∎

Soundness technique: whatever a proof reaches from true statements is true.

This is what 4a7 found by hand. Turned round, it is 3f's method: if some world has every given true and R false, then no proof from G reaches R. The cellar writes 3f's argument out as a proof. 4a19 writes the two directions out as a table.

4a17The End Mark

An argument needs a way to say it is finished. Here is its mark, brought in by 0a's rule:

The end mark ∎ is a statement, named whole in 0a's way: “the argument is complete”.

Typing an answer, with no key for the end mark? Write the word end in its place. It means the same thing, and the ladder reads it the same way.

0a promised that a letter names one thing for the length of an argument, and is free when the argument is over, and left the ending to this step. The end mark is where an argument is over, and 0a's flag on freeing a letter is closed. A letter named inside an argument keeps its meaning down to the end mark and no further. So the same letter can name different things in different arguments, as long as each argument ends before the next begins. Here are two cats, with a single letter, in an argument each:

Call the grey cat on the mat c. So c is the grey cat, until the end mark. c is asleep; that is given. ∎ The end mark frees c.

Call the ginger cat on the wall c. Now c is the ginger cat, in this argument only. c is hungry; that is given. ∎ And c is free again.

Inside either argument, c names one cat throughout, so every line can be checked. What you may not do is let a line in the second argument lean on a line in the first: across the end mark, c is a different cat.

4a18Proofs in a Series

A finished proof becomes a technique, and every later proof may cite it. 4a9’s rule lets a line be “a technique already earned”. That technique is the last line of some earlier proof, written by you or by someone else, perhaps long ago. You do not prove it again each time you use it.

So no proof stands alone. A proof that turns a sameness about leans on the proof that earned turn-about in 0b, and that one leans on 0b’s axioms. Mathematics, as it is done, is a series of proofs, each standing on the ones before. Your proof joins the series the moment it cites one.

The end mark of 4a17 closes an argument, so no later line may lean on its working. Its last line is different: it goes on as a technique. The series passes on what was earned, not the work that earned it.

4a19Yes Needs Every World, No Needs One

4a15 proved that whatever a proof reaches from true statements is true, world by world. Put the two sides of the ledger together. Say that givens G force R when R is true in every world where every member of G is true. Soundness, read again: whatever is reached from G is forced by G.

Showing “forced” takes every world. Nobody visits them, so the only road is a proof, or an argument in the margin about any world at all, which is what 4a15’s induction was. Showing “not forced” takes one world with G true and R false, and by soundness that same world shows “not reached”: this is 3f’s method, and the cellar wrote it as a proof. Showing “reached” takes a list.

The reverse of soundness is a different claim: that every line forced by G is reached by some proof from G. Nothing here proves it, and it is not an empty gap. It asks whether the techs of rungs 1 to 4 are enough, or whether some line true in every world could still have no proof. 38d answers it. Until then the ladder uses the direction it has: a proof settles yes, a world settles no.

Accepted names

A proof written as a list of lines is called a derivation. Proofs built from suppositions that open and close are called natural deduction; a line written as a pair of suppositions and a statement is called a sequent, often written S ⊢ R. Closing a supposition is called discharging it. The smallest closed collection of statements reached from G is G's deductive closure. The identity technique is the identity (or reflexivity) of ⊢, written P ⊢ P; the way of writing proofs called the sequent calculus lays it down as its identity axiom. The more-suppositions technique is called weakening. 4a.4.2 is the deduction theorem.

The swap axiom, as a line

0b's swap axiom had two halves. When n = b and P(n) is so, P(b) is so. And it runs either way: when n = b and P(b) is so, P(n) is so. Each half is an if. Together they are an exactly-when. So the axiom becomes one line, with 1d's double arrow for “either way”:

Read it from the outside in. Take any two names. If they name one thing, then a claim about the first is so exactly when the same claim about the second is so. The left-to-right half of the ⇔ is 0b's first half. The right-to-left half is “the other way”.

One thing in the line is not like the rest. The letters x and y are names, and the turn A speaks of every name. The letter P is a statement-with-a-gap, and no turn A stands in front of it. So the swap line is a pattern. You fill in P first, with any statement-with-a-gap you like, and then you have a line. Each P gives its own line. That is exactly what 0b said: “with P any statement-with-a-gap”.

Filling P is also where 0b's choice of places went. 0b said you may swap at whichever of a name's places you choose. In the line, you choose the places by choosing the gap. Take “n lives next door to n's sister”. To swap at the left place only, fill P with “… lives next door to n's sister”. To swap at both, fill P with “… lives next door to …'s sister”, with one name in both gaps. Choosing the places is choosing the pattern. The picture below lets you try each choice.

Here unpack is earned, because every piece of it is now on the table: the every (2b), the exactly-when (1d), the and (1c), and carrying an if through (1d). A word's meaning says a thing is the word exactly when it is what the word means: ∀x (x is a puppy ⇔ (x is young ∧ x is a dog)). To unpack “Gomez is a puppy”, use the every with Gomez. Unpack the ⇔ and keep its left-to-right half. Carry it through: Gomez is young and Gomez is a dog. Keep the part you want. Pack runs the other way: join the parts, keep the right-to-left half, carry it through. Unpack is not only an if: it is an every, around an exactly-when, around an and, each used in turn.

Unpack still stays a tech of its own. An if is a claim. It can be given, earned, refused or denied, and an argument that leans on it stands or falls with it. A word's meaning is not a claim in any argument. It comes with the word, the way a name comes with name = name by 0b's self-sameness axiom, and no column can deny it and keep the word. Every “from here on” line is a meaning line of the same kind: an every around an exactly-when, with the mark's letters as its placeholders.

Transform comes out of foreclosure here too. 1a said a transform is any move that changes how a claim is written and keeps what it says. With rung 1's marks the whole family is on the table, and each member of the family has its own warrant. Shape transforms: once rung 1's marks are on the table, “Mike is winged” and “Mike has wings” are both written W(m). Moving between them is the identity of a statement, earned above. The possibility axiom is what lets both shapes be written as W(m). Rewrites by sameness: wherever a name is written, a name earned the same as it may be written, by 0b's swap axiom. Rewrites by exactly-when: 1f's rewrites put a statement in place of one that is true exactly when it is.

The last two rows say what citing a technique is. 1a said that what a column proves is always “from these given lines, this foot”. In marks, that is an if: the givens joined, an arrow, the foot. When the column's letters were any names, nothing assumed of them but the givens, the if takes turn As in front. So every proved technique has a line behind it: a statement that packs the column that proved it. The technique licenses the move; the line is why the technique is sound. Citing a technique by name is using an every on its line and carrying an if through. Rung 0 had been using rung 1's techs all along, and calling them by words.

Given a rule in words, find its marks. “For any” is a turn A. “From … you may write …” is an if. “Either way” is an exactly-when.
