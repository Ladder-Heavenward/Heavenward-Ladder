40·now · research front

Can we trust a proof no one can read?

Can we trust a proof no one can read? 40d proved that five colours are enough for any map, and said that four are enough too, by a proof of 1976 that it did not give. That proof is the first famous one that no person has read from end to end: part of it is a computer checking well over a thousand cases. This page shows how such a proof is built. It counts a charge on each dot of a flat graph and finds that the charges always add to 12, which forces small pieces to appear. It shows what it means for a piece to be unable to sit in a smallest map needing five colours, and why the pieces that turn up are too many to check by hand. Then it asks the question the proof raised in 1979 and that has not gone away: is a check that no one can read a proof, and what do we trust when we accept it?

Give each dot of a flat graph of triangles the charge 6 less its links; the charges add to 12. Moving charge by rules shows that some piece from a fixed list must appear. If no piece on the list can sit in a smallest map needing five colours, four colours suffice. The 1976 list had near two thousand pieces, checked by computer; the 1997 list has 633; in 2005 a proof checker checked the whole proof.

40·now.1Four colours, guessed and wrongly proved

The guess came in 1852, from colouring a map of counties: four colours are enough for any map, so that countries sharing a border differ. As 40d did, put a dot in each country and a link across each border; the map becomes a flat graph and the guess is that every flat graph has four colours.

A proof was published in 1879 and believed for eleven years. It followed the five-colour technique of 40d: take a dot with at most five links, colour the rest, and free a colour for the dot by swaps. With at most four neighbours one swap works, as in 40d. With five neighbours the proof made two swaps, one after the other.

\text{neighbours coloured } 1, 2, 3, 4, 2 \text{ round the dot} @@ four colours used, two of them 2
\text{swap } 1 \leftrightarrow 3 \text{ from one neighbour 2, and } 1 \leftrightarrow 4 \text{ from the other} @@ each alone frees nothing for a 2
\text{each swap is safe alone: its chain is cut off by the other chain} @@ 40d’s swap
\text{after the first swap, the chains the second relied on can change} @@ the first swap recolours dots on them
\htmlClass{new}{\text{both swaps together can join what was apart, and leave five colours needed}} @@ found in 1890

The flaw was found in 1890, and the proof kept what it could: five colours, the five-colour technique of 40d. The guess for four stayed open for 86 more years.

40·now.2Charges that add to 12

Adding links never makes colouring easier, so it is enough to colour flat graphs whose faces are all triangles. Give each dot with d links the charge 6 − d.

Charge-count technique: in a flat graph with V ≥ 3 dots whose faces are all triangles, the charges 6 − d add to exactly 12.

3F = 2E @@ each face has 3 links, each link 2 faces
V - E + \tfrac23 E = 2,\ \ E = 3V - 6 @@ the flat-count technique (40d)
\textstyle\sum d = 2E = 6V - 12 @@ the ends-count technique (40a)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum (6 - d) = 6V - (6V - 12) = 12} \quad \blacksquare @@

So some dot has charge above 0, that is at most five links, the five-or-fewer technique of 40d again. The new use is to move the charge. Moving it between dots keeps the total 12, so if a rule for moving can be shown to leave every dot at 0 or below, something the rule assumed absent must be present.

Unavoidable-pair technique: a flat graph of triangles in which every dot has at least five links has a dot of five links next to a dot of five or six.

\text{suppose every neighbour of a five-dot has 7 or more links} @@ for contradiction
\text{each five-dot gives } \tfrac15 \text{ to each of its 5 neighbours} @@ the rule; its charge 1 goes to 0
\text{round a dot, neighbours next to each other are linked} @@ the faces are triangles
\text{so no two five-dots are next to each other round a dot of } d \ge 7 @@ else a five-dot would have a five-neighbour
d \ge 7:\ \ 6 - d + \tfrac15 \lfloor \tfrac d2 \rfloor \le 6 - \tfrac{9d}{10} < 0 @@ at most ⌊d/2⌋ five-neighbours
\text{six-dots keep } 0 @@ they give and get nothing
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every charge is at most } 0, \text{ but they add to } 12} \quad \blacksquare @@ a contradiction

40·now.3Pieces that cannot appear

Suppose some flat graph needs five colours, and take one with the fewest dots. A piece of graph is reducible if it cannot appear in such a smallest graph: whenever the rest is coloured in four, the colouring can be changed by swaps and finished on the piece.

\text{a dot of 3 links is reducible; a dot of 4 links is reducible} @@ a colour is free, or one swap frees one (40d)
\text{so a smallest graph has every dot of 5 or more links} @@ and its faces may be taken as triangles
\text{it has a five next to a five or six} @@ the unavoidable-pair technique
\text{a list of pieces is unavoidable if every such graph has one} @@ found by rules for moving charge
\htmlClass{new}{\text{an unavoidable list of reducible pieces proves four colours}} @@ no smallest graph needing five can exist

The pair of the unavoidable-pair technique is not reducible by itself, and the swaps that would free a colour fail as in 1879. So the list must hold larger pieces, each with a ring of dots round it, and to show a piece reducible means checking every four-colouring of its ring.

\text{colourings of a ring of } m \text{ dots in 4 colours: } 3^m + 3(-1)^m @@ a ring is a cycle
\text{renaming the colours: } 24 \text{ moves; a swap of two colours keeps the } 2 \text{ two-colourings in the others} @@ m even; the rest keep none but doing nothing
m = 14:\ \ \tfrac{1}{24}\,(3^{14} + 3 + 6 \cdot 2) = \tfrac{4\,782\,984}{24} @@ the average-kept technique (40·cb19)
\htmlClass{new}{= 199\,291 \text{ colourings of one ring of 14, each to be finished on the piece}} @@ for one piece of the list

40·now.4The proof by computer

The 1976 proof had an unavoidable list of near two thousand pieces, found partly by hand with rules for moving charge, and a computer checked each piece reducible, ring colouring by ring colouring, in some 1200 hours. The 1997 proof used a list of 633 pieces and 32 rules for moving charge, and both halves, unavoidable and reducible, were checked by computer in a few hours. In 2005 the whole proof, with the flat drawings and the curves of 40d, was written for a proof checker and checked, line by line, by its small core.

The four-colour result is now this: every flat graph can be coloured with four colours, so that linked dots differ. It is believed by everyone who works on it. No one has found a proof short enough to read, and no one has shown there is none.

1976:\ \text{the hand work, the programs, the machine} @@ an error in the hand part was found and repaired later
1997:\ \text{the charge rules by hand; programs anyone can run again} @@ two independent checks
2005:\ \text{the small core of the checker and the written statement} @@ the small-kernel technique (37·now)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the trust moves from a reader to a check anyone can repeat}} @@

40·now.5Is a check no one reads a proof?

In 1979 a philosopher argued that the 1976 proof was not a proof in the old sense. A proof, he said, is something a person can survey: read, follow and be convinced by. A computer run cannot be surveyed, so accepting it is trusting an experiment, like a measurement in a laboratory; mathematics with such proofs has a part that is known by experience, not by reason. 14g asked whether a plot drawn by a machine is a proof; this is the same question with the plot made exact.

The reply has three parts. Long arithmetic done by hand is not surveyed either: no one checks the multiplication of two hundred-digit numbers by holding it in mind, only that each step is a step. Every proof that people accept rests on checks they did not make, and errors in long proofs read by people have stood for years, as the 1879 proof did. And the 2005 check narrows what must be trusted to a small core that people can read, and to a statement that says what was proved. What the reply does not give is understanding: a list of 633 pieces says that four colours suffice, and does not say why.

\text{a proof must be surveyable by a person} @@ so the 1976 proof is an experiment
\text{a proof is a list of steps each of which can be checked} @@ by a person or a core a person can read
\text{both agree: the result is true beyond reasonable doubt} @@ no error has been found since 1997
\htmlClass{new}{\text{they disagree on whether the knowing is the same kind}} @@ the question stays open

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The four-colour result is the four colour theorem. The swap is a Kempe chain interchange. The charges and their moving are the discharging method; the charge-count technique is Euler's formula for triangulations, and the unavoidable-pair technique is Wernicke's theorem (1904). A smallest graph needing five colours is a minimal counterexample; a reducible piece is a reducible configuration, and its ring is the ring of the configuration. The 1979 question is the surveyability debate.

Who said it first

Francis Guthrie guessed four colours in 1852 and his brother passed the question to Augustus De Morgan. Alfred Kempe published his proof in 1879; Percy Heawood found the flaw in 1890 and proved five colours. Paul Wernicke found the unavoidable pair in 1904, and Heinrich Heesch, from the 1930s, the method of charges and reducible pieces. Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken, with John Koch, gave the computer proof in 1976. Neil Robertson, Daniel Sanders, Paul Seymour and Robin Thomas gave the 1997 proof, and Georges Gonthier, with Benjamin Werner, checked it in the Coq proof assistant in 2005. Thomas Tymoczko made the surveyability argument in 1979.

A proof that is not allowed

“A map that needs five colours would need five countries each bordering all the others. Five such countries cannot be drawn flat, since K₅ is not flat (40d). So four colours suffice.” Not allowed: a graph can need more colours than its largest group of dots all linked to each other. A ring of five dots has no three all linked and still needs three colours. Needing five colours does not mean holding K₅, and that is why the problem took 124 years.
