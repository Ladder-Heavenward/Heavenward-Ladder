Why Must Divers Come Up Slowly?

Why must divers come up slowly? A diver breathing compressed air at 30 metres soaks nitrogen into blood and body, more the deeper and longer they stay. Come up too fast and the nitrogen comes out of solution as bubbles, like a shaken bottle opened, in joints, skin and nerves. Navy divers and bridge builders were crippled by it before anyone knew why. In 1908 John Scott Haldane modelled the body as a few tissues, each filling with nitrogen at its own rate, by 17j’s growth and decay, and wrote the first safe tables. This page finds how much gas dissolves at depth, how fast each tissue fills and empties, how deep a diver may rise at once, and why holding one’s breath on the way up is the worst mistake of all.

Nitrogen dissolved is in proportion to its pressure. A tissue fills at a rate in proportion to the gap between the breathed pressure and its own: it closes on the breathed value by half each half-time. A tissue tolerates the outside pressure falling to about half the pressure it is loaded to, so the diver rises in stages, waiting at each for the fullest tissue to empty enough.

17bio21Gas Dissolves With Pressure

Sarah dives to 30 m. Every 10 m of sea water adds one atmosphere to the one of air above (16·cb12).

p = 1 + \tfrac{30}{10} = 4 \text{ atm} @@ at 30 m
\text{air is } 79\% \text{ nitrogen}:\ p_{\text{N}_2} = 0.79 \cdot 4 = 3.16 \text{ atm} @@ its share of the pressure
\text{dissolved nitrogen} \propto p_{\text{N}_2} @@ measured, for gases in liquids
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at 30 m the body can hold four times the nitrogen it holds ashore}} @@ once it has had time to fill

From here on, on this page, a tissue’s load is the nitrogen pressure it would be in balance with; ashore, 0.79 atm.

17bio22Tissues Fill by Halves

P^{\prime} = k\,(P_a - P) @@ fills faster the bigger the gap
P(t) = P_a - (P_a - P_0)\,e^{-kt} @@ growth and decay (17j)
\text{half-time } T = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k} @@ the gap halves every T
\htmlClass{new}{\text{Haldane’s tissues: half-times } 5, 10, 20, 40, 75 \text{ minutes}} @@ blood fast, fat and joints slow

Half-time technique: a tissue’s load closes the gap to the breathed pressure by half every half-time, and opens it the same way coming up. Fast tissues fill and empty in minutes; slow ones take hours both ways.

17bio23Forty Minutes at Thirty Metres

\text{gap at the start: } 3.16 - 0.79 = 2.37 \text{ atm} @@ ashore load to depth
\text{5-minute tissue: 8 half-times, gap } 2.37/256 \approx 0.01 @@ full: 3.15 atm
\text{40-minute tissue: 1 half-time, gap } 1.19 @@ half full: 1.97 atm
\htmlClass{new}{\text{75-minute tissue: } 3.16 - 2.37 \cdot 2^{-40/75} \approx 1.52 \text{ atm}} @@ 2⁻ˣ as e to a power (17g)

After a short deep dive the fast tissues are full and the slow ones nearly empty; after a long one, all are loaded. That is why the length of the dive matters as much as its depth.

17bio24Rising by Stages

Haldane found, testing on goats, that a body could rise at once from a pressure to half of it without harm. Read through the tissues: a tissue loaded to P is safe while the outside pressure stays above about half of P / 0.79.

\text{5-minute tissue: } 3.15 / 0.79 \approx 4 \text{ atm} @@ its balance pressure
\text{half: } 2 \text{ atm} = 10 \text{ m down} @@ rise to 10 m and stop
\text{wait: it empties by half every 5 minutes} @@ the same decay, downward
\htmlClass{new}{\text{then the next stop, shallower, and so on}} @@ the slowest tissue sets the last stops

A diver rises in stages, each stop as shallow as the most loaded tissue allows, and waits for that tissue to empty before going on. Today’s dive computers carry sixteen tissues, with half-times from 4 minutes to over 10 hours, and recompute the stops every few seconds.

17bio25Never Hold Your Breath

pV \text{ fixed for a held breath} @@ 15·chem’s gas law, warm and steady
10 \text{ m} \to 0 \text{ m}:\ p \text{ halves},\ V \text{ doubles} @@ the biggest change is near the top
\text{lungs cannot stretch to twice full} @@ they tear
\htmlClass{new}{\text{breathe out all the way up}} @@ the first rule taught

Bubbles from dissolved gas come on over minutes to hours; a torn lung is instant. Both come from the same law: gas grows as the pressure on it falls. Astronauts meet the first danger going out in low-pressure suits, and clear their nitrogen by breathing pure oxygen beforehand.

Accepted names

The bubbles are decompression sickness, “the bends”; a torn lung is pulmonary barotrauma. Dissolving in proportion to pressure is Henry’s law; the tissues are compartments with half-times, and the half rule Haldane’s 2 to 1 ratio, later refined to M-values. Today’s sixteen-tissue model is Bühlmann’s ZH-L16; the waits are decompression stops.

Who said it first

Boycott, Damant and Haldane published the tissue model and staged tables in 1908, for the Royal Navy, after tests on goats. Albert Bühlmann’s sixteen-tissue model of the 1980s runs in most dive computers today.
