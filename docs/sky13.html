∞13 · research front

What symmetry can be turned a little at a time?

What symmetry can be turned a little at a time? The six moves of a triangle come in jumps: a third of a turn, a flip, nothing in between. A ball can be turned by any angle at all, a millionth of a degree if you like, and its turns run into one another without a gap. Groups of that kind were invented in the 1870s to solve rules on rates the way rung 43’s groups sorted polynomials, and the key to them turned out to be the turns that are very nearly nothing. Two tiny turns about different axes, done and undone in the two orders, leave a turn much tinier still about a third axis. That leftover, the bracket, holds nearly everything about the group. From the bracket alone come the sizes of the pieces a symmetry can act on: one, two, three, four and on. Out of that come the two settings of an electron’s spin, the three orbitals of one shape, and the splitting of one line of light into three when a magnet is held near the lamp. The forces of the standard model are three groups of this kind, with one, three and eight directions of small turning. Why those three, and whether they are one group seen in pieces, nobody knows.

The slope at doing nothing of a smooth path of turns of space is a grid X with Xᵀ = −X, and these make a space of three directions. Two small turns done and undone leave the bracket [A, B] = AB − BA, and for space [J_x, J_y] = J_z. SU(2) has the same bracket as the turns of space, yet a full turn in it is −I: that is spin. In any whole piece, the bracket forces the stretches of L_z to run j, j − 1, …, −j, so pieces have 2j + 1 members. In a magnet a level of turning ℓ splits into 2ℓ + 1 levels μ_B B apart, and a line into three. SU(n) has n² − 1 directions, so U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3) has 1 + 3 + 8 = 12 carriers of force.

sky13.1The turns near nothing

27m found the turns of space: the 3 × 3 grids Q with QᵀQ = I and det 1, SO(3). Unlike the six moves of 19f they come in smooth families: a path Q(t), a turn for each t, with Q(0) = I. Its slope there, X = Q′(0), is a small turning: for small t, Q(t) ≈ I + tX.

Small-turn technique: the slope X at I of a smooth path of turns has Xᵀ = −X; and when Xᵀ = −X, e^(tX) is a turn for every t. Such X make a space of arrows of dimension 3, and Xv = u × v for one arrow u.

Q(t)^{\mathsf T}Q(t) = I \Rightarrow Q'^{\mathsf T}Q + Q^{\mathsf T}Q' = 0 @@ the product rule, entry by entry
t = 0:\ \ X^{\mathsf T} + X = 0 @@ Q(0) = I
X^{\mathsf T} = -X \Rightarrow (e^{tX})^{\mathsf T}e^{tX} = e^{-tX}e^{tX} = I @@ transpose each power; the grid-exponential technique (27·cb24)
\det e^{tX} = \pm 1 \text{ for every } t,\ \text{and } 1 \text{ at } t = 0 @@ the length-keeper technique (27m1); a det that moves smoothly cannot jump from 1 to −1
X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -z & y \\ z & 0 & -x \\ -y & x & 0 \end{pmatrix} = xJ_x + yJ_y + zJ_z @@ zeros down the middle, opposite numbers across it: three free entries
\htmlClass{new}{Xv = (x, y, z) \times v} \quad \blacksquare @@ the cross technique (27i3), row by row

J_z is the floor’s quarter turn of 27m1 with its up column emptied. With t = 0.01, e^(tJ_z) has 0.99995 and 0.0099998 where I + tJ_z has 1 and 0.01: off by 5 × 10⁻⁵, the size of t². The arrow u = (x, y, z) is the axis, and its length is the speed of turning: e^(tX) is 27m3’s turn by t|u| about u. So near doing nothing, a symmetry that turns a little at a time is a space of arrows, three directions for space. The group is curved; this space is flat, and the next footing finds what the curving leaves behind.

sky13.2What two small turns leave behind

Small turnings add as arrows, but the turns they make do not commute (27m3). The failure shows at the second order. Write [A, B] = AB − BA, the bracket.

Turn-bracket technique: e^(εA)e^(εB)e^(−εA)e^(−εB) = I + ε²[A, B] + terms in ε³ and smaller; if Aᵀ = −A and Bᵀ = −B then [A, B]ᵀ = −[A, B]; and for space, [J_x, J_y] = J_z, [J_y, J_z] = J_x, [J_z, J_x] = J_y.

e^{\pm\varepsilon A} = I \pm \varepsilon A + \tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2}A^2 + \cdots @@ the grid-exponential series
\varepsilon:\ \ A + B - A - B = 0 @@ the first order cancels
\varepsilon^2:\ \ (A^2 + B^2) + (AB - A^2 - AB - BA - B^2 + AB) = AB - BA @@ a square from each factor, then each pair of factors, in order
[A, B]^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T} - A^{\mathsf T}B^{\mathsf T} = BA - AB = -[A, B] @@ a bracket of small turnings is a small turning
J_xJ_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0 \end{pmatrix},\ \ J_yJ_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0 \end{pmatrix} @@ multiply out (27d)
\htmlClass{new}{[J_x, J_y] = J_z} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other two the same way, turning x → y → z → x

With ε = 0.01: a hundredth of a radian about x, then about y, then back about x, then back about y, leaves a turn of 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ radian about the up axis, exactly ε² to the four places a check by machine gives. In arrows, the bracket of the small turnings along u and v is the one along u × v: for space the bracket is the cross product. It also obeys [A, [B, C]] + [B, [C, A]] + [C, [A, B]] = 0, as multiplying out shows. Held in trust: near I, the bracket fixes how the group multiplies, by a series in brackets alone. So the bracket, a few numbers, carries the group near doing nothing.

sky13.3The same bracket, a different group

43·phys.2 met SU(2): the 2 × 2 grids of turning numbers with U*U = I (31d4) and det 1. Its small turnings, found as in the first footing, are the X with X* = −X and middle sum 0. A basis is K_k = −(i/2)σ_k, where σ_x has rows (0, 1), (1, 0); σ_y has rows (0, −i), (i, 0); σ_z has rows (1, 0), (0, −1).

Double-turn technique: the K_k have the brackets of the J_k, [K_x, K_y] = K_z and so on, yet e^(2πK_z) = −I, and only e^(4πK_z) = I. Held in trust: each turn of space comes from exactly two members of SU(2), U and −U.

\sigma_x\sigma_y = i\sigma_z,\ \ \sigma_y\sigma_x = -i\sigma_z @@ multiply out
[K_x, K_y] = (-\tfrac i2)^2(\sigma_x\sigma_y - \sigma_y\sigma_x) = -\tfrac14 \cdot 2i\sigma_z = K_z @@ the bracket of the J’s, and the other two alike
e^{\theta K_z} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix} @@ a diagonal grid: exponentiate down the middle
\theta = 2\pi:\ \ e^{-i\pi} = e^{i\pi} = -1 @@ 21·cb20’s turning by a half
\htmlClass{new}{e^{2\pi K_z} = -I,\ \ e^{4\pi K_z} = I} \quad \blacksquare @@ the full turn is minus one

So one bracket can belong to two groups, and the groups differ far from I. An electron’s spin arrow (37·phys.1) lives in ℂ² and is turned by SU(2): turn the whole laboratory once round, and the arrow comes back as its negative. A sign alone cannot be seen, but half a beam turned against the other half can, by interference (32·phys). In 1975 two groups did that with neutrons, turning the spin of half a beam in a magnetic field, and found that the pattern repeated after 720° of turning, not 360°.

sky13.4The sizes of the pieces

A piece of SU(2), as in 43·cb27, gives grids X_x, X_y, X_z on a space of dimension n with the same brackets. Put L_k = iX_k, so [L_x, L_y] = iL_z and its turnings; on spin, L_k = σ_k/2. Put L₊ = L_x + iL_y and L₋ = L_x − iL_y. Then [L_z, L₊] = L₊, [L_z, L₋] = −L₋ and [L₊, L₋] = 2L_z, by multiplying out.

Piece-ladder technique: on a whole piece of dimension n, the stretches of L_z are j, j − 1, …, −j, each once, and n = 2j + 1; so j is 0, ½, 1, 3/2, 2, ….

L_z v = mv \Rightarrow L_z(L_\pm v) = L_\pm L_z v \pm L_\pm v = (m \pm 1)L_\pm v @@ [L_z, L±] = ±L±: a ladder of stretches
L_+ v = 0,\ \ L_z v = jv \text{ for some } v \ne 0 @@ L_z has a stretch (25d); climb until the next rung is 0, as there are only n stretches
v, L_-v, \ldots, L_-^k v \ne 0,\ \ L_-^{k+1} v = 0 @@ stretches j, j − 1, …, j − k
\text{these make the whole piece: } n = k + 1 @@ L₊L₋ = L₋L₊ + 2L_z brings each one rung back; a kept space (43·cb27.1)
\text{middle sum of } L_z = \tfrac12\,\text{middle sum of } (L_+L_- - L_-L_+) = 0 @@ AB and BA have the same middle sum, Σ AᵢₖBₖᵢ
\htmlClass{new}{j + (j - 1) + \cdots + (j - k) = (k + 1)(j - \tfrac k2) = 0 \Rightarrow k = 2j,\ n = 2j + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the middle sum is the sum of the stretches (27l1)

j = ½ gives 2: the electron’s spin, and 43·phys.2’s proton and neutron. j = 1 gives 3: the arrows of space themselves, and 35·phys’s three orbitals of shape ℓ = 1. j = 3/2 gives 4: the row of four Δ’s in 43·phys.3. j = 2 gives the five orbitals of shape ℓ = 2. 35·phys counted 2ℓ + 1 orbitals by solving a rule on rates; the bracket gives the count without solving anything. And e^(2πX_z) is e^(−2πim) on each rung, (−1)^(2j) in all, so the turns of space themselves act only on the pieces with whole j.

sky13.5A magnet splits a line

A loop of current I round a flat area A acts as a small magnet of strength μ = IA along the loop’s axis, and in a field B at angle θ to it has energy −μB cos θ: a law, measured on every compass needle and motor coil, with B as in 28·phys.5. An electron going round a circle of radius ρ at speed v is a current e/(2πρ/v) round the area πρ². Its spin about the centre, 27·phys.2’s L = r × mv, has size mvρ.

Line-splitting technique: in a field B along z, a level whose turning is a piece with j = ℓ splits into 2ℓ + 1 levels, μ_B B apart, with μ_B = eħ/2m; and a line between levels ℓ = 2 and ℓ = 1 splits into three.

\mu = \tfrac{ev}{2\pi\rho}\,\pi\rho^2 = \tfrac{e}{2m}\,mv\rho = \tfrac{e}{2m}L @@ pointing against L, since the charge is −e
L_z = m_\ell\hbar,\ \ m_\ell = \ell, \ell - 1, \ldots, -\ell @@ the piece-ladder technique, in units of ħ
U = \tfrac{e\hbar}{2m}\,m_\ell B = \mu_B\,m_\ell B,\ \ \mu_B = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{J/T} = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV/T} @@ −μ · B, with μ against L
\Delta E = E_0 + \mu_B B\,\Delta m,\ \ \Delta m = -1, 0, 1 @@ light carries one unit of turning: a law, held in trust; the two spacings are equal
\Delta\nu = \mu_B B/h = 14.0\ \text{GHz per tesla} @@ 15 pairs of levels, 9 allowed falls, 3 colours
\htmlClass{new}{\text{cadmium’s red line, 643.8 nm, spins cancelled: three lines, } \Delta\lambda = \lambda^2\Delta\nu/c = 0.0194\ \text{nm apart at 1 T}} \quad \blacksquare @@ from ν = c/λ, a small change in ν is a change of λ²/c times it in λ

That is a part in 33 000 of the line’s 1.93 eV at a tesla, and in 1908 lines of sunspots split this way showed fields of about 0.3 T, thousands of times the earth’s 54 μT. But most lines split into four, six or more, sodium’s yellow pair into four and six, and that was a riddle for thirty years. The electron’s own spin, j = ½, is a magnet too, twice as strong for its turning as the orbit (2.0023 times, measured), and the two add as arrows: 37·phys’s adding of turnings, which needs the brackets of two pieces at once.

sky13.6The groups of the forces

43·phys.1 found charge kept by the turn of phase ψ ↦ e^(iθ)ψ: the group U(1), with one direction of small turning. 43·phys.4’s colour is kept by an exact SU(3). The weak force that turns a d quark into a u in 43·phys.6 has an SU(2). The standard model asks that each symmetry hold separately at every point of space and time, and then each direction of small turning needs a field of its own, whose ripples are particles: the carriers of the force.

Carrier-count technique: the small turnings of SU(n) are the n × n grids X with X* = −X and middle sum 0, a real space of dimension n² − 1.

X^* = -X:\ \ x_{kk} = -\overline{x_{kk}},\ \text{so } x_{kk} = i \times \text{a real number} @@ n real numbers down the middle
x_{jk} = -\overline{x_{kj}}:\ \ \tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2} \text{ free turning numbers above the middle} @@ n(n − 1) real numbers; the ones below are fixed by them
\det e^X = e^{\text{middle sum of } X} = 1 \Rightarrow \text{middle sum } 0 @@ for X with a basis of stretched arrows, both are the product of e^λ over its stretches (27l1); one condition
\htmlClass{new}{n + n(n - 1) - 1 = n^2 - 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the carrier-count technique
U(1) \times SU(2) \times SU(3):\ \ 1 + 3 + 8 = 12 @@ the photon, W⁺, W⁻ and Z, and eight gluons

All twelve have been seen: the gluons in jets of particles at Hamburg in 1979, the W and Z at CERN in 1983, at 80.37 and 91.19 GeV. The photon and the Z are mixtures of the U(1) direction and one direction of SU(2). The bracket of U(1) is 0, and light does not pull on light. The bracket of SU(3) is not 0, so gluons carry colour themselves and pull on one another, and that is held to be why quarks are never seen alone. The masses of the W and Z come from one more field, whose particle was found at CERN in 2012 at about 125 GeV. Past this: SU(5) has 24 directions, the 12 above and 12 more, and the 12 more would turn quarks into electrons, so protons would decay. 43·phys.6 found none in 10³⁴ years.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞13.oq.1Why these groups? Nothing in the standard model chooses U(1) × SU(2) × SU(3), or explains why each kind of matter comes in three copies of different mass, from the electron at 0.511 MeV to the top quark near 173 GeV. About nineteen numbers are measured and put in by hand.
Is there a reason for the groups and the copies, or are they accidents? It is hard to test, because every proposed reason so far predicts its differences only at energies no machine reaches.
∞13.oq.2Are the three groups one? The strengths of the three forces change with the energy of a collision, and they come close together near 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁶ GeV, though not exactly at one point. Groups like SU(5), with all three inside, predict that protons decay. None has been seen in 50 000 tonnes of water watched for decades: the bound is more than 10³⁴ years. A detector several times larger is being built in Japan.
If it sees nothing, is the idea dead, or only the simplest groups?
∞13.oq.3Does the theory of SU(3)’s carriers exist, as mathematics? Computers on a grid of points in spacetime, and every experiment, show that the lightest particle made only of gluons has a mass above 0. Nobody has proved that a quantum theory built on SU(3)’s small turnings exists in four dimensions, to the standard of this ladder, or that its lightest particle has mass. A prize for it was posted in 2000.
Why it is hard: the theory is defined as a sum over every possible field at every point, and no one can show such a sum settles.
∞13.oq.4Where does gravity go? The symmetry of gravity is every smooth change of coordinates of spacetime, a group with endlessly many directions of small turning, not one with 1, 3 or 8. No quantum theory that includes it beside the other three forces has been tested.
Must its symmetry join the others, or stay apart?
∞13.oq.5Are there cracks? The muon’s magnet is 2.0023… times its turning, like the electron’s, and the digits past that test every particle the model has. The final measurement, in 2025, agrees with the 2025 theory that uses computations on a grid; an older route through measured electron collisions disagrees with both.
Which route of the theory is right, and does anything remain once it is settled?
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

In the 1870s a Norwegian mathematician wanted for rules on rates what the groups of rung 43 had done for polynomials, and built groups that move continuously. He found that each is governed near doing nothing by its brackets. Between 1888 and 1894 the possible brackets of the simplest kind were sorted: first in German papers written at a small college far from the main universities, with gaps and errors, then in a French thesis that repaired them. The answer was four endless families and five exceptions. Many at the time saw it as geometry for its own sake. The repair became the textbook; how much of the credit belongs to the first author rather than the second is a question historians have argued in both directions.

In 1896, in Leiden, a sodium flame between the poles of a strong magnet showed its yellow lines grow wider. A theory of electrons going round in atoms, from the same city, explained a split into three at once, with the size of the split fixed by the charge and mass of the electron, a year before the electron was found in Cambridge. The prize in physics went to both in 1902. Then the trouble: most lines split into four, six or more, the anomalous splitting, and for nearly thirty years no picture explained it. It was explained in 1925 only by giving the electron a spin of j = ½ with a magnet twice as strong as its turning predicts, an idea dismissed at first by some of those who later built on it. In 1908, at a mountain observatory in California, the same splitting showed that sunspots are magnets.

From 1927 groups and their pieces entered quantum mechanics through two books, one from Zurich and one from Berlin and Princeton. Many physicists resisted, and the phrase the plague of groups circulated. A rival method of 1929 got the same spectra with determinants and no groups, and was welcomed as a way out. For a while both sides were right: the groups gave the reasons and the counts, the determinants gave the numbers. By the 1950s, with isospin and then SU(3) in 1961, the groups had won, and the predicted Ω⁻ of 43·phys.3 in 1964 made it hard to argue.

In 1954 a pair of physicists at Brookhaven asked that isospin hold separately at each point, and found carriers that must have no mass, like light. One of the most famous physicists of the day objected at their seminar that a massless carrier would give a force of endless reach, which the nuclear force does not have, and the idea lay mostly unused for a decade. In 1964 several groups in Brussels, London and Edinburgh found how a field could give carriers mass, and in 1967 and 1968 the weak and electric forces were joined as U(1) × SU(2). Even then it was doubted that the theory gave finite answers, until a proof from Utrecht in 1971. In 1973 the new kind of weak event the theory needed was seen at CERN, and the same year it was found that the SU(3) force weakens at short distances. CERN found the W and Z in 1983 and the field’s particle in 2012.

Past the standard model, the story is of predictions not yet met. SU(5) unification of 1974 predicted protons decaying within about 10³⁰ years, and huge tanks of water in mines in Japan and Ohio in the 1980s saw none; the simplest version was abandoned, and larger groups pushed the time out. A further symmetry pairing each particle with a partner was expected to show at CERN’s collider after 2010, and none has. A 2022 measurement of the W’s mass from Illinois came out high, which would have been a crack; measurements from CERN in 2024 found the usual value, and the 2022 result is still unexplained; most expect a flaw in it rather than new physics, and its authors have not withdrawn it. The muon’s magnet looked like a crack from 2021 to 2025, and the crack moved into the theory: two ways of computing one part disagree, and the grid computations side with the measurement.

The money is now the argument. A ring of about 91 km under the border of France and Switzerland, studied by CERN and costed in its 2025 report at about 15 billion Swiss francs for the first stage, would measure the field’s particle and the Z with great precision, and later collide protons at many times today’s energies. China has studied a ring of its own. One side says no question about the groups can be answered without such machines. The other says that with no prediction of where a crack will appear, the money would do more in smaller experiments, in neutrinos, in rare decays, in the sky. A decision is expected near the end of this decade.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A group whose members can be moved smoothly is a Lie group; the space of small turnings with its bracket is its Lie algebra, written so(3) for SO(3) and su(2) for SU(2), and the bracket is the Lie bracket, or commutator. The rule [A, [B, C]] + [B, [C, A]] + [C, [A, B]] = 0 is the Jacobi identity. The series that fixes the product from brackets is the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. The σ_k are the Pauli matrices. SU(2) is the double cover of SO(3), the spin group Spin(3). L± are the ladder operators, j the spin (or angular momentum) quantum number, and the piece of dimension 2j + 1 the spin-j representation. The line-splitting technique is the normal Zeeman effect, the splitting into more lines the anomalous Zeeman effect, μ_B the Bohr magneton, the 2.0023 the electron’s g-factor, and Δm = 0, ±1 a selection rule. A symmetry held separately at every point is a gauge symmetry, and its theory a gauge theory, or Yang–Mills theory; the carriers are gauge bosons, and the field that gives them mass the Higgs field. SU(5) unification is the Georgi–Glashow model. The extra pairing is supersymmetry. ∞13.oq.3 is the Yang–Mills existence and mass gap problem, one of the Millennium Prize Problems; the future ring is the Future Circular Collider.

Who said it first

Sophus Lie built continuous groups from 1873. Wilhelm Killing classified the simple Lie algebras (1888 to 1890) and Élie Cartan corrected and completed the work in his thesis (1894). Pieter Zeeman saw the splitting in 1896, Hendrik Lorentz explained it, and both shared the Nobel Prize in 1902; J. J. Thomson measured the electron in 1897. George Ellery Hale found the fields of sunspots at Mount Wilson in 1908. George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit proposed spin in 1925, and Wolfgang Pauli gave the σ’s in 1927. Hermann Weyl (1928) and Eugene Wigner (1931) wrote the two books; John Slater’s determinant method was the rival of 1929. Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne’eman proposed SU(3) in 1961. Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills wrote the theory of 1954, and Pauli made the objection. François Englert and Robert Brout, Peter Higgs, and Gerald Guralnik, Carl Hagen and Tom Kibble found the mass mechanism (1964); Sheldon Glashow (1961), Steven Weinberg (1967) and Abdus Salam (1968) built the electroweak theory; Gerard ’t Hooft and Martinus Veltman proved it finite (1971); David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer found that the SU(3) force weakens (1973). Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer led the W and Z discovery (1983). Howard Georgi and Sheldon Glashow proposed SU(5) (1974). Helmut Rauch’s group (Grenoble and Vienna) and Samuel Werner’s group (Missouri) turned neutron spins by 720° in 1975. The CDF collaboration at Fermilab published the high W mass in 2022; ATLAS and CMS reported theirs in 2024. The Muon g−2 collaboration at Fermilab gave the final value in 2025.

A proof that is not allowed

“The brackets of su(2) and so(3) agree, and the bracket fixes the group, so SU(2) and SO(3) are the same group.” Not allowed: the bracket fixes the group only near I. Far from I the two differ, since e^(2πK_z) = −I in SU(2) while the turn by 2π in SO(3) is I. The bracket fixes the group up to such coverings.
