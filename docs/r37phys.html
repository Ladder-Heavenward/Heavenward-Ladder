What sets the speed of sound, and what beats it?

What sets the speed of sound, and what beats it? 37a gave the rule, the square root of stiffness over density, and took the stiffness of air on trust. This page earns that stiffness, and finds that a cold morning in Truckee carries a shout more slowly than a hot afternoon. Then it moves things: a listener driving towards a siren, which rung 24 did not do, and a jet that outruns its own sound and drags a cone of it across the ground. Last, it blows across the top of a bottle and works out the note from the bottle’s size alone.

Air squeezed too fast to shed heat is stiffer than air squeezed slowly, by about four tenths; the speed grows with the square root of temperature. A listener moving towards a source hears a higher pitch, by a different rule from a moving source. A source faster than sound trails a cone whose half-angle has sine c over v. A bottle is a spring of air with a plug of air on top, and hums at a frequency fixed by its neck and its volume.

37phys1Quick squeezes are stiffer

37a3 used K = γP₀ for air with γ = 1.4. If air were squeezed slowly enough for its heat to leak away, its stiffness would be P₀ alone, the law of a gas kept at one temperature, and c would be √(P₀/ρ) = √(101 325/1.204) ≈ 290 m/s. A sound swings too fast for that: each squeeze warms the air a little and the warmth pushes back, so the stiffness is larger by the factor γ.

Warm-air technique: the speed of sound in air grows as the square root of the absolute temperature: c ≈ 331.3 √(T/273.15) m/s, about 0.6 m/s more for every degree. Proof, with the gas law held in trust: P₀/ρ is proportional to the absolute temperature T for a given gas, so c = √(γP₀/ρ) is proportional to √T, and at 0 °C it is measured at 331.3 m/s.

c(-20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) = 331.3\sqrt{253.15/273.15} @@ the warm-air technique
\approx 319 \text{ m/s} @@ against 343 at 20 °C

37phys2A listener on the move

24·phys found that a siren coming towards you at v is heard c/(c − v) times as often. That was a moving source in still air. A listener moving towards a still source at speed v meets the crests faster: they pass at c + v relative to the listener, a wavelength λ = c/f apart.

Moving-listener technique: a listener moving towards a still source at speed v hears it (c + v)/c times as often; moving away, (c − v)/c. Proof: crests λ = c/f apart arrive at relative speed c ± v, so (c ± v)/λ of them a second (37a4).

\text{siren at } 25 \text{ m/s toward a still listener}:\ 700 \cdot 343/318 \approx 755 \text{ Hz} @@ 24·phys

37phys3Faster than sound

Let a jet fly in a straight line at speed v > c. Each moment it sends out a sound that spreads as a sphere at c. After time t the sphere sent at the start has radius ct, and the jet is vt further on. Every such sphere touches one cone, with the jet at its tip.

Sound-cone technique: a source faster than sound trails a cone of sound whose half-angle θ has sin θ = c/v. Proof: the line from the jet’s present place touching the sphere is at right angles to its radius ct, and the distance to the centre is vt, so sin θ = ct/(vt) (21b’s sine as a ratio in a right triangle).

\sin\theta = c/v = 1/2 @@ the sound-cone technique
\theta = 30^\circ @@ 21b4, an arc found exactly
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at height } 10 \text{ km, heard about } 17 \text{ km after it passes overhead}} \quad \blacksquare @@ h / tan 30° = h√3

The cone is where all the squeezes pile up at once, and the ground under it hears one sharp double crack: the boom. It is not made once as the jet passes the speed of sound; it is dragged along for as long as the jet flies faster.

37phys4Why a bottle hums

Blow across the top of a wine bottle and it hums one low note. The air in the neck, a plug of mass ρAL for a neck of area A and length L, sits on the air in the body, volume V. Push the plug in by x and the body’s air is squeezed by the share Ax/V, so by 37a2’s stiffness law its pressure rises by γP₀Ax/V and pushes back on the plug with force γP₀A²x/V.

Bottle technique: a bottle hums at f = (c/2π)√(A/(VL)). Proof: the plug is 24·phys1’s spring with mass ρAL and stiffness γP₀A²/V, so it swings at (1/2π)√(γP₀A/(ρVL)), and γP₀/ρ = c².

A = \pi (0.0095)^2 \approx 2.84 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2,\ V = 7.5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^3,\ L \approx 0.08 \text{ m} @@ neck 19 mm across, 8 cm with its ends
f = \tfrac{343}{2\pi}\sqrt{\tfrac{2.84 \times 10^{-4}}{7.5 \times 10^{-4} \times 0.08}} @@ the bottle technique
\htmlClass{new}{f \approx 119 \text{ Hz}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a little above the B♭ two octaves below middle C, 116.5 Hz

Drink half and V halves: the note goes up by √2, half an octave. The note does not depend on the bottle’s shape, only on its volume and its neck, so a round flask and a square one of the same volume and neck hum alike.

The accepted names

Squeezing too fast for heat to leave is adiabatic, slowly at one temperature isothermal; γ is the heat-capacity ratio. The moving-listener and moving-source shifts are both the Doppler effect. The ratio v/c is the Mach number, the cone the Mach cone, and the crack the sonic boom. The bottle is a Helmholtz resonator.

Who said it first

Newton found 298 m/s in 1687 by taking the slow stiffness; Laplace put in γ in 1816. Christian Doppler proposed the shift in 1842, and Christophorus Buys Ballot tested it in 1845 with horn players on a train. Ernst Mach photographed the cone of a bullet in 1887. Hermann von Helmholtz used glass globes tuned this way to pick single turnings out of a sound, in the 1850s.

A proof that is not allowed

“Cold air is denser, and c = √(K/ρ), so cold air is slower.” Not allowed as a proof: the stiffness γP₀ is fixed at a given pressure while ρ grows as T falls, so the conclusion is right, but the line drops the step that P₀/ρ is proportional to T; without it, nothing follows about how much slower.
