22a · proof

A climb under a ceiling arrives

Picture a list of numbers that never ends and only ever goes up, like the savings in a jar where nobody takes anything out. Suppose there is a ceiling it never passes. Does it have to arrive somewhere? It cannot climb forever, because the ceiling is in the way, and it cannot fall back. This step proves that it does arrive, and it says exactly where: at the lowest ceiling of all. The proof uses nothing new. It uses the fact you earned on the eighth rung, that the line has no holes. On a line with holes, the same climb could come to rest at a gap and arrive nowhere.

A list that only goes up and never passes a ceiling heads to the lowest ceiling of all. A list that only goes down and never passes a floor heads to the highest floor. Being kept between a floor and a ceiling is not enough on its own: the list also has to keep going one way.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 15i (a list heading to a number), 8c (the least upper bound), 9·cb4 (sequences), 10a (the challenge and the answer). 22b finds a settling thread in any list kept between a floor and a ceiling.

22a1New words on this rung

Stands on 9·cb4 (sequences) and 15i (heads to).

Rung 15 said what it means for a list s₁, s₂, s₃, … to head to a number L: for every ε > 0 there is N such that every n ≥ N has |sₙ − L| < ε. This rung uses that sentence on every page, so it gets a plain word.

From here on, a sequence settles when there is a real L it heads to. L is then its limit, written lim sₙ = L or sₙ → L. A sequence that settles nowhere wanders. Accepted names: the sequence converges, or diverges.

Two more words. A sequence climbs when each term is at least the one before: sₙ ≤ sₙ₊₁ for every n. It falls when each term is at most the one before. A ceiling of the sequence is an upper bound of the set of its terms, as 8c defined; a floor is a lower bound.

Limit technique for sequences: a sequence settles at no more than one real. If it headed to L and to M with L ≠ M, take ε = |L − M|/2: far enough out, a term would be within ε of both, and the triangle technique (8h) says that is too close.

22a2The climb technique

Stands on 22a1, 8c (the least upper bound) and 10a (the challenge and the answer).

Climb technique: a sequence that climbs and has a ceiling settles, at its least upper bound. Let σ be the least upper bound of the set of terms. It exists by 8c, because the set has a member and a ceiling.

\sigma = \sup\{s_n : n \ge 1\} @@ 8c: the set has a member and a ceiling
\varepsilon > 0:\ \ \sigma - \varepsilon \text{ is not a ceiling} @@ σ is the least ceiling
s_N > \sigma - \varepsilon \text{ for some } N @@ not a ceiling: some term passes it
n \ge N:\ \ \sigma - \varepsilon < s_N \le s_n \le \sigma @@ the sequence climbs; σ is a ceiling
|s_n - \sigma| < \varepsilon @@ both ends of the line above
\htmlClass{new}{s_n \to \sigma} \quad \blacksquare @@ ε was any positive real: the answer is N

The answer N comes from σ itself: you do not need to know σ in digits to prove the climb arrives there. That is the pattern of this whole rung. Settling is proved first, and the value is found afterwards, sometimes much later.

Fall technique: a sequence that falls and has a floor settles, at its greatest lower bound. Turn it upside down: −sₙ climbs with a ceiling, and 8c's greatest lower bound is minus the least upper bound of the negatives.

22a3Where it needs no holes

Stands on 22a2 and 8·cb5 (the decimal of √2).

The climb technique spent one thing: the least upper bound. On the fractions there may be none. Take the decimal of √2 cut off after n places: 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, 1.4142, … It climbs, and 2 is a ceiling. Every term is a fraction.

t_n = \text{the decimal of } \sqrt2 \text{ cut after } n \text{ places} @@ every tₙ is a fraction; it climbs; 2 is a ceiling
t_n \to \sqrt2 \text{ in } \mathbb{R} @@ the climb technique: √2 is the least ceiling (8·cb5)
\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q} @@ 8a
\htmlClass{new}{\text{in } \mathbb{Q} \text{ alone, } (t_n) \text{ settles nowhere}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a limit is unique (22a1): in ℚ there is nothing for it to head to

So "settles" depends on which line you stand on. On the line with no holes, every climb with a ceiling arrives. On the fractions, a climb can head straight at a hole. That is why rung 8 was built before this rung could be.

22a4Climbs you can catch: settling by a rule

Stands on 22a2, 9·cb4 (recursion) and 4c (induction).

Many sequences are given by a rule: a start, and how to get each term from the one before. The climb technique settles them in three moves. Show by induction that the sequence climbs. Show by induction that it stays under a ceiling. Then find the limit from the rule.

s_n < 2 \Rightarrow s_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + s_n} < \sqrt4 = 2 @@ a ceiling, by induction (4c) from s₁ = 1 < 2
s_n < 2 \Rightarrow s_n^2 < 2 + s_n @@ (sₙ − 2)(sₙ + 1) < 0 when 0 < sₙ < 2
s_{n+1}^2 = 2 + s_n > s_n^2 \Rightarrow s_{n+1} > s_n @@ it climbs
s_n \to L,\ \ s_{n+1} \to L @@ the climb technique; the same sequence, one term on
L = \sqrt{2 + L} @@ √ is continuous (11): the limit of the rule is the rule at the limit
\htmlClass{new}{L^2 - L - 2 = 0,\ L \ge 1 \Rightarrow L = 2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other root, −1, is below every term

Fixed-point technique: if a sequence given by sₙ₊₁ = g(sₙ) settles, and g is continuous at its limit L, then L = g(L). The order matters. First prove it settles. Only then may you solve L = g(L).

22a5Illegal moves

Stands on 22a1 to 22a4.

|s_n| \le 1 \Rightarrow s_n \text{ settles}\,? @@ (−1)ⁿ is kept between −1 and 1 and wanders: it must also climb or fall
s_n \uparrow \Rightarrow s_n \text{ settles}\,? @@ sₙ = n climbs and passes every ceiling
s_n \to \text{its first ceiling found}\,? @@ it settles at the least ceiling, not at any ceiling: 1 − 1/n has ceiling 5 and settles at 1
s_{n+1} = 2s_n,\ L = 2L \Rightarrow L = 0\,? @@ solve L = g(L) only after proving it settles: from s₁ = 1 it wanders

Each line looks like a short cut. Each one fails on a sequence you can write in one line, which is how you find out a guess breaks apart.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A sequence that settles converges; one that wanders diverges. Climbing and falling are increasing (or non-decreasing) and decreasing; a ceiling and a floor are an upper and a lower bound. The climb and fall techniques together are the monotone convergence theorem. The fixed-point technique is often stated for a recursively defined sequence or a fixed-point iteration.

Who said it first

Bernard Bolzano (1817) and Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1821) put sequences on the footing of every-and-some; the claim that a bounded increasing sequence converges was taken for granted until Richard Dedekind (1872) saw that it is exactly what the line with no holes provides. Dedekind wrote that he had been unable to find a proof of it in any book, and built the reals (rung 8) to supply one. In 22a.4.5, the teacher is Dedekind, at the Polytechnic in Zürich in the autumn of 1858 (he tells the story in the preface of Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen, 1872).
