Can a Sparse Graph Be Well Connected Everywhere?

Can a sparse graph be well connected everywhere? Take a graph where every dot has the same small number of links, three or ten, however many dots there are. Can it still have no small cut: no way to split off a group of dots, up to half of them, by cutting only a few links per dot? A ring cannot. A graph chosen at random can, and that was known long before anyone could write one down. Such graphs are called expanders, and they are wanted everywhere a network must stay joined, a code must survive errors, or a little randomness must be stretched to look like a lot. The grid of a graph has stretches, and the gap between the first and the second measures how well it expands: a large gap forces every cut to be large, and a small cut forces a small gap. The second stretch cannot be pushed below a fixed number, twice the root of one less than the number of links per dot, and this page calls the graphs that reach it best-gap graphs. In 1988 they were built, by number theory, when one less than the number of links is a prime. In 2013 three mathematicians proved that two-sided ones exist for every number of links, by putting signs on the links and asking where the roots of a polynomial lie. Two months later the same idea answered a question about measurement in quantum mechanics that had stood since 1959. What is open now is how to build the ones that are not two-sided, quickly and in every degree, and how far the signs can be pushed. In December 2024 it was shown that a fixed share of large random graphs of this kind are already best-gap. This page builds the cuts, the second stretch, both halves of the inequality that ties them, the floor under the second stretch, the signs, and the polynomial argument, and states the rest.

For a d-regular graph, h(G) is the least |∂S|/|S| over |S| ≤ n/2, and λ₂ the second stretch of its grid A. Then (d − λ₂)/2 ≤ h(G) ≤ √(2d(d − λ₂)). Closed walks counted in a tree make the largest |λᵢ| other than ±d at least about 2√(d − 1). Signing the links gives A_s, the two-lift has the stretches of A and A_s, and the average over signings of det(xI − A_s) is the matching polynomial, whose roots are at most 2√(d − 1). The polynomials of the signings form an interlacing family, so some signing has every stretch within ±2√(d − 1): bipartite best-gap graphs in every degree (2013). Here ∂S, the edge of S, is the set of links with one end in S; it is not a partial slope.

inf63.1Few Links, No Small Cut

A graph (42a) is d-regular when every dot has d links. For a set S of dots, ∂S is the set of links with one end in S and one outside: 42f1’s cut, every capacity 1. The expansion is h(G), the least of |∂S|/|S| over all S holding at least one and at most half of the n dots. A family of d-regular graphs, d fixed and n growing, is a family of expanders when h stays above some fixed c > 0.

The ring of 8 dots has d = 2, and four dots in a row are cut off by 2 links: h = 2/4 = 0.5. A ring of n dots has h = 2/⌊n/2⌋, which goes to 0, so rings are not expanders. The star-and-ring graph (ten dots, d = 3: an outer five-ring, an inner five-star, five spokes) has h = 1, and so does the 3-cube; both checked over every S. No graph does much better than d/2.

Average-cut technique: a d-regular graph with an even number n of dots has h(G) ≤ (d/2) · n/(n − 1).

S \text{ a random set of } n/2 \text{ dots, every such set as likely} @@ 42j4’s method: average, then pick
P(\text{a given link crosses}) = 2 \cdot \dfrac{(n/2)(n/2)}{n(n-1)} = \dfrac{n}{2(n-1)} @@ one end in, the other out, either way round
\text{average } |\partial S| = \dfrac{nd}{2} \cdot \dfrac{n}{2(n-1)} @@ nd/2 links; averages add (30g)
\text{some } S:\ |\partial S| \le \text{the average} @@ not every value is above its average
\htmlClass{new}{h(G) \le \dfrac{|\partial S|}{n/2} \le \dfrac{d}{2}\cdot\dfrac{n}{n-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the average-cut technique

For the star-and-ring graph the bound is 1.5 · 10/9 ≈ 1.67, against the true 1. The same method, run the other way, shows that random graphs expand. Random-neighbours technique: give each of n left dots 10 links to right dots chosen at random, independently (repeats allowed); then with chance more than 1 − 4 · 10⁻⁸ every set S of at most n/40 left dots has more than 2|S| right neighbours.

|S| = s,\ |T| = 2s:\ \ P(\text{all } 10s \text{ links of } S \text{ land in } T) = (2s/n)^{10s} @@ independent picks multiply (30f)
\tbinom{n}{k} \le \dfrac{n^k}{k!} \le \Big(\dfrac{en}{k}\Big)^k @@ e^k ≥ k^k/k!, one term of its series
\tbinom{n}{s}\tbinom{n}{2s}\Big(\dfrac{2s}{n}\Big)^{10s} \le \Big[\dfrac{e^3}{4}\Big(\dfrac{n}{s}\Big)^3\Big(\dfrac{2s}{n}\Big)^{10}\Big]^s = \Big[256e^3\Big(\dfrac sn\Big)^7\Big]^s @@ count the pairs S, T
s/n \le 1/40:\ \ 256e^3/40^7 \approx 3.14 \cdot 10^{-8} = q @@ so the s-th term is at most qˢ
\htmlClass{new}{P(\text{some } S \text{ has at most } 2s \text{ neighbours}) \le q + q^2 + \cdots \lt 4 \cdot 10^{-8}} \quad \blacksquare @@ chances of a union add at most (30f); few neighbours means all inside some T

So nearly every such graph spreads every small set to more than twice its size. The proof names no graph. Writing one down, with a rule that says fast who is linked to whom, is the problem the rest of the page is about.

inf63.2The Second Stretch

The grid A of a graph has a 1 at (u, v) when u and v are linked. It is symmetric, so by 31d’s spectral technique it has n real stretches d ≥ λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λₙ along axes at right angles. For a d-regular graph the vector 1 of all ones is stretched by d, and by inf45.5’s row-bound technique no stretch is larger than d in size; so λ₁ = d. The spectral gap is d − λ₂.

Link-sum technique: for every vector x, xᵀ(dI − A)x is the sum over the links uv of (x_u − x_v)². Second-axis technique: every x at right angles to 1 has xᵀAx ≤ λ₂ xᵀx, with equality at an axis of λ₂.

x^{\mathsf T}Ax = \textstyle\sum_{u,v} A_{uv}x_ux_v = 2\sum_{\text{links } uv} x_ux_v @@ each link counted from both ends
d\,x^{\mathsf T}x = \textstyle\sum_u d\,x_u^2 = \sum_{\text{links } uv}(x_u^2 + x_v^2) @@ each dot is on d links
x^{\mathsf T}(dI - A)x = \textstyle\sum_{\text{links } uv}(x_u - x_v)^2 @@ subtract: the link-sum technique
x = \textstyle\sum_{i \ge 2} c_i q_i,\ \ q_1 = \mathbf 1/\sqrt n @@ the axes qᵢ of 31d3; x at right angles to 1 has no q₁ part
\htmlClass{new}{x^{\mathsf T}Ax = \textstyle\sum_{i\ge2}\lambda_i c_i^2 \le \lambda_2 \sum_{i \ge 2} c_i^2 = \lambda_2\,x^{\mathsf T}x} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second-axis technique

So the gap d − λ₂ is the least of Σ(x_u − x_v)²/Σx_u² over vectors x adding to 0: how little a vector balanced at 0 can change across the links. The star-and-ring graph has stretches 3, 1 (five times) and −2 (four times): gap 2. The 3-cube has 3, 1, 1, 1, −1, −1, −1, −3, which are inf45.4’s n − 2k with C(3, k) vectors each, the axes being inf45.2’s turnings: gap 2. The ring of 8 has 2cos(2πj/8): 2, √2, √2, 0, 0, −√2, −√2, −2, so its gap is 2 − √2 ≈ 0.59, and the ring of n has gap 2 − 2cos(2π/n), which shrinks to 0 like the ring’s cuts.

On a graph drawn on a group that turns about, with a link from each member to the members a few fixed steps away, the stretches are read off ∞20.1’s turnings: each turning is a direction the grid only stretches, and its stretch is the sum of the turning over the steps, by ∞20.5. The plan is the group with its steps; which dot is which is let go.

inf63.3The Gap and the Cut, Both Ways

Easy-cut technique: d − λ₂ ≤ 2h(G). Sweep-cut technique: h(G) ≤ √(2d(d − λ₂)). So a family is a family of expanders exactly when its gaps stay above some fixed number.

|S| = s \le n/2,\ |\partial S|/s = h;\ \ x_u = n - s \text{ on } S,\ -s \text{ off } S @@ a set where the least cut is met
\textstyle\sum_u x_u = s(n - s) - (n - s)s = 0 @@ x is at right angles to 1
\textstyle\sum_{\text{links}}(x_u - x_v)^2 = |\partial S|\,n^2,\ \ x^{\mathsf T}x = s(n-s)n @@ only cut links differ, by n
d - \lambda_2 \le \dfrac{|\partial S|\,n}{s(n - s)} @@ the second-axis and link-sum techniques
\htmlClass{new}{d - \lambda_2 \le 2|\partial S|/s = 2h} \quad \blacksquare @@ n − s ≥ n/2
  
Av = \lambda_2 v,\ v \perp \mathbf 1,\ P = \{u : v_u \gt 0\},\ |P| \le n/2 @@ change the sign of v if needed: the positive and negative dots share nothing
y = \max(v, 0):\ \ u \in P \Rightarrow (Ay)_u \ge (Av)_u = \lambda_2 y_u @@ y ≥ v entry by entry, and A has no negative entries
\textstyle\sum_{\text{links}}(y_u - y_v)^2 = \sum_{u \in P} y_u\,(dy_u - (Ay)_u) \le (d - \lambda_2)\sum y_u^2 @@ the link-sum technique; y is 0 off P
S_t = \{u : y_u^2 \gt t\} \subseteq P:\ \ \textstyle\int_0^\infty |\partial S_t|\,dt = \sum_{\text{links}} |y_u^2 - y_v^2| @@ a link is cut for t between its two values
|\partial S_t| \ge h\,|S_t| \Rightarrow \textstyle\sum_{\text{links}} |y_u^2 - y_v^2| \ge h \int_0^\infty |S_t|\,dt = h\sum y_u^2 @@ |S_t| ≤ n/2; a dot is in S_t for t below y_u²
\textstyle\sum |y_u - y_v|(y_u + y_v) \le \sqrt{\sum (y_u - y_v)^2}\sqrt{\sum (y_u + y_v)^2} @@ the product-bound technique (31a)
\textstyle\sum_{\text{links}}(y_u + y_v)^2 \le 2\sum_{\text{links}}(y_u^2 + y_v^2) = 2d\sum y_u^2 @@ (p + q)² ≤ 2p² + 2q²
\htmlClass{new}{h \le \sqrt{2d(d - \lambda_2)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ put the last four lines together and divide by Σ yᵤ²

Checked. Ring of 8: (2 − √2)/2 ≈ 0.29 ≤ 0.5 ≤ √(4(2 − √2)) ≈ 1.53. Star-and-ring: 2/2 = 1 ≤ 1 ≤ √12 ≈ 3.46, the easy side met exactly. The sweep-cut proof is also a method: sort the dots by the second axis and try the n cuts that take a top part; one of them is within the bound.

inf63.4No Better Than 2√(d − 1)

How large can the gap be? Let λ be the largest size |λᵢ| over the stretches other than d and, when the graph has two sides with every link across, −d. The answer comes from counting closed walks. By inf41.5’s signed-count technique a joined d-regular graph, as a shape of dots and links, has n(d − 2)/2 + 1 independent loops, so for d ≥ 3 it is full of them. The walks are counted as if there were none.

Tree-cover technique: in a d-regular graph, the closed walks of 2k steps from any dot number at least Cₖ(d − 1)ᵏ, where Cₖ = C(2k, k)/(k + 1).

T:\ \text{the walks from } v \text{ that never step straight back; } w \text{ joined to } w \text{ plus one step} @@ a tree: every dot of T has one way back to the empty walk
\text{a walk in } T \mapsto \text{the walk in } G \text{ through the last dots} @@ a step of T is a step of G
\text{the } T\text{-walk is read back from the } G\text{-walk} @@ a step back to the dot before is a step up the tree; any other is a step down
\text{closed in } T \Rightarrow \text{closed in } G,\ \text{and different in } T \Rightarrow \text{different in } G @@ so G has at least as many closed walks
\text{closed } T\text{-walks of } 2k \text{ steps} \ge C_k\,(d - 1)^k @@ inf53.3: Cₖ lists of k steps down and k up; each step down has at least d − 1 new dots
\htmlClass{new}{\#\{\text{closed walks of } 2k \text{ steps from } v\} \ge C_k(d - 1)^k} \quad \blacksquare @@ the tree-cover technique

Trace-bound technique: λ²ᵏ ≥ (n Cₖ(d − 1)ᵏ − 2d²ᵏ)/(n − 1) for every k; so as n grows with d fixed, λ cannot stay below (Cₖ(d − 1)ᵏ)^(1/2k) for any k, and these numbers climb to 2√(d − 1). By inf53.1’s trace-of-powers technique, tr A²ᵏ is the sum of the λᵢ²ᵏ, at most 2d²ᵏ + (n − 1)λ²ᵏ, and it is also the number of closed walks of 2k steps, at least n Cₖ(d − 1)ᵏ by the tree-cover technique. C(2k, k) is the largest of 2k + 1 terms adding to 4ᵏ, so Cₖ ≥ 4ᵏ/((2k + 1)(k + 1)), and (Cₖ(d − 1)ᵏ)^(1/2k) ≥ 2√(d − 1)/((2k + 1)(k + 1))^(1/2k), which tends to 2√(d − 1). For d = 3 the numbers are 2.30 at k = 10, 2.65 at k = 50, 2.72 at k = 100, against 2√2 ≈ 2.83.

A d-regular graph is best-gap when λ ≤ 2√(d − 1): the gap is as large as large graphs allow. The star-and-ring graph (λ = 2 ≤ 2.83), the 3-cube (λ = 1) and every complete graph are best-gap. Held in trust: the same floor holds for λ₂ alone, not just for the largest size (1980s, with a short proof published in 1991). In 1988 best-gap graphs were built for every d with d − 1 a prime, from number theory, and in 1994 for d − 1 any power of a prime. For other d, such as d = 7, no rule was known.

inf63.5Signs on the Links

inf45.4 put signs on the cube’s links to make a grid square to nI. Put signs on any graph’s links: a signing s gives each link a sign ±1, and A_s has s(uv) at (u, v). The two-lift of G by s has two copies u₀, u₁ of each dot; a link uv with sign + joins u₀v₀ and u₁v₁, and one with sign − joins u₀v₁ and u₁v₀. It is d-regular on 2n dots, and it has two sides when G has.

Two-lift technique: the stretches of the two-lift are the stretches of A together with those of A_s. Write A = A₊ + A₋ by the sign of each link, so A_s = A₊ − A₋. The lift’s grid has A₊ in the two corner blocks on its middle and A₋ in the other two. It sends (x, x) to (Ax, Ax) and (x, −x) to (A_s x, −A_s x); the axes of A and of A_s, taken this way, are 2n vectors at right angles. So if G is best-gap and some signing keeps every stretch of A_s within ±2√(d − 1), the lift is best-gap with twice the dots.

Average-signing technique: the average over all signings of det(xI − A_s) is μ(x) = Σₖ (−1)ᵏ mₖ xⁿ⁻²ᵏ, where mₖ is the number of sets of k links of G with no dot in common.

\det(xI - A_s) = \textstyle\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\prod_u (xI - A_s)_{\sigma(u)u} @@ 27e2’s sum over the shuffles σ
\sigma \text{ splits into rings: fixed dots give } x;\ \text{a ring } u_1 \to u_2 \to \cdots \to u_r \to u_1 \text{ gives } \textstyle\prod (-s(u_iu_{i+1})) @@ entries off the middle are −s on links, 0 elsewhere
r \ge 3:\ \text{the } r \text{ links of the ring are different} @@ r different dots in a row, joined back to the first
\text{average of a product of different independent signs} = 0 @@ each sign is +1 or −1 with chance ½
r = 2:\ (-s(uv))^2 = 1,\ \ \text{sign of a swap} = -1 @@ a swap of the two ends of one link
\htmlClass{new}{\text{average} = \textstyle\sum_k (-1)^k m_k\,x^{n - 2k}} \quad \blacksquare @@ only shuffles made of fixed dots and swaps along links survive: sets of links with no shared dot

For the complete graph on 4 dots, m₀ = 1, m₁ = 6, m₂ = 3, so μ = x⁴ − 6x² + 3, with largest root √(3 + √6) ≈ 2.334; the average of the 64 polynomials was checked to be exactly this. For the two-sided K₃,₃, m = 1, 9, 18, 6 and μ = x⁶ − 9x⁴ + 18x² − 6, largest root about 2.508 (checked over all 512 signings). Held in trust (1972): for every graph μ has only real roots, and when no dot has more than d links every root has size at most 2√(d − 1). Here 2.334 and 2.508 are below 2√2 ≈ 2.83.

inf63.6A Family of Polynomials

The average μ has its roots below 2√(d − 1). An average of numbers is at least one of them, but the largest root of an average of polynomials need not be at least the largest root of one of them: (x − 1)² and (x + 1)² average to x² + 1, which has no real root at all. The 2013 idea is a condition under which it is.

Monic polynomials f₁, …, f_m of degree n with real roots have a common interlacing when some β₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ βₙ₋₁ sit between the roots of every one of them: α₁ ≤ β₁ ≤ α₂ ≤ ⋯ ≤ βₙ₋₁ ≤ αₙ for each fᵢ. Common-interlacing technique: if f₁, …, f_m have a common interlacing and p₁, …, p_m are chances, then some fᵢ with pᵢ > 0 has its largest root at most the largest root of Σ pᵢ fᵢ.

f_i(x) = \textstyle\prod_j (x - \alpha_j^{(i)}),\ \ \beta = \beta_{n-1} @@ the factor technique (44c3)
\beta \le x \le \alpha_n^{(i)} \Rightarrow f_i(x) \le 0 @@ one factor x − αₙ is at most 0, the rest at least 0
r = \text{the least, over } p_i \gt 0, \text{ of the largest roots } \alpha_n^{(i)} @@ r ≥ β
F = \textstyle\sum p_i f_i:\ \ F(r) = \sum p_i f_i(r) \le 0 @@ each fᵢ at r is at most 0, by the second line
F \text{ is monic, so } F(x) \gt 0 \text{ for large } x @@ the chances add to 1
\htmlClass{new}{F \text{ has a root in } [r, \infty) \text{, so its largest root is at least } r} \quad \blacksquare @@ the in-between-value technique (12a)

The family. Choose the signs one link at a time. Each choice so far is a node of a tree, its polynomial the average of det(xI − A_s) over the signings that agree with it; the root is μ, the leaves the single signings. The 2013 proof shows that the two children of every node have a common interlacing; this is the real-rootedness lemma, proved by polynomials in several variables that have no root with every part in the upper half plane, held in trust here. Then walk down: by the common-interlacing technique one child’s largest root is at most its parent’s. The leaf reached is a signing with largest stretch at most the largest root of μ, so at most 2√(d − 1).

For a graph with two sides, the stretches of A_s come in pairs ±λ (change the sign of every dot on one side), so the bound on the largest is a bound on all. Start from K_{d,d}, whose stretches are d, −d and zeros: best-gap. Lift again and again. So for every d there are two-sided best-gap graphs with as many dots as wanted (2013). For K₃,₃ the signing (+, +, +, −, +, −, +, −, −), links listed from each left dot to the right dots in order, has stretches ±2, ±2, ±1, all below 2.508, and the lift is a 3-regular two-sided graph on 12 dots with stretches ±3, ±2, ±2, ±1 and four zeros: best-gap. Of the 512 signings, 192 have largest stretch at most 2.508.

inf63.7A Question From 1959

In 1959 a question was asked about measurement in quantum mechanics: if a reading of an infinite system is known, as sharply as possible, on every grid that is diagonal, is the reading of every other bounded grid fixed? It stayed open for fifty four years, and along the way it was shown to be the same as many questions in other subjects. One of them is about splitting vectors, and that is the one stated here.

Stated, not proved (2013): if vectors v₁, …, v_m have Σ vᵢvᵢᵀ = I and every |vᵢ|² ≤ ε, they can be split into two groups each of whose Σ vᵢvᵢᵀ has every stretch at most (1 + √(2ε))²/2. (This is the 2013 paper’s own bound for two groups, (1/√2 + √ε)², written another way.) The whole is I, with every stretch 1; the statement says each half can be close to I/2 when no single vector is large. At ε = 1/100 the bound is about 0.65. The proof replaces signs on links by a random group for each vector, takes the average of the characteristic polynomials of the two halves (a mixed characteristic polynomial), shows by the same real-rootedness that the choices form an interlacing family, and bounds the largest root of the average by a barrier argument, all held in trust.

v_1, v_2, v_3 \text{ in the plane at } 120^\circ,\ |v_i|^2 = \tfrac23:\ \ \textstyle\sum v_iv_i^{\mathsf T} = I @@ three equal vectors spread evenly
\text{any split puts two together:}\ v_1v_1^{\mathsf T} + v_2v_2^{\mathsf T} = I - v_3v_3^{\mathsf T} @@ it stretches the vector at right angles to v₃ by 1
\varepsilon = \tfrac23:\ \text{no split does better than } 1 @@ one vector is too large a piece of I
12 \text{ vectors at } 15^\circ,\ |v_i|^2 = \tfrac16:\ \ \text{alternate ones: six at } 30^\circ @@ each half spread evenly round half a turn
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each half} = \tfrac12 I} \quad \blacksquare @@ checked; the bound at ε = 1/6 is about 1.24, weaker than this split

Why it is the same question as the graphs: in both, an average over random signs has a polynomial whose roots are controlled, and the interlacing family turns the average into one good choice. Neither proof says how to find the choice fast; for the graphs a method of polynomial time was given in 2016.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞63.oq.1For every d, build best-gap graphs that do not have two sides, with a rule that says fast who is linked to whom. They are known for d − 1 a power of a prime (1988, 1994); the 2013 proof gives two-sided ones only; a method of polynomial time for two-sided ones came in 2016; and a proof posted in December 2024 shows that a fixed share of large random d-regular graphs are best-gap, which gives one-sided ones in every fixed degree d ≥ 3 but builds none. Whether a rule exists that names the links of a dot without building the whole graph, in every degree, is open as far as this page has checked.
Why it is hard: the 1988 rules come from number theory, which reaches only prime powers, and the 2013 walk down the tree needs the averages, which are slow to compute.
∞63.oq.2Does every d-regular graph have a signing with every stretch of A_s, the most negative included, of size at most 2√(d − 1)? This was asked in 2006; the 2013 proof bounds only the largest stretch, which is why it needs two sides.
Why it is hard: the interlacing family controls the largest root of one polynomial; controlling the smallest at the same time needs the same signing to win twice.
∞63.oq.3What is the best bound in the splitting statement of inf63.7, as a function of ε, and into how few groups must vectors be split to bring every half below a given size? The 2013 bound (1 + √(2ε))²/2 is not known to be the best possible; this page has not revisited the later improvements.

A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Networks that are sparse and well joined were wanted before they could be built: for switching, for codes that correct errors, and later for stretching a little randomness into a lot. Random graphs were known to do the job, and the first explicit families were found in the 1970s; this page has not revisited the details of those first constructions.

In the 1980s the second stretch was tied to the cut, by the inequality of inf63.3, and the floor of about 2√(d − 1) was found. In 1988 two constructions, published independently, built graphs that meet the floor exactly, for d − 1 a prime, using a deep fact from number theory; in 1994 the construction was carried to every prime power. For other degrees no construction was known.

Random graphs came close. In 2004 a proof was posted that for each fixed d and every ε > 0, a large random d-regular graph has every stretch other than ±d within 2√(d − 1) + ε with chance near 1; it was published as a memoir in 2008. In a paper posted in 2003 and published in 2006 the signs and lifts of inf63.5 were proposed as a road to best-gap graphs of every degree, with a guess that a good signing always exists.

The question of 1959 lived in another world, the algebras of grids used in quantum mechanics. Over five decades it was found equal to many questions in other subjects, among them the splitting of vectors in inf63.7; this page has not revisited the list. In April 2013 a team of three posted the proof of two-sided best-gap graphs in every degree, and in June 2013 the solution of the 1959 question. The applied mathematics society of the United States gave them a prize in 2014, and both papers appeared in the Annals of Mathematics in 2015.

What is argued now is what existence is worth. The 2013 proofs show that a good signing exists and give no fast way to find it; a method of polynomial time came in 2016. In December 2024 a proof was posted that a fixed share of large random regular graphs are already best-gap, settling a question of whether the floor is the rule or the exception for random graphs; the share is near 69 percent. The open part is construction: a rule, in every degree, that names the neighbours of a dot without building the graph.

The accepted names

h(G) is the edge expansion (Cheeger constant) of the graph; d − λ₂ the spectral gap; the easy-cut and sweep-cut techniques are the two halves of the discrete Cheeger inequality (Dodziuk 1984; Alon and Milman 1985; Alon 1986), after Jeff Cheeger’s 1970 inequality for manifolds. The second-axis technique is the Courant–Fischer (Rayleigh quotient) characterisation of λ₂. The random-neighbours technique is a Pinsker-style counting proof of vertex expansion. The trace-bound technique is a weak form of the Alon–Boppana bound, published with a short proof by A. Nilli (1991); the tree T is the universal cover of G. Ramanujan graphs are named for Srinivasa Ramanujan’s conjecture on the coefficients of the discriminant modular form, proved by Pierre Deligne (1974). The two-lift is a 2-lift in the sense of Yonatan Bilu and Nati Linial (“Lifts, discrepancy and nearly optimal spectral gap”, Combinatorica 2006), whose conjecture is oq.2. μ is the matching polynomial; the average-signing technique is due to Chris Godsil and Ivan Gutman (1981); the real roots and the bound 2√(d − 1) are the Heilmann–Lieb theorem (1972). The common-interlacing technique and the tree of polynomials are the method of interlacing families; the real-rootedness lemma uses real stable polynomials. The question of 1959 is the Kadison–Singer problem, of Richard Kadison and Isadore Singer; the splitting statement is Nik Weaver’s KS₂ form; the paving form is Joel Anderson’s.

Who said it first

Grigory Margulis (1988) and Alexander Lubotzky, Ralph Phillips and Peter Sarnak (“Ramanujan graphs”, Combinatorica 1988) built Ramanujan graphs for d − 1 prime; Moshe Morgenstern (1994) for every prime power. Joel Friedman proved Alon’s second eigenvalue conjecture (arXiv 2004; Memoirs of the AMS 2008). Adam Marcus, Daniel Spielman and Nikhil Srivastava, “Interlacing families I: bipartite Ramanujan graphs of all degrees” (arXiv April 2013; Annals 182, 2015) and “Interlacing families II: mixed characteristic polynomials and the Kadison–Singer problem” (arXiv June 2013; Annals 182, 2015); “Interlacing families IV: bipartite Ramanujan graphs of all sizes” (2015). They received the 2014 George Pólya Prize of SIAM. Michael B. Cohen, “Ramanujan graphs in polynomial time” (FOCS 2016). Jiaoyang Huang, Theo McKenzie and Horng-Tzer Yau, “Ramanujan property and edge universality of random regular graphs” (arXiv December 2024), reported by Quanta Magazine in April 2025.

A proof that is not allowed

“The average of det(xI − A_s) has its largest root below 2√(d − 1), so a random signing has its largest stretch below 2√(d − 1).” Not allowed: a random signing can be bad, and many are (for K₃,₃, 320 of the 512 are above the average’s root). Only the interlacing family guarantees that one leaf is at most its parent, and only one is promised.
