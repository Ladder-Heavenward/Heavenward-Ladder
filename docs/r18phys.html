18·phys · physics

Why do planets travel on ellipses?

Why do planets travel on ellipses? For two thousand years astronomers drew the planets on perfect round paths, and added smaller round paths on top when the first ones failed. Mars would not fit. Around 1600 one astronomer spent years on the best round path he could make, with its centre set off from the sun. It missed the measured places of Mars by about a quarter of the width of the full moon, and he threw it away for an oval. What makes it hard is that no one sees a planet’s path. We see a point of light from an earth that is moving too, and every place has to be worked out. And the word why asks for a cause, a pull from the sun, which this rung has no tools to write down. So this page asks a narrower question, one it can answer. Do the measured places of a planet fit an ellipse with the sun at a focus, and how could you tell? It lifts each place by its distance from the sun, and finds the lifted points on one tilted plane through the cone of 18·cb16. The tilt is the stretch of the orbit. Then it reads the orbit’s size off a line, finds the sun at a focus and not at the centre, and finds the nearest and farthest distances.

Measure each place of a planet from the sun. Then raise the place straight up by its own distance from the sun. Every raised point is on a cone with its tip at the sun, because its height equals its distance out. If all the raised points also lie on one tilted plane, the path is a cut of that cone, and 18·cb16 says the cut is an ellipse when the plane is less steep than the cone. Seen side on, the plane is a straight line: distance against place across. Its slope is the stretch, and where it crosses gives the size. The sun turns out to sit at a focus, off to one side of the centre.

Places of Mars

Put the sun at (0, 0) in the plane of Mars’s path. Let the x axis point toward the place where Mars comes nearest. Measure in the accepted unit AU, the earth’s mean distance from the sun, about 149.6 million km. Here is a model: seven places of Mars, worked out from modern tables and rounded to a thousandth of an AU. The tables themselves rest on centuries of sightings.

The last row is each place’s distance from the sun, r = √(x² + y²), by distance-laws (18a). It runs from 1.381 to 1.665. So the path is not a round one about the sun: on such a path r would never change.

Lift each place by its distance

18·cb16 built the double cone x² + y² = z². Its upper half, z ≥ 0, is the set of points whose height equals their distance out from the z axis. Raise each place (x, y) of Mars to height r. The raised point (x, y, r) has r² = x² + y², so it lies on the upper cone. That holds for any place at all. The question is whether the raised points also lie on one plane.

Lift technique: if the distance from the sun is a straight-line function of x, then the path is a cut of the cone by a plane, and the slope sets which kind of cut. In marks: suppose every place has r = ℓ − ex, with ℓ > 0. Then the places lie on (1 − e²)x² + 2eℓx + y² = ℓ². That is an ellipse when 0 ≤ e < 1, a parabola when e = 1, and a hyperbola when e > 1.

(x, y, r):\ \ r^2 = x^2 + y^2,\ r \ge 0 @@ the raised place is on the upper cone (18·cb16)
r = \ell - ex \iff (x, y, r) \text{ is on the plane } z = \ell - ex @@ the plane z = mx + c of 18·cb16, with m = −e and c = ℓ
x^2 + y^2 = (\ell - ex)^2 @@ put the plane’s z into the cone
(1 - e^2)x^2 + 2e\ell x + y^2 = \ell^2 @@ multiply out and collect
\htmlClass{new}{|e| \lt 1:\ \text{ellipse};\ \ |e| = 1:\ \text{parabola};\ \ |e| \gt 1:\ \text{hyperbola}} \quad \blacksquare @@ cone-cut (18·cb16): the tilt alone sorts the cut

So the test is a line. Draw r against x for the seven places. If the points lie on one straight line, the path is a cut of the cone, and the line’s slope is −e.

Two places fix the line, and the other five test it. Every one of them sits within a thousandth or two of it, about the rounding of the table. So the lifted places of Mars lie on one plane, tilted much less than the cone. The path is an ellipse, and it is nearly round: e is less than a tenth.

Size, stretch, and where the sun sits

Complete the square in x (8i), and the equation shows its centre and half-widths. This is the completed-squares technique of 18h on the lift technique’s equation.

(1 - e^2)\Big(x + \tfrac{e\ell}{1 - e^2}\Big)^2 + y^2 = \tfrac{\ell^2}{1 - e^2} @@ completing the square (8i)
a = \tfrac{\ell}{1 - e^2},\ \ b = \tfrac{\ell}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}},\ \ \text{centre } (-ea,\ 0) @@ divide by ℓ²/(1 − e²): the ellipse of 18e, moved
\text{Mars}:\ \ a \approx \tfrac{1.510}{0.9913} \approx 1.523,\ \ b \approx 1.517,\ \ ea \approx 0.142 @@ the long half-width barely beats the short one
c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = ea @@ b² = a²(1 − e²), so a² − b² = e²a²
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the foci are } (0, 0) \text{ and } (-2ea,\ 0)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ellipse (18e): the foci sit c either side of the centre

Focus technique: the sun sits at a focus of the orbit, not at its centre, and the other focus is empty. For Mars the centre is 0.142 AU from the sun, and the empty focus is at (−0.285, 0). By ellipse (18e), every place has its two distances to the foci adding to 2a ≈ 3.047.

\text{day } 60:\ \ d\big((1.114, 0.859),\ (0, 0)\big) = 1.407 @@ the r of the table
d\big((1.114, 0.859),\ (-0.285, 0)\big) = \sqrt{1.399^2 + 0.859^2} \approx 1.642 @@ distance-laws (18a)
1.407 + 1.642 = 3.049 \approx 2a @@ ellipse (18e), within the table’s rounding

Could the sun sit at the centre after all? On an ellipse centred at the sun, the distance r is the same at x and at −x. The table says no. Near x = 1.114 Mars is 1.407 from the sun. Near x = −1.216, a little further across on the other side, it is 1.624. One side of the path is nearer the sun than the other.

Nearest and farthest

q = \tfrac{\ell}{1 + e} = a(1 - e) \approx 1.381 @@ orbit-ends (18h): at the end of the long axis toward x
Q = \tfrac{\ell}{1 - e} = a(1 + e) \approx 1.666 @@ the far end
\htmlClass{new}{\ell = \tfrac{2qQ}{q + Q},\ \ e = \tfrac{Q - q}{Q + q}} @@ run backwards: two distances give the whole line

Mars ranges from about 207 to 249 million km from the sun. Halley’s comet comes in to 0.586 AU and goes out to 35.1 AU, so e ≈ 0.967: its lifting plane is nearly as steep as the cone. A body seen in 2017 came from outside the solar system with e ≈ 1.20. Its plane is steeper than the cone, its path is one branch of a hyperbola, and it will not come back. The three cuts of 18·cb16 are all in the sky.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

That a planet moves on an ellipse with the sun at one focus is a measured law, not a technique. Its range is one planet and the sun, alone. Its exceptions are known, and they are small. The other planets pull too, so Mars’s ellipse wobbles and slowly turns. Mercury’s turns by a tiny extra amount each century that the pulls of the planets could not explain until 1915. And every measured place carries its own error.

State the law, how closely it fits, and the size of the misses you expect.

The astronomer who threw away the round path did so because its miss was bigger than the misses of the sightings he trusted. A fit is judged against the error of the measurements, never alone.

A round path set off from the sun misses Mars’s measured places by about a quarter of the full moon’s width. The sightings are good to about a fifteenth of it. Would you keep the round path?

Who said it first

Kepler wrote that, since the divine goodness had given us in Tycho an observer so careful that an error of eight minutes could not be allowed, it was fitting to acknowledge that gift and use it: those eight minutes alone had led the way to reforming all of astronomy.

Johannes Kepler, Astronomia nova (1609), chapter 19; paraphrase

The astronomer of the opening and of 18·phys.4.5 is Johannes Kepler. His best round path, the vicarious hypothesis, missed Tycho Brahe’s sightings by 8 minutes of arc, where Tycho was good to about 2. The one who derived the ellipse from a pull is Isaac Newton (1687), who wrote that he framed no hypotheses about its cause. Mercury’s extra turning, about 43 seconds of arc a century, was found by Urbain Le Verrier (1859) and explained by Albert Einstein (1915). The visitor of 2017 is ʻOumuamua.

Accepted names: the law is Kepler’s first law; e is the eccentricity; ℓ is the semi-latus rectum; the nearest and farthest places of a path round the sun are the perihelion and aphelion; a is the semi-major axis.
