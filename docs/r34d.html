34d · proof

Finitely many suffice

Rung 12 proved that a continuous curve on a closed stretch is bounded and reaches its highest and lowest values. Rung 22 proved that a list trapped between a floor and a ceiling has a thread that settles. Rung 30 covered a closed stretch by intervals and found that finitely many of them already did the job. These are three faces of one property, and this step names it. A space is finitely covered when every way of covering it by open sets has a finite part that still covers it. Closed stretches are finitely covered, and on the line the finitely covered parts are exactly the closed and bounded ones. Continuous maps carry the property along, and highest values follow in any finitely covered space. With a distance, every list in such a space has a settling thread, and every cover has a reach below which no ball can escape it. Pairs of finitely covered spaces are finitely covered. And when the pairs become endless families, the property and the axiom of choice of rung 6 turn out to stand or fall together.

Every cover of a finitely covered space by open sets has a finite part that still covers. Closed stretches are finitely covered. A closed part of a finitely covered space is finitely covered, a finitely covered part of a space that keeps points apart is closed, and on the line the finitely covered parts are the closed and bounded ones. Continuous images of finitely covered spaces are finitely covered, so a continuous real map on one reaches a highest value. With a distance, every list has a settling thread, and every cover has a reach that every small ball respects. Products of two finitely covered spaces are finitely covered, and if every product of finitely covered spaces were, choice would follow.

34d1Covers, and finite parts of them

An open cover of a space X is a family of open sets whose union is X. A finite part of it is finitely many of its members. X is finitely covered when every open cover of X has a finite part whose union is still X. Its accepted name is compact, from the Latin compactus, packed together: this step’s one new academy word.

(0, 1) is not finitely covered: the stretches (1/n, 1) cover it, and finitely many of them reach down only to their smallest 1/n. The line is not: (−n, n) covers it. A finite space is, always.

Closed-stretch-covered technique: every closed stretch [v, w] is finitely covered.

\mathcal U \text{ an open cover of } [v, w] @@ any cover, not only a list of intervals
s = \sup\{x \in [v, w] : [v, x] \text{ lies in finitely many members of } \mathcal U\} @@ v lies in some member, so the set has a member (8c)
s \in U \in \mathcal U,\ \ (s - r, s + r) \cap [v, w] \subseteq U @@ s is in [v, w], which the cover covers; U is open
[v, x] \text{ lies in finitely many members, for some } x \in (s - r, s] @@ else s − r would be an upper bound
[v, y] \text{ lies in those and } U,\ \text{for every } y < s + r,\ y \le w @@ add U; so s ≥ min(s + r/2, w), and s = w
\htmlClass{new}{[v, w] \text{ lies in finitely many members of } \mathcal U} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same, with y = w

This is the middle of 30a1’s cover-total proof. There it ran on a list of intervals. Here it runs on any family of open sets at all.

34d2Closed and bounded

Closed-part-covered technique: a closed part of a finitely covered space is finitely covered.

C \subseteq X \text{ closed},\ \ \{U_i \cap C\} \text{ an open cover of } C @@ open in a part: Uᵢ open in X (34b5)
\{U_i\} \cup \{X \setminus C\} \text{ covers } X @@ the leftover of C is open
X = U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n \cup (X \setminus C) @@ X is finitely covered; add the leftover if it was not picked
\htmlClass{new}{C = (U_1 \cap C) \cup \cdots \cup (U_n \cap C)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the leftover holds no point of C

Covered-is-closed technique: in a space that keeps points apart, a finitely covered part is closed.

K \text{ finitely covered},\ \ x \notin K @@ show the leftover is open at x
\text{each } y \in K:\ \ y \in V_y,\ x \in U_y,\ \ U_y \cap V_y = \emptyset @@ the space keeps points apart
K \subseteq V_{y_1} \cup \cdots \cup V_{y_n} @@ the V_y cover K
\htmlClass{new}{U_{y_1} \cap \cdots \cap U_{y_n} \text{ is open, holds } x, \text{ misses } K} \quad \blacksquare @@ finitely many overlaps (34a2); it misses each V_{y_i}

Closed-and-bounded technique: a part of the line is finitely covered exactly when it is closed and bounded. If K is finitely covered, it is closed by the covered-is-closed technique, and the stretches (−n, n) cover it, so finitely many do, and it is bounded. If K is closed and bounded, it is a closed part of some [−n, n], which is finitely covered by 34d1, so K is too by the closed-part-covered technique.

On the line, then, closed and bounded is the same as finitely covered. In other spaces it is not, and the illegal moves show where.

34d3Highest values, again

Image-covered technique: the image of a finitely covered space under a continuous map is finitely covered.

\{V_i\} \text{ an open cover of } f(X) @@ as a part: Vᵢ open, or overlaps of open sets with f(X)
\{f^{-1}(V_i)\} \text{ an open cover of } X @@ continuity; every f(x) lies in some Vᵢ
X = f^{-1}(V_1) \cup \cdots \cup f^{-1}(V_n) @@ X is finitely covered
\htmlClass{new}{f(X) \subseteq V_1 \cup \cdots \cup V_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ apply f
  
f : X \to \mathbb R \text{ continuous},\ X \text{ finitely covered, not empty} @@ the setting
f(X) \text{ closed and bounded} @@ the image-covered and closed-and-bounded techniques
\htmlClass{new}{\sup f(X) \in f(X)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sup is the limit of members (8c), and a closed set keeps its limits (34a3)

That is 12b’s bound and 12c’s highest-and-lowest technique together, and it now holds on any finitely covered space. A continuous temperature on the whole skin of the earth has a hottest point and a coldest point, once 34d5 shows that the sphere is finitely covered.

34d4Threads, and a reach for every cover

Covered-thread technique: in a finitely covered space with a distance, every list has a thread that settles.

\text{suppose no thread of } (x_n) \text{ settles at any point} @@ for contradiction
\text{each } x \text{ has a ball holding } x_n \text{ for only finitely many } n @@ else pick n₁ < n₂ < ⋯ with xₙₖ in B(x, 1/k): a thread settling at x
\mathcal U = \text{all the balls that hold } x_n \text{ for only finitely many } n @@ an open cover of X; no pick is needed to name it
X = B_1 \cup \cdots \cup B_m @@ X is finitely covered
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only finitely many } n \text{ in all}} \quad \blacksquare @@ but there is a term for every n: a false line

22b’s gathering technique is this, on a closed stretch, proved there by halving. The other way also holds in a space with a distance: if every list has a settling thread, the space is finitely covered. One of the standing questions asks for it.

Cover-reach technique: for every open cover of a finitely covered space with a distance, some reach r > 0 makes every ball of reach r lie inside one member of the cover.

\mathcal V = \{B(x, s) : B(x, 2s) \text{ lies in some member}\} @@ every point has such a ball: its member is open
X = B(x_1, s_1) \cup \cdots \cup B(x_m, s_m),\ \ r = \min(s_1, \ldots, s_m) @@ X is finitely covered; finitely many reaches
y \in B(x_i, s_i) \text{ for some } i @@ the finite part covers
z \in B(y, r) \Rightarrow d(x_i, z) < s_i + r \le 2s_i @@ a detour never wins
\htmlClass{new}{B(y, r) \subseteq B(x_i, 2s_i) \subseteq \text{a member}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the choice of 𝒱

34g will need this reach to cut a path into pieces short enough to handle one at a time.

34d5Products, and the choice they spend

Box-covered technique: if X and Y are finitely covered, so is X × Y.

\mathcal U \text{ an open cover of } X \times Y \text{ by boxes} @@ every open set is a union of boxes (34b3); cover by those boxes
\{x\} \times Y \subseteq U_1 \times V_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n \times V_n,\ \text{each } U_i \ni x @@ a copy of Y, finitely covered; keep the boxes that meet it
W_x = U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n \ni x,\ \ W_x \times Y \subseteq U_1 \times V_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n \times V_n @@ a finite overlap is open; a point (x′, y) of the strip has y in some Vᵢ and x′ in that Uᵢ
X = W_{x_1} \cup \cdots \cup W_{x_m} @@ the W_x cover X, which is finitely covered
\htmlClass{new}{X \times Y \text{ lies in finitely many boxes}} \quad \blacksquare @@ m strips, each in finitely many

So the square, the cube, and every closed and bounded part of the plane or of space are finitely covered: the last are closed parts of a square or a cube. The sphere, the torus, and every surface of 34f are too.

Now endless families. For spaces Xᵢ, one for each i in a set I, their product is the set of choosing-style functions f with f(i) in Xᵢ for every i, and its open sets are the unions of boxes that restrict finitely many entries to open sets and leave the rest free. Is every product of finitely covered spaces finitely covered?

Products-choose technique: if every product of finitely covered spaces is finitely covered, then every family of non-empty sets has a choosing function.

X_i \ne \emptyset,\ \ Y_i = X_i \text{ with one new point } \infty_i @@ for instance (0, x) for x in Xᵢ, and ∞ᵢ = (1, 0)
\text{open sets of } Y_i:\ \emptyset,\ \{\infty_i\},\ Y_i @@ a family of open sets; Yᵢ is finitely covered, since only Yᵢ holds a point of Xᵢ
F_i = \{f : f(i) \in X_i\} \text{ is closed} @@ its leftover, f(i) = ∞ᵢ, is a box
F_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap F_{i_n} \ne \emptyset @@ pick one point in each of finitely many Xᵢ (6c, no axiom); ∞ elsewhere
\textstyle\bigcap_i F_i \ne \emptyset @@ else the leftovers would be an open cover of the product with no finite part: it is finitely covered
\htmlClass{new}{f \in \textstyle\bigcap_i F_i:\ f(i) \in X_i \text{ for every } i} \quad \blacksquare @@ a choosing function (6b)

The other way holds too: if choice is laid down, every product of finitely covered spaces is finitely covered. That is proved much higher up the ladder, with 6e’s top of every chain. So the two stand or fall together. Without choice, a product of spaces, each of them finitely covered with three open sets, can fail to be finitely covered.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Finitely covered is compact. The closed-and-bounded technique is the Heine–Borel theorem. The covered-thread technique and its converse say that a metric space is compact exactly when it is sequentially compact (Bolzano–Weierstrass). The reach of the cover-reach technique is a Lebesgue number. The box-covered technique is proved by the tube lemma. The statement that every product of compact spaces is compact is Tychonoff’s theorem, and the products-choose technique is Kelley’s proof that it implies the axiom of choice.

Who said it first

Eduard Heine used uniform continuity on closed intervals in 1872. Émile Borel proved in 1895 that a countable cover of a closed interval by open intervals has a finite part; Pierre Cousin proved a version the same year, and Henri Lebesgue proved it for every cover in 1904. Arthur Schoenflies gave it the names of Heine and Borel. Pavel Alexandrov and Pavel Urysohn made compactness central in the 1920s. Andrey Tychonoff proved that products of compact spaces are compact, in 1930 and 1935, and John Kelley showed in 1950 that it gives choice.

A proof that is not allowed

“Every point of (0, 1) lies in a small open stretch. Finitely many stretches suffice, because (0, 1) is bounded.” Not allowed: bounded is not enough, and neither is closed alone. The cover (1/n, 1) has no finite part. On the line it takes both closed and bounded, and in other spaces even both are not enough.
