26f · proof

No hole, no total

Once round a circle, one over z totals two pi i, and every other power totals nothing. The difference is a hole: one over z has no value at the centre. This step proves that the hole is the whole story. A function with a complex slope everywhere inside a disc totals nothing round every loop in that disc. The proof cuts a triangle into four, keeps the worst quarter, and cuts again, until the triangle is so small that the function is nearly a straight line on it, and a straight line totals nothing.

A function with a complex slope on a disc totals zero round every triangle in it, and so has an undoing on the disc, and so totals zero round every loop there. One point without a slope does not spoil this, if the function stays continuous there.

26f1Round a triangle

Triangle-total technique: if f has a complex slope at every point of an open set holding a closed triangle T and its inside, then ∮ round T of f is 0. Cut, keep the worst quarter, and repeat.

\oint_T f = \sum_{j=1}^{4}\oint_{T_j} f @@ join the midpoints: the four inner edges are run once each way and cancel
\Big|\oint_{T_1} f\Big| \ge \tfrac14\Big|\oint_T f\Big| @@ the triangle (25c2): one of the four is at least a quarter
T \supset T_1 \supset T_2 \supset \cdots,\ \ \Big|\oint_{T_n} f\Big| \ge 4^{-n}\Big|\oint_T f\Big| @@ repeat; each side and the perimeter halve: Pₙ = P/2ⁿ
\text{corners of } T_n \to z_0 \in T @@ the corners crowd (22c2), and T is closed
f(z) = f(z_0) + f'(z_0)(z - z_0) + \varepsilon(z)(z - z_0),\ \varepsilon(z) \to 0 @@ the complex slope at z₀ (26d1)
\oint_{T_n} \big(f(z_0) + f'(z_0)(z-z_0)\big)\,dz = 0 @@ it has an undoing (26e2)
\Big|\oint_{T_n} f\Big| \le \max_{T_n}|\varepsilon|\cdot \tfrac{P}{2^n}\cdot\tfrac{P}{2^n} @@ the path fence (26e1): |z − z₀| ≤ Pₙ on Tₙ
\Big|\oint_T f\Big| \le 4^n\cdot\max_{T_n}|\varepsilon|\cdot\tfrac{P^2}{4^n} = P^2\max_{T_n}|\varepsilon| \to 0 @@ the last two lines
\htmlClass{new}{\oint_T f = 0} \quad \blacksquare

The same holds if f is continuous on the set and has a complex slope at every point but one. Cut T so that the bad point sits in a triangle as small as you like; that triangle’s total is at most max |f| times its perimeter, which is as small as you like, and the other triangles total 0.

26f2An undoing on a disc

Disc-undoing technique: if f is continuous on an open disc D and has a complex slope at every point of D but at most one, then f has an undoing F on D, and ∮ round every loop in D of f is 0.

F(z) = \int_{[c,\,z]} f @@ along the straight segment from the centre c of D to z
F(z+h) - F(z) = \int_{[z,\,z+h]} f @@ the triangle c, z, z + h lies in D; its total is 0 (26f1)
\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}{h} - f(z) = \frac1h\int_{[z,\,z+h]} (f(w) - f(z))\,dw @@ ∫ along [z, z + h] of the constant f(z) is f(z)h
\Big|\ldots\Big| \le \max_{[z,z+h]}|f(w) - f(z)| \to 0 @@ the path fence (26e1); f is continuous at z
\htmlClass{new}{F' = f \text{ on } D,\ \ \oint f = 0 \text{ round every loop in } D} \quad \blacksquare @@ only the ends count (26e2)

So inside a disc where f has a complex slope, the road between two points never matters. A loop totals something only if it goes round a point where f fails, as 1/z fails at 0.

26f3The hole is the whole story

Hole technique: round the circle |z| = 1, 1/z totals 2πi; so 1/z has no undoing on any set holding that circle, though it has a complex slope at every point but 0. The disc-undoing technique needs the disc to hold no hole.

\oint_{|z|=1} \tfrac{dz}{z} = 2\pi i \ne 0 @@ the circle-powers technique (26e3)
F' = 1/z \text{ on a set holding the circle} \Rightarrow \oint \tfrac{dz}{z} = 0 @@ only the ends count (26e2)
\htmlClass{new}{1/z \text{ has no undoing round } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two lines above

Inside any disc that does not hold 0, 1/z has an undoing: one of the logarithms of 26c, chosen to be continuous there. Round 0 there is none. That is the hole.

\oint_{|z|=1} \tfrac{dz}{z} = 0 \text{ by the disc-undoing technique}\,? @@ 1/z is not continuous at 0, and has no value there: the exception point must keep f continuous
\oint \overline{z}\,dz = 0 \text{ round every loop}\,? @@ the flip has no complex slope (26d3); round |z| = 1 it totals 2πi
\text{the four quarter-triangles' totals are each a quarter}\,? @@ only their sum is the whole; one of them is at least a quarter

26f.4.5seamA great analyst proved in 1825 that a loop’s total is 0 for a function with a complex slope, assuming that the slope f′ is itself continuous. For seventy-five years that assumption stayed in the technique. In 1900 another mathematician removed it by cutting the region into small squares, and a critic soon showed that triangles cut more cleanly, as 26f1 does.

The accepted names

The triangle-total technique is Goursat’s theorem (or the Cauchy–Goursat theorem); the disc-undoing technique is Cauchy’s theorem for a disc, or for a convex set. A set with no holes is simply connected; the general theorem holds there.

Who said it first

Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the loop total is zero in 1825, assuming the slope f′ is continuous. Édouard Goursat removed that assumption in 1900 by the cutting argument used here.

In 26f.4.5, the analyst of 1825 is Cauchy, the mathematician of 1900 is Goursat, who cut into squares, and the critic is Alfred Pringsheim (1901), who cut into triangles. Cauchy’s later proof of 1846 went through a double integral, and needed the continuous slope.
