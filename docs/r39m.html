39m · proof

Divide and conquer

A program that cuts its job into smaller copies of itself has a count given by its own smaller counts. The rung on endless sums solved the commonest shape, where a job of size n becomes several jobs of size n over some fixed number, plus a cost like a power of n for the cutting and joining: the top level wins, every level ties, or the bottom wins. Real programs do not always cut so neatly. Some shrink the job by one item, not by a share, and pay for it with a square. Some cut into pieces of different sizes. One famous program finds the middle item of a list without sorting it, by cutting into a fifth and seven tenths, and the shares add to less than one, which turns out to be all that matters. And some pay a log for each cut as well as a power. This step solves each of those shapes, then gives the one method that handles any shape at all, at the cost of having to guess the answer first: guess, and check by induction that the rule keeps the guess.

Shrinking by one item adds up the costs, often to a square. Cutting into shares that add to less than one costs only in proportion to the size. A log on each cut adds a log to the answer. Any shape can be solved by guessing and checking the guess by induction.

39m1Shrinking by one

22l1 unrolled a rule that halves the job. Some programs take one item off and do the rest the same way: find the smallest, set it first, sort the rest. Their rule is T(n) = T(n − 1) + f(n).

Shrink-by-one technique: if T(1) = c and T(n) = T(n − 1) + f(n) for n ≥ 2, then T(n) = c + f(2) + f(3) + ⋯ + f(n).

T(n) = T(n - 1) + f(n) = T(n - 2) + f(n - 1) + f(n) @@ the rule, used twice
T(n) = T(1) + f(2) + \cdots + f(n) @@ induction on n (4c)
f(n) = n - 1:\ \ T(n) = 0 + 1 + 2 + \cdots + (n - 1) = \tfrac{n(n-1)}{2} @@ selection sort, 17·comp’s count, with c = 0
f(n) = 1:\ \ T(n) = c + n - 1 @@ a step per item
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shrinking by one turns a cost of } n \text{ per call into } n^2/2} \quad \blacksquare @@ 4k’s sum of the first n − 1 numbers

Set that beside merge sort, T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n, which is n log₂ n. The work at each call is the same, about n. What changes is how fast the job shrinks: by a share, there are log₂ n levels; by one item, there are n. The depth of the cutting is the whole difference between n log n and n².

39m2Uneven pieces

To find the middle item of a list without sorting it: cut the list into groups of five, find the middle of each group, find the middle of those middles by the same program, and use it as 39l2’s pivot. At least three tenths of the items lie on each side of it, so the side that holds the middle item has at most seven tenths of the list. The program then works on that side alone. Its count obeys T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + cn, with pieces of two different sizes.

Shares-below-one technique: if T(n) ≤ T(αn) + T(βn) + cn with α + β < 1, then T(n) ≤ cn/(1 − α − β): the count grows only in proportion to n.

K = \tfrac{c}{1 - \alpha - \beta} @@ the guess’s constant
T(m) \le Km \text{ for every } m < n @@ strong induction (4h): the claim at every smaller size, and at the small sizes by choosing K large enough
T(n) \le K\alpha n + K\beta n + cn @@ the rule, with the claim at αn and βn
= n\big(K(\alpha + \beta) + c\big) = n\big(K - K(1 - \alpha - \beta) + c\big) = Kn @@ K(1 − α − β) = c
\htmlClass{new}{\alpha = \tfrac15,\ \beta = \tfrac{7}{10}:\ \ T(n) \le 10cn} \quad \blacksquare @@ 1 − 9/10 = 1/10

So the middle of a million items can be found in a count in proportion to a million, without the log a sort would cost. Run with c = 1 by machine, the rule gives about 8.6n at a million, under the bound of 10n. The shares tell the story: if the pieces add to less than the whole, each level does less work than the one above, and the top level wins, as in 22l2’s first case.

39m3Guess, then check

The master technique of 22l2 needs the cost of cutting to be a power nᵈ. 22l5 marked T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n log₂ n as outside it. Any rule can be solved by guessing the answer and then proving by induction that the rule keeps the guess: the method of the last footing, made general.

Guess-and-check technique: if g meets the rule at the smallest sizes, and the rule applied to g at the smaller sizes gives at most g at n, then T ≤ g at every size.

T(1) = c,\ \ T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n\log_2 n,\ \ n = 2^k @@ the rule
\text{guess } g(2^k) = 2^k\big(c + \tfrac{k(k + 1)}{2}\big) @@ divide by 2ᵏ, as 22l1 did: the cost per item rises by k at size 2ᵏ
2g(2^{k-1}) + 2^k k = 2^k\big(c + \tfrac{(k - 1)k}{2} + k\big) = 2^k\big(c + \tfrac{k(k + 1)}{2}\big) @@ the rule applied to the guess
g(1) = c @@ the smallest size
\htmlClass{new}{T(n) = n\big(c + \tfrac12\log_2 n\,(\log_2 n + 1)\big) \sim \tfrac12 n\log_2^2 n} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on k (4c); checked by machine at n = 1024: 57 344 with c = 1

Guessing is not cheating. The guess can come from anywhere, a picture of the levels, a run by machine, a hunch; only the check is a proof. A split of a product of two square grids into seven half-size products, 7T(n/2) + n², falls to 22l2 directly: r = 7/4, the bottom wins, n^(log₂ 7) ≈ n^2.807. At n = 1024 that is 7¹⁰ ≈ 2.8 · 10⁸ products against 1024³ ≈ 1.07 · 10⁹.

The accepted names

Cutting a job into smaller copies is divide and conquer, and its rules are recurrences. Guess-and-check is the substitution method; laying out the levels is the recursion-tree method; the master technique is the master theorem, and its extension to uneven pieces is the Akra–Bazzi method. The middle-finding program is median-of-medians selection, or BFPRT. The split of a grid product into seven is Strassen’s algorithm. The crosswalk items here are the recurrences, divide and conquer and selection of MIT 6.006 and Stanford CS 161.

Who said it first

Manuel Blum, Robert Floyd, Vaughan Pratt, Ronald Rivest and Robert Tarjan found linear-time selection in 1973. Volker Strassen gave his seven-product split in 1969; the best known exponent for grid products has since fallen below 2.372. Mohamad Akra and Louay Bazzi solved uneven recurrences in general in 1998. Jon Bentley, Dorothea Haken and James Saxe set out the master technique’s general form in 1980.

A proof that is not allowed

“The middle-finding program cuts into a fifth and seven tenths, and those are fixed shares, so by the master technique it is n log n.” Not allowed: the master technique needs equal pieces, a of size n/b. With pieces of different sizes, the shares’ total decides, and here it is 9/10, below 1, so the count is linear.
