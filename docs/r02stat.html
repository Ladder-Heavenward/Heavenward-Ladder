Who Is in Either Group, and Who Is in Both?

Who is in either group, and who is in both? A survey sorts people. It asks each person a question, and the answers split the people into those who said yes and those who did not. Ask two questions and there are two groups, and the groups overlap. Some people are in one, some in the other, some in both and some in neither. People have asked this since the first lists of households were made. What makes it hard is the word or. Ask whether someone did this or that, and a person who did both may answer yes, or may answer no, depending on how the or is read. Then the answers no longer tell you who is in both. This page treats each group as a set sifted from the roster of people asked. It finds either, both and neither as sets the rung has already built. It draws two overlapping groups as regions and reads each region as a row. And it shows why a question with an or in it can lose what two separate questions keep.

A group of people who answered yes is a set sifted from the roster. Either group is a union, both groups is an intersection, and neither is the roster less the union. Two overlapping groups cut the roster into regions, one for each way of answering, and each person on the roster lands in one. A question that asks about two things with an or can be read two ways, and its answers cannot be split back into the regions. Ask about each thing on its own, and every region can be sifted.

2stat2Either, Both and Neither

“In either group” means in h or in k, the generous or of 1c. That is the union. “In both groups” means in h and in k. That is the intersection. 2i4 says the same thing from the side of the questions: sifting by “yes to the first ∨ yes to the second” gives h ∪ k, and sifting by the ∧ gives h ∩ k.

“In neither group” needs the roster again. There is no set of everyone not in h, since 2f9 found no set of everything to sift it from. Neither means: on the roster, and not in the union. So it is r ∖ (h ∪ k), which is {Fay}.

1c11’s neither technique gives a second way to write it. Not in h ∨ k is not in h, and not in k. So the people in neither are the roster less h, and also the roster less k: (r ∖ h) ∩ (r ∖ k). Chase a member through both and they match, so by same-members they are one set.

Either is a union, both is an intersection, and neither is the roster less the union.

2stat3Two Overlapping Groups, Drawn

Draw the roster as a dashed border, and h and k as overlapping patches inside it. 2i1 showed that each region is a row. Here the rows are ways of answering. The region inside both patches is the row TT: yes and yes, h ∩ k. Inside h only is TF, h ∖ k. Inside k only is FT, k ∖ h. Outside both is FF, the people in neither.

Every person on this roster lands in exactly one region. That rests on 2i11’s settled-only step. Each person is in h or not in h, and that is settled because each person answered. A roster is listed, and every answer is in, so the step is licensed here.

On a listed roster where everyone answered, the regions share out the roster, each person in exactly one. So the roster is the union of the four regions, and no two regions share anyone.

Now let one person leave the second question blank. That person is in h or not, but nobody can say whether they are in k. Their membership in k is not settled, so they have no row, and they sit in no region. The four regions no longer make up the roster. A survey that lets people skip a question has a fifth answer, and the picture has no place for it.

2stat4Or, Read Two Ways

A shorter survey asks one question: “did you climb Mount Baker or paddle to the San Juan Islands this summer?”. Ann, Ben, Dev and Eva did one of the two, and they answer yes. Fay did neither and answers no. Cal did both. What does Cal answer?

Read with the inclusive or, Cal answers yes, and the yes group is h ∪ k. Read with the exclusive or of 1c11, “one or the other, but not both”, Cal answers no. Then the yes group is the people in exactly one region of the pair, (h ∖ k) ∪ (k ∖ h). In English the word or is used both ways. So each person who did both picks a reading, and the yes group can be either set, or something in between.

Anexact, yet rigorous · loose, then locked

A survey question is gathered loose. The asker has a rule in mind, and the word or carries it to each person. Each person then locks it, by reading the or one way. The answers come back already locked, and the lock does not say which reading was used.

Lock the reading before you ask, not after the answers are in.

Unlocking runs the steps backward, and here it cannot be done. The yes group is a set, nothing but its members. It does not keep the reading that made it.

A form asks: “do you own a cat or a dog?”. One person owns both and ticks yes. Another owns both and ticks no. Which of them read the or wrongly?

Ask about each thing on its own, and nothing is lost. From the answers to each question, every region is a sift by a compound condition, so each can be found by 2i4. Both readings of the or come out too, as h ∪ k and as (h ∖ k) ∪ (k ∖ h).

From the answers to the single or question, the both region cannot be found, even when everyone reads the or inclusively. Here is a witness. In one club, h = {Ann} and k = {Ben}. In another, h = {Ann, Ben} and k = {Ann, Ben}. Both give the yes group {Ann, Ben}. The first club has nobody in both; the second has both of its members there. The same answers fit two different both regions.

Split-the-question technique: from the answers to each part, every region can be sifted; from the answers to the or alone, the both region cannot.

Accepted names

The roster is the sampling frame, and a person on it is a respondent. The region picture is a Venn diagram. The people in neither group are the complement of the union, taken in the roster. The people in exactly one group are the symmetric difference of the two. A question that asks about two things at once is a double-barrelled question, and survey writers are taught to split it. A form that lets each person tick every group that fits is a check-all-that-apply question; one that allows one tick is select one.

Who said it first

John Venn drew overlapping regions for classes in 1880; Leonhard Euler had drawn circles for them in the 1760s. Courts have long fought over or in written law. In 1935 the Supreme Court of Wisconsin called the lawyer’s “and/or” “that befuddling, nameless thing, that Janus-faced verbal monstrosity” (Employers’ Mutual Liability Insurance Co. v. Tollefsen). In 2·stat.6.5, the two forms are real: the United States census asked each person to mark one race until 1990, and from 2000 let each person mark one or more, after the federal standards were revised in 1997.
