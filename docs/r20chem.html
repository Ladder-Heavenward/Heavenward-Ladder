Can a Molecule Be Left-Handed?

Can a molecule be left-handed? Your two hands match in every length, yet a left glove will not go on a right hand. More than a century and a half ago, chemists found substances that came in two forms with the same atoms, joined the same way, at the same distances. The two forms melted at the same heat and weighed the same. Yet they twisted a beam of light opposite ways, and some of them smelled different. What makes it hard is that no one could see a molecule, and every measured distance in the two forms agreed. This page shows, with the handedness of rung 20, that a carbon with four different neighbours can be built two ways that no turn carries one onto the other, how to tell which is which by looking from one neighbour, and why a living body, built from one-handed parts, can tell the two apart.

Look at a carbon from one of its neighbours. The other three go round either clockwise or anticlockwise. A turn about the line to your eye keeps that way round, and a mirror reverses it. When all four neighbours differ, no turn at all carries the molecule onto its mirror image, so the two are different molecules. When two neighbours are alike, the mirror image is the molecule itself. A pocket in a living cell with three sites on its wall fits only one of the two hands.

20chem1A Mirror Swaps Two Neighbours

A carbon with four neighbours sits at the centre of a tetrahedron, with a neighbour at each corner (19·chem.4). Put the carbon at the origin of space, one neighbour W straight up, and the other three below, evenly round.

From here on, on this page, the mirror is the move (x, y, z) ↦ (x, −y, z): 20c’s flip across the floor, done at every height. The mirror image of a molecule is the molecule after the mirror.

Place W and one more neighbour A on the standing plane y = 0. The mirror keeps them. The other two, B and X, sit on either side of that plane, at the same height.

W = (0,\ 0,\ 3),\ \ A = (2\sqrt2,\ 0,\ -1) @@ both have y = 0: the mirror keeps them
B = (-\sqrt2,\ \sqrt6,\ -1) \mapsto (-\sqrt2,\ -\sqrt6,\ -1) = X @@ the flip across the floor, at height −1
|W - A|^2 = 8 + 16 = 24 = |A - B|^2 = 18 + 6 @@ distance in space (18a): every two neighbours are √24 apart
\htmlClass{new}{\text{mirror image} = \text{the molecule with B and X swapped}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the mirror sends X to B’s place the same way

Mirror-swap technique: the mirror image of a carbon’s four neighbours is the same carbon with two of its neighbours swapped. Any other mirror through the carbon gives the same image, turned, since two mirrors through one line make a turn about it (20e). So when two neighbours are atoms of one kind, put them in the swapped places: the mirror image is the molecule itself.

20chem2Seen From One Neighbour

Hold the molecule with W toward your eye, straight above the carbon. You see the other three as dots on the floor: their shadows, (x, y), with the height dropped. Read which way round they go with 20g’s D, always naming the kinds in one fixed order.

From here on, on this page, a turn about the up line is the move (x, y, z) ↦ (ax − by, bx + ay, z), for a turn (a, b) of the plane: 20c’s turn, done at every height. It keeps the carbon and W.

\text{F} = (2,\ 0),\ \ \text{Cl} = (-1,\ \sqrt3),\ \ \text{Br} = (-1,\ -\sqrt3) @@ the shadows, evenly round
D(\text{F},\ \text{Cl},\ \text{Br}) = (-3)(-\sqrt3) - (\sqrt3)(-3) = 6\sqrt3 \gt 0 @@ which way round (20g1): anticlockwise
\text{a turn about the up line turns the shadows by } (a, b) @@ the height plays no part
D \text{ is kept} @@ the turns-keep-orientation technique (20g2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the mirror: } (x, y) \mapsto (x, -y),\ \ D = -6\sqrt3} \quad \blacksquare @@ a flip of the shadows; the flips-reverse technique (20g3)

Seen-from technique: a turn about the line to W keeps which way round the other three go, seen from W, and the mirror reverses it. The order of the names matters. Swap two names and D changes sign (20g1). So a reading means something only with the order of kinds fixed: here F, then Cl, then Br.

20chem3No Turn Carries One Hand to the Other

A molecule tumbles in space in every way at once, not only about the up line. One fact about all those tumbles is held in trust here: every turn of space, however it was made, is a turn about one line, and it keeps the points of that line and no others, unless it does nothing. 27m closes it.

\text{suppose a turn } T \text{ carries CHBrClF onto its mirror image} @@ aiming to refute (7f)
T(\mathrm{C}) = \mathrm{C},\ \ T(\mathrm{H}) = \mathrm{H} @@ each atom goes to the one of its kind; there is one of each, and the mirror through C, H and F kept them
T \text{ is a turn about the line through C and H} @@ held in trust: a turn about one line, keeping C and H
D(\text{F},\ \text{Cl},\ \text{Br}) = 6\sqrt3 \text{ before},\ \ -6\sqrt3 \text{ after} @@ the seen-from technique: T keeps D, but the mirror image has −D
6\sqrt3 = -6\sqrt3,\ \ \bot @@ 6√3 is not 0: the three shadows are not on one line
\htmlClass{new}{\text{four different neighbours: no turn carries the molecule onto its mirror image}} \quad \blacksquare @@ refute (7f)

Handed-carbon technique: a carbon with four different neighbours and its mirror image are two molecules, since no turn carries one onto the other. With two neighbours alike, the mirror-swap technique made the mirror image the molecule itself. So a single carbon is handed exactly when its four neighbours all differ.

Carvone has one such carbon. One hand smells of spearmint, a crop of the mint farms of Washington and Oregon. The other smells of caraway seed. The same atoms, at the same distances, give two smells.

20chem4A One-Handed Lock

The proteins of living things are built from one hand of the amino acids. So the pockets they fold into are one-handed too. Picture a pocket as a wall with three sites, P₁, P₂ and P₃, each fitting one kind of neighbour. A molecule reaches the wall from the water side, with its fourth neighbour pointing back into the water.

Seen from the water, the three sites go round one way, fixed by the wall. At the wall a molecule can slide and turn, but it cannot be flipped over: that would carry its fourth neighbour through the wall.

D(P_1, P_2, P_3) \gt 0 \text{ seen from the water} @@ fixed by the wall
\text{a fit carries the three neighbours onto } P_1, P_2, P_3 \text{ by a slide and a turn} @@ the fourth stays toward the water
D \text{ of the three, seen from the fourth, is kept} @@ turns-keep-orientation (20g2); a slide keeps every displacement
\htmlClass{new}{\text{only the hand with } D \gt 0 \text{ can fit}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other hand has D below 0 (20·chem.2)

Three-point technique: a pocket with three sites on its wall can fit one hand of a handed carbon, and never the other. Two sites are not enough: two points can be matched by a slide and a turn whichever way round the third goes.

That is how a body tells the two hands of a drug apart. Naproxen, taken for pain, is sold as one hand only. Ibuprofen is sold as a half-and-half mix, and the body turns much of the idle hand into the working one.

The accepted names

A molecule that no turn carries onto its mirror image is chiral, from the Greek for hand; the two hands are enantiomers. A carbon with four different neighbours is a stereocentre, or asymmetric carbon. Chemists label the hands R and S by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules: rank the neighbours, hold the lowest-ranked pointing away, and read the other three clockwise (R) or anticlockwise (S). This page holds it toward the eye, which reverses the reading. Placements that differ at some handed carbons but are not mirror images are diastereomers. A handed molecule that is its own mirror image, as in 20·chem.6.3, is a meso compound. A half-and-half mix is racemic. Twisting light is optical activity, marked + or −. The three-point technique is the three-point attachment model (Easson and Stedman, 1933). The fact held in trust is that every turn of space has an axis (Euler’s rotation theorem).

Who said it first

Louis Pasteur (1848) sorted two forms of tartrate crystals by hand under a lens, mirror images of each other, and found that their solutions twisted light opposite ways. Jacobus Henricus van ’t Hoff and Joseph Achille Le Bel (1874) explained it by a tetrahedral carbon with four different neighbours, and the square guess died for good. Lord Kelvin gave the word chiral (1893). In 20·chem.6.5, the meteorite is the one that fell at Murchison, Australia, in 1969. Whether life’s hand was chance or law is still argued. Thalidomide, sold from 1957, is the hard lesson of 20·chem.4: one hand calmed, the other harmed unborn children, and the body turns each hand into the other, so selling one hand alone would not have helped.
