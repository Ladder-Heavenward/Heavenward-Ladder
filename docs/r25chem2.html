What Is the Least Heat a Plant Must Buy?

What is the least heat a plant must buy? A chemical plant heats some of its streams and cools others, and pays twice: once for steam to heat, and again for cooling water to carry heat away. Yet a hot stream that must be cooled could warm a cold stream that must be heated. How much can be traded, and how much must still be bought? This page draws each stream as a line of heat against temperature (18), adds the lines into two curves, slides them until they come a set gap apart, reads off what is traded and what is bought, using 19·eng2’s exchangers, and finds the one rule that keeps a plant at its least.

Heat flows from hot to cold only, and an exchanger needs a temperature gap to work. Add all the hot streams into one curve and all the cold into another, and slide them together until their closest point is the gap. Where they overlap is heat traded; the ends that stick out must be bought. The closest point, the pinch, splits the plant in two, and heat must never cross it.

25chem23Slide the Curves Until They Are a Set Gap Apart: the Pinch

An exchanger needs the hot side at least 10 °C above the cold side. Slide the cold curve across until it is everywhere at least 10 °C below the hot curve, and just 10 °C at its closest. A quick way: lower every hot temperature by 5, raise every cold one by 5, and cascade heat down through the temperature bands.

25chem24The Overlap Is Heat Traded; the Ends Are What Must Be Bought

\text{steam} = 20 \text{ kW} @@ added at the top
\text{cooling} = 30 \text{ kW} @@ left at the bottom
480 - 30 = 450 \text{ kW traded} @@ hot streams warming cold ones in exchangers (19·eng2)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{steam } 470 \to 20,\ \text{cooling } 480 \to 30} @@ check: 30 − 20 = 480 − 470

Pinch technique: shift hot temperatures down and cold up by half the least gap, cascade the surplus of each band down, and the most negative running total is the least heat to buy; what is left at the bottom is the least cooling. No arrangement of exchangers can buy less. Joy redesigns the exchangers, and after the rebuild the plant’s meters read 20 kW of steam and 30 kW of cooling.

25chem25Never Pass Heat Across the Pinch

Above the pinch, the plant is short of heat; below, it has heat to spare. Suppose an exchanger moves X kW from a hot stream above the pinch to a cold stream below it.

\text{above: short by } X \text{ more}:\ \text{steam} = 20 + X @@ 
\text{below: } X \text{ more to spare}:\ \text{cooling} = 30 + X @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every kilowatt across the pinch is bought twice}} @@

Heat must not cross the pinch: no exchanger moves heat from above it to below, no steam is used below it, and no cooling above it. An energy engineer checking an existing plant looks for exactly these three faults, and each one found is money saved every hour.

Accepted names

This is pinch analysis; the hot and cold curves are composite curves, the cascade is the problem table algorithm, the least gap is ΔT_min, and the steam and cooling are the hot and cold utilities. The heat rate is the stream’s heat capacity flowrate, CP.

Who said it first

Bodo Linnhoff and colleagues developed pinch analysis in the late 1970s, first at ICI and then at Leeds and Manchester (not yet revisited for the dates).
