43g · proof

Primes Are Endless and Thin

Rung four proved that the primes never run out: hand the proof a list, and it finds a prime not on it. It said nothing about how many there are below a thousand, or a million, or how far apart they sit. Among the first ten numbers four are prime; among the first thousand, one hundred and sixty eight; among the first million, about one in thirteen. They thin out. This step asks how fast. First, not too fast: the sum of one over each prime grows without bound, slowly, as the log of a log, so the primes are far more plentiful than the squares. Second, the primes are packed into one endless product, and that product is the sum of one over every power of every number, the start of a road that runs to the open question at the end of this rung. Third, a single count of choices fences the number of primes below a bound from above and below. The exact rate, one over the log, was guessed by a boy of fifteen from tables and proved a century later; this step states it and holds it in trust. And the primes are shared out fairly among the hours of every clock, which the step proves in two cases.

The sum of one over each prime passes every bound, at the rate of the log of a log. The sum of one over each power of every number is a product over the primes. The middle count of choices fences how many primes lie below a bound. Near x, about one number in ln x is prime, and each unit hour of a clock gets its fair share of primes.

43g1Thin, but Not Too Thin

22f proved that 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + ⋯ settles, and 22d that 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ passes every bound. The squares are thin enough that their reciprocals settle. Are the primes?

Thin-but-endless technique: for every x ≥ 3, the sum of 1/p over the primes p ≤ x is at least ln ln x − 1, so it passes every bound.

\textstyle\prod_{p \le x} \frac{1}{1 - 1/p} = \prod_{p \le x}\big(1 + \tfrac1p + \tfrac1{p^2} + \cdots\big) @@ the geometric sum (22e), one for each prime
\phantom{\textstyle\prod} \ge \textstyle\sum_{n \le x} \tfrac1n \ge \ln x @@ multiply out: each n ≤ x has one prime list (4j18), all its primes ≤ x; the sum beats the area under 1/t (17a)
\textstyle\sum_{p \le x} -\ln(1 - \tfrac1p) \ge \ln\ln x @@ ln of both sides (17c)
-\ln(1 - t) \le t + t^2 \text{ for } 0 \le t \le \tfrac12 @@ the two sides agree at 0, and the slopes are 1/(1 − t) ≤ 1 + 2t (14’s slopes control heights)
\textstyle\sum_p \tfrac1{p^2} \le \sum_{n \ge 2} \tfrac{1}{n(n - 1)} = 1 @@ the sum folds: 1/(n − 1) − 1/n
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{p \le x} \tfrac1p \ge \ln\ln x - 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last three lines

Below a million the sum is about 2.89 and ln ln 10⁶ is about 2.63. The bound is weak and the growth is slow: to pass 4 the sum needs primes into the hundreds of millions. But it does pass every bound, and the squares’ sum never passes 2. So the primes are endless again, in a stronger form, and they are not as thin as the squares.

43g2One Product Over the Primes

From here on, ζ (the Greek letter zeta, in its small form) names the function on s > 1 with ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + ⋯, which settles by 22f’s p-sums. Its capital form is not used. 32b found ζ(2) = π²/6.

Prime-product technique: for every s > 1, ζ(s) is the endless product over the primes of 1/(1 − p^(−s)).

P(x) = \textstyle\prod_{p \le x} \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}} = \sum_{n \text{ with every prime} \le x} n^{-s} @@ multiply the geometric sums: terms never negative, so the order is free (22g); one prime list each (4j18)
0 \le \zeta(s) - P(x) \le \textstyle\sum_{n \gt x} n^{-s} @@ every n ≤ x is in P(x)’s sum, and only the larger ones can be missing
\textstyle\sum_{n \gt x} n^{-s} \to 0 \text{ as } x \to \infty @@ the tail of a settling sum (22d)
\htmlClass{new}{\zeta(s) = \textstyle\prod_p \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the partial products settle on ζ(s)

Take s = 2. The product of (1 − 1/p²) over all primes is 6/π², about 0.6079. Read through rung 30’s chance, it is the share of pairs of numbers that share no prime: a pair misses sharing 2 with chance 1 − ¼, sharing 3 with chance 1 − 1/9, and so on, independently, as the remainder technique (43c) makes exact on each finite clock. So about 61% of pairs of large numbers have gcd 1. The open question of 43·now2 is about where ζ, carried into the complex plane, is 0.

43g3A Fence From the Middle Count

Write π(x) for the number of primes at most x. Here π names a count, not the round number of rung 21: the letter has a further job, with a counting number or a real inside the brackets, and its old meaning stands wherever it stands alone. So π(10) = 4 and π(100) = 25.

Middle-count technique: for every n ≥ 1, π(2n) · ln(2n) ≥ n ln 4 − ln(2n + 1).

\tbinom{2n}{n} \ge \tfrac{4^n}{2n + 1} @@ the largest of the 2n + 1 counts of choices, which add to 2²ⁿ (4k’s binomial technique)
\text{the power of } p \text{ in } m! \text{ is } \textstyle\sum_{j \ge 1} \lfloor m/p^j \rfloor @@ count the multiples of p, of p², … up to m (the whole part, 43f4)
\text{in } \tbinom{2n}{n}:\ \textstyle\sum_j \big(\lfloor 2n/p^j\rfloor - 2\lfloor n/p^j\rfloor\big),\ \text{each term } 0 \text{ or } 1 @@ ⌊2t⌋ − 2⌊t⌋ is 0 or 1; terms with pʲ > 2n are 0
p^k \mid \tbinom{2n}{n} \Rightarrow p^k \le 2n @@ k is at most the number of j with pʲ ≤ 2n
\tbinom{2n}{n} \le (2n)^{\pi(2n)} @@ only primes up to 2n divide it, each to a power at most 2n
\htmlClass{new}{\pi(2n)\ln(2n) \ge n\ln 4 - \ln(2n + 1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ ln of the first and fifth lines

So π(x) is at least about (ln 2 / 2) · x/ln x for large x. The other side of the fence uses the same count: every prime between n and 2n divides (2n)!/(n! n!) once, so their product is at most 4ⁿ, and the product of all primes up to x is below 4ˣ, a standing question. That caps π(x) at a fixed multiple of x/ln x. For n = 50 the fence gives π(100) ≥ 14; the truth is 25.

43g4About One in ln x

Prime-count technique, held in trust: the share of primes among the numbers up to x settles toward 1/ln x, so that π(x) divided by x/ln x settles on 1. Its proof, through ζ in the complex plane, is much higher up the ladder.

\pi(10^3) = 168,\ \ 10^3/\ln 10^3 \approx 144.8,\ \ \text{ratio } 1.161 @@ counted
\pi(10^6) = 78\,498,\ \ 10^6/\ln 10^6 \approx 72\,382,\ \ \text{ratio } 1.084 @@ counted
\pi(10^9) = 50\,847\,534,\ \ 10^9/\ln 10^9 \approx 48\,254\,942,\ \ \text{ratio } 1.054 @@ counted
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the ratios fall toward } 1 \text{, slowly}} @@ the technique says they settle there

Fair-shares, held in trust: if gcd(a, n) = 1, the clock of n has endlessly many primes in the hour [a], and each of the c(n) unit hours gets, in the long run, the same share of them. The hours that are not units hold at most one prime each, since every member shares a divisor with n. The step proves two cases whole.

Four-clock technique: the hours [1] and [3] of the clock of 4 each hold endlessly many primes.

p_1, \ldots, p_k \equiv 3 \pmod 4;\ \ N = 4p_1\cdots p_k - 1 \equiv 3 @@ a list of primes in the hour [3]
\text{a product of numbers in } [1] \text{ is in } [1] @@ carry-to-the-clock (43a2)
\text{some prime } q \mid N \text{ is in } [3],\ q \ne p_i @@ N is odd and in [3]; q = pᵢ would divide 1 (4j16)
q_1, \ldots, q_k \equiv 1;\ \ M = (2q_1\cdots q_k)^2 + 1 @@ a list in the hour [1]
r \mid M \Rightarrow -1 \equiv (2q_1\cdots q_k)^2 \pmod r @@ r is odd and none of the qᵢ
\htmlClass{new}{-1 \text{ is a square on the clock of } r \text{, so } r \equiv 1 \pmod 4} \quad \blacksquare @@ the square-test technique (43f2): a new prime in [1]

Harder questions that rely on your own making. The step is complete without them; the rung's angel wings need them.

43g.6.1buildBuild the other side of the fence. Prove the product of the primes up to x is below 4ˣ, by induction on x: for odd x = 2m + 1, the primes between m + 2 and 2m + 1 divide the count (2m + 1 choose m), which is at most 4ᵐ.
Turn it into an upper bound for π(x) of the shape C · x/ln x, and find a C that works.
43g.6.2edgeProve these are n numbers in a row with no prime among them, so gaps between primes grow without bound.
Find a run of at least 7 numbers with no prime below 100, and compare its length with ln 100. Is a long gap against the prime-count technique?
43g.6.3converseThe thin-but-endless technique says the primes’ reciprocals pass every bound. Turn it round: if a set S of numbers has reciprocals passing every bound, must S hold endlessly many primes? Must it hold arithmetic progressions of every length?
Settle what you can. What is still open?
43g.6.4seama book of 1940: the theory of numbers, gloriously uselessA mathematician wrote in 1940 that the theory of numbers had no use in war or commerce, and was the cleaner for it. In 1977 a lock was published whose key is two primes of a hundred digits each. The prime-count technique says how many such primes there are: about one number in 230 near 10¹⁰⁰.
Was the 1940 claim wrong, or is use the wrong way to judge it? Is the prime-count technique pure or applied, and does the answer change when 43i uses it?

The accepted names

The thin-but-endless technique is Euler’s proof that Σ 1/p diverges; the sharper Σ_{p ≤ x} 1/p = ln ln x + M + o(1) is Mertens’s second theorem. ζ is the Riemann zeta function, and the prime-product technique the Euler product. π(x) is the prime-counting function. The middle-count technique and the upper bound of 43g.6.1 are Chebyshev’s bounds, and the power of p in m! is Legendre’s formula. The prime-count technique is the prime number theorem. Fair shares is Dirichlet’s theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, in its strong form (de la Vallée Poussin, 1896). The four-clock technique is its first two cases. Progressions of every length in the primes is the Green–Tao theorem (2004); that a set with reciprocals passing every bound has them is the Erdős conjecture on arithmetic progressions, open.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler found the product and the divergence in 1737. Carl Friedrich Gauss, around 1792, aged fifteen, guessed from tables that the primes thin like 1/ln x; Adrien-Marie Legendre guessed a like formula in 1798. Pafnuty Chebyshev proved the fence in 1850. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved primes in progressions in 1837. Bernhard Riemann carried ζ into the complex plane in 1859, and Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin proved the prime number theorem separately in 1896. Atle Selberg and Paul Erdős found proofs without complex numbers in 1948. G. H. Hardy’s A Mathematician’s Apology (1940) is the book of 43g.6.4.

A proof that is not allowed

“The chance that a large n is prime is 1/ln n, and the chance that n + 2 is prime is 1/ln(n + 2), so the chance both are is about 1/(ln n)², and summing over n, there are endlessly many twin primes.” Not allowed: primes are not chances, and the two events are not independent: if n is odd, n + 2 is odd too. The count it gives is a guess, the kind 43·now2 weighs. Twin primes are still open.
