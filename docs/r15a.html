15a · proof

Partitions, lower and upper sums

Rung 9 drew the graph of a function as a set of points in the plane. Under the graph of a function that is never negative there is another set of points: everything between the graph and the across axis. Nothing on the ladder so far says how big that set is. This step lays down the three rules that anything called area must obey. Then it does one thing with those rules: cut the interval into pieces, stand a box on each piece, and add up the boxes. Boxes that fit under the graph give a total that is too small. Boxes that reach over it give a total that is too big. The area, whatever it turns out to be, is caught between them.

Cut the interval at finitely many points. On each piece, the least value of the function gives a box that fits under the graph, and the greatest gives one that reaches over it. Add the boxes' widths times heights. The area is at least the first total and at most the second.

15a2Boxes, and three rules for area

Stands on 5f (sets of pairs) and 8 (reals).

From here on, for p < q and c < d, the box [p, q] × [c, d] is the set of points (x, y) of the plane with p ≤ x ≤ q and c ≤ y ≤ d. Its width is q − p and its height is d − c. The mark [p, q] × [c, d] is a name: it names one set of points. Its accepted name is a rectangle.

From here on, for f with f(x) ≥ 0 at every x in [a, b], the region under f on [a, b] is the set of points (x, y) with a ≤ x ≤ b and 0 ≤ y ≤ f(x).

Area is to be a number given to some sets of points. Nobody has yet said which number. Three rules are laid down as the starting point. They are premises about what the word area must mean, chosen because every way of measuring land or cloth already obeys them:

Box-area axiom: the box [p, q] × [c, d] has area (q − p)(d − c), its width times its height.
Inside-no-more axiom: if one set lies inside another, and both have areas, the first area is no more than the second.
Side-by-side axiom: if two sets with areas share no points except some on a single line x = k, then the two together have the sum of the two areas. Each is earned by what breaks without it. Without box-area, a rectangle has no size. Without inside-no-more, a part could be bigger than the whole. Without side-by-side, two halves need not make the whole.

By the side-by-side axiom, boxes standing side by side have areas that add. [0, 1] × [0, 2] and [1, 3] × [0, 5] share only points with x = 1, so together they have area 2 + 10 = 12. Nothing else is assumed. In particular, nothing yet says that the region under x² has an area at all.

15a3Cutting the interval

Stands on 15a2 and 4j (Σ).

From here on, a partition of [a, b] is a finite list of inputs a = x₀ < x₁ < ⋯ < xₙ = b. For each i from 1 to n, the interval [xᵢ₋₁, xᵢ] is its i-th piece, and Δxᵢ = xᵢ − xᵢ₋₁ is the piece's width. Δxᵢ is a name, for one number. Its accepted name is a partition, and the xᵢ are its points. The equal partition into n pieces has xᵢ = a + i(b − a)/n, every width (b − a)/n.

From here on, Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ t(i) is 4j's Σ taken over the i with 1 ≤ i ≤ n: t(1) + t(2) + ⋯ + t(n). It is still an operator: carried out, it produces one number, the total.

The widths add to the whole. Σ Δxᵢ = (x₁ − x₀) + (x₂ − x₁) + ⋯ + (xₙ − xₙ₋₁). Every point but the two ends appears once added and once taken away, leaving xₙ − x₀, the length of the whole interval. (By induction on n, with sum-one-more: adding a piece adds xₙ₊₁ − xₙ to a total of xₙ − x₀.)

15a4The least and the greatest on each piece

Stands on 15a3, 8c (inf and sup) and 12b (bounded-both-ways).

From here on, for f bounded on [a, b] and a partition P of it, mᵢ is the inf, and Mᵢ the sup, of the values of f on the i-th piece. The lower sum is L(f, P) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ mᵢ Δxᵢ, and the upper sum is U(f, P) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ Mᵢ Δxᵢ. mᵢ and Mᵢ are names of numbers. L(f, P) and U(f, P) are names too, each the packed form of a whole sum of boxes. Their accepted names are the lower sum and the upper sum.

The inf and the sup exist by 8c's greatest-lower-bound and no-holes, because f is bounded on [u, v] (12b's bounded-both-ways) and so on each piece. When f is continuous, 12c's highest-and-lowest says more: f reaches mᵢ and Mᵢ on the piece, so they are its least and greatest values there.

Lower-below-upper technique: for f bounded on [u, v] and any partition P of [u, v], L(f, P) ≤ U(f, P).

x_{i-1} \text{ is on the } i\text{-th piece} @@ the piece is not empty
m_i \le f(x_{i-1}) \le M_i @@ inf is a lower bound, sup an upper bound (8c)
\Delta x_i \gt 0 @@ the points are listed in increasing order
m_i\,\Delta x_i \le M_i\,\Delta x_i @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree (8d): multiplying by a positive keeps order
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \Delta x_i \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} M_i \Delta x_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on n with sum-one-more: adding the same order to both sides (order-and-arithmetic-agree)

15a5The boxes trap the area

Stands on 15a4 and 15a2 (the three rules).

Boxes-trap-the-area technique: let f ≥ 0 be bounded on [u, v], and P a partition. If the region under f on [u, v] has an area A obeying the three area axioms, then L(f, P) ≤ A ≤ U(f, P).

\text{the lower boxes } [x_{i-1}, x_i] \times [0, m_i] @@ mᵢ ≥ 0, since 0 is a lower bound of f there and mᵢ is the greatest
(x, y) \in \text{lower box} \;\Rightarrow\; 0 \le y \le m_i \le f(x) @@ mᵢ is a lower bound for f on the piece
\text{every lower box lies in the region} @@ so their union does too
\text{area of their union} = \textstyle\sum m_i \Delta x_i = L(f, P) @@ box-area for each box; side-by-side, adding one box at a time (sum-one-more): neighbours share only x = xᵢ
L(f, P) \le A @@ inside-no-more
\text{the upper boxes } [x_{i-1}, x_i] \times [0, M_i] \text{ cover the region} @@ a point (x, y) of the region has x in some piece, and y ≤ f(x) ≤ Mᵢ
\htmlClass{new}{L(f, P) \le A \le U(f, P)} \quad \blacksquare @@ box-area, side-by-side, inside-no-more again, the other way

A box of height 0 is not a box by the definition above, since it needs c < d. Where mᵢ = 0 the lower box is left out, and its term mᵢ Δxᵢ = 0 changes nothing.

One early mathematician asked his readers to think of a plane figure as woven of all its lines, one at each x, like a cloth of its threads. Two figures were to be compared by comparing their lines one pair at a time.

Had the partition been 0, 1.5, 2.5, 4, the greatest value on the middle piece would be f(2) = 4. That is at a critical point inside it, and neither end would give it. The heights are the least and greatest on each piece, wherever on the piece they fall.

Who said it first

Cavalieri asked his readers to think of a plane figure as woven of all its lines, one at each x, like a cloth of its threads, and to compare two figures by comparing their lines one pair at a time.

Cavalieri, Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum (1635); paraphrase

The closer’s heap of lines is Bonaventura Cavalieri’s (1635); the critic is Paul Guldin. 10d’s q is Dirichlet’s function.

Accepted names: the lower and upper sums are also called lower and upper Darboux sums, after Gaston Darboux.
