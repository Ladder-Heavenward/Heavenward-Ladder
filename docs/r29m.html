29m · technique

Perspective

Stand between the rails of a straight railway and look along it. The rails never meet, yet in your eye, and in any photograph, they run together at one point on the horizon. Painters in Florence worked out the rule six hundred years ago by looking through a window and tracing what they saw on the glass. This step does the same with arrows. Every straight line in the world is painted as a straight line, and lines that run the same way are painted running into one point. Taking those meeting points seriously gives a plane with extra points far off, one for each direction, where any two lines meet, much as on the sphere. Then the moves of a painter are found, and one number made from four points on a line that every such move keeps, so that a photograph can be measured.

A window in front of the eye paints each straight line as a straight line, and lines with the same direction as lines through one vanishing point. Adding a point far off for each direction gives a plane where any two lines meet in one point, found by a cross product. The painter’s moves keep one ratio of four points on a line.

29m1The painter’s window

Stands on 18c (lines), 27i3 (the cross technique) and rung 10 (limits).

Put the eye at O and a window, the plane z = 1, in front of it. A point X = (x, y, z) with z > 0 is painted where the line from the eye to X crosses the window, at the picture point (x/z, y/z). Write the window’s two places as u across and w up. A line of the world runs X(t) = P + tv, with P on it and v its direction.

Vanishing-point technique: let a line X(t) = P + tv miss the eye, with v₃ > 0. Its picture lies on one line of the window, and as t grows the picture heads to (v₁/v₃, v₂/v₃), which depends on v alone. So lines of the world that run the same way are painted running into one point, their vanishing point.

n = P \times v \ne 0 @@ the line misses O, so P and v are not along one line (27i3)
n\cdot X(t) = n\cdot P + t\,n\cdot v = 0 @@ the cross is at right angles to P and to v (27i3)
n\cdot\big(\tfrac{x}{z},\ \tfrac{y}{z},\ 1\big) = \tfrac{1}{z}\,n\cdot X = 0 @@ divide by z > 0: every picture point has n₁u + n₂w + n₃ = 0
n_1, n_2 \text{ not both } 0 @@ otherwise n₃ ≠ 0 and n · v = n₃v₃ = 0, against v₃ > 0
\Big(\tfrac{P_1 + tv_1}{P_3 + tv_3},\ \tfrac{P_2 + tv_2}{P_3 + tv_3}\Big) \to \Big(\tfrac{v_1}{v_3},\ \tfrac{v_2}{v_3}\Big) @@ divide top and bottom by t, and let t grow (rung 10)

\text{ground } y = -1.6,\ \ \text{rails } x = \pm 0.7175,\ \ v = (0, 0, 1) @@ an eye 1.6 m up; standard gauge is 1.435 m between the rails
\text{rail at } z:\ \ \big(\pm\tfrac{0.7175}{z},\ -\tfrac{1.6}{z}\big) \to (0, 0) @@ the vanishing-point technique: both rails run to one point
\text{sleepers at } z = 2, 4, 6, 8:\ \ w = -0.8,\ -0.4,\ -0.267,\ -0.2 @@ equal steps on the ground, shrinking steps in the picture
v = (v_1, 0, v_3) \text{ on level ground}:\ \ \text{vanishing point } (v_1/v_3,\ 0) @@ every level direction
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every level line vanishes on the horizon } w = 0,\ \text{at eye height}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

A line with v₃ = 0 runs across the window, square to the line of sight. Its picture never ends at a point, and two such lines that run the same way stay running the same way in the picture. So the upright corners of a building stay upright in a photograph taken level, and they lean together when the camera is tipped up.

29m2Points at infinity

Stands on 29m1, 27i3 (the cross technique), 29b1 (the lines-always-meet technique) and 18c.

The window point (u, w) is seen along the line through the eye and (u, w, 1). Take that line through O as the point itself. Then every arrow p ≠ 0 names a point, written [p₁ : p₂ : p₃], and p and every multiple sp, s ≠ 0, name the same one. The window points are the [u : w : 1]. The points [p₁ : p₂ : 0] are new: the points at infinity, one for each direction of the window, with a direction and its reverse counted as one. The vanishing point of a direction v is the point [v₁ : v₂ : v₃].

A line becomes a plane through O, a · X = 0, written by its normal as [a₁ : a₂ : a₃]. The window line a₁u + a₂w + a₃ = 0 is [a₁ : a₂ : a₃], and it holds the point at infinity [−a₂ : a₁ : 0] of its own direction. The plane z = 0 gives one more line, [0 : 0 : 1], the line at infinity, which holds every point at infinity.

Meet-by-cross technique: in the plane with its points at infinity, two different lines [a] and [b] meet in exactly one point, [a × b]; and two different points [p] and [q] lie on exactly one line, [p × q].

a, b \text{ not multiples, so } a\times b \ne 0 @@ the cross technique (27i3)
a\cdot(a\times b) = 0,\ \ b\cdot(a\times b) = 0 @@ the cross is at right angles to each of its arrows: [a × b] is on both lines
a\cdot x = 0,\ b\cdot x = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ x = t\,(a\times b) @@ two planes through O meet in one line through O, as in the lines-always-meet technique (29b1)
\text{the point } [p] \text{ is on the line } [a] \iff p\cdot a = 0 @@ the same condition read the other way round
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one meeting point } [a\times b];\ \ \text{one joining line } [p\times q]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines, with points and lines swapped
  
u - w = 0:\ [1 : -1 : 0],\ \ u - w + 1 = 0:\ [1 : -1 : 1] @@ two parallel lines of the window (18c)
(1, -1, 0)\times(1, -1, 1) = (-1, -1, 0) @@ the cross technique
[-1 : -1 : 0] = [1 : 1 : 0] @@ multiply by −1: the same point
u + w = 2:\ [1 : 1 : -2];\ \ (1, 1, -2)\times(1, -1, 0) = (-2, -2, -2) @@ an ordinary pair, for comparison
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the parallels meet at infinity along } (1, 1);\ \text{the other pair at } [1 : 1 : 1] = (1, 1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ every pair of different lines meets once

Each line through O meets the sphere of radius 1 in two opposite points. So this plane is the sphere of 29b with each pair of opposite points taken as one point, and its lines are the great circles. The lines-always-meet technique of 29b1 met in two opposite points; here those two are one. The parallel statement of 18c, proved for ℝ², fails in the larger plane, because its parallels meet at infinity.

29m3The painter’s moves, and the four-point ratio

Stands on 29m2, 27e4 (the nonzero-det technique) and 25f4 (the four-number technique).

A painter’s move sends [p] to [Mp], for a 3 × 3 grid M with det M ≠ 0. It sends lines to lines: if a · p = 0, then b · (Mp) = 0 for b = (M⁻¹)ᵀa, since b · Mp = a · M⁻¹Mp = a · p. Moving the window, tipping the head, and photographing a photograph are all painter’s moves. On one line, with its points written [t : 1] and the point at infinity [1 : 0], a painter’s move onto another line is t ↦ (at + b)/(ct + d) with ad − bc ≠ 0, the four-number maps of 25f4. Lengths are not kept, and neither are ratios of lengths. One number is.

Four-point-ratio technique: for four different numbers t₁, t₂, t₃, t₄, the ratio (t₁ − t₃)(t₂ − t₄) / ((t₂ − t₃)(t₁ − t₄)) is kept by every four-number map that sends none of them to infinity.

f(s) - f(t) = \tfrac{(as + b)(ct + d) - (at + b)(cs + d)}{(cs + d)(ct + d)} @@ f(t) = (at + b)/(ct + d), over one bottom
f(s) - f(t) = \tfrac{(ad - bc)(s - t)}{(cs + d)(ct + d)} @@ multiply out: the terms acst and bd cancel
\tfrac{(f_1 - f_3)(f_2 - f_4)}{(f_2 - f_3)(f_1 - f_4)} = \tfrac{(ad - bc)^2 (t_1 - t_3)(t_2 - t_4)\,/\,B}{(ad - bc)^2 (t_2 - t_3)(t_1 - t_4)\,/\,B} @@ fᵢ = f(tᵢ); top and bottom each carry B = (ct₁ + d)(ct₂ + d)(ct₃ + d)(ct₄ + d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the ratio of the } f_i \text{ equals the ratio of the } t_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ cancel (ad − bc)² and B, none of them 0
  
w = -\tfrac{1.6}{z}:\ \ a = 0,\ b = -1.6,\ c = 1,\ d = 0,\ \ ad - bc = 1.6 @@ along the ground between the rails, the window is a four-number map of the distance z (29m1)
\text{ground } 2, 4, 6, 8:\ \ \tfrac{(2 - 6)(4 - 8)}{(4 - 6)(2 - 8)} = \tfrac{16}{12} = \tfrac43 @@ sleepers 2 m apart
\text{picture } -0.8, -0.4, -0.267, -0.2:\ \ \tfrac{(-0.533)(-0.2)}{(-0.133)(-0.6)} = \tfrac43 @@ the four-point-ratio technique, checked
\htmlClass{new}{\text{from three pictured sleepers and } \tfrac43,\ \text{the fourth is found}} \quad \blacksquare @@ one unknown in one sameness

So a photograph can be measured. A surveyor who knows that four posts stand equally spaced, or that one stands halfway, reads the ratio 4/3, or another known ratio, off the picture, and finds where an unseen fifth must stand.

The accepted names

Painting through the window is central (or linear) perspective, and the picture map is a perspective projection. The vanishing point and the horizon line keep their names; a picture with one vanishing point for its main lines is in one-point perspective. The plane with its points at infinity is the projective plane, ℝP², and [p₁ : p₂ : p₃] are homogeneous coordinates. The points at infinity are also called ideal points, and they lie on the line at infinity. That points and lines can swap roles in every technique is the principle of duality. A painter’s move is a projective transformation (a homography, or collineation). The four-point ratio is the cross-ratio, and the four-number maps on a line are its projective (or Möbius, or linear fractional) maps.

Who said it first

Filippo Brunelleschi showed a painted panel of the Baptistery of Florence, around 1415 to 1420, that matched the building seen through a hole in the panel. Leon Battista Alberti set out the rules for painters in On Painting in 1435, with the window and the point where the lines meet. Girard Desargues wrote in 1639 that parallel lines meet at a point infinitely far away, and treated the conic sections as pictures of a circle. Jean-Victor Poncelet built projective geometry in his Treatise of 1822, begun as a prisoner of war in Saratov in 1813. August Möbius in 1827 and Julius Plücker in 1830 brought in the coordinates that name a point by a whole line of arrows. Pappus of Alexandria knew around 320 CE that a ratio of four points on a line survives the drawing of lines from a point. Poncelet and Joseph Gergonne quarrelled in the 1820s over who first saw duality.
