How Do You Read an Organic Molecule?

How do you read an organic molecule? A bottle’s label gives a name, a balance gives a formula, and a drawing gives the bonds. Each is a function, and they do not all run both ways. A name gives one drawing. A formula can give several. And one drawing can stand for many shapes, because a molecule turns about its single bonds and does not turn about its double ones. This page reads names, counts what a formula still tells, and then asks how many kinds of “the same molecule” a chemist needs.

A name is a function to a drawing, a formula is many to one, and a substance in a jar is a class of shapes reached by turning. So reading a molecule is knowing which kind of sameness each way of writing it keeps.

9chem21A Table of Named Parts

Sarah reads the label on a bottle of vinegar: ethanoic acid. On the bottle beside it: ethanol. The first halves match and the endings differ. The ending is where the chemistry is.

2chem9 named two parts, hydroxyl and phenyl, and 2chem11 gave the letter R for “any part, joined by one gap”. A named part is a statement with one gap left for R. The parts below are the ones a first course in the subject meets.

The table is a function from parts to endings: one part in, one ending out. Two molecules that carry the same part answer the same reagent in the same way, whatever R is. That is why chemists sort by part and not by size: the part is the sift (2chem9), and R is what the sift ignores.

Biology hangs on this table. An amino acid is one carbon holding an amine, a carboxylic acid, a hydrogen and an R. The twenty things of 4bio4 are twenty values of R in one statement. Join the acid of one to the amine of the next and the link is an amide: a protein is one part repeated.

9chem22A Name Is a Set of Directions

2chem10 found the amine of 4-aminophenol by counting round the ring from the oxygen. Rung 9 can say what kind of thing that is. Drawing from name is a function: one name in, one molecule out. If two people drew different molecules from one name, the name would have failed.

The other way round is not a function until techniques are added. A chain of four carbons with an OH on the second can be counted from either end: butan-2-ol or butan-3-ol. Counting from the end that gives the named part the smaller number picks one. With that, name from drawing has one output too.

Lowest-numbers technique: find the parent, the longest chain that holds the named part, or the ring if the ring is larger; number it from the end nearest the named part; if two numberings still tie, give the second group the lowest number it can have, and after that go by alphabetical order.

9chem23Same Count, Different Molecule

5chem10 found two molecules for C₂H₆O. Four carbons and ten hydrogens give two as well: a straight chain, and a chain of three with the fourth carbon on the middle one. The count by kind is a function from molecules to formulas, and it is many to one.

The count still tells something. A chain of n carbons with every gap filled by a single bond holds 2n + 2 hydrogens. Each ring and each double bond uses up two of them.

\text{C}_6\text{H}_6:\ \ \frac{14 - 6}{2} = 4 @@ benzene shows them as one ring and three double bonds
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6:\ \ \frac{14 - 12}{2} = 1 @@ oxygen changes nothing in the count
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each N adds one hydrogen to the full count; each halogen stands in for a hydrogen}} @@ by their gap counts: 3, and 1

Glucose and fructose (2chem9) are both C₆H₁₂O₆, and the count gives 1 for each: one C=O in the open chain, or one ring when the chain closes on itself. A cell’s enzymes tell the two apart by where that one is.

9chem24Turning About a Bond

Jean builds ethane from a model kit: two carbons joined by one peg, three hydrogens on each. Holding one carbon still, Jean spins the other. Nothing breaks. Every statement of 2chem5 is still true: who is bonded to whom has not changed.

The energy has. Look down the peg. When the three hydrogens at the back sit in the spaces between the three at the front, the energy is lowest. Turn 60° and each one at the back is hidden behind one at the front; the energy is 12.6 kJ/mol higher. Turn another 60° and it is back at the lowest. Energy is a function of the angle, and it repeats every 120°.

Butane has four carbons in a row, and turning about its middle bond moves the two end carbons past each other.

angle between the two end carbonsenergy above the lowest, kJ/mol
180° (far apart)0
120°15.9
60° (side by side)3.8
0° (one behind the other)19.2

At room temperature molecules knock each other with enough energy to clear hills of this height many times a second. So a jar of butane holds one substance, not three: the shapes change into each other faster than anyone could separate them.

From here on, on this page, the shapes a molecule reaches by turning about its single bonds, with no bond broken, are its conformations.

Turn technique: shapes of a molecule that differ only by turns about single bonds are one substance, and its energy is a function of the angles of turn.

9chem25Rings That Pucker

Claire closes a chain of six model carbons into a ring and tries to press it flat on the table. It springs up. A flat hexagon needs 120° at each corner, and the four gaps of a carbon sit closer to 109.5° apart (the reason is held in trust until 19chem4). The ring gets its angle by leaving the plane: three corners up, three down, in turn.

In that shape each carbon has one outside bond pointing along the ring’s axis, up or down, and one pointing outward round the rim. Push the three high corners down and the three low ones up: the ring flips, every axis position becomes a rim position and the reverse. The hill between the two is about 42 kJ/mol, low enough that the flip happens constantly at room temperature.

Put one methyl group on the ring and the two flipped shapes no longer match in energy. On the axis the group is crowded by the two hydrogens that share its side; on the rim it is clear. The rim shape is lower by 7.3 kJ/mol, so most of the molecules, most of the time, hold the group on the rim. The bigger the group, the stronger the preference.

Rim technique: on a puckered six-ring a group is lower in energy on the rim than on the axis, and the larger the group the more so.

Join two such rings along an edge the right way and neither can flip: the shape is locked. The hormones built on four joined rings are locked this way, each in one shape.

9chem26Bonds That Do Not Turn

Randy joins two model carbons with two pegs and tries the spin of 9chem24. It will not go without pulling a peg out. A double bond does not turn at room temperature.

That makes a new kind of difference. Put one methyl on each carbon of the double bond. They can sit on the same side or on opposite sides, and no turn carries one arrangement to the other.

The gap statements are word for word the same, and these are two substances with two boiling points. So “is bonded to” does not always settle which molecule you have. The name has to say which side, and it does.

Same-side technique: a double bond does not turn, so when each of its two carbons holds two different groups there are two substances, one with the like groups on the same side and one with them on opposite sides.

The eye runs on this. The molecule that catches light in the retina has one double bond in the same-side arrangement. Light gives it the energy to turn, once, to the opposite-side arrangement. The molecule straightens, the protein around it is pushed, and that push is the first event of seeing.

A ring of the benzene kind is this footing six times over. None of its ring bonds turns, so the ring is flat and has no shapes to choose among. Why none of them turns, when only three are drawn double, is held in trust until 31chem5. What still turns is the single bond between the ring and each group on it: the O-H of phenol swings, the ring does not.

One link is drawn single and still does not turn: the amide link, C(=O)-N. Like the ring, it needs two gap statements to describe it, and the pair of them stiffens it. A protein’s backbone repeats three links: one stiff amide, two free single bonds. Every shape a protein takes is a choice of those two angles, link after link.

9chem27Four Kinds of Same

The page has used “the same molecule” in four strengths. Each is a sameness relation in 2i15’s sense, and each cuts the classes of the one before into smaller classes.

A substance in a jar is a class of the third kind. That is the level at which things can be bottled, because turning is fast and everything above it needs a bond broken.

Accepted names

A named part is a functional group. The naming techniques are the IUPAC rules. Molecules with one formula are isomers, and rings plus double bonds is the degree of unsaturation. A shape reached by turning is a conformation, and one at the bottom of an energy dip a conformer; the view down a bond is a Newman projection, the low shape of ethane is staggered and the high one eclipsed, and butane’s 180° and 60° shapes are anti and gauche. The puckered six-ring is the chair, its flip the ring flip, an axis position axial and a rim position equatorial. Same side and opposite sides are cis and trans, or Z and E. A ring of the benzene kind is aromatic. The amide link between amino acids is the peptide bond. The molecule in the retina is retinal, which light turns from 11-cis to all-trans. A hormone of four locked rings is a steroid; estradiol is one.

Where the numbers come from

The energies on this page (12.6; 3.8, 15.9 and 19.2; 42; 7.3 kJ/mol) are a first course’s values, 3.0; 0.9, 3.8 and 4.6; 10; 1.74 kcal/mol, turned into kJ at 4.184. Other sources give slightly different values. They have not yet been revisited against a second source. Nor have these: that a double bond does not turn at room temperature; that the turning of retinal is the first event of seeing; that the amide link is stiff, with two free angles for each link of a protein; that hormones of four joined rings are locked in one shape.
