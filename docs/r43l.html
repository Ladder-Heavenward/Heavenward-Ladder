Boolean algebra standing on its own

Rung one wrote a rulebook for and, or and not, and proved each entry by a table. Rung four wired the tables into adders, and rung thirty eight built every table from one gate. Through all of it the tables came first and the rules were read off them. Turn that round. Lay down a short list of rules for two ways of combining and one way of flipping, and ask what follows from the list alone, with no tables at all. Most of the rulebook does. The list has a mirror in it: swap and with or and nothing with everything, and every rule becomes another rule, so every proof becomes another proof for free. Read with either-but-not-both as plus, the same things are a ring of step 43a in which every member is its own square, and in that ring each table is exactly one polynomial. That gives a way to simplify a circuit by arithmetic. Last, every such algebra with finitely many members is the parts of some set, so the list says no more and no less than the sets of rung two.

A short list of rules for and, or and not gives the rest of the rulebook, and it is its own mirror, so every proved rule has a twin. With either-but-not-both as plus and and as times, the same algebra is a ring where every member is its own square; there one plus one is nothing, and every table is exactly one polynomial. Every finite such algebra is the parts of a set.

43l1A short list

A Boolean algebra is a set B with two combinings ∨ and ∧, a flip ¬, and two members 0 and 1, obeying for all x, y, z: turn-about for ∧ and for ∨; regroup for each; spread ∧ over ∨ and ∨ over ∧; units, x ∨ 0 = x and x ∧ 1 = x; and the two clash rules x ∨ ¬x = 1, x ∧ ¬x = 0. Ten lines, all from 1f’s rulebook. 1e’s {0, 1} is one, and so are the parts of any set, with ∪, ∩ and the rest.

Short-list technique: say-it-again, absorb and both not-through rules of 1f follow from the ten lines alone.

x = x \lor 0 @@ units
\phantom{x} = x \lor (x \land \neg x) @@ clash, x ∧ ¬x = 0
\phantom{x} = (x \lor x) \land (x \lor \neg x) @@ spread ∨ over ∧
\phantom{x} = (x \lor x) \land 1 = x \lor x @@ clash; units
\htmlClass{new}{x \lor x = x} \quad \blacksquare @@ no table was drawn

Absorb, x ∨ (x ∧ y) = x, goes the same way: x ∨ (x ∧ y) = (x ∧ 1) ∨ (x ∧ y) = x ∧ (1 ∨ y), and 1 ∨ y = 1 by a line like the one above. Not-through needs one more idea: ¬x is the only z with x ∨ z = 1 and x ∧ z = 0 (43l.7.1 asks for it), so to show ¬(x ∨ y) = ¬x ∧ ¬y, check that ¬x ∧ ¬y passes both tests.

43l2The mirror

The mirror of a statement about a Boolean algebra swaps ∨ with ∧ and 0 with 1, and leaves ¬ alone.

Mirror technique: if a statement follows from the ten lines, so does its mirror.

\text{the mirror of each of the ten lines is one of the ten} @@ turn-about to turn-about, spread to spread, x ∨ 0 = x to x ∧ 1 = x, x ∨ ¬x = 1 to x ∧ ¬x = 0
\text{mirror every line of a proof} @@ each axiom used becomes an axiom; each move of 0b and 1 stays a move
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the mirrored lines are a proof of the mirrored statement}} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction on the length of the proof (4c)

So the proof of x ∨ x = x in 43l1, mirrored, proves x ∧ x = x with no more work, and absorb’s twin x ∧ (x ∨ y) = x comes free. In the parts of a set the mirror swaps ∪ with ∩ and ∅ with the whole set. A statement and its mirror need not say the same thing: x ∧ 0 = 0 and x ∨ 1 = 1 are twins and different.

43l3As a ring

In a Boolean algebra put x + y = (x ∧ ¬y) ∨ (¬x ∧ y), either-but-not-both, and xy = x ∧ y. Then B is a ring of 43a, with 0 and 1 as its 0 and 1, and the negative of x is x itself. In the parts of a set this is 43a2’s ring of parts. Every member is its own square: xx = x ∧ x = x.

Own-square technique: in any ring in which every member is its own square, 1 + 1 = 0 and the ring turns about.

x + y = (x + y)^2 = x^2 + xy + yx + y^2 = x + xy + yx + y @@ spread, both sides; squares are themselves
xy + yx = 0 @@ take away x + y
x = y:\ \ x + x = 0,\ \text{so } x = -x @@ the line above with y = x; and 1 + 1 = 0 is the case x = 1
\htmlClass{new}{xy = -yx = yx} \quad \blacksquare @@ the second line, then the third on yx

The way back: in such a ring put x ∨ y = x + y + xy, x ∧ y = xy and ¬x = 1 + x, and the ten lines hold. So a Boolean algebra and a ring of own squares are one thing written two ways. 39a read 1e’s either-but-not-both as the sum by 2 and and as the product: that was the two-member case.

43l4One polynomial for each table

Over ℤ/2 with x² = x, a polynomial in x₁, …, xₙ is a sum of products of distinct letters, each product used once or not at all. There are 2ⁿ such products, so 2^(2ⁿ) such sums, and there are 2^(2ⁿ) tables of n inputs, since a table picks 0 or 1 for each of its 2ⁿ rows (4k).

One-polynomial technique: every table of n inputs is exactly one sum of products of distinct inputs over ℤ/2. Each table is some such sum, since each row with a 1 is a product of xᵢ and 1 + xⱼ, and rows that never both hold add as either-but-not-both. Different sums give different tables, since the counts match: 2^(2ⁿ) sums onto 2^(2ⁿ) tables.

(x \land y) \lor (x \land \neg y) \lor (\neg x \land y) @@ the rows 11, 10, 01, as 4·comp wrote a table
xy + x(1 + y) + (1 + x)y @@ the three rows never hold together, so each ∨ is a +
= xy + x + xy + y + xy @@ spread
= x + y + xy @@ xy + xy = 0
\htmlClass{new}{= x \lor y} \quad \blacksquare @@ the way back of 43l3

4·comp found “at least two of three” as A · B ∨ B · C ∨ A · C by merging rows. Its polynomial is AB + BC + CA: at 1, 1, 1 it gives 1 + 1 + 1 = 1. The same three ands, with either-but-not-both gates in place of ors. Which form is cheaper depends on the gates on hand, as 39f counted.

43l5Every finite one is the parts of a set

In a Boolean algebra write x ≤ y when x ∧ y = x. An atom is a member a ≠ 0 with nothing strictly between 0 and a.

Atoms technique: a Boolean algebra with finitely many members is matched with the parts of its set of atoms, x going to the atoms below it, keeping ∨, ∧ and ¬; so it has 2ᵏ members, k the number of atoms.

x \ne 0 \Rightarrow \text{some atom } a \le x @@ go down while something lies strictly between; finitely many members, so it stops
a \text{ an atom},\ a \le x \lor y \Rightarrow a \le x \text{ or } a \le y @@ a = a ∧ (x ∨ y) = (a ∧ x) ∨ (a ∧ y), and each part is 0 or a
a \le \neg x \iff a \not\le x @@ a ∧ x and a ∧ ¬x are each 0 or a, and they join to a and meet in 0
x = \textstyle\bigvee\{a \text{ atom} : a \le x\} @@ the join w of those atoms has w ≤ x; if x ∧ ¬w ≠ 0, an atom below it is below x and not below w, ⊥
\htmlClass{new}{x \mapsto \{a : a \le x\} \text{ is a matching with the parts of the atoms}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above: one-to-one, onto, and keeping ∨, ∧, ¬

So there is no Boolean algebra with 6 members, or 12, and the divisors of 30, with the greatest common divisor as ∧ and the least common multiple as ∨ and 30/x as ¬, are the parts of {2, 3, 5}. Endless ones need not be the parts of anything: the parts of ℕ that are finite or have a finite rest are a Boolean algebra that can be listed, and the parts of an endless set cannot (35c).

The accepted names

The ten lines are Huntington’s postulates for a Boolean algebra, in one of their forms; say-it-again is idempotence, and not-through is De Morgan’s laws. The mirror technique is the duality principle. A ring of own squares is a Boolean ring, and the own-square technique says every Boolean ring is commutative of characteristic 2. The polynomial of a table is its algebraic normal form, or Zhegalkin polynomial, and the sum of rows is the disjunctive normal form. The atoms technique is the finite case of Stone’s representation theorem.

Who said it first

George Boole wrote an algebra of logic in 1847 and 1854, with x² = x as his law of duality. Ernst Schröder noticed the mirror in the 1890s. Edward Huntington gave lists of postulates in 1904 and 1933. Ivan Zhegalkin wrote the polynomial form in 1927, and Marshall Stone showed in 1936 that Boolean algebras and Boolean rings are one thing, and that every Boolean algebra is an algebra of sets.

A proof that is not allowed

“x ∨ y = x + y + xy, and x ∧ y = xy, so by the mirror technique x ∧ y = x + y + xy too.” Not allowed: the mirror technique works on statements built from ∨, ∧, ¬, 0 and 1 alone. The + of the ring is not one of them, and its mirror is not defined.
