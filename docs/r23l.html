23l · technique

Numerical integration

Many areas have no antiderivative you can write down, and the bell-shaped curve that statistics lives on is one of them. But an area can be computed from a handful of heights. Cut the base into strips and replace the curve on each strip by something simple whose area is easy: a straight chord, which makes trapezoids, or a parabola through three heights. The matching polynomials of this rung measure how wrong that is. The chord is off by about the bend of the curve times the square of the strip width, so halving the width cuts the error to a quarter. The parabola does far better: halving cuts it to a sixteenth. And if you may choose where to measure the heights, two well-placed heights do the work of three evenly spaced ones.

Replace the curve on each strip by a chord or a parabola, and add the areas. The error shrinks like the square of the strip width for chords, and like its fourth power for parabolas. Heights at the best places do better still.

23l1Trapezoids

Cut [u, v] into N strips of width h = (v − u)/N, with heights f₀, f₁, …, f_N at the cuts. Join neighbouring heights by chords. The area under the chords is the trapezoid sum Tₕ = h(½f₀ + f₁ + ⋯ + f_{N−1} + ½f_N).

Trapezoid technique: if |f″| ≤ K on [u, v], then |Tₕ − ∫ᵤᵛ f| ≤ (v − u)Kh²/6. Halve h, and the bound falls to a quarter.

f(m+t) = f(m) + f'(m)\,t + R_1(t),\ \ |R_1(t)| \le \tfrac12 K t^2 @@ the match of degree 1 about the strip's middle m (23a2), and its error ceiling (23b2)
\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} (f(m) + f'(m)t)\,dt = h f(m) = \tfrac{h}{2}\big((f(m) - f'(m)\tfrac h2) + (f(m) + f'(m)\tfrac h2)\big) @@ for a line, the chord is the curve: the rule is exact
\text{strip error} = \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} R_1 - \tfrac{h}{2}\big(R_1(-\tfrac h2) + R_1(\tfrac h2)\big) @@ the line parts cancel
|\text{strip error}| \le \tfrac{K}{3}\big(\tfrac h2\big)^3 + \tfrac h2\cdot K\big(\tfrac h2\big)^2 = \tfrac{K h^3}{6} @@ ∫ ½Kt² over [−s, s] is Ks³/3, with s = h/2
\htmlClass{new}{|T_h - \textstyle\int_u^v f| \le N\cdot\tfrac{Kh^3}{6} = \tfrac{(v-u)K h^2}{6}} \quad \blacksquare @@ N = (v − u)/h strips; the errors add (15e)

ln 2 = ∫₁² dx/x (17a), and f″ = 2/x³ ≤ 2 there. Four strips give T = 0.697024, off by 0.00388; the bound is 2/(6·16) = 0.0208. Eight strips give 0.694122, off by 0.000975: a quarter. A finer argument trims the 6 in the bound to 12, which is the most it can be trimmed.

23l2Parabolas

Take the strips in pairs, N even. On a pair [m − h, m + h] use the weights h/3, 4h/3, h/3 on the three heights. The parabola sum is Sₕ = (h/3)(f₀ + 4f₁ + 2f₂ + 4f₃ + ⋯ + 4f_{N−1} + f_N). On each pair it is the area under the parabola through the three heights, since it is exact for every polynomial of degree 2 or less.

Sixteenth technique: if |f⁽⁴⁾| ≤ K on [u, v], then |Sₕ − ∫ᵤᵛ f| ≤ (v − u)Kh⁴/45. Halve h, and the bound falls to a sixteenth.

\int_{-h}^{h} 1 = 2h = \tfrac h3(1 + 4 + 1),\ \ \int_{-h}^{h} t^2 = \tfrac{2h^3}{3} = \tfrac h3(h^2 + 0 + h^2) @@ exact for 1 and t²
\int_{-h}^{h} t = 0 = \tfrac h3(-h + 0 + h),\ \ \int_{-h}^{h} t^3 = 0 = \tfrac h3(-h^3 + 0 + h^3) @@ exact for t and t³: odd, both sides 0
f(m+t) = T_3(t) + R_3(t),\ \ |R_3(t)| \le \tfrac{K t^4}{24} @@ the match of degree 3 about m (23a2), and its error ceiling (23b2)
\text{pair error} = \int_{-h}^{h} R_3 - \tfrac h3\big(R_3(-h) + 4R_3(0) + R_3(h)\big) @@ the cubic parts cancel; R₃(0) = 0
|\text{pair error}| \le \tfrac{K h^5}{60} + \tfrac h3\cdot\tfrac{2Kh^4}{24} = \tfrac{Kh^5}{60} + \tfrac{Kh^5}{36} = \tfrac{2Kh^5}{45} @@ ∫ Kt⁴/24 over [−h, h] is Kh⁵/60
\htmlClass{new}{|S_h - \textstyle\int_u^v f| \le \tfrac{v-u}{2h}\cdot\tfrac{2Kh^5}{45} = \tfrac{(v-u)K h^4}{45}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (v − u)/(2h) pairs; the errors add

Built from parabolas, the rule is exact for cubics, a gift of the symmetry. For ln 2, f⁽⁴⁾ = 24/x⁵ ≤ 24. Four strips give S = 0.693254, off by 0.000107, and eight give 0.6931545, off by 0.0000074. A finer argument trims the 45 to 180.

The two rules are related. (4T_{h/2} − T_h)/3 = S_{h/2}, exactly: the trapezoid errors shrink like h², and this blend of two trapezoid sums cancels that part of the error. It is 23k's habit again: an error with a known shape can be removed.

23l3The best choice of points

Both rules measure heights at evenly spaced points. Free the points as well as the weights. Best-points technique: on [−1, 1], f(−1/√3) + f(1/√3) is exact for every polynomial of degree 3 or less; and no rule that uses n heights is exact for every polynomial of degree 2n. Two well-placed heights do the work of the parabola rule's three.

w f(-r) + w f(r) \text{ is exact for } t \text{ and } t^3 @@ odd: both sides are 0
\text{exact for } 1:\ 2w = 2,\ \text{so } w = 1 @@ ∫ 1 over [−1, 1] is 2
\text{exact for } t^2:\ 2r^2 = \tfrac23,\ \text{so } r = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} @@ ∫ t² over [−1, 1] is ⅔
\htmlClass{new}{\int_{-1}^{1} p = p\big(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\big) + p\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\big) \text{ for every cubic } p} @@ a cubic is a sum of multiples of 1, t, t², t³; areas add (15e)
p(t) = (t - x_1)^2 (t - x_2)^2 \cdots (t - x_n)^2 @@ any n points x₁, …, xₙ: degree 2n
\int_{-1}^{1} p > 0,\ \text{but the rule gives } \textstyle\sum w_i\,p(x_i) = 0 @@ p is 0 at each xᵢ and above 0 everywhere else
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no } n \text{ heights are exact for every polynomial of degree } 2n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the line above

On a strip [m − s, m + s], put x = m + st (16d): the heights go at m ± s/√3, each with weight s. For ∫₀¹ e^(−x²) dx = 0.746824, one pair of best points gives 0.746595, off by 0.00023, while the parabola rule with three heights is off by 0.00036. With n heights, well placed, a rule is exact up to degree 2n − 1, the most the last line allows. Where those places are is decided by a family of polynomials much higher up the ladder.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

Computing areas from heights is numerical integration, or quadrature. The trapezoid sum is the trapezoidal rule; the parabola sum is Simpson's rule, with error bound (v − u)Kh⁴/180 in its sharp form. Blending two trapezoid sums to cancel the h² part is Richardson extrapolation, and done again and again it is Romberg integration. The best-points rule is Gaussian quadrature; its points are the roots of the Legendre polynomials. Rules on evenly spaced points are the Newton–Cotes rules, and the weights 1, 3, 3, 1 of 23l.4.1 make Simpson's three-eighths rule.

Who said it first

Johannes Kepler used a parabola rule to gauge wine barrels in 1615. Bonaventura Cavalieri (1639) and James Gregory (1668) had the parabola rule before Thomas Simpson published it in 1743. Isaac Newton and Roger Cotes worked out the rules for evenly spaced points (1676 to 1711). Carl Friedrich Gauss found the best points in 1814, and Carl Gustav Jacobi explained them in 1826. Lewis Fry Richardson blended trapezoid sums in 1911, and Werner Romberg made a method of it in 1955.
