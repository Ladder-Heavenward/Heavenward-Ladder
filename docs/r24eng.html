How Does a Thermostat Hold a Room Steady?

How does a thermostat hold a room steady? On a January night in Truckee the air outside is well below freezing, and heat leaks out of a house through every wall and window. A furnace in the cellar can put heat back faster than it leaks. A small box on the wall decides when. It cannot see the weather, the open door or the oven. It can only read the room. What makes the question hard is that the box has to act on a room that answers slowly: by the time the room is warm enough, the furnace has already put in more. This page shows, with rung twenty-four’s rules on rates and their states that hold, how fast a house cools, why a simple thermostat makes the room swing back and forth, why a heater that pushes in proportion to the gap leaves a gap that never closes, and what a heater must remember to close it.

A house loses heat in proportion to how much warmer it is than the outside, so with a steady heater it settles at a temperature that holds, slowly, over a time set by how much heat it stores against how fast it leaks. A thermostat that switches the furnace on below a set point and off above it makes the room swing across a narrow band, with the furnace on for just the share of time that matches the heat the house needs. A heater that pushes harder the colder the room is settles where its push matches the leak, a little short of the set point, and that shortfall never closes. A heater that adds up the shortfall over time can only rest where there is none.

24eng1A House That Leaks

A house stores heat: it takes c joules to warm it by one degree. It leaks heat to the outside at λ watts for each degree it is warmer. A furnace puts in P watts. With the outside at T_o, the heat balance is a rule on rates.

c\,T' = P - \lambda(T - T_o) @@ heat in, less heat leaked, per second
T' = \tfrac{1}{\tau}\big(T^* - T\big),\ \ T^* = T_o + \tfrac{P}{\lambda},\ \ \tau = \tfrac{c}{\lambda} @@ divide by c, and gather
\text{the slope of the right side in } T \text{ is } -\tfrac1\tau \lt 0 @@ the state T* holds (24k)
\htmlClass{new}{T = T^* + (T_0 - T^*)\,e^{-t/\tau}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the target technique (24·then.2)

Closing-gap technique: a house with a steady heater settles at the outside temperature plus P/λ, closing 63% of what is left of the gap in each τ = c/λ. Take a Truckee house with λ = 250 W per degree and c = 1.8 × 10⁷ J per degree, so τ = 72 000 s, 20 hours. On a −6 °C night, holding 20 °C needs 250 × 26 = 6500 W. A 10 kW furnace left on would carry the house toward 34 °C.

24eng2Switching at a Set Point

The simplest thermostat switches the furnace fully on when the room falls to 19.5 °C and fully off when it climbs to 20.5 °C. With the furnace on, the room heads for T*_on = 34 °C; with it off, for T_o = −6 °C. Each half of the swing follows the closing-gap technique, and a log undoes it.

t = \tau\ln\tfrac{T_0 - T^*}{T_1 - T^*} @@ the closing-gap technique solved for the time from T₀ to T₁ (17c)
t_{\text{on}} = 20\ln\tfrac{34 - 19.5}{34 - 20.5} \approx 1.43 \text{ h} @@ heading for 34 °C
t_{\text{off}} = 20\ln\tfrac{20.5 + 6}{19.5 + 6} \approx 0.77 \text{ h} @@ heading for −6 °C
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{t_{\text{on}}}{t_{\text{on}} + t_{\text{off}}} \approx 0.65 = \tfrac{6500}{10\,000}} \quad \blacksquare @@ on for the share of the heat the house needs

On-share technique: a switching thermostat makes the room swing across its band, with the furnace on for the share of time that matches the heat the house needs. Narrow the band and the swings come faster, while the share stays the same. A real furnace and real air take minutes to answer, so the room runs a little past each end of the band.

24eng3Heating in Proportion to the Gap

A smoother heater pushes harder the colder the room: P = G(T_s − T), where T_s is the set point and G the watts per degree of gap. Put it into the leak rule.

c\,T' = G(T_s - T) - \lambda(T - T_o) @@ heater by the gap, less the leak
c\,T' = -(\lambda + G)\,T + G\,T_s + \lambda\,T_o @@ gather the T terms
T^* = \tfrac{G\,T_s + \lambda\,T_o}{\lambda + G},\ \ \text{holding, with time scale } \tfrac{c}{\lambda + G} @@ slope −(λ + G)/c < 0 (24k)
\htmlClass{new}{T_s - T^* = \tfrac{\lambda\,(T_s - T_o)}{\lambda + G}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gap at the state that holds

Droop technique: a heater that pushes in proportion to the gap settles short of the set point, by a gap that shrinks as G grows and never closes. It cannot close: with no gap there is no push, and the house still leaks. In the Truckee house with G = 2000 W per degree, the room settles 2.9 degrees short; with G = 5000, 1.2 short. A larger G also answers faster, with τ = c/(λ + G). It cannot grow without limit, since the furnace tops out at 10 kW.

24eng4A Heater That Remembers

To push with no gap, a heater must know something besides the gap. One way is to add up the gap as time passes. Let e = T_s − T, and let the heater push P = Ge + I, where I grows by κe each second: I′ = κe. While the room is cold, I climbs; while it is warm, I falls. This e is not 17e's number e.

c\,T' = Ge + I - \lambda(T - T_o),\ \ I' = \kappa e @@ two rules on rates, for T and for I
\text{a state left alone: } T' = 0 \text{ and } I' = 0 @@ both rates at once
I' = 0 \Rightarrow e = 0 @@ κ ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{T^* = T_s,\ \ I^* = \lambda(T_s - T_o)} \quad \blacksquare @@ then T′ = 0 gives I*

Summing technique: a heater that adds up the gap over time can be left alone only where the gap is 0, and there its sum is exactly the heat the house leaks. Nobody tells it the weather: on a colder night I simply climbs further. Whether that state holds is a question about two rules at once, held in trust for higher up the ladder. Too large a κ makes the room overshoot and swing; a modest one settles.

The accepted names

λ is the house’s heat loss coefficient, c its heat capacity, and τ its time constant. The switching thermostat is bang-bang or on-off control, and its band the hysteresis or deadband; the room’s swing is cycling. Heating by the gap is proportional control, G the gain, and the gap that never closes the steady-state error, or droop. Adding up the gap is integral action; together the two make a PI controller, and with a push from the gap’s rate as well, a PID controller. Pushing in advance with the weather is feedforward.

Who said it first

Cornelis Drebbel built a furnace held steady by a mercury switch for his egg incubator, around 1620. Warren Johnson patented an electric room thermostat in 1883. James Watt’s steam governor (1788) worked by the gap and drooped. James Clerk Maxwell, On Governors (1868), first treated such devices by their rules on rates, and set those that only drooped apart from those that added up the gap. Nicolas Minorsky (1922) gave the three-part rule for steering ships. In 24·eng.6.5, Maxwell called the drooping kind moderators and kept the name governor for the kind that closes the gap.
