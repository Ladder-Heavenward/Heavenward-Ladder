40l · technique

Convincing without telling

Can you convince someone that a map can be coloured with three colours without showing them a colouring, or anything at all they could not have made up themselves? It sounds like a trick. Rung thirty seven made a proof a list of lines that anyone can check, and a list of lines tells everything. This step changes the game. There are two players. One knows the answer and has unlimited time. The other has a few coins and little time, and asks questions. The first has to answer so that a liar would be caught, almost surely, and an honest answer gives nothing away. With locked boxes, which the rung on clock arithmetic will build, it can be done for three-colouring, and by the last step’s reduction, for every question with short certificates. The checker ends up sure, with a stated chance of being fooled, and with nothing to show anyone else. This step does it by hand, with numbers: how often a cheat is caught, how many rounds bring the chance down, and why the record of a round is something the checker could have written alone.

A checker with coins questions a prover; a liar is caught at a known rate each round, and rounds multiply. With locked boxes and shuffled colours, a three-colouring is proved by opening one link at a time, and the checker learns nothing it could not have made up. By reduction, every question with short certificates has such a proof.

40l1Two players, and a liar caught

Two maps are the same shape when a matching of their dots (9c) carries links to links and non-links to non-links. Nobody knows a fast way to decide this, and no short certificate is known that two maps are not the same shape. Yet a prover can convince a checker of it.

Which-one technique: if two maps are not the same shape, a prover names which of them the checker shuffled every time; if they are the same shape, any prover names it with chance exactly one half; so k rounds fool the checker with chance at most 2⁻ᵏ.

Twenty rounds bring the chance of being fooled below one in a million. The checker’s work is fast: shuffling and comparing an answer. The prover’s work may be enormous, and the checker does not care how it was done. This is a proof with a stated chance of error, of the kind 40i set beside proofs with none.

40l2Locked colours

A locked box holds a colour. Once sent, its content cannot be changed, and the checker cannot see it until the prover sends the key. Boxes like this are built from functions that are fast to compute and believed slow to undo; the ladder holds them in trust until rung 42 builds them. With them the prover proves a three-colouring of a map with l links.

Locked-colours technique: if the map has no three-colouring, each round catches the prover with chance at least 1/l; so k · l rounds fool the checker with chance at most e⁻ᵏ.

40l3Nothing learned

What does the checker see in a round of the locked-colours technique? A link it chose, and two different colours at its ends. Because the colours were renamed at random, the pair it sees is any of the 6 ordered pairs of different colours, each with chance 1/6, whatever the colouring was.

Self-made-record technique: the record of each round, the link and the two colours shown, has the same chances as a record the checker could make alone, with its own coins and no colouring.

So the checker gains certainty and no information. It cannot show the record to anyone else as evidence, since it could have made it up. Its accepted name is a zero-knowledge proof. The lock is what makes it work: without it, the prover could choose the colours after seeing the link, and with it, the boxes not opened stay unopened.

The accepted names

The two players are the prover and the verifier of an interactive proof system, and IP is the class of sets with such proofs; IP = PSPACE is Shamir’s theorem (1990), after Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan. The which-one technique is the protocol for graph non-isomorphism, and two maps of the same shape are isomorphic graphs. A locked box is a commitment scheme. The locked-colours technique and the self-made-record technique are the zero-knowledge protocol for graph 3-colouring; the checker’s lone method of making a record is a simulator.

Who said it first

Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff defined interactive and zero-knowledge proofs in a paper circulated in 1985; László Babai gave a version with public coins the same year. Oded Goldreich, Silvio Micali and Avi Wigderson (1986) gave the protocols for non-isomorphism and three-colouring, and so for all of NP. Adi Shamir proved IP = PSPACE in 1990. Goldwasser and Micali received the Turing Award in 2012.

A proof that is not allowed

“The checker saw the same link coloured red and blue in round 3 and blue and green in round 7, so it has learned that the link’s ends differ in every colouring.” Not allowed as learning: that the ends differ is what a three-colouring promises, and the checker was told the map has one. The colours themselves were renamed, so the two rounds say nothing about which colours the true colouring uses.
