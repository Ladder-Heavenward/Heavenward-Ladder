Which Few Directions Carry Most of the Variation?

Which few directions carry most of the variation? A hospital lab in Sacramento reads how strongly each of 20,000 genes is switched on in tumour samples from 100 patients. That is a cloud of 100 points in a space with 20,000 directions, far too many to look at. 31o found the directions of most spread of a cloud as the axes of its spread grid, and showed how much spread each keeps. This page asks what is different when the cloud is genes. Why may we always use the axes: why is the spread grid never stuck in 44·cb27’s nearly diagonal form with ones above the diagonal? Why can 100 samples show at most 99 directions, however many genes are read? How can the lab find them from a grid of size 100 instead of 20,000? And why does the answer change when one trait is measured in centimetres and another in kilograms?

A symmetric grid has no ones above the diagonal in its nearly diagonal form, so the spread grid always has a full set of axes at right angles. With n samples whose average is taken away, at most n − 1 axes carry any spread, and their stretches are those of the small n by n grid XXᵀ, with the axes Xᵀu. Scaling each trait by its own spread first gives a different answer, and which to use is a choice. Keeping the first r axes keeps their share of the total spread, and the rest is the average squared distance left over.

44bio1The Spread Grid Never Needs Ones

44·cb27 showed that a grid over ℂ can be put in a nearly diagonal form, with ones just above the diagonal where its least polynomial has a repeated zero. The spread grid C of 31o is symmetric, and its stretches are real (31d).

No-block technique: a real symmetric grid C has no repeated zero in its least polynomial, so by the plain-diagonal technique it is diagonal in a basis of stretched vectors. If (C − λI)²v = 0 for a real λ, then |(C − λI)v|² = vᵀ(C − λI)ᵀ(C − λI)v = vᵀ(C − λI)²v = 0, so already (C − λI)v = 0. A one above the diagonal would need such a v with (C − λI)v ≠ 0.

C = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix},\ \ C - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} @@ every row of C − 2I is the same
(C - 2I)(C - 8I) = 0 @@ multiplied out: each entry is 2 · (−4 + 2 + 2) = 0
\text{least polynomial } (x - 2)(x - 8),\ \text{no repeated zero} @@ the no-block technique, checked
\htmlClass{new}{\text{stretch } 8 \text{ on } (1, 1, 1),\ \ 2 \text{ on the flat at right angles}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 8 of the 12 of spread: 67% on one axis

The axis (1, 1, 1) is all three traits rising together: a size. In gene data the first axis is often of this kind, a batch of samples handled on one day, all a little brighter, before anything about the disease shows.

44bio2Few Samples, Many Genes

Stack the n samples, with the average of each gene taken away, as the rows of X, so that C = XᵀX/n is a grid with one row and one column for each of the p genes.

Few-samples technique: n samples, with their average taken away, give a spread grid with at most n − 1 stretches other than 0, however many genes p are read. C·d = Xᵀ(Xd)/n lies in the build of the n rows of X, and those rows add to 0, so they build at most n − 1 free directions (27b). All the other axes carry no spread at all.

X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} @@ each column adds to 0: the average is taken away
\text{row}_3 = -\text{row}_1 - \text{row}_2 @@ only two free rows
\text{stretches of } X^{\mathsf T}X:\ \ 12,\ 8,\ 0,\ 0 @@ found below by the small-side technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{at most } 3 - 1 = 2 \text{ axes with spread}} \quad \blacksquare @@ four genes, but only two directions

With 100 patients and 20,000 genes, at most 99 directions carry spread, and 19,901 carry none. That does not mean the other genes do nothing: it means 100 samples cannot tell. A study that keeps the top direction of so few samples can be keeping noise.

44bio3Working From the Small Side

Small-side technique: if XXᵀu = λu with λ other than 0, then Xᵀu is a stretched vector of XᵀX with the same stretch λ, and every stretch of XᵀX other than 0 comes this way. Multiply XXᵀu = λu by Xᵀ on the left: XᵀX(Xᵀu) = λ(Xᵀu). And Xᵀu is not 0, since |Xᵀu|² = uᵀXXᵀu = λ|u|². The same step backwards, with X in place of Xᵀ, gives the last part.

XX^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & -4 & -4 \\ -4 & 6 & -2 \\ -4 & -2 & 6 \end{pmatrix} @@ dot products of the samples, two at a time
XX^{\mathsf T}(2, -1, -1) = (24, -12, -12) = 12 (2, -1, -1) @@ multiplied out
X^{\mathsf T}(2, -1, -1) = (0, -6, 0, 6) @@ the axis in gene space: genes 2 and 4 against each other
\htmlClass{new}{X^{\mathsf T}X\,(0, -6, 0, 6) = 12\,(0, -6, 0, 6)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the small-side technique, checked

In the same way (0, 1, −1) has stretch 8 under XXᵀ, and Xᵀ(0, 1, −1) = (2, 2, −2, 2) is the second axis. The total spread of XᵀX is 2 + 8 + 2 + 8 = 20, its middle sum, and 12 + 8 = 20: the two axes keep all of it, 60% and 40%. For the hospital lab, the grid of the 100 samples’ dot products is 100 by 100, against 20,000 by 20,000 the other way: forty thousand times fewer entries.

44bio4Units Change the Answer

Measure the height of plants in centimetres, with spread 100, and the weight of their seed heads in grams, with spread 1, and a co-spread of 6. Most of the spread is height only because centimetres make big numbers.

Own-units technique: dividing each trait by the square root of its spread turns the spread grid into the grid of correlations, with 1 on the diagonal, and its axes and shares are in general different from those of the spread grid. The new co-spread of traits j and k is C_jk/√(C_jj C_kk), 30g’s correlation, and the total spread becomes p, one for each trait.

\begin{pmatrix} 100 & 6 \\ 6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}:\ \ \lambda^2 - 101\lambda + 64 = 0 @@ the stretch-test technique: middle sum 101, determinant 100 − 36
\lambda_1 \approx 100.36,\ \ \text{share } \tfrac{100.36}{101} \approx 99.4\% @@ nearly all of it height
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}:\ \ \lambda = 1.6,\ 0.4 @@ 6/√(100 · 1) = 0.6; axes (1, 1) and (1, −1)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{share } \tfrac{1.6}{2} = 80\% \text{ on height and weight together}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a different first axis

Neither answer is the right one. Gene readings all come in the same units and are usually left as they are; traits measured in different units are usually scaled. The choice is made before the axes are found, and a report should state which was used.

44bio5How Much to Keep

Kept-share technique: keeping the first r axes keeps the share (λ₁ + ⋯ + λᵣ)/(λ₁ + ⋯ + λₚ) of the spread, and the average squared distance from the points to the flat piece built by those axes is the rest, λᵣ₊₁ + ⋯ + λₚ. Each point’s squared size is the sum of its squared readings on all the axes, which are at right angles (44·bio.1). Dropping axes r + 1 to p drops those readings, and their squares average to the stretches (31o1’s spread-along technique).

\text{total spread} = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 = 8 + 2 + 2 @@ the middle sum
r = 1:\ \ \tfrac{8}{12} \approx 67\%,\ \ \text{left over } 4 @@ the kept-share technique
r = 2:\ \ \tfrac{10}{12} \approx 83\%,\ \ \text{left over } 2 @@ but which second axis? any in the flat of stretch 2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{keep one axis: the next two are tied}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a tie means the data pick no second direction

A study of about 3000 people from across Europe, each read at about half a million places in their DNA, kept just two axes. Each kept well under one percent of the spread, and yet the people’s readings on those two axes, drawn as a picture, made a map of Europe, with each country near its place. A small share can still be the part that means something: the kept-share technique counts spread, not meaning.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The axes are the principal components, and finding them is principal component analysis, PCA. The spread grid is the covariance matrix, and the scaled one the correlation matrix. The no-block technique is part of the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices; with the Jordan form of 44·cb27 it says that symmetric matrices have only blocks of size 1. The small-side technique is the reason PCA is computed by the singular value decomposition, or from the Gram matrix XXᵀ, when p is far larger than n. The share kept is the explained variance ratio, and a plot of the stretches in falling order is a scree plot. The tie of 44·bio.5 means the second component is not identifiable.

Who said it first

Karl Pearson found the lines and planes of closest fit to a cloud of points in 1901, and Harold Hotelling named the principal components in 1933. Luca Cavalli-Sforza drew maps of human genetic variation by principal components from the 1970s. The map of Europe from about 3000 people is the 2008 study of John Novembre and colleagues, “Genes mirror geography within Europe”.

A proof that is not allowed

“The first two axes separate the tumours that responded from those that did not, so these axes are what makes a tumour respond.” Not allowed. The axes were found without knowing which tumours responded, and they are the directions of most spread. That they line up with a response is a finding to test on new samples, and spread is not cause (30).
