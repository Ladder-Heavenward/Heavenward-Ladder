29i · technique

Arrows Tangent to a Curved Space

29h built curved spaces from flat pieces glued by an atlas. A space that is flat up close should have flat arrows up close too: the velocities of everything moving through a point. They form a flat space of their own, tangent to the curved one, with as many directions as the space has. This step finds those arrows for a surface given by an equation, using 28f’s implicit functions, and puts one arrow at every point: a field of arrows, like the wind over the globe.

On the surface g = 0 with ∇g ≠ 0, the arrows tangent at p are those with ∇g(p)·v = 0: the velocities of curves in the surface. They form a flat space of dimension one less than the room. A field of arrows chooses one tangent arrow at each point; on the sphere, (−y, x, 0) turns everything about the axis.

29i1Velocities of Curves in the Space

Tangent technique: if a surface is g = 0 with ∇g(p) ≠ 0, the velocities at p of curves lying in the surface are exactly the arrows v with ∇g(p)·v = 0.

\gamma \text{ in the surface: } g(\gamma(t)) = 0 \Rightarrow \nabla g \cdot \gamma^{\prime} = 0 @@ the chain technique (28c)
\text{conversely, near } p \text{ the surface is a graph over the plane } \nabla g \cdot v = 0 @@ implicit functions (28f)
\text{lift the straight line } tv \text{ to the graph: a curve with velocity } v @@ the graph has slope 0 there in the direction of ∇g
\htmlClass{new}{T_p = \{v : \nabla g(p)\cdot v = 0\}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a flat space through p

From here on, Tp is the tangent space at p: all velocities of curves through p in the space. For a space of dimension n, by 29h’s charts, it has dimension n.

29i2A Field of Arrows

\nabla g = (2x, 2y, 2z) @@ the arrow straight out
V = (-y,\ x,\ 0) @@ a choice at every point
\nabla g \cdot V = -2xy + 2xy + 0 = 0 @@ tangent everywhere
\htmlClass{new}{V = 0 \text{ only at the poles}} @@ the axis it turns about

Field technique: a field of arrows on a space chooses, smoothly, one tangent arrow at each point. The turning field above stops at two poles. Every field on a sphere must stop somewhere, a fact held in trust here that 29f’s count of holes explains: you cannot comb a hairy ball flat. On a doughnut, a field can flow everywhere without stopping.

29i3Arrows Without a Room

A space given by an atlas, with no room around it, still has arrows: an arrow at p is a curve through p, up to curves with the same velocity in a chart. Two charts give two coordinate descriptions, and 28b’s best flat map of the change of chart carries one to the other.

\text{chart } u,\ \text{chart } w = \phi(u) @@ a change of chart (29h)
\text{the same curve: } w(t) = \phi(u(t)) @@ two descriptions
w^{\prime}(0) = D\phi\big(u(0)\big)\,u^{\prime}(0) @@ the chain technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the arrow is the same; only its numbers change, by } D\phi} @@ no room needed

An arrow on a curved space is a velocity of a curve, and its numbers in different charts are related by the best flat map of the change of chart. That is how space-time, which sits in no larger room, has arrows: 29·now’s gravitational waves are ripples in fields of them.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

Tp is the tangent space; a field of arrows is a vector field; the combing fact is the hairy ball theorem (Poincaré-Hopf). A space built from charts is a smooth manifold, and its arrows defined through curves are tangent vectors.

Who said it first

Henri Poincaré proved the combing fact for the sphere in 1885 and L. E. J. Brouwer in all dimensions in 1912; Hermann Weyl gave the modern definition of a manifold by charts in 1913.
