29i · technique

Spherical triangles by hand

A navigator on a ship, a pilot over the Pacific and a phone that shows the distance to another city all solve the same small problem. Three points on a globe, joined by great circles, make a triangle, and some of its sides and angles are known while others are wanted. On a flat page the third side of a triangle comes from two sides and the angle between them. On a globe the same question has a new answer, and this step proves it from the dot product of arrows of length one drawn from the centre. Then the north pole becomes a corner of every triangle. With it, the distance between two cities comes straight from their latitudes and longitudes, together with the direction a plane must point as it leaves. A rule for sines follows, and with it the farthest north a great circle route ever climbs.

On a sphere of radius one the third side of a triangle comes from two sides and the angle between them, by a cosine rule with sines and cosines of the sides. With the pole as a corner, it gives the distance and the starting heading between two places from their latitudes and longitudes. A sine rule gives the farthest north a route climbs.

29i1The cosine rule on a sphere

Stands on 27i1 (the angle-between technique), 27i3 (the cross technique), 27m1 (the length-keeper technique), 27m3 (the axis-turn technique) and 29b1 (great circles as lines; the angle between two great circles).

Work on the sphere of radius 1 about O. A point of it is an arrow A from O of length 1. Take a triangle ABC whose sides are arcs of great circles, each shorter than π. Its side a is the arc from B to C, its length the angle at O between B and C; likewise b from A to C and c from A to B. Its angle at A, written A as well, is the angle between the planes of the two great circles through A, which is the angle between their normals (29b1). On a sphere of radius R, multiply each side by R; the angles do not change.

Sphere-cosine technique: in a triangle on the sphere of radius 1, cos a = cos b cos c + sin b sin c cos A.

\cos a = B\cdot C,\ \ \cos b = A\cdot C,\ \ \cos c = A\cdot B @@ the angle-between technique (27i1), with |A| = |B| = |C| = 1
B = \cos c\,A + \sin c\,u,\ \ C = \cos b\,A + \sin b\,w @@ u = (B − cos c A)/sin c has u · A = 0 and |u|² = (1 − cos² c)/sin² c = 1; w likewise
A\times B = \sin c\,(A\times u),\ \ A\times C = \sin b\,(A\times w) @@ the cross is straight in each slot, and A × A = 0 (27i3)
\cos A = (A\times u)\cdot(A\times w) = u\cdot w @@ the normals of the two planes (29b1); x ↦ A × x is the quarter turn about A on arrows at right angles to A (27m3), and a turn keeps dots (27m1)
B\cdot C = \cos b\cos c + \sin b\sin c\,(u\cdot w) @@ multiply out, with A · A = 1 and A · u = A · w = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\cos a = \cos b\cos c + \sin b\sin c\cos A} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above
  
b = c = \tfrac{\pi}{3},\ \ A = \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ two sides of one sixth of a great circle, square to each other
\cos a = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 0 = \tfrac14 @@ the sphere-cosine technique
a = \arccos\tfrac14 \approx 1.318 @@ the arc-is-arccos technique (21m)
\text{flat: } a^2 = b^2 + c^2 = \tfrac{2\pi^2}{9},\ \ a \approx 1.481 @@ the cosine-side technique (21h2) with a right angle
\htmlClass{new}{\text{on the sphere the third side is shorter: } 1.318 < 1.481} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sides bow outward from the centre, and the third side cuts across

For small sides the sphere-cosine technique turns back into the flat one. With cos x close to 1 − x²/2 and sin x close to x (23b), it reads 1 − a²/2 ≈ (1 − b²/2)(1 − c²/2) + bc cos A, and dropping the terms of fourth order leaves a² ≈ b² + c² − 2bc cos A, the cosine-side technique of 21h2. A small triangle on a globe is nearly flat, as 29b4 found from its area.

29i2Distance and heading from latitude and longitude

Stands on 29i1, 21e (the complement technique) and 29b1.

Give a place its latitude φ, north of the equator (south counts as less than 0), and its longitude λ, east of Greenwich (west counts as less than 0). The north pole N, a place P = (φ₁, λ₁) and a place Q = (φ₂, λ₂) make a triangle. Its side NP is π/2 − φ₁, its side NQ is π/2 − φ₂, and its angle at N is the gap in longitude Δλ = λ₂ − λ₁, because the planes of the two half great circles from N, the meridians, meet at that angle along the earth’s axis. The heading of a route at P is its angle with the meridian toward N, measured toward the east or the west.

Great-circle technique: the great circle distance from P to Q on a sphere of radius R is Rδ, where cos δ = sin φ₁ sin φ₂ + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos Δλ; and the route leaves P at the heading h, from north toward Q’s side, with sin φ₂ = sin φ₁ cos δ + cos φ₁ sin δ cos h.

NP = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi_1,\ \ NQ = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi_2,\ \ \angle N = \Delta\lambda @@ the triangle NPQ on the sphere of radius 1
\cos\delta = \cos NP\,\cos NQ + \sin NP\,\sin NQ\,\cos\Delta\lambda @@ the sphere-cosine technique (29i1), side PQ facing N
\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi) = \sin\varphi,\ \ \sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi) = \cos\varphi @@ the complement technique (21e)
\cos\delta = \sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2\cos\Delta\lambda @@ put them in
\cos NQ = \cos NP\,\cos\delta + \sin NP\,\sin\delta\,\cos h @@ the sphere-cosine technique again, side NQ facing P, whose angle is h
\htmlClass{new}{\sin\varphi_2 = \sin\varphi_1\cos\delta + \cos\varphi_1\sin\delta\cos h} \quad \blacksquare @@ the complement technique; then multiply by R for distance
  
P = (49.19^\circ,\ -123.18^\circ),\ \ Q = (35.76^\circ,\ 140.39^\circ) @@ Vancouver airport and Tokyo’s Narita airport
\Delta\lambda = 263.57^\circ,\ \ \cos\Delta\lambda = \cos 96.43^\circ \approx -0.1120 @@ the whole-turns technique (21b): 360° − 263.57° = 96.43°, westward
\cos\delta \approx 0.4423 - 0.0594 \approx 0.3829,\ \ \delta \approx 1.1778 @@ the great-circle technique: sin φ₁ sin φ₂ ≈ 0.4423, cos φ₁ cos φ₂ ≈ 0.5303
R\delta \approx 6371 \times 1.1778 \approx 7504\ \text{km} @@ the earth taken as a sphere of radius 6371 km
\cos h = \tfrac{\sin\varphi_2 - \sin\varphi_1\cos\delta}{\cos\varphi_1\sin\delta} \approx 0.4879,\ \ h \approx 60.8^\circ @@ the second half of the technique, solved for cos h
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about 7500 km, leaving on a heading } 60.8^\circ \text{ west of north}} \quad \blacksquare @@ Tokyo lies south of Vancouver, yet the route sets off to the north west

Tokyo is south of Vancouver, and still the plane turns north as it leaves. A path that kept to one heading would be longer, and a path along a circle of latitude is not a line of the sphere at all (29b5). 29·nav asks why so many routes to Asia pass so far north.

29i3The sine rule, and how far north a route climbs

Stands on 29i1, 27i3 (the cross technique), 27k2 (the expansion technique), 27e1 (the column-slide technique), 21e, 12c (highest and lowest values on a closed interval) and 14a (a greatest value inside is a critical point).

The flat sine rule (21h3) says each side over the sine of the angle it faces is the same for all three. On a sphere the sides are arcs, and they take sines too.

Sphere-sine technique: in a triangle on the sphere of radius 1, sin A / sin a = sin B / sin b = sin C / sin c.

B\times C = \cos c\sin b\,(A\times w) + \sin c\cos b\,(u\times A) + \sin b\sin c\,(u\times w) @@ B and C split as in 29i1; the cross is straight in each slot, and A × A = 0
A\cdot(B\times C) = \sin b\sin c\;A\cdot(u\times w) @@ the cross is at right angles to each of its arrows (27i3), so the first two terms dot A to 0
u\times w = \pm\sin A\;A @@ u and w are at right angles to A, at the angle A to each other; their cross is at right angles to both, so along A, of size sin A (27i3)
|\det[A\ B\ C]| = |A\cdot(B\times C)| = \sin A\,\sin b\,\sin c @@ expand det along its first column (27k2): the stand-ins are the places of B × C
\tfrac{\sin A}{\sin a} = \tfrac{|\det[A\ B\ C]|}{\sin a\,\sin b\,\sin c} @@ divide by sin a sin b sin c
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\sin A}{\sin a} = \tfrac{\sin B}{\sin b} = \tfrac{\sin C}{\sin c}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same lines from B or from C give the same right side: swapping columns only flips the sign of det (27e1)

Farthest-north technique: a great circle route that leaves latitude φ₁ at heading h, not due north or due south, reaches its highest latitude φₘ where cos φₘ = cos φ₁ sin h; there it heads due east or due west.

V \text{ the highest point of the whole great circle} @@ its latitude is continuous on a closed loop, so it has a greatest value (12c, once round the loop)
\text{at } V \text{ the route crosses the meridian at a right angle} @@ at a greatest value the latitude stops rising (14a): the route has no northward part there
\text{triangle } NPV:\ \ NP = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi_1,\ \ NV = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \varphi_m,\ \ \angle P = h,\ \ \angle V = \tfrac{\pi}{2} @@ N the pole, P the start
\tfrac{\sin(\pi/2)}{\sin NP} = \tfrac{\sin h}{\sin NV} @@ the sphere-sine technique: each angle against the side it faces
\htmlClass{new}{\cos\varphi_m = \cos\varphi_1\sin h} \quad \blacksquare @@ the complement technique (21e)
  
\varphi_1 = 49.19^\circ,\ \ h \approx 60.8^\circ,\ \ \sin h \approx 0.8729 @@ from 29i2
\cos\varphi_m \approx 0.6534 \times 0.8729 \approx 0.5704 @@ the farthest-north technique
\htmlClass{new}{\varphi_m \approx 55.2^\circ\ \text{N}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the arc-is-arccos technique (21m): the route crosses the coast of south east Alaska

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The sphere-cosine technique is the spherical law of cosines (for sides), and the sphere-sine technique the spherical law of sines. Working with the sides and angles of triangles on a sphere is spherical trigonometry. The great-circle technique gives the great-circle distance and the initial bearing (or initial course) of the route; the triangle of the pole and two places is the navigational triangle, and with a star in place of the second place it is the astronomical triangle. The farthest-north technique is a case of Clairaut’s relation, cos φ sin h stays the same all along a great circle. The highest point is the vertex of the great circle. A route that keeps one compass heading is a rhumb line (or loxodrome). Navigators long used half of 1 − cos δ, the haversine, because it keeps its digits when δ is small.

Who said it first

Menelaus of Alexandria worked with spherical triangles around 100 CE. Abu al-Wafa al-Buzjani and his contemporaries in Baghdad gave the spherical sine rule in the tenth century. Regiomontanus wrote the cosine rule for sides in On Triangles of All Kinds, finished about 1464 and printed in 1533. Alexis Clairaut found his relation in 1743 for paths on surfaces made by turning a curve about an axis. Pedro Nunes showed in 1537 that a fixed compass heading is not a great circle. James Inman published tables of haversines for seamen in 1835.
