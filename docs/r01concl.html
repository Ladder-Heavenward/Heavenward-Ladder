1·∎ · conclusion

Techniques change and combine statements

Each joining word comes with a way to make a statement with it and a way to use one. Put together, those few techs do all the reasoning, even the job of saying exactly what it would take for a statement to fail.

What this stands on

The survey of rung 1, and its proof steps: 1a (an empty row to work on), 1b (not, and the totally false), 1c (and, or), 1d (if, and exactly when), 1e (every and some). It opens once 1e is ticked, and it gains a line each time you finish a step or annex after it.

The conclusion · the rung's claim, in full, and its proof

This page opens once every question on 1e every and some is ticked. The claim below is proved from the steps of this rung, so it waits for them.

What the rung set out to show

The survey of rung 1 promised that reasoning is a short list of techs on statements, each tech read off a joining word. 1a built supposing: suppose something for a while, reason under it, and keep one conclusion when you drop it. 1b gave “not” and the totally false, with clash, refute, and when nothing is true, everything is permitted. 1c gave “and” and “or”: join, keep either part, add an option, reason by cases.

1d gave “if” and “exactly when”: carry an if through, and prove an if. 1e gave “every” and “some”: speak of everything by speaking of anything, use an every, show an example, use a some. This page lists the techs in one place. Then it makes them work together on the commonest job in reasoning: pushing a “not” inward. Every argument below uses techs from several steps of the rung, and nothing else.

The claim, in full

Each joining word has a tech that makes it and a tech that uses it, and these techs combine: by them alone, “not either” is “neither”, and “no A is B” is “every A is not B”.

Let P and Q be statements, and A and B statements with a gap.

A supposition is kept for a while, and dropping it leaves one conclusion.

Not: refute makes it; clash uses it; from the totally false, any line may be written.

And: join makes it; keep either part uses it.

Or: add an option makes it; reason by cases uses it.

If: prove an if makes it; carry an if through uses it. Exactly when is an if each way, joined.

Every: speak of everything by speaking of anything makes it; use an every uses it.

Some: show an example makes it; use a some, with a fresh name, uses it.

Then ¬(P ∨ Q) and ¬P ∧ ¬Q each give the other.

And ¬∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)) and ∀x (A(x) ⇒ ¬B(x)) each give the other.

No tech goes from ¬¬P to P. That step is left open until rung 7.

The proof

The techs themselves are not proved. They are read off the words, as the survey said, and laid down on 1a to 1e. What is proved is what they do together. Start with “or”. The neither technique of 1c goes from ¬(P ∨ Q) to ¬P ∧ ¬Q. Here is the other way, from techs alone.

Start from ¬P ∧ ¬Q. Suppose, until we drop it, P ∨ Q. Take the cases. Case one: suppose P. Keep left, on what we have: ¬P. Clash: totally false. Case two: suppose Q. Keep right: ¬Q. Clash: totally false. Both cases reach ⊥, so by cases, ⊥. Drop the supposition P ∨ Q, by refute: ¬(P ∨ Q).

With the neither technique, each gives the other. “Not either” and “neither” are the same claim on this rung, in both directions.

Now “some” and “every”, with “and”, “if” and “not” inside them. “No A is B” denies a some. “Every A is not B” is an every around an if. Every word on the rung has a tech in the next two arguments.

Start from ¬∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)): no A is B. Let n be anything. Suppose A(n). While that stands, suppose B(n) as well. Join: A(n) ∧ B(n). Show an example, n: ∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)). That clashes with what we have. Drop the second supposition, by refute: ¬B(n). Drop the first, by prove an if: A(n) ⇒ ¬B(n). Nothing was assumed about n, so speak of everything: ∀x (A(x) ⇒ ¬B(x)).

The other way. Start from ∀x (A(x) ⇒ ¬B(x)): every A is not B. Suppose, until we drop it, ∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)). Use the some: call such a thing b, a fresh name, and suppose A(b) ∧ B(b). Keep left: A(b). Keep right: B(b). Use the every with b: A(b) ⇒ ¬B(b). Carry it through: ¬B(b). That clashes with B(b): totally false. ⊥ does not mention b, so it stands once we are done with b. Drop the supposition, by refute: ¬∃x (A(x) ∧ B(x)).

So “no A is B” and “every A is not B” each give the other. Suppose each argument's given line, run the argument under that supposition, drop it by prove an if, and join the two ifs. The exactly-when that results needs nothing given at all: it is always so, and a technique from nothing may write it anywhere.

The last part of the claim is a fact about the list, and you can check it by reading the list. Refute only ever concludes a statement that begins with ¬. Clash needs a statement and its denial. Everything permitted needs ⊥. None of them turns ¬¬P into P, and the not-not-in technique of 1b goes only from P to ¬¬P. The same gap stops “not both” from becoming “one fails”, and “not every” from becoming “some fails”. Rung 7 is where it is taken up.

What the rest of the rung adds

Each step and annex after the proof steps adds a line to the claim. A line appears here once you have finished its page.

  • 1f tables: Gives each joining word a table and checks that every tech keeps a row true, so everything the techs earn is valid by the tables. The one place the two part company is the gap this claim leaves open: by the tables P follows from ¬¬P, yet no tech earns it.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 1g rewriting by the rulebook: Turns the techniques into a rulebook for rewriting, each entry with a name. The pushes proved here are entries in it (not-through for or, both ways), and its licence column marks which directions wait for rung 7.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 1h every and some by hand: Drills every and some at speed. “No A is B” and “every A is not B” are two readings of one of the four oldest sentences of logic, and the page sets the four in a diagram of how they oppose one another.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 1i proof shapes: Reads a proof's shape off the goal's outermost mark. The arguments on this page are those shapes: a not opens with a supposition to refute, an every with “let n be anything”, an if with a supposition.its line appears when you finish it.
  • 1·comp what does a computer check when it reads "if"?: It shows that a machine settles a condition by working the one row it finds, and that stopping early is about the order a machine reads in, which techniques about statements leave open.its line appears when you finish it.
Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

1e set beside the techs a second kind of care, the kind that measures instead of proving: say whether a statement is about each one, some one, or the crowd as a whole.

The techs of this claim speak of members one at a time: every is each one, some is at least one. A statement about the crowd as a whole is a third size, and the techs of every and some do not carry it down to one member, or up from one. “The flock is heading south” is not “every bird is heading south”, and no argument turns one into the other.

That is a first handful of techs, and you have all of them now. Take them slowly; you are just getting started.
