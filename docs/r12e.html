12e · technique

Bisection by hand

12a proved that a continuous function that changes sign on a closed interval has a zero there. It did not say where. Bisection finds out. Keep the zero trapped between two inputs whose values have opposite signs. Test the middle, throw away the half that cannot be relied on, and repeat. It is slow, it never fails on a trap, and on a polynomial with whole-number coefficients it needs nothing but whole-number multiplication. By the end of this step you will have the square root and the cube root of 2 to three decimal places, by hand. You will have a way to say in advance how many tests any accuracy costs. And you will have two proofs: that a polynomial of odd degree always has a zero, and that a continuous function takes intervals to intervals.

Keep a closed interval whose ends have values of opposite sign; test the middle; keep the half whose ends still disagree. Each test halves the trap, so ten tests shrink it about a thousand times.

12e1The trap

From here on, a trap for f is a closed interval [c, d] on which f is continuous, with f(c) and f(d) of opposite signs: one negative and the other positive. By 12a's in-between-value a trap holds a zero of f strictly inside it. To bisect a trap is to test its middle m = (c + d)/2. Then keep [c, m] or [m, d], whichever is still a trap. The method is called bisection. Bisection is from the Latin bi-, “two”, and secare, “to cut”.

The middle is strictly inside: c < (c + d)/2 < d, by 5f's another-between read in ℝ. It is 8h's middle of the stretch from c to d. And f is continuous on each half. Every input of a half is an input of [c, d]. At the cut m, f is continuous (m is inside [c, d]), so from each side.

Middle-of-a-trap technique: for a trap [c, d] with middle m, exactly one is true: f(m) = 0; or [c, m] is a trap; or [m, d] is a trap. Three-way-order on ℝ splits f(m) three ways. If f(m) has the sign of f(d), then f(c) and f(m) disagree and [c, m] is a trap. If it has the sign of f(c), then [m, d] is. ∎ So only the sign of f(m) matters, never its size. The tempting illegal move is to keep the half whose middle value is nearer 0. Nearness of a value to 0 says nothing about where the zero is, and the sign says everything.

12e3To three decimal places

From here on, 5h's rounding and its mark ≈ apply to any positive real x, not only to rationals. To round to m places, take the number k with k/10^m ≤ x < (k + 1)/10^m. It exists. Some number passes 10^m · x (counting-passes-any-real, 8e), and the least number that passes it is k + 1 (least-member-for-any-set, 7·cb4). The rest of 5h's line is unchanged, and x ≈ b is still a statement, as on 5h: it says that b is x rounded to as many places as b shows.

After ten halvings the trap is 1/1024 wide, less than 0.001. Is that √2 to three places, in 5h's sense of rounding? Not yet. The trap runs from 1.4140625 to 1.4150390625, and it holds 1.4145, the boundary between rounding down to 1.414 and up to 1.415. Width alone does not settle a rounding: the trap must also be clear of the boundary. Two more halvings would do it (the trap becomes 1.4140625 to 1.414306640625). Or test the boundary itself.

1.414^2 = 1.999396 \lt 2 @@ so √2 > 1.414 (powers-keep-order, 8f)
1.4145^2 = 2.00081025 \gt 2 @@ so √2 < 1.4145
\htmlClass{new}{1.414 \lt \sqrt2 \lt 1.4145} @@ every real here rounds to 1.414: √2 ≈ 1.414 (5h's rounding, for reals)

That last move is licensed because a split point need not be the middle. Any input strictly inside a trap splits it into two closed intervals, and by three-way-order on ℝ one of them is a trap or the split point is a zero. The middle is merely the choice that guarantees the trap halves.

12e4Cube roots: split where the arithmetic is short

12e5How fast the trap closes

Halvings-width technique: starting from a trap [v, w], if no tested middle is a zero, the trap after n halvings has width (w − v)/2ⁿ. By induction on n. At the start the width is w − v = (w − v)/2⁰ (power-zero). Each halving takes a width w to w/2, which is (w − v)/2^{S(n)} by power-of-a-next and 5h's fractions-of-rationals. ∎

So to get the width below a tolerance t you need 2ⁿ > (w − v)/t. No new function is needed to find n: double until you pass.

[1, 2],\ t = 0.001:\ \ 2^n \gt \tfrac{1}{0.001} = 1000 @@ what to pass
2^9 = 512,\ \ \htmlClass{new}{2^{10} = 1024} @@ double until you pass 1000: n = 10
t = 0.000001:\ \ 2^n \gt 10^6 @@ six places
2^{20} = (2^{10})^2 = 1\,048\,576 \gt 10^6 @@ power-of-a-power (4g); n = 20, since 2¹⁹ = 524 288 falls short
\text{width } 3,\ t = 0.01:\ \ 2^n \gt 300 @@ 2⁸ = 256 falls short, 2⁹ = 512 passes: n = 9

Because 2¹⁰ = 1024 is just over a thousand, ten halvings buy about three decimal places, every time. If you report the middle of the final trap, the zero is within half the width of it, so one halving fewer puts the middle within t. Run it backwards and the same line tells you where you started: if twelve halvings left a trap 0.0025 wide, it began 0.0025 · 4096 = 10.24 wide.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the cloud, trapped

8·cb5 gave √2's decimals a width, 8·phys gave a measurement one, 8·eng added widths on purpose, and 9·stat watched a histogram's bins change the shape it showed. That is a principle of the second kind of care: when a thing can't be pinned to a dot, give the stretch it stays inside and say how wide. The trap is that stretch, built on purpose. After ten halvings the zero of x² − 2 is somewhere in a stretch 1/1024 wide, and nowhere else.

Be rigorous about the trap, not the dot.

The width is yours to choose. Ten halvings buy three places and twenty buy six. What you cannot choose is width 0: no number of halvings reports √2 as a dot. 12a promised the middle is there. The trap says where, to the width you paid for. Written 8·eng's way, the answer is the middle of the trap, ± half its width.

A calculator shows √2 as 1.414213562. How wide is the trap it is reporting, and how would you check that √2 is really inside it?

12e6The choices are bits

Write 0 for “keep the left half” and 1 for “keep the right half”. Then the choices are 4i's bits, and the trap can be read off them.

Trap-from-its-bits technique: the choices, read as a numeral in twos, say exactly where the trap is. Precisely: start from [v, w], and make choices e₁, e₂, ⋯, e_n. Let k be the value of the bits e₁e₂⋯e_n read in twos by 4i's reading rule. (0s in front are allowed, as 5h allowed them for decimal places.) Then the trap is [v + k(w − v)/2ⁿ, v + (k + 1)(w − v)/2ⁿ]. By induction: the reading rule's step takes k to 2k + e_{S(n)}. Choosing left keeps [v + 2k(w − v)/2^{S(n)}, v + (2k + 1)(w − v)/2^{S(n)}], which is the left half. Choosing right adds 1. ∎

\text{√2's choices: } 0, 1, 1, 0, 1 @@ left, right, right, left, right
01101_2 = 13 @@ 4i's reading rule in twos: 8 + 4 + 1
[1 + \tfrac{13}{32},\ 1 + \tfrac{14}{32}] = [\tfrac{45}{32}, \tfrac{46}{32}] @@ trap-from-its-bits with v = 1, w − v = 1: the trap after five tests
\text{ten choices } 0110101000:\ \ k = 424 @@ [1 + 424/1024, 1 + 425/1024] = [181/128, 1449/1024]: the table's last trap

So bisection on [1, 2] spells √2 out one bit per test. After n tests, √2 − 1 lies between k/2ⁿ and (k + 1)/2ⁿ, with k the first n bits read in twos. That is much as 5h's division place by place spelled a fraction out in tens. The bits are a list, read by 4i's rule; no new mark is needed.

12e7Why the trap always holds a zero

12a proved a trap holds a zero by a least upper bound. Bisection gives a second proof: the traps close on a single real, and it is a zero. It is the proof put in a note to a course of lectures of 1821, and the idea behind the 1817 paper's own.

Halving-traps technique: let [v, w] be a trap with f(v) < 0 < f(w), and bisect it again and again. Either some middle is a zero, or the left ends c_n and right ends d_n close on a real z with f(z) = 0. (Here c_n and d_n are terms of sequences, 9·cb4's low marks, and z is a letter naming a real.)

f(c_n) \lt 0 \lt f(d_n),\ \ c_n \le c_{S(n)} \lt d_{S(n)} \le d_n @@ by induction with middle-of-a-trap: keep the half whose left end is negative
z = \htmlClass{new}{\sup\{c_n : n \in \mathbb N\}} @@ no-holes (8c): every c_n is below d₀ = w
c_n \le z \le d_n \text{ for every } n @@ z an upper bound; each d_n is an upper bound (c_k ≤ d_k ≤ d_n or c_k ≤ c_n < d_n), and z is least
|c_n - z|,\ |d_n - z| \le d_n - c_n = \tfrac{w - v}{2^n} \le \tfrac{w - v}{S(n)} @@ halvings-width; 2ⁿ ≥ S(n) by induction
c_n \to z,\ \ d_n \to z @@ the squeeze (10d), in 10f's form for sequences, with (w − v)/S(n) → 0 by one-over-n and constant-factor
f(z) \gt 0 \Rightarrow \text{a window of } z \text{ where } f \gt 0 \text{ holds some } c_n:\ \bot @@ sign-kept-near-an-input; the window's reach as a gap for c_n → z
f(z) \lt 0 \Rightarrow \text{a window where } f \lt 0 \text{ holds some } d_n:\ \bot @@ sign-kept-near-an-input's negative form
f(z) = 0 \quad \blacksquare @@ three-way-order on ℝ; the remaining-option technique twice

Notice the one line that needs rung 8 again: z = sup of the left ends. Run it over the fractions, on x² − 2. The left ends climb 1, 5/4, 11/8, 45/32, 181/128, ⋯ and the right ends fall, and they close on the hole of 12d. There is no fraction for them to close on. Bisection, like the sup argument, needs a line with no holes.

12e8A polynomial of odd degree has a zero

9g's leading-term said the leading term decides the large inputs, and said that rung 12 would show an odd-degree polynomial must have a zero. Here it is, with 10f's words.

Odd-degree-passes-bounds technique: if p has odd degree n and leading coefficient u_n > 0, then p(x) passes every bound as x → ∞ and passes below every bound as x → −∞.

|x| \ge M_0 \Rightarrow |p(x) - u_n x^n| \le \tfrac12 |u_n x^n| @@ leading-term (9g), its M written M₀ here because M names a greatest value in this step: M₀ = max(1, 2C/|u_n|)
x \ge M_0:\ \ p(x) \ge \tfrac12 u_n x^n \ge \tfrac12 u_n x @@ unpack-the-bars; xⁿ ≥ x past 1 (9e)
\text{given } K:\ N = \max(M_0, \tfrac{2|K|}{u_n}),\ \ x \gt N \Rightarrow p(x) \gt |K| \ge K @@ 10f's line “passes every bound as x → ∞”, with threshold N
x \le -M_0:\ \ x^n = -|x|^n,\ \ p(x) \le \tfrac12 u_n x^n \le -\tfrac12 u_n |x| @@ n odd: even-or-odd-powers (9e); unpack-the-bars the other way
\text{given } K:\ x \lt -N \Rightarrow p(x) \lt -|K| \le K \quad \blacksquare @@ passes below every bound as x → −∞

Odd-degree-zero technique: every polynomial of odd degree has a zero. If its leading coefficient is negative, take −p, which has the same zeros. By odd-degree-passes-bounds with the bound 0, some N₁ has p(x) > 0 for every x > N₁, and some N₂ has p(x) < 0 for every x < N₂. Let c = min(N₁, N₂) − 1 and d = max(N₁, N₂) + 1. Then c < d, p(c) < 0 < p(d), p is continuous on [c, d] (polynomials-are-continuous), and in-between-value gives a zero. ∎ 9g's promise is kept.

p(x) = x^3 - x - 1:\ \ p(1) = -1,\ \ p(2) = 5 @@ a trap, found by trying whole numbers, with no need for N
\tfrac32:\ 0.875 \gt 0;\ \ \tfrac54:\ -0.296875 \lt 0;\ \ \tfrac{11}{8}:\ 0.2246\cdots \gt 0 @@ left, right, left
\tfrac{21}{16} \lt 0;\ \ \tfrac{43}{32} \gt 0;\ \ \tfrac{85}{64} \gt 0;\ \ \tfrac{169}{128} \lt 0 @@ right, left, left, right
[\tfrac{169}{128}, \tfrac{85}{64}] = [1.3203125,\ 1.328125] @@ the zero is 1.3247⋯; ten tests give [1.32421875, 1.3251953125]

Even degree promises nothing. x² + 1 is at least 1 everywhere (square-never-negative), with no zero. x⁴ − 5x² + 4 has four, at −2, −1, 1 and 2. The step of odd-degree-zero that needs “odd” is the sign of xⁿ for negative x.

12e9Intervals to intervals

Closed-to-closed technique: a continuous function takes a closed interval to a closed interval, or to a single real. Precisely: let f be continuous on [v, w], with least value m and greatest value M (12c's highest-and-lowest). Then the set of its values {f(x) : x ∈ [v, w]} is [m, M] when m < M, and {m} when m = M.

\text{call } c, d \in [v, w]:\ f(c) = M,\ f(d) = m @@ highest-and-lowest (12c)
x \in [v, w] \Rightarrow m \le f(x) \le M @@ the values lie in [m, M]
\begin{aligned}&m \lt y \lt M \Rightarrow \text{some } x \text{ strictly}\\ &\quad\text{between } c \text{ and } d \text{ has } f(x) = y\end{aligned} @@ c ≠ d since m < M; three-way-order on ℝ orders them; in-between-value, general form (12a) on the closed interval between them
y = m \text{ at } d,\ \ y = M \text{ at } c @@ the ends are reached
\{f(x) : x \in [v, w]\} = [m, M] \quad \blacksquare @@ chase a member both ways; same-members

On any interval I, closed or not, the same in-between-value, general form gives the property that matters. If y₁ and y₂ are values of a function continuous on I, every real between them is a value too, because the closed interval between the two inputs lies inside I. No-gaps technique: for f continuous on an interval I, if f(x₁) < y < f(x₂) with x₁, x₂ ∈ I, then y is a value of f on I. ∎ And a set of reals with no gaps is an interval. The gap-free-sets technique: a set S of reals with two members, such that every real between two members is a member, is one of 8g's intervals. Suppose S is bounded below and above, and let α = inf S and β = sup S (greatest-lower-bound, no-holes). Every y with α < y < β has a member below it (y is not a lower bound) and a member above it (not an upper bound), so y ∈ S. Hence (α, β) ⊆ S ⊆ [α, β]. The either-way principle's four cases on α ∈ S and β ∈ S give (α, β), [α, β), (α, β] or [α, β]. A side with no bound gives ∞ on that side the same way. ∎ So a continuous function takes an interval to an interval, or to a single real.

x^2 \text{ on } [-1, 2] \;\to\; [0, 4] @@ least 0 at 0, greatest 4 at 2 (closed-to-closed)
x^2 \text{ on } (-1, 2) \;\to\; [0, 4) @@ an open interval to one that is neither: 0 reached, 4 not (no-gaps, gap-free-sets)
\tfrac1x \text{ on } (0, 1] \;\to\; [1, \infty) @@ passes every bound as x → 0⁺: an interval with no right end
\lfloor x \rfloor \text{ on } [0, 2] \;\to\; \{0, 1, 2\} @@ not an interval: jumps leave gaps. Continuity is needed

12e10When the trap fails

No sign change to start: x² − ¼ on [−1, 1] is positive at both ends and has two zeros, ±½. Equal signs at the ends tell you nothing; split first until the signs disagree. A zero that touches without crossing: (x − 1)² is 0 at 1 and positive elsewhere, so no trap ever holds it. A discontinuity inside: 1/x on [−1, 2] has values −1 and ½ at the ends. Bisection will close happily on 0, where there is an infinite discontinuity (11f) and no zero. The hypothesis “continuous on [c, d]” was false. 11f's hunt for discontinuities comes before any bisection.

The oldest geometry book that has come down whole proves a fact behind every halving. From the greater of two unequal sizes, take away more than its half; from the remainder, more than its half; and so on. Some remainder will be less than the lesser size. Halving again and again gets below any size you name.

12e12Running it backwards

Handed a trap and a list of choices, read the final trap off the bits (trap-from-its-bits). Handed a final trap and its width, double back to the starting width. Handed an accuracy, double until 2ⁿ passes (w − v)/t. Handed an equation of odd degree, say at once that it has an answer, then find two whole numbers whose values disagree in sign and start halving. Handed an interval and a continuous function, find its least and greatest values and say the set of values is the closed interval between them. Found upward; written downward.

Who said it first

The note of 1821 is in Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s Cours d’analyse; the 1817 paper is Bernard Bolzano’s. In question 12e.4.4 the two sides are L. E. J. Brouwer and David Hilbert.

Euclid proves that if from the greater of two unequal magnitudes more than its half is taken away, and from the remainder more than its half, and so on, some remainder will be less than the lesser magnitude. Halving again and again gets below any size you name.

Euclid, Elements X.1, after Heath (1908)

Accepted names: the halving-traps technique is the nested interval property, used as a proof of the intermediate value theorem; the either-way principle is the law of excluded middle; halving a trap again and again is bisection.
