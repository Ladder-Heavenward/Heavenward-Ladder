Why Does the Power Grid Swing Back and Forth?

Why does the power grid swing back and forth? The voltage at a wall socket is not steady: it swings from plus to minus and back fifty or sixty times a second. Coils and capacitors answer it out of step, and a motor turns because three such swings chase each other round. Rung 26’s eiθ turns all of this into arithmetic. This page writes a swinging voltage as a turning arrow, finds each part’s complex resistance, measures the power that really arrives, shows why power lines run at huge voltages, and adds three swings to get nothing.

Write V₀cos(ωt) as the real part of V₀e^(iωt). A resistor, a coil and a capacitor answer with complex resistances R, iωL and 1/(iωC); in a line of parts they add, and I = V/Z. The power that arrives on average is ½V₀I₀cos φ, φ the angle between voltage and current. Loss in a line grows as the current squared, so send power at high voltage. Three swings a third of a turn apart add to 0.

26eng1A Swing as a Turning Arrow

Terry’s workshop takes power from the grid. Its voltage swings as V(t) = V₀ cos(ωt), with V₀ about 325 volts and ω = 2π · 50 a second. Rung 26 writes cos as the shadow of a turning arrow.

e^{i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + i\sin(\omega t) @@ e to an imaginary power (26a)
V(t) = \mathrm{Re}\,(V_0 e^{i\omega t}) @@ the real part, the arrow’s shadow
\dfrac{d}{dt} e^{i\omega t} = i\omega\, e^{i\omega t} @@ a complex slope (26d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{taking the slope is multiplying by } i\omega} @@ a quarter turn, stretched by ω

From here on, on this page, a voltage or current that swings at ω is named by the complex number it multiplies eiωt by: its size is the swing’s height and its angle is how far ahead the swing runs.

26eng2Complex Resistance

A resistor obeys V = RI. A coil pushes back against change, V = L I′. A capacitor stores charge, I = C V′. With every swing a multiple of eiωt, each becomes V = Z I for a complex number Z.

\text{resistor: } Z = R @@ in step
\text{coil: } V = L\,(i\omega I) \Rightarrow Z = i\omega L @@ the voltage a quarter turn ahead
\text{capacitor: } I = C\,(i\omega V) \Rightarrow Z = \dfrac{1}{i\omega C} @@ a quarter turn behind
\htmlClass{new}{\text{parts in a line: } Z = Z_1 + Z_2 + \cdots,\ \ I = V/Z} @@ as resistances add

Arrow technique: for swings at one ω, every part is a complex resistance, they add in a line as resistances do, and the current is the voltage divided by their sum. A machine with R = 30 ohms and a coil with ωL = 40 ohms has Z = 30 + 40i.

26eng3The Power That Really Arrives

Power at each instant is V(t) · I(t). With the current behind by φ, average it over one swing.

V_0 I_0 \cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega t - \varphi) @@ power at each instant
= \tfrac12 V_0 I_0\,[\cos\varphi + \cos(2\omega t - \varphi)] @@ the sum formulas (21d)
\text{the second part averages to } 0 \text{ over a swing} @@ a full turn of cos (21)
\htmlClass{new}{P = \tfrac12 V_0 I_0 \cos\varphi = \tfrac12 \cdot 325 \cdot 6.5 \cdot 0.6 \approx 634 \text{ W}} @@ and ½I₀²R = 634 too

Power technique: the average power is half the voltage height times the current height times cos φ; the coil’s share swings in and out and arrives nowhere. The wires must still carry the full 6.5 A, so grids charge large works for a poor cos φ, and fit capacitors to cancel the coils.

26eng4Why the Lines Run at Huge Voltages

A power line of resistance Rline wastes I²Rline as heat. To send a power P at voltage V takes a current about P/V.

\text{loss} = I^2 R_{\text{line}} = \dfrac{P^2 R_{\text{line}}}{V^2} @@ I = P/V
V = 10\,000:\ I = 100,\ \text{loss} = 50\,000 \text{ W} @@ a twentieth lost
V = 100\,000:\ I = 10,\ \text{loss} = 500 \text{ W} @@ a two-thousandth
\htmlClass{new}{\text{ten times the voltage, a hundredth the loss}} @@ the loss falls as 1/V²

Line loss falls as the square of the voltage, so power is sent at high voltage and stepped down near its use. A transformer, two coils on one iron ring, does the stepping, and it works only on swinging voltages: that is why the grid swings.

26eng5Three Swings That Add to Nothing

Large grids send three swings on three wires, each a third of a turn behind the last. Write w = e2πi/3, a root of x³ = 1 other than 1 (25).

w^3 = 1,\ w \ne 1 @@ a third of a turn, three times
(w - 1)(1 + w + w^2) = w^3 - 1 = 0 @@ factor (5i)
1 + w + w^2 = 0 @@ no zero product (5e): w − 1 ≠ 0
\htmlClass{new}{V_0 e^{i\omega t}(1 + w + w^2) = 0} @@ the three voltages add to 0 at every instant

Three-phase technique: three equal swings a third of a turn apart add to zero, so with balanced loads the return wire carries nothing, and the power they deliver together is steady. Fed to three coils set round a ring, they make a pull that turns steadily round the ring, and a motor follows it.

26eng7Storing Power, and the Full-Output Share

A grid must make, each moment, just what is used that moment. Sun and wind give power when they give it. Two numbers measure the gap: how much of the year a plant works as if at full power, and how much stored energy carries the rest.

From here on, on this page, the full-output share of a plant is the energy it delivers in a year over what it would deliver at its rated power every hour of the year.

\dfrac{300\,000 \text{ MWh}}{100 \text{ MW} \times 8\,760 \text{ h}} \approx 0.34 @@ the wind farm’s share
\text{sun at a share of } 0.2:\ 100 / 0.2 = 500 \text{ MW of panels for an average of } 100 @@ 
\text{14 dark hours} \times 100 \text{ MW} = 1\,400 \text{ MWh stored} @@ and about 1.15 times that charged, losing a seventh in and out
\htmlClass{new}{\text{rated power says what a plant can do; the share says what it does}} @@

Full-output technique: divide the year’s energy by rated power times 8,760 hours; to give steady power from a source with share s, build 1/s times the power and store enough energy for the hours it gives none. Shown on a grid’s yearly record: each plant’s delivered energy over its rating times the hours gives a share, and wind farms on land come out near a third, panels in a sunny place near a fifth.

26eng8How Much Power a Line Can Carry

Two ends of a long line each hold a voltage that turns as an arrow (26eng1), the sending end ahead of the receiving end by an angle δ. The line acts mostly as a coil, a complex resistance iX (26eng2).

I = \dfrac{V_1 e^{i\delta} - V_2}{iX} @@ the gap between the arrows, over the line
P = \dfrac{V_1 V_2}{X}\,\sin\delta @@ the part of the power that really arrives (26eng3)
\text{most at } \delta = \tfrac{\pi}{2}:\ P_{\max} = \dfrac{V_1 V_2}{X} @@ a quarter turn
\htmlClass{new}{\text{asked for more, the ends slip out of step}} @@ past the top, more angle carries less

Line-limit technique: a line carries V₁V₂ sin δ/X, so it can never carry more than V₁V₂/X, at a quarter turn; it is run well below, so a sudden extra load only widens δ instead of tipping it over the top. Doubling the voltage carries four times the power on the same line, which is why long lines run at the highest voltages (26eng4).

Accepted names

A swing written as a complex number is a phasor, and the complex resistance is the impedance, with iωL and 1/(iωC) its reactance. cos φ is the power factor, and fitting capacitors to raise it is power-factor correction. Swinging current is alternating current (AC), steady current direct current (DC), and 325 volts at the peak is 230 volts rms. Three swings a third of a turn apart are three-phase power, and the motor that follows them an induction motor.

Who said it first

Nikola Tesla patented the motor run by several phases in 1888. Charles Proteus Steinmetz showed in 1893 how complex numbers turn swinging circuits into arithmetic.
