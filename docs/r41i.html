41i · proof

The Unavoidable

Seattle has more than seven hundred thousand people, and nobody has more than about a hundred and fifty thousand hairs on their head. So at least five people in Seattle have exactly the same number of hairs, and no one needs to count a single head to know it. Some facts are forced by counting alone, before anything is measured. Rung 8 used the simplest of them to prove that every fraction’s digits repeat: more letters than boxes puts two letters in one box. This step sharpens it and puts it to work on a row of numbers, where it forces a long run that only rises or only falls. Then it turns to counting what is left when some things are struck out, by adding back what was struck out twice, taking away what was added back too often, and so on. That gives the chance that when every guest at a dinner takes a coat at random, nobody gets their own: about 37 in 100, nearly the same for ten guests as for ten thousand, and the number behind it is e.

If more than n times k things go into n boxes, some box gets more than k. So a row of n² + 1 different numbers holds a rising run or a falling run of n + 1. To count what lies in none of several sets, take away the sizes, add back the overlaps of two, take away those of three, and on. The arrangements that move everything are n! times the sum of (−1)ᵏ/k!, the nearest whole number to n!/e.

41i1A Crowded Box

8·cb5’s pigeonhole technique put S(q) things into q boxes and found two in one. The count can be pushed.

Crowded-box technique: if more than n · k things go into n boxes, some box gets more than k. If every box got at most k, the boxes would hold at most n · k in all, by 4k’s separate-piles technique. With k = 1 it is the pigeonhole technique.

\text{boxes: } 0, 1, \dots, 150\,000 \text{ hairs; } n = 150\,001 @@ an upper bound, generous, on hairs on one head
\text{people: } 737\,015 \text{ in Seattle in 2020} @@ the census count
737\,015 > 4 \cdot 150\,001 = 600\,004 @@ k = 4
\htmlClass{new}{\text{some five people have exactly the same number of hairs}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the crowded-box technique

Rise-or-fall technique: any row of n² + 1 different numbers holds n + 1 of them, in their order in the row, that rise, or n + 1 that fall.

L(i) = \text{the length of the longest rising run starting at place } i @@ a label for each of the n² + 1 places
\text{if some } L(i) \ge n + 1 \text{, done; else every } L(i) \in \{1, \dots, n\} @@ suppose not
n^2 + 1 > n \cdot n:\ \text{some } n + 1 \text{ places share a label} @@ the crowded-box technique, k = n
i < j \text{ with } L(i) = L(j):\ \text{if } r_i < r_j \text{ then } L(i) \ge L(j) + 1 @@ put rᵢ in front of the longest rising run from j
\htmlClass{new}{\text{so } r_i > r_j:\ \text{the } n + 1 \text{ places with one label fall}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the numbers differ

41i2Adding Back What Was Struck Twice

How many of the numbers 1 to 100 are divisible by none of 2, 3 and 5? Strike out the 50 even ones, the 33 multiples of 3, the 20 multiples of 5. The multiples of 6, 10 and 15 were struck twice, and the multiples of 30 three times.

Over-and-under technique: the number of members of U lying in none of A₁, …, Aₘ is |U| minus the sizes of the Aᵢ, plus the sizes of the overlaps of two, minus those of three, and so on to the overlap of all m.

\text{a member of } U \text{ in none of the } A_i:\ \text{counted once, by } |U|, \text{ and nowhere else} @@ it lies in no overlap
\text{a member in exactly } r \ge 1 \text{ of them: counted } 1 - \binom{r}{1} + \binom{r}{2} - \cdots \pm \binom{r}{r} \text{ times} @@ it lies in the overlaps of every j of its r sets, and of no others (choosing-counts-parts, 4k)
= (1 - 1)^r = 0 @@ the two-term-power technique (4k)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the total counts exactly the members in none of them}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 
  
100 - (50 + 33 + 20) + (16 + 10 + 6) - 3 @@ multiples of 2, 3, 5; of 6, 10, 15; of 30, counted by dividing and rounding down

41i3Nobody Gets Their Own Coat

An arrangement of {1, …, n}, a shuffle in 19’s word, moves everything when no i is sent to i. Let Dₙ count them. With Aᵢ the arrangements keeping i where it is, an overlap of j of the Aᵢ keeps j places and arranges the other n − j freely.

Wrong-coats technique: Dₙ = n!(1 − 1/1! + 1/2! − ⋯ + (−1)ⁿ/n!), and for n ≥ 1 it is the whole number nearest n!/e.

D_n = \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n} (-1)^j \binom{n}{j} (n - j)! @@ the over-and-under technique; an overlap of j has (n − j)! members (4k)
= n! \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!} @@ n choose j times (n − j)! is n!/j!
e^{-1} = \textstyle\sum_{j \ge 0} \frac{(-1)^j}{j!} @@ the exp-as-a-sum technique (22j) at −1
\big|\tfrac{n!}{e} - D_n\big| < \tfrac{n!}{(n + 1)!} = \tfrac{1}{n + 1} \le \tfrac12 @@ the alternating bracket (22g): the error is below the first term left out
\htmlClass{new}{D_4 = 9,\ \ D_5 = 44,\ \ D_{10} = 1\,334\,961} \quad \blacksquare @@ the nearest whole numbers to 8.83, 44.15, 1 334 960.9

So the chance that n guests taking coats at random all get the wrong one is Dₙ/n!, which heads to 1/e ≈ 0.3679, and is within 1/(n + 1)! of it. For ten guests it is 0.367879, already the same to six places.

The accepted names

The crowded-box technique is the generalized pigeonhole principle. The rise-or-fall technique is the Erdős–Szekeres theorem. The over-and-under technique is the inclusion–exclusion principle. Arrangements that move everything are derangements, and Dₙ is the subfactorial, written !n; the coats are the hat-check problem, or the problème des rencontres.

Who said it first

Pierre Rémond de Montmort counted derangements in 1708, for a card game, and Nicolaus Bernoulli and Leonhard Euler found the formula and its link with e. Abraham de Moivre wrote inclusion–exclusion in 1718; James Joseph Sylvester gave it its general form in 1883. Paul Erdős and George Szekeres proved the rise-or-fall technique in 1935, in the paper that also began 41j. The hair count goes back at least to a book of 1622 by Jean Leurechon, who made the argument for Paris.

A proof that is not allowed

“The chance that guest i gets their own coat is 1/n, so the chance that nobody does is (1 − 1/n)ⁿ, which heads to 1/e.” Not allowed: the n events are not independent (if every guest but one has their own coat, so does the last). The answer, 1/e, is right in the limit and wrong for every n: for n = 4 the product is 0.316 and the true chance 9/24 = 0.375.
