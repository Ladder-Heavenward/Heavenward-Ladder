When Do Local Answers Glue?

When do local answers glue? Rung 29 built a surface from pages that agree where they overlap, and rung 34 glued spaces from pieces. Ask the same of answers. Give each open piece of a space the set of answers that make sense on it, with a way to cut an answer down to a smaller piece. Sometimes answers on pieces that agree on every overlap come from exactly one answer on the whole, and sometimes they do not. Continuous functions glue; bounded ones do not. Rung 41’s tables of readings on two photons agree on every overlap and still have no joint answer: that is the limit on a plan of answers, said as a failure to glue. This reading of the qubit experiments was set out in 2011. Whether failure to glue is what makes quantum machines faster is open.

Data on open sets: a set F(U) for each open U, and a cutting-down map F(U) → F(V) whenever V ⊆ U, with two cuts the same as one. F glues when, for a cover by pieces Uᵢ, answers sᵢ that agree on each overlap Uᵢ ∩ Uⱼ come from exactly one answer on the whole. Continuous functions glue; bounded functions do not. Four tables of readings that agree on each shared question but have no joint table are answers that do not glue; round a loop of overlaps the failure can be counted.

inf17.1Pieces and Answers

The open sets of a space X, with an arrow V → U exactly when V ⊆ U, are a category by 47a2: “inside” is an order. To each open set U assign a set F(U), the answers on U. For example, F(U) is the set of continuous functions from U to the line, where U is a space of its own by 34b5.

An answer on U can be cut down to a smaller V: a function on U is cut down to V by forgetting its values outside V. Write the cut-down of s as s|V. The cut goes from the bigger set to the smaller, against the arrow V → U, so F is a map of categories that turns arrows round, as 47b3 named them.

Two-cuts technique: for W ⊆ V ⊆ U and an answer s on U, (s|V)|W = s|W: cutting in two steps is cutting at once.

x \in W \Rightarrow x \in V @@ W ⊆ V
(s|_V)|_W\,(x) = s|_V\,(x) = s(x) @@ each cut keeps the values it does not forget
s|_W\,(x) = s(x) @@ the same
\htmlClass{new}{(s|_V)|_W = s|_W} \quad \blacksquare @@ two functions on W with the same value at every point

That is the turned-round technique of 47b3, (g ∘ f)* = f* ∘ g*, for the arrows W → V → U. Data on open sets with cutting down is all the rest of the page needs.

inf17.2The Gluing Technique

Cover an open set U by open pieces Uᵢ. Answers sᵢ on the pieces agree on overlaps when sᵢ|Uᵢ ∩ Uⱼ = sⱼ|Uᵢ ∩ Uⱼ for every i and j. The data glues when, for every cover, answers that agree on overlaps come from exactly one answer s on U, with s|Uᵢ = sᵢ for each i. Two demands: at least one s, and at most one.

An atlas is the first case. 29h2’s pages show a surface piece by piece, and the change of page is the agreement on an overlap; 29h3’s page-agree technique says that lengths and angles measured on overlapping pages agree, so they are one length or angle on the surface.

Continuous-glue technique: continuous functions glue: continuous sᵢ on Uᵢ that agree on overlaps come from exactly one continuous s on U.

s(x) = s_i(x) \text{ for } x \in U_i @@ define s piece by piece
x \in U_i \cap U_j \Rightarrow s_i(x) = s_j(x) @@ agreement on the overlap: s is well defined
s^{-1}(O) = \textstyle\bigcup_i s_i^{-1}(O) \text{ for } O \text{ open} @@ x lands in O exactly when its piece’s answer does
s_i^{-1}(O) \text{ open in } U_i,\ U_i \text{ open in } U \Rightarrow s_i^{-1}(O) \text{ open in } U @@ continuity of sᵢ (34b2); an open part of an open part
t|_{U_i} = s_i \text{ for all } i \Rightarrow t(x) = s(x) \text{ at every } x @@ every point is in some piece
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one and only one continuous } s \text{ with } s|_{U_i} = s_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ a union of open sets is open (34b2)

inf17.3Bounded Ones Do Not

Let F(U) be the bounded functions on U, those whose values all lie between some −M and M. Cutting down keeps a function bounded, so F is data on open sets. Cover the line by the pieces Uₙ = (n − 1, n + 1), one for each whole number n, and on each take sₙ(x) = x. On Uₙ its values lie between n − 1 and n + 1, so sₙ is bounded there, and the sₙ agree on overlaps.

Bounded-fails technique: the bounded functions do not glue: answers that agree on overlaps can have no glued answer.

s|_{U_n} = s_n \text{ for every } n \Rightarrow s(x) = x \text{ at every } x @@ every x lies in some Uₙ
s(n) = n \text{ for every whole } n @@ no M bounds them all
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no bounded } s \text{ has } s|_{U_n} = s_n} \quad \blacksquare @@ at most one glued answer, and it is not bounded

“At most one” held; “at least one” failed. Being continuous is decided near each point, so it survives gluing. Being bounded is decided on the whole, so it does not. The constant functions fail too: on two pieces that do not meet, 0 on one and 1 on the other agree on the empty overlap, and no constant gives both. The functions that are constant near each point do glue.

inf17.4A Table With No Joint Answer

Two labs each choose one of two questions: a or a′ in the first, b or b′ in the second, each answered +1 or −1. The pieces are the four pairs that can be asked together: {a, b}, {a, b′}, {a′, b}, {a′, b′}. An answer on a piece is a list of the outcomes that can happen there. Two pieces overlap in one question, and their answers agree there when they allow the same outcomes for that question. A joint answer is a plan of 41·phys.3: one value each for a, a′, b and b′, allowed on every piece.

Take this table. On {a, b}, {a, b′} and {a′, b} the two outcomes are equal, +1 +1 or −1 −1; on {a′, b′} they are opposite, +1 −1 or −1 +1. On every piece each question can come out +1 or −1, so the answers agree on every overlap.

No-joint technique: this table agrees on every overlap and has no joint answer.

a = b,\ a = b^{\prime},\ a^{\prime} = b @@ the three pieces of equal outcomes
a^{\prime} = b = a = b^{\prime} @@ chain the equalities
a^{\prime} = -b^{\prime} @@ the fourth piece
\htmlClass{new}{a^{\prime} = -a^{\prime}\text{, impossible for } \pm 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ no plan is allowed on all four pieces

This table needs no chances, and S, the sum of 41·phys.3, is 4 for it. No pair of qubits gives it: the most a quantum pair reaches is 2√2. But the qubit tables fail in the same way with chances. On each piece put the quantum chances of Φ at 41·phys.3’s angles. They agree on every overlap, because each lab’s chances are ½ and ½ whatever the other lab asks, which is 41·phys.4’s no-message condition. A joint table of chances would be a plan, and a plan keeps S between −2 and 2; the quantum S is 2√2. So the no-message condition is agreement on overlaps, and the broken bound is a failure to glue.

inf17.5Counting Failures Round a Loop

Arrange the four questions in a loop, a, b, a′, b′ and back to a, with each piece the step between two neighbours. On each step the table says same or opposite; write +1 for same and −1 for opposite. A plan gives each question a value, and the step’s sign is the product of its two ends.

Loop-sign technique: a plan can fit the steps only if the product of the signs round the loop is +1.

t_{ab}\, t_{ba^{\prime}}\, t_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\, t_{b^{\prime}a} = (ab)(ba^{\prime})(a^{\prime}b^{\prime})(b^{\prime}a) @@ each step’s sign is the product of its ends
\phantom{t_{ab}\cdots} = a^2\, b^2\, a^{\prime 2}\, b^{\prime 2} @@ each question appears at two steps
\htmlClass{new}{= +1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the square of ±1 is 1

The table of the last footing has signs +1, +1, −1, +1 round the loop: product −1, so no plan. The same count runs round a circle. Cover a circle by two arcs whose overlap is two pieces, and glue a copy of the pair {+1, −1} over each arc, with the copies matched on each overlap piece by same or opposite. Relabelling one arc’s copy flips both matchings and leaves the product alone. So there are exactly two ways to glue, product +1 or −1: a band, and a band with a twist, whose edge goes round twice before it closes. The −1 cannot be cut away on any piece; it lives on the loop, as 34g4’s winding number does.

∞Ξ.oq.1Is failure to glue what makes quantum machines faster? A 2014 paper showed that, for one leading design of a fault-tolerant quantum machine built from systems with an odd prime number of levels, the extra states it needs to outrun an ordinary machine must have tables that fail to glue. Whether failure to glue is needed for every design, and whether its size measures the speed-up, is open.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The idea of data on open sets that glues was set out in the 1940s, while its inventor was held as a prisoner of war, in work on equations and the shapes of spaces. It later became a standard tool of the geometry of equations, where a space is studied through the answers it carries.

The bound of 1964 on plans of answers, met on rung 41, was not first stated in this language. In 2011 two researchers recast them: the pieces are the sets of questions that can be asked together, the answers are tables of outcomes, the no-message condition is agreement on overlaps, and the failure of any plan is the failure to glue. Techniques that had been proved one at a time came out of one frame.

The frame showed how to count. In 2012 the sign round a loop of overlaps was given its general form, a count of twists borrowed from the study of shapes; it catches many failures and was shown not to catch every one. In 2014 a paper tied failure to glue to the states a leading design for a quantum machine needs to outrun an ordinary one.

The accepted names

Data on open sets with cutting down is a presheaf, and cutting down is restriction; data that glues is a sheaf, and the glued answer is a global section. The two demands are gluing and locality. The functions constant near each point form the locally constant sheaf. The table with no joint answer is the Popescu–Rohrlich box; the quantum bound 2√2 is Tsirelson’s bound. A table that agrees on overlaps is no-signalling; one with no global section is contextual, and the four-question case is non-local. The loop-sign count is a class in the first Čech cohomology; the twisted band is the Möbius band.

Who said it first
