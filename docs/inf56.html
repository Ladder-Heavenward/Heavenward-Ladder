Which Whole Numbers Are the Area of a Right Triangle With Fraction Sides?

Which whole numbers are the area of a right triangle with fraction sides? The triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 has a right angle and area 6, so 6 is one. Halve and stretch the sides cleverly and 5 turns up, with sides three halves, twenty thirds and forty-one sixths, and 7 too, with sides that need sixtieths. But 1 is not, nor 2, nor 3, and proving that a number is not is harder than finding a triangle when it is. A manuscript of the tenth century already asked the question, a book of 1225 showed that 5 is such an area, and in the seventeenth century a proof that 1 is not was written in the margin of a book: if there were one triangle there would be a smaller one, and a smaller, without end. In the twentieth century the question changed shape. A number is such an area exactly when a certain curve has a point with fraction coordinates off its axis, and the points of such a curve can be added to each other, so one triangle makes endlessly many. In 1922 it was shown that all the points come from finitely many. In 1983 a test was found that decides the question by counting the ways to write a number as a sum of squares with fixed weights, and the test is proved in one direction; the other direction rests on a question about curves, posed in the 1960s, that carries a prize of a million dollars and is still open. This page turns triangles into points and back, proves that 1 is not an area, proves how points add, runs the counting test on the first few numbers, and states the prize question in the language of the primes.

A right triangle with fraction sides p, q, r and area n gives the point (nq/(r − p), 2n²/(r − p)) on y² = x³ − n²x, with y ≠ 0, and every such point gives one back. No whole right triangle has a square area, by descent on x⁴ − y⁴ = z², so 1 is not an area. The chord through two points of fraction coordinates meets the curve again at fraction coordinates, so the points add, and 3, 4, 5 doubles to 7/10, 120/7, 1201/70, another triangle of area 6. Counting x² + 2y² + 8z² = n against x² + 2y² + 32z² = n decides odd n, if the prize question holds.

inf56.1The Question

18a4’s right-triangle technique says that legs p and q and hypotenuse r make a right angle exactly when p² + q² = r². Call a whole number n an area number when some right triangle with fraction sides has area n, that is pq/2 = n.

3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2,\quad \tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 6 @@ 6 is an area number
\left(\tfrac32\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{20}3\right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{4} + \tfrac{400}{9} = \tfrac{1681}{36} = \left(\tfrac{41}6\right)^2,\quad \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac{20}3 = 5 @@ 5 is
\htmlClass{new}{\left(\tfrac{24}5\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{35}{12}\right)^2 = \left(\tfrac{337}{60}\right)^2,\quad \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{24}5 \cdot \tfrac{35}{12} = 7} \quad \blacksquare @@ 7 is; checked with exact fractions by script

Square-scale technique: for a whole k ≥ 1, n is an area number exactly when nk² is. Stretch every side by k and the area is multiplied by k²; shrink by k and it is divided by k². So 24 = 6 · 2² is one (6, 8, 10), and only numbers with no square factor above 1 need asking about: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15, …

Finding a triangle proves a number is an area number. Nothing yet proves one is not. Trying fractions with bottoms up to a million and finding nothing for 1 shows only that the triangle, if there is one, has large bottoms.

inf56.2From Triangle to Curve

44·crypt.1 took a curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2 on a prime clock. Take instead y² = x³ − n²x, with x and y fractions.

Area-to-curve technique: if p² + q² = r² and pq = 2n, with p, q, r fractions and r ≠ p, then x = nq/(r − p), y = 2n²/(r − p) lies on y² = x³ − n²x with y ≠ 0. If (x, y) lies on it with y ≠ 0, then p = (x² − n²)/y, q = 2nx/y, r = (x² + n²)/y satisfy p² + q² = r² and pq = 2n.

x^3 - n^2 x = x(x^2 - n^2) = \frac{nq}{r - p} \cdot \frac{n^2 q^2 - n^2 (r - p)^2}{(r - p)^2} @@ put x in
q^2 - (r - p)^2 = (r - p)(r + p) - (r - p)^2 = 2p\,(r - p) @@ since q² = r² − p²
x^3 - n^2 x = \frac{2n^3 pq}{(r - p)^2} = \frac{4n^4}{(r - p)^2} @@ since pq = 2n
\htmlClass{new}{= y^2,\ \ y \ne 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ y = 2n²/(r − p)
  
p^2 + q^2 = \frac{(x^2 - n^2)^2 + 4n^2x^2}{y^2} = \frac{(x^2 + n^2)^2}{y^2} = r^2 @@ multiply out the squares
pq = \frac{2n\,x(x^2 - n^2)}{y^2} = \frac{2n\,(x^3 - n^2 x)}{y^2} @@ multiply out
\htmlClass{new}{= 2n} \quad \blacksquare @@ the point is on the curve
  
n = 6:\ (3, 4, 5) \to (12, 36);\quad 12^3 - 36 \cdot 12 = 1296 = 36^2 @@ and back to 3, 4, 5
n = 5:\ \left(\tfrac32, \tfrac{20}3, \tfrac{41}6\right) \to \left(\tfrac{25}4, \tfrac{75}8\right) @@ checked by script
\htmlClass{new}{n = 7:\ \left(\tfrac{24}5, \tfrac{35}{12}, \tfrac{337}{60}\right) \to (25, 120)} \quad \blacksquare @@ 15625 − 1225 = 14400 = 120²

A point with y = 0 gives no triangle: those are (0, 0), (n, 0) and (−n, 0), the zeros of x(x − n)(x + n). Negative sides from a point are made positive by turning the signs of p and q together, which keeps pq = 2n. So n is an area number exactly when the curve has a fraction point off the axis, and the question about triangles has become a question about a curve.

inf56.3Whole Triangles

To prove that 1 is not an area number, clear the bottoms. If fraction sides p, q, r have area 1 and d is a common bottom, then dp, dq, dr are whole and the area is d²: a whole right triangle with a square area. So it is enough to show no whole right triangle has a square area. First, every whole right triangle.

Call p, q, r with p² + q² = r² primitive when no prime divides all three. Both p and q odd is impossible, since then p² + q² leaves 2 on the clock of 4 and no square does; so one is even.

Triple-maker technique: if p² + q² = r² is primitive with q even, then p = m² − k², q = 2mk, r = m² + k² for whole m > k > 0 sharing no prime, one even and one odd.

\left(\tfrac q2\right)^2 = \tfrac{r - p}{2} \cdot \tfrac{r + p}{2} @@ q² = r² − p², with p and r odd
\text{a prime dividing both factors divides their sum } r \text{ and difference } p @@ 4j’s divides-a-sum and divides-the-rest
\text{then it divides } q^2 = r^2 - p^2, \text{ and so } q @@ 4j14; this is ruled out by primitive
\text{each prime of the product appears to an even power, all in one factor} @@ 4j18, one prime list
\tfrac{r + p}{2} = m^2,\ \ \tfrac{r - p}{2} = k^2 @@ so each factor is a square
\htmlClass{new}{p = m^2 - k^2,\ q = 2mk,\ r = m^2 + k^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ add and subtract; r odd makes m, k of opposite parity

Example: m = 2, k = 1 gives 3, 4, 5; m = 3, k = 2 gives 5, 12, 13; m = 4, k = 1 gives 15, 8, 17. The area of the primitive triangle is mk(m² − k²) = mk(m − k)(m + k), and these four share no prime in pairs. So if the area is a square, each of m, k, m − k, m + k is a square, by 4j18 again. With m = X² and k = Y², the leg m² − k² = X⁴ − Y⁴ is also (m − k)(m + k), a product of two squares, so it is a square z². A stretched triangle has area k² times a primitive one, so the primitive one has a square area too. Everything now hangs on one equation.

inf56.4One Is Not

7f4’s endless-descent and 4j19’s no-double-square both took a least solution and made a smaller one. The same shape works here.

Fourth-powers technique: there are no whole x, y, z, all above 0, with x⁴ − y⁴ = z². No-square-area technique: so no whole right triangle has a square area, and 1 is not an area number.

\text{take a solution with } x \text{ least} @@ 4h, least member
\text{a prime } \ell \text{ dividing } x, y \text{ gives } (x/\ell,\, y/\ell,\, z/\ell^2) @@ ℓ⁴ divides z², so ℓ² divides z; smaller, so none

A script found no solution with x below 300 and no primitive triangle with m below 40 whose area is a square, which agrees and proves nothing; the descent proves it for every size. inf56.5Adding Points

44·crypt.2 added points of a curve on a clock: draw the line through P and Q, find the third zero R of the cubic it cuts out, and flip it; P + Q is (x_R, −y_R), with the far point O as the do-nothing. Nothing there used the clock except to do arithmetic, so it works with fractions too.

Fraction-chord technique: if P and Q lie on y² = x³ − n²x with fraction coordinates, then so does P + Q, with λ = (y_Q − y_P)/(x_Q − x_P), or λ = (3x_P² − n²)/(2y_P) when Q = P, and x = λ² − x_P − x_Q, y = λ(x_P − x) − y_P.

y = y_P + \lambda (t - x_P) \text{ put into } y^2 = t^3 - n^2 t @@ the line, with λ a fraction
t^3 - \lambda^2 t^2 + \cdots = 0,\ \text{zeros } x_P,\ x_Q,\ x_R @@ 44c3, factors for the two known zeros
x_P + x_Q + x_R = \lambda^2 @@ 9g’s sum of zeros: minus the next-to-top coefficient
\htmlClass{new}{x_R = \lambda^2 - x_P - x_Q \text{ is a fraction, and so is } y} \quad \blacksquare @@ only adding, taking, multiplying, dividing

Order-two technique: a point P ≠ O has P + P = O exactly when its y is 0; on y² = x³ − n²x these are (0, 0), (n, 0) and (−n, 0). P + P = O means P is its own flip, (x, y) = (x, −y), so y = 0; and y = 0 means x(x − n)(x + n) = 0. P + Q = Q + P since the line through P and Q is the line through Q and P. That the adding regroups, (P + Q) + S = P + (Q + S), is true and is held in trust: its proof by algebra runs to pages, and a script checked it on the points below.

P = (12, 36):\ \lambda = \tfrac{3 \cdot 144 - 36}{72} = \tfrac{11}{2} @@ the tangent at P
x = \tfrac{121}{4} - 24 = \tfrac{25}4,\ \ y = \tfrac{11}2\left(12 - \tfrac{25}4\right) - 36 = -\tfrac{35}8 @@ 2P = (25/4, −35/8)
p = \tfrac{(25/4)^2 - 36}{35/8} = \tfrac{7}{10},\ \ q = \tfrac{2 \cdot 6 \cdot 25/4}{35/8} = \tfrac{120}{7} @@ inf56.2 back, signs turned
\htmlClass{new}{\left(\tfrac7{10}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{120}7\right)^2 = \left(\tfrac{1201}{70}\right)^2,\ \ \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac7{10} \cdot \tfrac{120}7 = 6} \quad \blacksquare @@ a second triangle

Doubling again gives a point whose x has a bottom of 19600, and the triangle after that has sides with bottoms of six, seven and twelve digits. A point that is not one of the four of the order-two technique never returns to O, so one triangle gives endlessly many. The statement of 1922: every fraction point of such a curve is a whole combination of finitely many, a list called the makers. It is stated here, not proved. For these curves the only points that return to O are O and the three on the axis (held in trust), so n is an area number exactly when at least one maker never returns: the count of such makers is the rank, and n is an area number exactly when the rank is at least 1.

The points of the curve with fraction coordinates are, by ∞ר‎.5, the maps from the curve’s ring to ℚ; a map from ℚ into a larger field carries them along, so a fraction point stays a point there. Adding points uses only sums, products and division of coordinates, and so is the same in every field. The plan is the ring of the curve; the arrangement of each triangle’s sides is let go, since one curve carries every triangle of area n.

inf56.6A Test by Counting

In 1983 a test was found. For an odd n with no square factor, count the whole solutions of two equations; for an even one, of two others.

Stated (the 1983 test): let A(n) and B(n) count the whole (x, y, z) with x² + 2y² + 8z² = n and x² + 2y² + 32z² = n, and C(n), D(n) those with x² + 4y² + 8z² = n/2 and x² + 4y² + 32z² = n/2. If an odd n is an area number, then A(n) = 2B(n); if an even n is, then C(n) = 2D(n). If the prize question of inf56.7 holds, the converses hold too, and the test decides every n.

n = 1:\ A = 2\ (x = \pm 1),\ B = 2 @@ 2 ≠ 4: not an area number, proved
n = 2:\ C = 2,\ D = 2 @@ not, proved
n = 3:\ A = 4\ (x, y = \pm 1),\ B = 4 @@ not, proved
n = 5:\ A = 0,\ B = 0 @@ 0 = 0: consistent; the triangle of inf56.1 proves it
n = 6:\ C = 0,\ D = 0;\ \ n = 7:\ A = 0,\ B = 0 @@ consistent; triangles known
\htmlClass{new}{n = 41:\ A = 32,\ B = 16} \quad \blacksquare @@ 32 = 2 · 16: the test says yes, if the prize question holds

The direction that is proved is the one that says no: whenever A(n) ≠ 2B(n), n is not an area number, with no triangle searched for and no descent. The direction that says yes would need the curve to have a point of infinite order, and nothing in the counting builds one. The test itself is fast: loop over y and z and ask whether what is left is a square, about n steps in all.

inf56.7The Prize Question

44·now.5 counted the points of a curve on the clock of each prime ℓ and wrote the count as ℓ + 1 − a_ℓ. Here is a guess from the 1960s, made by counting on an early computer. If a curve has many fraction points, it should have more points than usual on most clocks too, since each fraction point lands somewhere on each clock. So multiply N_ℓ/ℓ, the count over its usual size, over the primes up to some bound.

n = 1:\ 1.69;\quad n = 2:\ 1.27;\quad n = 3:\ 0.98 @@ rank 0: the product wanders
n = 5:\ 9.04;\quad n = 6:\ 12.34;\quad n = 7:\ 7.41 @@ rank 1: the product grows
\htmlClass{new}{\text{growing like } (\ln x)^{\text{rank}}\,?} \quad \blacksquare @@ the guess, read from cases

Put the counts into a product over primes as 43·now2.3 put 1/ℓˢ into ζ: L(s) is the product over primes of 1/(1 − a_ℓ ℓ⁻ˢ + ℓ¹⁻²ˢ). At s = 1 each factor is ℓ/N_ℓ, so L(1) is, roughly, the reciprocal of the product above. The product converges only for larger s, and carrying L to s = 1 is the hard part.

Stated, the prize question (1960s): the rank of the curve equals the order of the zero of L(s) at s = 1. For n an area number it predicts L(1) = 0. The 1983 test is exactly a formula for whether L(1) = 0, written as counts of sums of squares. Three results since 2000. In 2001 the last cases were proved of the statement that every such curve’s list a_ℓ is the list of a modular form (44·now.5), so L can be carried to s = 1 for every curve. From 2010 on it was shown that, ordering curves by size, the average rank is at most 1.5, and soon below 1. And in 2014 the prize question was proved for a positive share of all curves, in fact more than half.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞4.oq.1Is the prize question true? It says that a count read from all the clocks at once, the order of the zero of L at 1, equals the number of independent fraction points. It is proved when that order is 0 or 1, and for a positive share of curves; nobody can prove it for a curve whose L has a zero of order 2 or more, and it is one of the seven problems of 2000 with a prize of a million dollars.
∞4.oq.2Is the rank bounded? Curves of rank at least 28 were found in 2006, at least 29 in 2024, and at least 30 and then at least 31 in August 2026, and at least 32 later in 2026, as the standard table of records lists it (one source). A heuristic of 2016, published in 2019, predicts that only finitely many curves have rank above 21. Whether ranks go on without end is open.
∞4.oq.3Is every number that leaves 5, 6 or 7 on the clock of 8 an area number? 5, 6 and 7 are. If the prize question holds, all of them are, because for these numbers the order of the zero of L at 1 is odd, and an odd order is not 0. Without it, infinitely many have been proved to be area numbers in each of the three classes, but not all.
∞4.oq.4Can the converse of the 1983 test be proved without the prize question? For n with A(n) = 2B(n), a triangle must exist, but nobody knows how to build its point from the counts. The points the curve must then have can be enormous, so a search is no substitute.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Right triangles with whole sides were listed on a clay tablet in Babylon (18a4), and the question of which areas they could have is old. A manuscript of the tenth century asks for a right triangle with fraction sides and a given area. In 1225 a book on squares showed that 5 is such an area, with the triangle of inf56.1, and that the problem is the same as finding a square that stays a square when n is added and when n is taken away.

In the seventeenth century a note in the margin of an old book gave the descent of inf56.4: no whole right triangle has a square area. It settles 1.

In the twentieth century the question was seen as one about the curve y² = x³ − n²x. In 1922 the points of such curves were shown to come from finitely many makers; but the proof gives no way to find the makers, or even to count them. In the 1960s, on an early computer at Cambridge, two mathematicians counted points on many curves over many clocks and guessed the link to the zero of L at 1. For most curves the guess could not yet even be checked, since L was not known to reach s = 1.

In 1983 the counting test of inf56.6 was proved in one direction, using modular forms. In 1995 to 2001 the link was proved for every curve over the fractions, the same work that settled the claim of 44·now. In 2000 the prize question became one of seven problems with a prize of a million dollars, offered by a private institute.

Since 2010 the subject has moved from single curves to all of them at once. Counting how many curves of a given size have points of each kind gave bounds on the average rank and a proof of the prize question for most curves, work recognised with the field’s highest medal in 2014. For area numbers themselves, it was proved in the 2010s that infinitely many numbers in each of the classes 5, 6, 7 on the clock of 8 are area numbers. What is argued now is whether ranks are bounded: a heuristic says yes, while record curves of ever higher rank keep being found by large computer searches, at least 29 in 2024 and at least 31 in 2026.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

An area number is a congruent number, and the question is the congruent number problem. The square-scale technique says that congruence depends only on the square-free part. The curve y² = x³ − n²x is the congruent number curve, an elliptic curve, a quadratic twist of y² = x³ − x; the area-to-curve technique is the standard bijection between rational right triangles of area n and rational points with y ≠ 0. The triple-maker technique is the parametrisation of primitive Pythagorean triples, the fourth-powers technique is Fermat’s proof that x⁴ − y⁴ = z² has no nontrivial solution, and the no-square-area technique is Fermat’s right triangle theorem, proved by infinite descent. The fraction-chord technique is the chord-and-tangent group law; the points that return to O are the torsion subgroup, here ℤ/2 × ℤ/2; the statement of 1922 is Mordell’s theorem (the Mordell–Weil theorem, with Weil’s 1929 extension); the makers are generators and their count the rank. The test of inf56.6 is Tunnell’s theorem. L(s) is the Hasse–Weil L-function of the curve; the prize question is the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, in its weak form; the 2001 result is the modularity theorem; the odd order for 5, 6, 7 mod 8 is the root number −1 and the parity conjecture.

Who said it first

Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) treated the problem in the Liber quadratorum of 1225 and found the triangle for 5. Pierre de Fermat’s descent for x⁴ − y⁴ = z² survives as a marginal note in his copy of Diophantus. Louis Mordell proved the finite generation in 1922. Bryan Birch and Peter Swinnerton-Dyer made their computations on the EDSAC at Cambridge and published their conjecture in the 1960s. Jerrold Tunnell published his criterion in 1983. Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond and Richard Taylor completed the modularity theorem in 2001, building on Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor (1995). The Clay Mathematics Institute named the conjecture a Millennium Prize Problem in 2000. Manjul Bhargava and Arul Shankar bounded the average rank (2010 on), and Bhargava, Christopher Skinner and Wei Zhang proved the conjecture for more than half of all curves (2014); Bhargava received the Fields Medal in 2014. Ye Tian proved in 2014 that each class 5, 6, 7 mod 8 holds infinitely many congruent numbers. Noam Elkies found a curve of rank at least 28 in 2006, and Elkies and Zev Klagsbrun one of rank at least 29 in 2024. Levent Alpöge and Ava Howell, working with an AI model, reported curves of rank at least 30 and at least 31 in August 2026. Jennifer Park, Bjorn Poonen, John Voight and Melanie Matchett Wood gave the heuristic for bounded ranks.
