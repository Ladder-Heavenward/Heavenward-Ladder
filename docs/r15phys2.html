When Does a Chain Reaction Run Away?

When does a chain reaction run away? When a heavy nucleus of uranium splits, it frees two or three neutrons, and each can split another nucleus, which frees more. Whether the chain dies out, holds steady or runs away depends on one number: how many of those neutrons go on to cause another split. This page counts neutrons by generations, adds them up with 15i’s geometric sums, finds that below one the total settles and above one it grows without end, and finds why a reactor can be steered at all: a small share of neutrons come late.

Let k be the number of new splits each split causes. Generation n has kⁿ splits. The total over all generations is 1 + k + k² + …, which settles to 1/(1 − k) when k < 1 and grows without end when k > 1. A reactor runs at k = 1, and holds there because a few neutrons arrive seconds late.

15phys21Each Split Frees Neutrons

A nucleus of uranium-235 that absorbs a slow neutron splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and, on average, about 2.4 new neutrons. Some of these escape the fuel, some are absorbed without causing a split, and the rest each split another nucleus.

From here on, on this page, k is the average number of new splits caused by the neutrons from one split. It depends on the fuel, its shape and size, and what surrounds it, not on the uranium alone.

If 2.4 neutrons are freed and 40% of them cause another split, k = 0.96.

15phys22The Generations

Start with one split. Its neutrons cause k splits on average; those cause k² ; and so on.

\text{generation } 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n:\ 1,\ k,\ k^2, \ldots, k^n @@ each multiplies by k
\text{total through generation } n:\ 1 + k + \cdots + k^n @@ 
\htmlClass{new}{= \dfrac{1 - k^{n+1}}{1 - k}\quad (k \ne 1)} @@ geometric sums (15i)

15phys23Below One, the Total Settles

When k < 1, kⁿ⁺¹ shrinks toward 0 as n grows, so the total closes in on 1/(1 − k). One split starts a chain that dies out after a finite total.

\text{total} \to \dfrac{1}{1 - 0.96} = 25 @@ one split leads to about 25 in all
\text{a source of } S \text{ new neutrons each generation:}\ \ S \cdot 25 @@ each starts its own dying chain
\htmlClass{new}{k \to 1^{-}:\ \dfrac{1}{1 - k} \to \text{as large as you like}} @@ close to 1, a big but finite total

A pile with k below 1 multiplies any neutrons fed into it by 1/(1 − k), and stays steady; the closer k is to 1, the bigger the multiplication. Builders of the first reactor read this number as they added layers: the count from their neutron detectors climbed faster with each layer as k neared 1, telling them how close they were before they got there.

15phys24Above One, It Grows Without End

When k > 1, kⁿ grows past any bound: each generation is bigger than the last. How fast depends on how long a generation takes. In a reactor, neutrons from a split cause the next split in about a ten-thousandth of a second.

\text{generations per second} = 10\,000 @@ 
1.001^{10\,000} \approx 22\,000 @@ by repeated squaring, or a calculator
\htmlClass{new}{\text{twenty-two thousand times the splits each second}} @@ a tenth of a percent over 1

Multiplication technique: a chain with k splits per split, starting from one, has kⁿ splits in generation n and a total that settles to 1/(1 − k) if k is below 1 and grows without end if k is above 1. At k only 0.1% above 1, a reactor would run away far faster than any control rod could move.

15phys25Steering Near One: Why a Few Late Neutrons Matter

Not every neutron comes at once. About 0.65% of the neutrons from uranium-235 splits come from the split pieces themselves, seconds later. If k exceeds 1 by less than 0.0065, the chain cannot grow on the prompt neutrons alone: each generation has to wait for the late ones.

\text{prompt neutrons alone: } 0.9935 \cdot 1.001 \approx 0.9945 < 1 @@ below one without the late ones
\text{the waiting makes a generation about } 0.1 \text{ s} @@ set by the late neutrons
1.001^{10} \approx 1.01 \text{ each second} @@ ten generations a second
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 1\% \text{ a second instead of } 22\,000 \text{ times}} @@ slow enough to steer

A reactor is kept just below the point where the prompt neutrons alone would keep the chain going; the late neutrons make up the rest, and make each generation slow enough for control rods to follow. A reactor operator nudges k by moving rods that absorb neutrons, watching the power climb or fall by a percent or so a second. Bombs are built to do the opposite: to exceed 1 on prompt neutrons alone.

Accepted names

k is the effective multiplication factor; k = 1 is critical, below is subcritical, above is supercritical. The late neutrons are delayed neutrons, their share β ≈ 0.0065 the delayed neutron fraction; exceeding 1 on prompt neutrons alone is prompt criticality. Multiplication by 1/(1 − k) is subcritical multiplication.

Who said it first

Leo Szilard conceived the neutron chain reaction in 1933. Enrico Fermi’s team brought the first reactor, Chicago Pile-1, to a slightly supercritical k, about 1.0006, on 2 December 1942.
