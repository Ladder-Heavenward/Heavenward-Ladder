15i · technique

Geometric Sums

15h found the area under each power up to the cube, each from its own sum of powers, and the sums get harder as the power grows. Around 1640 a way was found to do every power at once: the shrinking cut. Instead of equal pieces, it cuts the interval at points that shrink by a fixed factor toward 0, so that the boxes' areas shrink by a fixed factor too. A sum whose terms are each a fixed multiple of the one before has a short formula, the first in this step. The second asks what such a sum becomes when it never stops.

Multiply a geometric sum by one less its factor, and it collapses to two terms. When the factor is smaller than one in size, its powers head to 0, and the endless sum has a total. Cut the interval at points that shrink by a fixed factor, and the boxes under any power form a geometric sum. Its total, as the factor heads to 1, is the area.

15i1The Finite Sum

The finite-ratio-sum technique (the geometric sum, first met at 9h): for r ≠ 1 and whole n ≥ 1, Σᵢ₌₀ⁿ⁻¹ rⁱ = 1 + r + ⋯ + rⁿ⁻¹ = (1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r). Its accepted name is the sum of a finite geometric series; r is its ratio.

(1 - r)\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r^i = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (r^i - r^{i+1}) @@ sums-are-linear, spreading (spread on ℝ)
= 1 - r^n @@ collapsing-sum (15g), with uᵢ = −rⁱ: a sum of differences collapses
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} r^i = \tfrac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by 1 − r ≠ 0 (positive-reciprocal)
  
1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 512 = \tfrac{1 - 2^{10}}{1 - 2} = 1023 @@ r = 2, n = 10
1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac{1}{16} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{4^{n-1}} = \tfrac43\big(1 - \tfrac{1}{4^n}\big) @@ r = ¼

15i2When rⁿ Shrinks

From here on, a list s₁, s₂, s₃, … heads to L when for every ε > 0 there is N such that every n ≥ N has |sₙ − L| < ε. This is 10f's far out, read on the whole numbers. Accepted names: the sequence converges to L, written lim sₙ = L; a list that heads to no number diverges.

Power-of-one-plus technique: for h ≥ 0 and whole n, (1 + h)ⁿ ≥ 1 + nh. By induction. n = 0 gives 1 ≥ 1. And (1 + h)ⁿ⁺¹ ≥ (1 + nh)(1 + h) = 1 + (n + 1)h + nh² ≥ 1 + (n + 1)h, by order-and-arithmetic-agree with 1 + h > 0 and nh² ≥ 0. Its accepted name is Bernoulli's inequality.

Small-powers-vanish technique: if |r| < 1, then rⁿ heads to 0.

r = 0: \text{ every } r^n = 0 \text{ for } n \ge 1 @@ nothing to show
0 \lt |r| \lt 1:\ \ \tfrac{1}{|r|} = 1 + h,\ \ h \gt 0 @@ positive-reciprocal; order-and-arithmetic-agree
\tfrac{1}{|r|^n} = (1 + h)^n \ge 1 + nh \gt nh @@ power-of-one-plus
|r^n - 0| = |r|^n \lt \tfrac{1}{nh} @@ positive-reciprocal, reciprocals turn the order about for positives
\text{fix } \varepsilon;\ \text{call } N:\ \tfrac1N \lt \varepsilon h @@ everyday-counting-passes (8e)
n \ge N \;\Rightarrow\; |r^n| \lt \tfrac{1}{nh} \le \tfrac{1}{Nh} \lt \varepsilon \quad \blacksquare @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree, twice

15i3The Endless Sum

From here on, for a list u₀, u₁, u₂, …, the endless sum Σᵢ₌₀^∞ uᵢ is the number its partial sums sₙ = Σᵢ₌₀ⁿ⁻¹ uᵢ head to, when they head to one. Accepted names: a series; its partial sums; the series converges, and its sum is that number, or it diverges. The mark Σᵢ₌₀^∞ uᵢ is a name, for that number, when there is one.

Endless-ratio-sum technique: if |r| < 1, then Σᵢ₌₀^∞ c rⁱ = c/(1 − r). If |r| ≥ 1 and c ≠ 0, the series diverges.

s_n = c\tfrac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} @@ finite-ratio-sum, sums-are-linear
\Big|s_n - \tfrac{c}{1 - r}\Big| = \tfrac{|c|}{|1 - r|}\,|r|^n @@ the difference is −c rⁿ/(1 − r)
\text{heads to } 0 @@ small-powers-vanish, scaled by a constant
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} c r^i = \tfrac{c}{1 - r}} @@ ︿ the endless sum
|r| \ge 1:\ \ |c r^n| \ge |c| \text{ for every } n @@ powers-keep-order: so sₙ₊₁ − sₙ never shrinks below |c|, and the sₙ cannot settle within |c|/2 of any number

15i4Decimals and an Old Remainder

0.999\ldots = \tfrac{9}{10}\big(1 + \tfrac{1}{10} + \tfrac{1}{100} + \cdots\big) @@ endless-decimal: the decimal names the number its truncations head to
= \tfrac{9}{10} \cdot \tfrac{1}{1 - 1/10} = 1 @@ endless-ratio-sum with r = 1/10
0.272727\ldots = \tfrac{27}{100} \cdot \tfrac{1}{1 - 1/100} = \tfrac{27}{99} = \tfrac{3}{11} @@ repeating-decimal: a repeating decimal is a fraction, and here is which
\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{1 - 1/2} = 1 @@ halves without end, and a total

An ancient mathematician found the region between the graph of y = x² and one of its chords to be a certain figure inside it times 1 + ¼ + ¼² + ⋯. He had no endless sums. He proved instead that 1 + ¼ + ⋯ + ¼ⁿ⁻¹ + ⅓ · ¼ⁿ⁻¹ = 4/3 for every n. So the remainder after any number of terms is exactly a third of the last term. Then he showed the area could be neither more nor less than 4/3 of the figure.

15i5The Shrinking Cut: Every Power at Once

Area-under-a-power technique: for every whole k ≥ 0 and b > 0, ∫₀ᵇ xᵏ dx = bᵏ⁺¹/(k + 1).

0 \lt q \lt 1;\ \ \text{points } 0 \lt bq^N \lt \cdots \lt bq^2 \lt bq \lt b @@ a partition of [0, b] with N + 1 pieces
\text{the piece } [bq^{i+1}, bq^i]:\ \ \text{width } bq^i(1 - q) @@ i from 0 to N − 1; the first piece [0, bq^N] apart
\text{its upper box: } (bq^i)^k \cdot bq^i(1 - q) = b^{k+1}(1 - q)\,(q^{k+1})^i @@ xᵏ increasing on [0, ∞) (powers-keep-order): greatest at the right end
\text{its lower box: } q^k \text{ times that} @@ least at the left end, bqⁱ⁺¹
U = b^{k+1}(1 - q)\tfrac{1 - q^{(k+1)N}}{1 - q^{k+1}} + (bq^N)^{k+1} @@ finite-ratio-sum with ratio qᵏ⁺¹; the first piece's box
L \ge q^k\, b^{k+1}(1 - q)\tfrac{1 - q^{(k+1)N}}{1 - q^{k+1}} @@ the first piece's lower box is 0
  
\tfrac{1 - q}{1 - q^{k+1}} = \tfrac{1}{1 + q + \cdots + q^k} @@ finite-ratio-sum read backwards
\text{as } N \text{ grows: } q^{(k+1)N} \to 0,\ \ (bq^N)^{k+1} \to 0 @@ small-powers-vanish
\tfrac{q^k\,b^{k+1}}{1 + q + \cdots + q^k} \le \textstyle\int_0^b x^k\,dx \le \tfrac{b^{k+1}}{1 + q + \cdots + q^k} @@ L ≤ ∫ ≤ U for every N (continuous-is-integrable); bounds kept in the limit (as limits-keep-order, 10b)
\text{as } q \to 1:\ \ 1 + q + \cdots + q^k \to k + 1,\ \ q^k \to 1 @@ polynomials are continuous (11d); k + 1 terms, each heading to 1
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_0^b x^k\,dx = \tfrac{b^{k+1}}{k + 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ squeezed between two bounds with the same limit (squeeze, 10d)

No sum of kth powers was needed. The pieces were unequal on purpose: their widths shrink by q, their heights by qᵏ, and their boxes by qᵏ⁺¹. So the boxes make a geometric sum whatever k is. 15h's pattern goes on for every whole power, and by interval-cut-in-two, ∫ᵤᵛ xᵏ dx = (vᵏ⁺¹ − uᵏ⁺¹)/(k + 1) for 0 ≤ u < v.

The finder of the shrinking cut wrote that he had found the areas of all the curves of the form yᵏ proportional to xᵐ. He divided the line into parts in continued proportion, so that the boxes inside the region and the boxes over it form a progression whose sum can be found.

Who said it first

Fermat wrote that he had found the areas of all the curves of the form yᵏ proportional to xᵐ, by dividing the line into parts in continued proportion, so that the inscribed and circumscribed figures form a progression whose sum can be found.

Fermat, Treatise on quadratures (about 1658, printed 1679); paraphrase

The remainder identity is Archimedes’, from his Quadrature of the Parabola. Power-of-one-plus is Bernoulli’s inequality. In the closer, the monk is Guido Grandi (1703), the inventor is Gottfried Leibniz (1713), the calculator is Leonhard Euler, and the textbook of 1821 is Augustin-Louis Cauchy’s Cours d’analyse.
