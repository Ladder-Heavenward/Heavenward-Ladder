What Does a Reaction Keep?

What does a reaction keep? Five chemistry annexes, from rung 5 to rung 42, each worked with molecules: weighed them, balanced them, turned them and held them to a mirror. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. A reaction is an arrow from one bag of molecules to another, and it keeps the atoms; whatever is worked out from the atoms is kept with them. The balances of a reaction are what one straight arrow sends to nothing. A compound is one piece of a picture whose arrows are a molecule’s moves. And a left hand and a right hand have the same parts with no arrow between them. Last, the kinds of a molecule are the pieces of a map that soon outgrows a climber, and then a machine.

atoms(in) = atoms(out), and w = u ∘ atoms, give w(in) = w(out), along any row of reactions. 0 → balances → ℝⁿ → ℝʳ → 0 exact: free balances = n − r. Arrows out of x: |G| of them, |K| to each thing of its piece; Kgx = gKxg⁻¹. q(end) = q(start) for every arrow: things with different q have no arrow between them. Trees on n named dots, n! renamings: a kind kept by k renamings has n!/k drawings.

infchem.1What Passes Through the Atoms Is Kept

5·chem1 weighed everything going in and coming out, and the totals matched. 5·chem3 balanced propane’s burning by counting each element on each side. The second explains the first, and arrows say how.

A bag is a list of how many of each molecule. A reaction is an arrow from the bag that goes in to the bag that comes out. Let atoms send a bag to its count of each element. The law that atoms are not made or destroyed says: atoms(in) = atoms(out), for every reaction.

Atoms-carry technique: if a weight w on bags is u ∘ atoms for some u on atom counts, then w(in) = w(out) for every reaction, and for every row of reactions, in any order.

w(\text{in}) = u(\text{atoms}(\text{in})) = u(\text{atoms}(\text{out})) = w(\text{out}) @@ atoms are kept; u gives one output for one input
w(\text{bag}_0) = w(\text{bag}_1) = \cdots = w(\text{bag}_k) @@ a row of k reactions: induction on k (4c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{what passes through the atoms is kept along every row}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the conserved-weight technique (∞15), with its reason
  
\mathrm{C_3H_8} + 5\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 3\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2O}:\quad \text{atoms} = (3\,\mathrm{C},\ 8\,\mathrm{H},\ 10\,\mathrm{O}) \text{ on both sides} @@ 5·chem3
\text{mass} = 12\,\mathrm{C} + 1\,\mathrm{H} + 16\,\mathrm{O}:\quad 44 + 5 \cdot 32 = 204 = 3 \cdot 44 + 4 \cdot 18 @@ mass passes through the atoms: the ledger of 5·chem1
\htmlClass{new}{\text{molecules}:\ 6 \text{ in},\ 7 \text{ out}} @@ the count of molecules does not pass through the atoms, and is not kept

What hides is the arrangement, which molecules the atoms sit in, and the sequence, the order in which the reactions ran. The balance in Paris read a number that never looked inside a molecule.

infchem.2The Balances Are What One Arrow Loses

27·chem1 wrote a reaction’s atom grid A, one row for each element and one column for each species, and found that a list of amounts balances exactly when A sends it to zero. 27·chem2, 3 and 4 then met one balance, many balances and no balance, each by solving. One row of arrows holds all three cases.

Balance-count technique: let A have n columns and reach a space of dimension r. Then 0 → balances → ℝⁿ → reached → 0 is a row in which each arrow loses exactly what the one before it reaches, and the balances have dimension n − r.

0 \to \text{balances} \xrightarrow{\ \text{put in}\ } \mathbb{R}^n \xrightarrow{\ A\ } \text{reached} \to 0 @@ the balances are what A loses: the atom-grid technique (27·chem1)
\text{put in loses only } 0;\quad A \text{ loses exactly the balances};\quad A \text{ reaches all of reached} @@ each arrow loses what the one before reaches
\htmlClass{new}{\dim(\text{balances}) = n - r} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lost-plus-reached technique (27c)
  
\mathrm{C_3H_8} + \mathrm{O_2} \to \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}:\quad n = 4,\ r = 3,\ \ 4 - 3 = 1 @@ one balance up to stretching, (1, 5, 3, 4): the one-up-to-scale technique (27·chem2)
\mathrm{C_3H_8} + \mathrm{O_2} \to \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{H_2O}:\quad n = 5,\ r = 3,\ \ 5 - 3 = 2 @@ a plane of balances: the many-ways technique (27·chem3)
\htmlClass{new}{\mathrm{H_2} \to \mathrm{O_2}:\quad n = 2,\ r = 2,\ \ 2 - 2 = 0} @@ only the zero list: the missing-species technique (27·chem4)

What hides is the sequence: the clearing of one unknown after another. The count of free balances is read off two numbers before any solving. What the count cannot do is choose the mix when it is 2 or more. That is set by the fire, as 27·chem3 said.

infchem.3A Compound Is One Piece of a Picture

19·chem2 put two chlorines on a benzene ring and sorted the 15 placements into 3 compounds. 19·chem3 counted each class by the moves that keep one of its placements. Draw the placements as things, and for each move g and each placement x draw an arrow from x to gx. Arrows compose because moves do, and each has an undo. A compound is one piece of this picture: the things joined to one another by arrows.

Arrows-by-ends technique: the arrows out of a placement x number |G|, one for each move. Those that end at any one y of its piece number |K|, the keepers of x. So |G| = (things in the piece) · |K|. And the keepers of gx are gKg⁻¹, so every thing of one piece has equally many keepers.

\{h : h\,x = y\} = gK \text{ for any } g \text{ with } g\,x = y,\qquad |gK| = |K| @@ a shifted copy (19d); the class-times-keepers technique (19·chem3)
|G| = \textstyle\sum_{y \text{ in the piece}} |K| = (\text{things in the piece}) \cdot |K| @@ sort the arrows out of x by where they end
k\,x = x \Rightarrow (g k g^{-1})(g\,x) = g\,k\,x = g\,x @@ so gKg⁻¹ keeps gx; and k ↦ gkg⁻¹ has the undo k′ ↦ g⁻¹k′g
\htmlClass{new}{K_{gx} = g\,K_x\,g^{-1},\quad |K_{gx}| = |K_x|} \quad \blacksquare @@ the keepers travel with the thing
  
\text{square, 8 moves}:\quad \text{a side has } 2 \text{ keepers},\ \tfrac82 = 4 \text{ things};\quad \text{a diagonal has } 4,\ \tfrac84 = 2 @@ 4 + 2 = 6 pairs: two pieces
\text{tetrahedron, 12 moves}:\quad \text{an edge has } 2 \text{ keepers},\ \tfrac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ things} @@ all 6 pairs: one piece
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two compounds, or one}} @@ the shelf holds one (19·chem4)

What hides is the arrangement: the drawing of the molecule. Only the moves and what they do to the placements are used. The shape is tested through its arrows alone.

infchem.4Same Parts, and No Arrow Between

20·chem3 proved that no turn carries CHBrClF onto its mirror image. The two have the same atoms, joined the same way. Its proof found a number, D, that every turn keeps and the mirror reverses. That is a technique about arrows of any kind.

Kept-apart technique: if q(end) = q(start) for every arrow, then two things with different q have no arrow between them, and no row of arrows.

x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_k:\quad q(x_0) = q(x_1) = \cdots = q(x_k) @@ every arrow keeps q; induction on k (4c)
\htmlClass{new}{q(x) \ne q(y) \Rightarrow \text{no row of arrows from } x \text{ to } y} \quad \blacksquare @@ turn the line above round (7f)
  
\text{things: the placements of CHBrClF in space; arrows: the turns} @@ a molecule tumbles; it does not pass through a mirror
q = \text{the sign of } D(\mathrm{F}, \mathrm{Cl}, \mathrm{Br}) \text{ seen from H}:\quad \text{every turn keeps it} @@ the seen-from technique (20·chem2)
q(\text{molecule}) = +,\quad q(\text{mirror image}) = - @@ 6√3 and −6√3 (20·chem3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{two pieces: two molecules}} @@ the handed-carbon technique (20·chem3)

What hides is the arrangement: the atoms, which are the same in both hands and so decide nothing. “The same” is always said for a kind of arrow. Allow mirrors as arrows and the two hands are one piece. Allow only turns, as space does, and they are two. The first footing used the same technique without its name: a bag with a different count of atoms cannot be reached by any row of reactions.

infchem.5A Map Too Big for a Climber

42·chem.2 counted the kinds of alkane: 2 for four carbons, 75 for ten, 366 319 for twenty. Draw every carbon tree on n named dots as a thing and every renaming of the dots as an arrow. There are n! renamings. Two drawings are one kind when a renaming carries one to the other. So a kind is a piece.

Kinds-as-pieces technique: a kind kept by k renamings has n!/k drawings, and the drawings of all the kinds together are all the carbon trees on n named dots.

|G| = n!,\qquad \text{drawings in the piece} = \tfrac{n!}{k} @@ the arrows-by-ends technique (third footing), with the renamings as the moves
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\sum_{\text{kinds}} \tfrac{n!}{k} = \text{the carbon trees on } n \text{ named dots}} \quad \blacksquare @@ every drawing lies in exactly one piece
  
\text{the path}:\quad k = 2,\ \ \tfrac{24}{2} = 12 @@ leave it, or turn it end for end
\text{the star}:\quad k = 6,\ \ \tfrac{24}{6} = 4 @@ the shuffles of its three ends
\htmlClass{new}{12 + 4 = 16} @@ the 16 trees on four named dots (42b): two kinds, butane and isobutane (42·chem.1)
  
\text{a climber reads}:\quad n = 4,\ \ 24 \text{ renamings},\ 16 \text{ drawings} @@ dozens of arrows, as above
\text{a machine reads}:\quad n = 10,\ \ 10! = 3\,628\,800 \text{ renamings},\ 75 \text{ kinds} @@ it lists the kinds, and the sum above checks its list
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither reads}:\quad n = 20,\ \ 20! \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18} \text{ renamings}} @@ never drawn; the 366 319 kinds are counted without them (42·chem.2)

The same map is drawn inside one molecule. Each hydrogen of water is light or heavy: 4 labellings of the two places, one swap, 3 pieces. Oxygen has three stable weights, so stable water comes in 3 · 3 = 9 kinds. Inside a nucleus every proton or neutron added is a new arrow. By oxygen that map is past a climber, and by lead it is past all but the largest machines (cellar).

What a climber keeps, where the map cannot be read, is the question to ask of it: what does every arrow keep? A renaming keeps the number of links at each dot. The path has two ends and the star three, so no renaming joins them, by the kept-apart technique, with no map drawn. What hides is the arrangement: the names of the dots.

The accepted names

A bag is a multiset, and the bags of molecules with reactions as arrows form a Petri net’s category of executions (∞15); an amount kept by every reaction is a conserved quantity, or an invariant, and that mass is one is the conservation of mass. The row in which each arrow loses exactly what the one before reaches is an exact sequence; what A loses is its kernel, or null space, what it reaches is its image, r is its rank, and n − r is the rank-nullity count. The picture with placements as things and moves as arrows is the action groupoid; its pieces are the orbits, the keepers are the stabilizer, the count is the orbit-stabilizer theorem, and gKg⁻¹ is a conjugate subgroup. A molecule with no arrow to its mirror image is chiral, the two hands are enantiomers, and sameness for a chosen kind of arrow is isomorphism in a category. A drawing on named dots is a labelled tree, a kind is an isomorphism class, or an unlabelled tree, the renamings are the symmetric group, and the keepers of a drawing are its automorphism group. Molecules that differ only in the weights of their atoms are isotopologues. Working a nucleus out from the forces between its protons and neutrons is an ab initio calculation: it was done for lead-208, with 208 parts, in 2022 (Hu and others, Nature Physics 18, 1196; U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Science highlight on the same work). The count of nuclei known is in the thousands, a chart a machine reads with ease; the inside of each heavy one is the map that is not read.

What the arrows let hide

In the first footing the arrows hide which molecules hold the atoms and the order of the reactions. In the second they hide the order of solving. In the third and fourth they hide the drawing of the molecule and the atoms themselves. In the last footing the map itself hides, by its size. A climber reads a map of dozens of arrows, and a machine one of thousands or millions. Past that, what every arrow keeps is still read, and it is the climber’s to ask for. Two tools carry the page. What passes through a kept thing is kept: mass through the atoms. And what every arrow keeps, no row of arrows can change: the atom count of a bag, the piece of a placement, the hand of a molecule. The page’s one dated claim is the lead-208 calculation of 2022, given above with its two sources.
