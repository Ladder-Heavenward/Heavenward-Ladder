39·chem · chemistry

Why Is a Chip Made of Sand?

A chip begins as sand. Quartz is silicon and oxygen; furnaces strip the oxygen, and the silicon is cleaned until almost nothing else is left. Then, on purpose, a few atoms in a million are put back.

39·phys found why silicon has a gap, how warmth lifts electrons across it, and how one phosphorus atom frees one electron. This page treats the crystal as a chemist treats a beaker: electrons and holes as two things that form and recombine in balance, a level they settle to, dopants picked from the table by their outer electrons, compounds that copy silicon’s crystal, and the freezing that makes it pure.

A semiconductor is a solution: its electrons and holes keep a fixed product, as acid and base do in water.

39chem1Electrons and Holes in Balance

Pure water holds H⁺ and OH⁻ at 10⁻⁷ mol/L each. Add acid, and H⁺ rises to 10⁻³ while OH⁻ falls to 10⁻¹¹: the product stays 10⁻¹⁴ (17·chem). Water breaks into the two and they rejoin, and the balance fixes the product, not the amounts.

Silicon does the same. A bond breaks into a free electron and a hole, and an electron that meets a hole fills it. From here on, n and p are the counts per cm³ of free electrons and of holes.

Fixed-product technique: in a semiconductor in balance, n · p = nᵢ², whatever has been added; for silicon at 300 K, nᵢ = 10¹⁰ per cm³.

n = N_c\,e^{-(E_c - \mu)/kT} @@ the two-shares technique (38·phys) in its rare form, over the levels near the upper band edge
p = N_v\,e^{-(\mu - E_v)/kT} @@ a hole is a level left empty: share 1 − (share filled), in the rare form
n\,p = N_c N_v\,e^{-(E_c - E_v)/kT} @@ multiply: μ cancels
\htmlClass{new}{n\,p = N_c N_v\,e^{-E_g/kT} = n_i^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ the right side holds only the crystal and the warmth
  
n = N_d = 10^{16} @@ every donor freed (39·phys4)
p = n_i^2/n = 10^{20}/10^{16} = 10^4 @@ the fixed-product technique
\htmlClass{new}{n/p = 10^{12}} @@ pure silicon has n = p = 10¹⁰

Adding donors is adding a base: the holes are swept down as the electrons rise. That is why one kind of carrier carries nearly all the current in doped silicon.

39chem2The Level Electrons Settle To

Two tanks of water joined by a pipe settle to one height, though one is wide and one narrow. Electrons settle the same way, to one level, and the level is μ, the one in the two-shares technique.

One-level technique: materials joined and in balance have one μ throughout; in silicon, μ sits kT ln(n/nᵢ) above the middle of the gap, and kT ln(p/nᵢ) below it.

n = n_i\,e^{(\mu - \mu_i)/kT} @@ divide the n of 39chem1 by its value at n = nᵢ; μᵢ is μ in pure silicon, near the middle of the gap
\mu - \mu_i = kT\ln\tfrac{n}{n_i} @@ the log undoes the exponential (15)
n = 10^{16}:\ \ 0.0259 \cdot \ln 10^6 = 0.358\ \text{eV} @@ n-type, above the middle
p = 10^{16}:\ \ \mu_i - \mu = 0.358\ \text{eV} @@ p-type, below the middle
\htmlClass{new}{0.358 + 0.358 = 0.716\ \text{V}} \quad \blacksquare @@ join them: μ must become one, so the bands shift by the difference

That is 39·phys5’s barrier of 0.71 V, found here by levels alone. The barrier is not a store of energy. It is what one level across two materials looks like when their bands sit at different heights.

39chem3Choosing a Dopant From the Table

Silicon sits in column 14, with four outer electrons: 3s² 3p² by 38·chem’s order. Its neighbours in the table differ by one.

One-column-over technique: an atom from the column to the right of the host, in the host’s place, gives the crystal one electron; an atom from the column to the left takes one, leaving a hole.

DopantColumnMeasured bindingBy 39·phys4
phosphorus150.045 eV0.026 eV
arsenic150.054 eV0.026 eV
boron130.045 eVnear 0.03 eV
indium13about 0.16 eVnear 0.03 eV

The donor technique treats the extra charge as a point inside a smooth crystal, so it gives every donor the same binding. The measured bindings differ, because near its own nucleus the electron feels the atom and not the crystal. Phosphorus, arsenic and boron are bound so loosely that only deep cold keeps their carriers home. Indium is not: at 0.16 eV, six kT at room warmth, most of its holes stay bound, so indium is a poor dopant for silicon. The column chooses the sign; the atom chooses how well it works.

39chem4Same Count, Wider Gap

Silicon’s crystal needs four outer electrons an atom, for 38·chem6’s four arms. Two atoms can share the work: one from column 13 and one from column 15 bring 3 + 5, the same 8 between them as two of silicon.

Eight-a-pair technique: a pair of atoms with eight outer electrons between them can build silicon’s crystal; across a row, the further apart their columns, the wider the gap.

CrystalColumnsOuter electronsGap at 300 KLight at the gap

zinc selenide, ZnSe12 + 162 + 62.7 eV459 nm, blue
\lambda = \tfrac{1240\ \text{eV nm}}{E_g} @@ the gap-to-wavelength technique (36·phys3)
\tfrac{1240}{0.66} = 1880,\ \ \tfrac{1240}{1.424} = 871,\ \ \tfrac{1240}{2.7} = 459 @@ nm
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the same row, the same spacing, three colours}} \quad \blacksquare @@ Ge and GaAs are 0.5646 and 0.5653 nm apart, ZnSe nearly the same

The three sit in the same row of the table, with nearly the same spacing. What changes is how evenly the pair holds its electrons. In germanium the two atoms pull alike; in zinc selenide the selenium pulls harder, the bond is partly a gift of charge, and the gap opens. This trend is read off the measurements, not proved here. It is how a light’s colour is chosen: an electron falling across the gap gives off one photon of that energy, so gallium arsenide glows in the infrared, and a blue light needs a gap as wide as zinc selenide’s.

39chem5Freezing Out the Dirt

Sea ice is far fresher than the sea it froze from. As water freezes, the crystal keeps out most of the salt, and the salt is left in the water still liquid.

Molten silicon does the same with what is dissolved in it. From here on, the keep-share k of an impurity is the share of it per atom in the solid that freezes, against its share in the melt just beside it.

Freeze-out technique: if a melt with impurity share C₀ freezes from one end, slowly enough to stay mixed, the solid frozen when a part f has frozen holds k C₀ (1 − f)k − 1.

C_L(1 - f) = \text{impurity left in the melt} @@ the melt’s share once a part f has frozen
d\big[C_L(1 - f)\big] = -\,k\,C_L\,df @@ a thin slice df freezes, taking k times the melt’s share with it
(1 - f)\,dC_L = (1 - k)\,C_L\,df @@ two slopes of a product (13), rearranged
\ln\tfrac{C_L}{C_0} = -(1 - k)\ln(1 - f) @@ the side-by-side technique (24f), from f = 0
\htmlClass{new}{C_S = k\,C_0\,(1 - f)^{k - 1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the solid takes k times the melt
ImpurityKeep-share kSolid at f = 0.5, against C₀

Freezing cleans out aluminium two hundred and fifty times over in one pass, and barely touches boron. So boron has to be taken out by chemistry before the silicon is ever melted, by turning the silicon into a liquid compound and boiling it apart from boron’s, and freezing does the rest. The same keep-share decides how evenly a dopant spreads along a crystal pulled from the melt, which is one reason boron, with k near 1, is the favourite for p-type.

The accepted names

The fixed-product technique is the law of mass action for carriers, and nᵢ the intrinsic carrier concentration; N_c and N_v are the effective densities of states. Recombination and generation are the two directions of the balance. μ is the electrons’ chemical potential, called the Fermi level, and μᵢ the intrinsic level. A dopant’s binding is its ionization energy; the difference from the hydrogen-like value is the central-cell correction, and a dopant bound too tightly is a deep level. Eight-a-pair is the Grimm–Sommerfeld rule, and GaAs and ZnSe are III–V and II–VI compounds in the zincblende crystal; the partial gift of charge is ionicity. The keep-share is the equilibrium segregation (distribution) coefficient, the freeze-out technique is the Scheil equation, and many passes of a melted band along a bar are zone refining. Sand becomes silicon through quartz, metallurgical silicon and trichlorosilane, which is distilled.

What was measured

The dopant bindings, the gaps and spacings of Ge, GaAs and ZnSe, and the keep-shares of boron, phosphorus and aluminium are measured values, each checked in two sources; indium’s binding is given as about 0.16 eV because the sources differ in the third figure. nᵢ = 10¹⁰ per cm³ is the value 39·phys used. That boron is taken out by distilling trichlorosilane, before any melting, rests on one source and has not yet been revisited. Everything else on the page is computed.
