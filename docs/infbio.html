What Is Handed On?

What is handed on? Five biology annexes, from rung 2 to rung 42, each followed something passed along: descent from an ancestor, a gene from a parent, a signal from one cell to the next, changes along a line of descent. This page reads them again with rung forty-seven’s arrows. Descent is an arrow that composes, so two whole branches are nested or apart. A cross of two genes is two crosses side by side, so its grid need not be drawn. A ring of parts that each stop the next is a row of opposites closed on itself, and whether it can rest is read from n. And lengths add along a path, so a tree of descent is read from its table three ends at a time.

x → y → p gives x → p; ancestors in a line: gp and gq with a shared member are nested. look = (look₁, look₂) on C₁ × C₂: counts of looks multiply. x = ¬ⁿx: two states for even n, none for odd. In a tree the paths among p, q, r meet in one dot x, and d(p, x) = ½(d(p, q) + d(p, r) − d(q, r)).

infbio.1Two Branches Are Nested or Apart

2·bio3 made a whole branch gp from an ancestor p and all its descendants, and proved that a branch lets no descendant out. Draw an arrow x → y when x is y or is descended from y. Descent is transitive, so arrows compose. The keep-every-descendant technique is then one row: x → y → p.

On a tree of descent the ancestors of any one thing lie in a line: of any two, one is descended from the other. Take that as given, as 2·bio4 took its facts.

Nested-or-apart technique: two whole branches that share a member are nested: one lies inside the other.

x \in g_p \text{ and } x \in g_q:\quad x \to p,\ \ x \to q @@ a shared member
p \to q \ \text{ or } \ q \to p @@ the given: the ancestors of x lie in a line
p \to q:\quad y \to p \to q \text{ for every } y \in g_p @@ arrows compose: the keep-every-descendant technique (2·bio3)
\htmlClass{new}{g_p \subseteq g_q,\ \text{ or } \ g_q \subseteq g_p} \quad \blacksquare @@ the other case is the same
  
\text{mammals and } g_o \text{ share the orca}:\quad \text{mammals} \subseteq g_o @@ nested, as the technique says (2·bio4)
\text{the fish by gills and the mammals}:\quad \text{no shared member} @@ apart
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the swimmers with fins and the mammals share the orca; the bat and the salmon}} @@ neither inside the other: the swimmers are no whole branch

What hides is the arrangement: the list of traits. No fossil is dug up for the last line. A known whole branch and one shared member settle it.

infbio.2The Grid Need Not Be Drawn

5·bio5 drew sixteen cells for two genes and counted 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. The count did not need the cells. A cell of the two-gene grid is a pair (cell of the first grid, cell of the second), and its look is the pair of their looks: the two crosses run side by side.

Looks-multiply technique: if the cells are C₁ × C₂ and look(a, b) = (look₁(a), look₂(b)), then the cells with the look (u, v) number n₁(u) · n₂(v). For k genes handed on separately, the counts of the k grids multiply.

\{(a, b) : \mathrm{look}(a, b) = (u, v)\} = \{a : \mathrm{look}_1(a) = u\} \times \{b : \mathrm{look}_2(b) = v\} @@ a pair has the look (u, v) exactly when each side has its own
\htmlClass{new}{n(u, v) = n_1(u) \cdot n_2(v)} \quad \blacksquare @@ stages multiply (4k); for k genes, induction (4c)
  
\text{a climber reads}:\quad 2 \text{ genes},\ 16 \text{ cells};\quad (3, 1) \text{ by } (3, 1) = 9, 3, 3, 1 @@ 5·bio5’s count, from the sides
\text{a machine reads}:\quad 10 \text{ genes},\ 4^{10} = 1\,048\,576 \text{ cells} @@ all ten dominant looks: 3¹⁰ = 59 049 cells, by the technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither reads}:\quad 100 \text{ genes},\ 4^{100} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{60} \text{ cells}} @@ the share with all hundred dominant looks is still (¾)¹⁰⁰

What hides is the arrangement: the cells. What must be asked first is whether the grid is a product with equal chances. Two genes close together on one chromosome are handed on together more often than not, and the counts do not multiply.

infbio.3Once Round the Ring

24·bio3 set two genes to hold each other down and found two states that hold. 24·bio4 set three in a ring and found that they take turns for ever. 39·bio4 built a clock from a ring of an odd number of nerve cells, and 39g2 one from not gates. The three are one row of arrows. Read each part as on or off, and each as the opposite of the part before it. Once round a ring of n parts is a row of n opposites.

Ring-rest technique: a ring of n parts, each the opposite of the one before, has two states that hold when n is even and none when n is odd.

x_1 = \lnot x_n = \lnot\lnot x_{n-1} = \cdots = \lnot^{\,n} x_1 @@ once round the ring
\lnot \circ \lnot = \text{do-nothing} @@ so ¬ⁿ is do-nothing for even n, and ¬ for odd n
n \text{ even}:\quad x_1 = x_1,\ \text{ either value, and the rest follow} @@ two states
\htmlClass{new}{n \text{ odd}:\quad x_1 = \lnot x_1,\ \text{ no value}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the opposite keeps nothing fixed (∞1)
  
n = 2:\quad (\text{on}, \text{off}),\ (\text{off}, \text{on}) @@ the switch technique’s two held states, 3.73 and 0.27 (24·bio3)
n = 3:\quad \text{no state holds} @@ the ring technique: the genes take turns (24·bio4)
\htmlClass{new}{n \text{ odd, nerve cells}:\quad \text{period } n\,(\delta + \tau \ln\tfrac{RI}{RI - d})} @@ the inhibitory-ring technique (39·bio4)

What hides is the arrangement: the chemistry of each part. The on-or-off reading leaves out the middle. An odd ring has one rest there, and it holds when the switches are gentle (the odd-ring technique, 39g2; 24·bio4). So the ring says where to look for a clock, and the steepness says whether there is one.

infbio.4A Tree Read Three Ends at a Time

42·bio.2 gave each link of a tree a length and called the total along the one path between two ends their distance. A path is a row of links, and its length is the sum along the row: joining rows adds lengths. 42·bio.3 found each end link “from three distances”. Here is the technique behind that line.

Three-ends technique: in a tree the paths among three ends p, q, r meet in exactly one dot x, and d(p, x) = ½(d(p, q) + d(p, r) − d(q, r)).

\text{the paths } p \text{ to } q \text{ and } p \text{ to } r \text{ run together to a dot } x \text{ and part for good} @@ if they met again there would be a cycle (42b)
q \text{ to } x \text{ to } r \text{ is the path from } q \text{ to } r @@ the one path (42b2)
d(p, q) = a + b,\quad d(p, r) = a + c,\quad d(q, r) = b + c @@ lengths add along rows; a, b, c the distances from x
\htmlClass{new}{a = \tfrac12\big(d(p, q) + d(p, r) - d(q, r)\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ add the first two, take away the third
  
\text{human, horse, chicken}:\quad \tfrac12(12 + 13 - 11) = 7,\ \ \tfrac12(12 + 11 - 13) = 5,\ \ \tfrac12(13 + 11 - 12) = 6 @@ the table of 42·bio.3
\text{human, chicken, tuna}:\quad \tfrac12(21 + 17 - 13) = 12.5,\ \ \tfrac12(21 + 13 - 17) = 8.5 @@ the tuna joins 8.5 from the human: 1.5 past the first meeting dot
\htmlClass{new}{7 + 1.5 + 12.5 = 21} @@ 42·bio.3’s tree, in the order human, horse, chicken, tuna; any order gives it
  
\text{a climber reads}:\quad 4 \text{ ends},\ 3 \text{ trees},\ 6 \text{ distances} @@ as above
\text{a machine reads}:\quad 20 \text{ ends},\ 190 \text{ distances},\ 4845 \text{ fours to test} @@ the four-point technique (42·bio.2), on every four
\htmlClass{new}{\text{neither reads}:\quad 20 \text{ ends},\ 2.2 \times 10^{20} \text{ trees}} @@ the graft technique (42·bio.1): never listed, and never needed

What hides is the sequence: which animals were measured first. The tree is built from the table three ends at a time and tested four at a time. Whether the table fits a tree is the question to put to the machine, and the four-point technique says what answer to expect.

The accepted names

A whole branch is a clade, or a monophyletic group; a branch with some descendants taken away, as the fish by gills, is paraphyletic. Descent with x → x allowed is a partial order, and that two branches are nested or apart makes the clades a laminar family, or a hierarchy. Where lines of descent join, by two parents, by hybrid kinds, or by genes passed between branches (horizontal gene transfer), descent is a network and not a tree. The grid is the Punnett square; genes handed on separately show independent assortment, genes handed on together are linked, and a look worked out side by side is a product map. Parts that stop one another form a negative feedback loop when the ring is odd and a positive one when it is even; the switch is a toggle switch, or bistable, the three-gene clock is the repressilator, and the ring of cells is a ring oscillator. A table that fits a tree is an additive metric, or tree metric; the meeting dot of three ends is their median, and ½(d(p, q) + d(p, r) − d(q, r)) is the Gromov product. Building a tree from fours is the quartet method.

What the arrows let hide

In the first three footings the arrows hide arrangement: the traits, the cells of the grid, the chemistry of each part. In the fourth they hide sequence: the order in which the kinds were measured. Twice the map outgrows a climber and then a machine, the grid of a hundred genes and the trees of twenty kinds, and both times the technique reads what is wanted without the map. What stays with the climber is the question each technique asks first: is descent a tree here, is the grid a product, are the switches steep, does the table fit a tree. No dated claim is made on this page.
