26k · technique

Real integrals by residues

Some integrals along the whole line resist every technique of rung sixteen. The area under the cosine divided by one plus x squared has no antiderivative anyone can write down. Yet its value is exact and simple: pi over e. The road to it leaves the line. Run along the floor from far left to far right, then come back over the top on a half circle, and the road is a loop. Rung twenty six totals a loop by its holes alone, a few residues read off at a few points. If the half circle contributes less and less as it grows, the loop’s total is the line’s total. This step builds that road, fences the half circle, and uses it three times: once where the answer can be checked by the arctan, once where nothing else reaches, and once for the sine divided by x, an integral that settles only because its waves take turns.

Close the line with a half circle over the top. The loop totals two pi i times the residues above the floor. When the half circle’s share fades as it grows, what is left is the integral along the line.

26k1Closing the line

Stands on 26h1 (the pole-split technique), 26h2 (the residue technique), 26f2 (the disc-undoing technique), 26e2 (the path-ends technique), 26g1 (the rim technique) and 25c2.

From here on, for R > 0, the half-disc loop H_R runs along the floor from −R to R, then along the upper half of the circle |z| = R from R back round to −R. Along the floor it is γ(t) = t, with γ′ = 1, so that piece of ∮ round H_R of f is the real integral ∫ from −R to R of f(x) dx.

26h2 totalled a function round a circle. The half-disc loop is not a circle, so the residue technique must be carried over to it.

Half-disc technique: let f have a complex slope at every point of ℂ except finitely many poles, none on the floor and none of size R; then ∮ round H_R of f is 2πi times the sum of the residues of f at the poles above the floor of size less than R.

a = s - ti,\ t > 0,\ c = \tfrac{R^2 + 1}{t}:\ \ |z - ic|^2 \le R^2 + c^2 \text{ on } H_R @@ |z − ic|² = |z|² − 2c·(upright part of z) + c², with |z| ≤ R and upright part ≥ 0
|a - ic|^2 \ge (t + c)^2 = t^2 + 2(R^2 + 1) + c^2 > R^2 + c^2 + 1 @@ the upright part of a − ic is −(t + c)
\text{the disc } |z - ic|^2 < R^2 + c^2 + 1 \text{ holds } H_R \text{ and not } a @@ the two lines above; a pole above the floor of size more than R is outside the disc |z| < |a|, which holds H_R
\oint_{H_R} P_a = 0 \text{ for every pole } a \text{ outside the half-disc} @@ the trouble part Pₐ has a complex slope on that disc (26h1): the disc-undoing technique (26f2)
\oint_{H_R} \tfrac{dz}{(z-a)^k} = 0 \text{ for } k \ge 2 @@ an undoing −1/((k − 1)(z − a)ᵏ⁻¹), with a slope on H_R (26e2)
\oint_{H_R} \tfrac{dz}{z-a} + \oint_{L_R} \tfrac{dz}{z-a} = \oint_{|z|=R} \tfrac{dz}{z-a} = 2\pi i @@ L_R: the lower half circle, then the floor back from R to −R; the floor is run once each way and cancels; the kernel of the rim technique (26g1)
\oint_{L_R} \tfrac{dz}{z-a} = 0 \text{ for } a \text{ above the floor} @@ the first three lines turned upside down: a disc holds L_R and not a
\oint_{H_R} \big(f - \textstyle\sum_j P_j\big) = 0 @@ the pole-split technique (26h1) at every pole leaves a complex slope everywhere; the disc-undoing technique on |z| < R + 1
\htmlClass{new}{\oint_{H_R} f = 2\pi i \textstyle\sum_{a \text{ above, } |a| < R} \operatorname{Res}(f, a)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above: only the 1/(z − a) piece of each pole inside survives

So 26h2’s residue technique holds round the half-disc loop: the poles below the floor and the poles beyond the arc count for nothing.

26k2The arc fades

Stands on 26k1, 26e1 (the path-fence technique), 26h1, 25c2 (the plane-triangle technique) and 16i (improper integrals, comparison).

Fading-arc technique: if |f(z)| ≤ M_R on the upper half circle of radius R, and R·M_R heads to 0 as R grows, then the integral of f along that half circle heads to 0. The half circle has length πR, so the path fence gives at most πR·M_R.

\operatorname{Res}\big(\tfrac{1}{1+z^2}, i\big) = \tfrac{1}{2i} @@ a pole of order 1: p(a)/q′(a), with q′(i) = 2i (26h1)
\int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{dx}{1+x^2} + \int_{\text{arc}} \tfrac{dz}{1+z^2} = 2\pi i\cdot\tfrac{1}{2i} = \pi @@ the half-disc technique (26k1), R > 1: −i is below the floor
|1 + z^2| \ge |z|^2 - 1 = R^2 - 1 \text{ on the arc} @@ the plane-triangle technique (25c2)
\Big|\int_{\text{arc}}\Big| \le \pi R\cdot\tfrac{1}{R^2 - 1} \to 0 @@ the fading-arc technique
\textstyle\int_0^\infty \text{ and } \int_{-\infty}^0 \text{ converge} @@ comparison for areas with 1/x² beyond 1 (16i3), as 16i3 did
\htmlClass{new}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tfrac{dx}{1+x^2} = \pi} \quad \blacksquare @@ both halves converge, so the whole is the limit of ∫ from −R to R

Check: arctan runs from −π/2 to π/2 (21m), a rise of π. The loop got there without the arctan. For any quotient p/q of real polynomials whose bottom has no root on the floor and has degree at least the top’s plus 2, the arc fades the same way: far out, |p| is at most a constant times Rᵐ and |q| at least a constant times Rⁿ (the far-out technique, 25d1), so R·M_R heads to 0 when n ≥ m + 2.

26k3Waves along the line

Stands on 26k1, 26k2, 26b2 (the growth-and-turn technique), 26i3 (cos in the plane) and 16d (odd and even areas).

Upper-wave technique: for k ≥ 0 and z with upright part y ≥ 0, |e^(ikz)| = e^(−ky) ≤ 1; so to find ∫ cos(kx) f(x) dx over the line, take the floor part of the total of e^(ikz) f(z) round H_R, and the arc fades whenever it fades for f. Use e^(ikz), never cos kz.

e^{iz} = e^{-y + ix},\ \ |e^{iz}| = e^{-y} \le 1 \text{ for } y \ge 0 @@ the growth-and-turn technique (26b2), z = x + iy
\Big|\tfrac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}\Big| \le \tfrac{1}{R^2 - 1} \text{ on the arc} @@ 26k2's fence; the arc fades
\operatorname{Res}\big(\tfrac{e^{iz}}{1+z^2}, i\big) = \tfrac{e^{i\cdot i}}{2i} = \tfrac{e^{-1}}{2i} @@ p(a)/q′(a) (26h1)
\int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}\,dx + \int_{\text{arc}} = 2\pi i\cdot\tfrac{e^{-1}}{2i} = \tfrac{\pi}{e} @@ the half-disc technique (26k1)
\int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{e^{ix}}{1+x^2}\,dx = \int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx + i\int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{\sin x}{1+x^2}\,dx,\ \text{the second } 0 @@ the turn-growth technique; the sine piece is odd (16d)
\htmlClass{new}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tfrac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx = \tfrac{\pi}{e} \approx 1.1557} \quad \blacksquare @@ the arc fades; both halves converge, as (1 + cos x)/(1 + x²) lies between 0 and 2/(1 + x²) (16i3)

Why not cos z itself? On the upright, cos(iR) = (e^(−R) + e^R)/2 (26i3), which outgrows every power of R. The arc would not fade. e^(iz) shrinks over the top and grows underneath; cos z is half of each, so it grows on both sides.

26k4The sine integral

Stands on 26k1, 26e1, 26f2, 26b2, 21f (sin h / h heads to 1), 14d (the slope-sign-order technique), 16d and 16i1.

∫ from 0 to ∞ of sin x / x dx is harder. The bottom x shrinks the waves only as fast as 1/R, so the fading-arc technique fails: R · (1/R) does not head to 0. What saves it is that e^(iz) is tiny over most of the arc, and large only near the floor.

Sine-chord technique: for 0 ≤ t ≤ π/2, sin t ≥ 2t/π; so for R > 0, ∫ from 0 to π of e^(−R sin t) dt < π/R. The sine stays above its chord from 0 to π/2.

g(t) = t\cos t - \sin t:\ g(0) = 0,\ g'(t) = -t\sin t \le 0 @@ the product rule; slopes of sin and cos (21g)
g(t) \le 0 \text{ on } [0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}] @@ the slope-sign-order technique (14d)
\big(\tfrac{\sin t}{t}\big)' = \tfrac{g(t)}{t^2} \le 0,\ \text{so } \tfrac{\sin t}{t} \ge \tfrac{\sin(\pi/2)}{\pi/2} = \tfrac{2}{\pi} @@ falls on (0, π/2] (14d)
\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin t}\,dt = 2\int_0^{\pi/2} e^{-R\sin t}\,dt \le 2\int_0^{\pi/2} e^{-2Rt/\pi}\,dt @@ sin(π − t) = sin t (21e4), so the two halves agree (16d); smaller function, smaller area (15e)
\htmlClass{new}{\int_0^\pi e^{-R\sin t}\,dt \le \tfrac{\pi}{R}\big(1 - e^{-R}\big) < \tfrac{\pi}{R}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the area of e^(−2Rt/π) (16c)
  
f(z) = \tfrac{e^{iz} - 1}{z},\ f(0) = i @@ (e^(iz) − 1)/z = i + i²z/2! + i³z²/3! + ⋯, a power sum: continuous at 0
\oint_{H_R} f = 0 @@ a complex slope at every z ≠ 0 (26d1, 26d2): the disc-undoing technique with one point excepted (26f2)
\int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{e^{ix} - 1}{x}\,dx = \int_{-R}^{R} \tfrac{\cos x - 1}{x}\,dx + i\int_{-R}^{R}\tfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx = 0 + 2i\int_0^R \tfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx @@ the first is odd, the second even (16d)
\int_{\text{arc}} \tfrac{dz}{z} = \int_0^\pi \tfrac{iRe^{it}}{Re^{it}}\,dt = i\pi @@ the arc as Re^(it), 0 ≤ t ≤ π (26e1)
\Big|\int_{\text{arc}} \tfrac{e^{iz}}{z}\,dz\Big| \le \int_0^\pi \tfrac{e^{-R\sin t}}{R}\,R\,dt < \tfrac{\pi}{R} @@ |e^(iz)| = e^(−R sin t) on the arc (26b2); the sine-chord technique
2i\int_0^R \tfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx + \int_{\text{arc}} \tfrac{e^{iz}}{z}\,dz - i\pi = 0 @@ the floor piece plus the arc piece of the loop
\htmlClass{new}{\int_0^\infty \tfrac{\sin x}{x}\,dx = \tfrac{\pi}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by 2i; the arc piece heads to 0; the limit as R grows is the integral (16i1)

At x = 0 the integrand has the value 1, since sin h / h heads to 1 (21f); the only end that runs off is ∞. The integral settles, but not in size: the waves take turns, and their sizes alone add up without a ceiling, as the third special challenge shows. Comparison could never prove it settles; the loop did.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The half-disc loop is the standard semicircular contour, and the half-disc technique is the residue theorem on it. The fading-arc technique is the ML-inequality applied to the arc; the sine-chord technique is Jordan’s inequality, and its bound on the arc is Jordan’s lemma. ∫ from 0 to ∞ of sin x / x = π/2 is the Dirichlet integral. Computing real integrals this way is contour integration.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler and Pierre-Simon Laplace found many such integrals in the eighteenth century by passing from real to imaginary values, a method that Siméon Denis Poisson and others distrusted. Augustin-Louis Cauchy presented a memoir on definite integrals in 1814, printed in 1827, which began the loop method; his residues followed from 1826. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet studied the sine integral in 1829 in his work on Fourier series. Camille Jordan’s lemma appeared in his Cours d’analyse of 1894.
