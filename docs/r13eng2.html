How Does a Wall Keep the Heat In?

How does a wall keep the heat in? A house loses heat through its walls at a steady rate on a cold night, a vacuum flask keeps coffee hot all day, and a spacecraft in sunlight on one side and black sky on the other is wrapped in thin shiny foil. Heat leaks by three paths: through solid matter, carried by moving air, and as light given off by every warm surface. This page measures the rate heat flows through a layer, adds layers one after another, finds why the thinnest layer of still air matters most, and finds why a stack of foil sheets with nothing between them insulates so well.

Heat flows through a layer at a rate kAΔT/L. Each layer is a resistance L/(kA), and layers in a row add, so the weakest insulator does least and the best does most. Every surface also shines away σT⁴ per area; a shiny sheet between two surfaces halves what passes, and N sheets cut it to 1/(N + 1).

13eng21A Steady Leak

Evita’s wall is 10 cm of brick. It is 20 °C inside and 0 °C outside, and the night is long enough that the temperature through the wall stops changing.

q = \dfrac{kA\,\Delta T}{L} @@ rate of heat in watts; k how well the stuff conducts
\text{brick: } k \approx 0.7 \text{ W per m per K} @@ measured
\text{per square metre: } \dfrac{0.7 \cdot 20}{0.1} = 140 \text{ W} @@ a small heater for every square metre
\htmlClass{new}{\text{double the thickness, halve the leak}} @@ q falls as 1/L

From here on, on this page, a layer’s resistance is R = L/(kA): the temperature drop it needs to pass one watt.

13eng22Layers in a Row

R_{\text{brick}} = \tfrac{0.1}{0.7} \approx 0.14,\ \ R_{\text{fibre}} = \tfrac{0.1}{0.04} = 2.5 @@ kelvin per watt
\text{the same } q \text{ passes both: } \Delta T = q R_{\text{brick}} + q R_{\text{fibre}} @@ nothing piles up in between
q = \dfrac{\Delta T}{R_{\text{brick}} + R_{\text{fibre}}} = \dfrac{20}{2.64} \approx 7.6 \text{ W} @@ resistances add
\htmlClass{new}{\text{18 times less than brick alone}} @@ the fibre does nearly all the work

Layer technique: in layers one after another the same heat passes each, so their resistances add, and the best insulator sets the leak. Fibreglass works because it traps air, and still air, k = 0.026, is one of the best insulators there is; a gap of air between two panes of glass is why double glazing helps.

13eng23Glowing in the Dark

Every surface gives off light, mostly too red to see at room temperature, at a rate set by its temperature to the fourth power. Shiny metal gives off and soaks up only a few hundredths as much.

\text{per square metre: } \varepsilon\,\sigma T^4,\ \ \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} @@ T in kelvin; ε from 1 (black) to 0.03 (foil)
\text{walls at 293 K facing walls at 273 K: } \sigma(293^4 - 273^4) \approx 100 \text{ W} @@ black surfaces
\text{with foil, } \varepsilon \approx 0.03:\ \text{a few watts} @@ shiny surfaces barely trade
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a vacuum stops conduction; silvering stops the glow}} @@ the vacuum flask, both at once

Heat crosses empty space only as light, at a rate that grows as the fourth power of temperature and nearly vanishes between shiny surfaces. James Dewar’s flask of 1892, two silvered walls with a vacuum between, is still how coffee stays hot.

13eng24Sheets of Foil in Space

A spacecraft has no air to trap, so it is wrapped in many thin shiny sheets with vacuum between. Put one sheet between a hot surface at Th and a cold one at Tc, all black for simplicity.

\text{steady: the sheet takes in what it gives off} @@ its temperature T₁ settles
q = \sigma(T_h^4 - T_1^4) = \sigma(T_1^4 - T_c^4) @@ the same q across each gap
\text{add: } 2q = \sigma(T_h^4 - T_c^4) @@ T₁ cancels
\htmlClass{new}{N \text{ sheets: } q = \dfrac{\sigma(T_h^4 - T_c^4)}{N + 1}} @@ N + 1 equal gaps, added the same way

Sheet technique: N thin sheets in a vacuum divide the glow passing between two surfaces by N + 1, and shiny sheets divide it far more. Spacecraft blankets stack ten to twenty layers, and the blanket on the Webb telescope’s sunshield, five layers wide as a tennis court, keeps its mirror below 50 K in full sunlight.

13eng25Where Walls Really Leak

\text{paths side by side: their leaks add} @@ each path its own q
\text{wall: } 9.5 \text{ m}^2 \text{ insulated at } 7.6 \text{ W per m}^2 @@ 72 W
\text{plus } 0.5 \text{ m}^2 \text{ bare brick at } 140 \text{ W per m}^2 @@ 70 W
\htmlClass{new}{\text{5\% of the area leaks half the heat}} @@ side by side, the worst path wins

Layers in a row add resistances, so the best layer rules; paths side by side add leaks, so the worst path rules. That is why builders chase gaps, steel studs and window frames, and why a coat with a zip open is barely a coat.

13eng27The Leak a Code Allows, and a Year of Heat

Heat runs down a temperature difference through a resistance, as current runs down a voltage. A building code limits each wall’s leak per square metre per degree, which is one over the sum of the wall’s resistances per square metre, including the thin still air on each face.

R = 0.13 + 0.14 + 2.5 + 0.04 \approx 2.8 \text{ m}^2\text{K/W} @@ inside air film, brick, fibre, outside air film (13eng22)
U = \tfrac{1}{R} \approx 0.36 \text{ W per m}^2 \text{ per K} @@ 
\text{a winter of } 3\,000 \text{ degree-days}:\ 0.36 \times 100 \times 3\,000 \times 24 \approx 2.6 \times 10^6 \text{ Wh} @@ each day counts its degrees below 18 °C
\htmlClass{new}{\text{about } 2\,600 \text{ kWh a year through this wall}} @@ double the fibre: about 1,400

Year-of-heat technique: the wall’s leak per square metre per degree is one over its summed resistances, and a winter’s heat through it is that leak times its area times the winter’s degree-days times 24 hours. Shown in a test box: a wall panel between a warm room held at 20 °C and a cold room at 0 °C passes a steady heat flow, metered by the power needed to keep the warm side warm, of exactly ΔT/R per square metre.

Accepted names

Heat through matter is conduction (Fourier’s law, q = kAΔT/L, k the thermal conductivity); carried by moving fluid, convection; as light, thermal radiation (the Stefan-Boltzmann law, ε the emissivity). R is the thermal resistance, sold per area as an R-value, and its inverse as a U-value. The foil stack is multi-layer insulation (MLI); a gap in insulation is a thermal bridge.

Who said it first

Joseph Fourier published the law of heat conduction in 1822. Josef Stefan found the fourth-power law from measurements in 1879 and Ludwig Boltzmann derived it in 1884. James Dewar made the vacuum flask in 1892.
