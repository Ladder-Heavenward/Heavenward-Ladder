What Is the Quickest Way to Sort a List?

What is the quickest way to sort a list? A machine sorting a list can only compare two items at a time and act on the answer. So a fair measure of a method's cost is how many comparisons it makes. This page counts them for three methods. Two take about half the square of the number of items in comparisons, by 4k’s sum. The third splits the list in half, sorts the halves, and zips them together, and it takes at most about the number of items times the number of halvings. With a million items that is the difference between a few seconds and a few days. Then the other way to go faster, many machines at once, and the technique that caps what they can do.

Selection sort always takes the same number of comparisons, about half the square of the count. Insertion sort takes from one fewer than the count to the same as selection sort, about a quarter of the square on average. Merge sort takes at most about the count times the number of halvings. When only part of the work can be split over many machines, the part that cannot be split caps the speedup, however many machines there are.

17comp1The Cost of a Sort

A list of n numbers is to be put in rising order. A method for doing it, written as steps a machine can follow, has the accepted name an algorithm. The word comes from a scholar of Baghdad, whose book on reckoning was read in Latin as Algoritmi de numero Indorum. A sorting method that learns about the items only by comparing two of them is a comparison sort. Its cost is the number of comparisons it makes, as a function of n. How that number grows has the accepted name the method's time complexity.

17comp2Selection Sort

Look through all n items for the least, and swap it into the first place. Then look through the other n − 1 for the least of those, and swap it into the second place. Continue until one item is left. Its accepted name is selection sort.

Selection-count technique: on every list of n ≥ 1 items, selection sort makes exactly n(n − 1)/2 comparisons.

\text{the least of } m \text{ items}:\ m - 1 \text{ comparisons} @@ keep the least so far; compare it with each of the rest once
(n - 1) + (n - 2) + \cdots + 1 + 0 @@ the passes over n, n − 1, …, 1 items, whatever the items are
= \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ doubled-sum (4k) with n − 1 in place of n

17comp3Insertion Sort

Keep the front of the list sorted. Take the next item and walk it toward the front, comparing it with each item before it, and stop when the one before is not larger. Its accepted name is insertion sort. A pair of items in the wrong order, the larger first, has the accepted name an inversion.

Insertion-range technique: on a list of n items, insertion sort makes at least n − 1 and at most n(n − 1)/2 comparisons. It makes n − 1 on a sorted list and n(n − 1)/2 on a list in falling order.

\text{item } i \text{ (from } 2 \text{ to } n\text{)}:\ 1 \text{ to } i - 1 \text{ comparisons} @@ at least one look back; at most one for each item before it
(n - 1) \cdot 1 \le \text{count} \le 1 + 2 + \cdots + (n - 1) @@ add over the items
\htmlClass{new}{n - 1 \le \text{count} \le \tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ doubled-sum; a sorted list stops each walk at once, a falling list walks each item to the front

Insertion-average technique: over all n! orders of n different items, the average number of inversions is n(n − 1)/4, and insertion sort's comparisons are at least the inversions and at most the inversions plus n − 1.

\begin{aligned}&\text{each step of a walk swaps}\\ &\quad\text{one inverted neighbour pair}\end{aligned} @@ the swap removes exactly that inversion, and no other pair changes order
\text{comparisons} = \text{inversions} + (\text{walks that stop early}) @@ every comparison but a walk's last is a swap
\text{a list and its reverse}:\ \text{inv} + \text{inv} = \tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2} @@ each of the n(n − 1)/2 pairs is out of order in exactly one of the two
\text{average over the } n! \text{ orders} = \htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{4}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the orders split into reversed pairs (arrangements, 4k); each pair averages n(n − 1)/4

17comp4Merge Sort

Two sorted lists can be merged into one by looking only at their fronts: compare the two front items, move the smaller to the output, repeat. Each comparison moves one item. When one list runs out, the rest of the other follows with no comparison. So merging s items with t items takes at most s + t − 1 comparisons. Split a list in half, sort each half by the same method, and merge. Its accepted name is merge sort; a method that uses itself on smaller pieces has the accepted name recursive. It was written out in 1945, among the first programs for a stored-program machine.

Merge-count technique: for n = 2ᵏ, merge sort makes at most M(n) comparisons, where M(1) = 0 and M(2n) = 2M(n) + 2n − 1. So M(n) = n log₂ n − n + 1.

M(2^{k+1}) \le 2M(2^k) + 2^{k+1} - 1 @@ sort two halves of 2ᵏ, then merge 2ᵏ⁺¹ items
\varphi(k):\ M(2^k) = k2^k - 2^k + 1 @@ the claim, for whole k
\varphi(0):\ M(1) = 0 = 0 - 1 + 1 @@ one item needs no comparison
2(k2^k - 2^k + 1) + 2^{k+1} - 1 = (k + 1)2^{k+1} - 2^{k+1} + 1 @@ the step, assuming φ(k)
\forall k\ \varphi(k):\ \ \htmlClass{new}{M(n) = n\log_2 n - n + 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction; log₂ 2ᵏ = k (log-base-p, 17g)
  
n = 1024:\ \ \tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2} = 523{,}776;\ \ M(n) = 9217 @@ about 57 times fewer
n = 2^{20} \approx 10^6:\ \ 5.5 \times 10^{11} \text{ against } 2.0 \times 10^7 @@ at 10⁸ comparisons a second, 90 minutes against a fifth of a second
\tfrac{n\log_2 n}{n(n - 1)/2} = \tfrac{2}{\ln 2}\cdot\tfrac{\ln n}{n - 1} \to 0 @@ log-loses-to-powers with p = 1: no fixed factor makes up the gap

17comp5Splitting the Work

Another way to finish sooner is to give parts of a job to several processors at once. Working one step after another has the accepted name sequential computing. Working on parts at the same time on one machine is parallel computing. Spreading the work over many machines joined by a network is distributed computing. The speedup is the sequential time divided by the parallel time. Some part of almost every job cannot be split: reading the input, the final merge, waiting on the slowest piece.

Speed-up-limit technique: let a job take time 1 on one processor. Let a fraction p of it, with 0 ≤ p < 1, be shared evenly among N processors, while the rest cannot be shared. Then the speedup is S(N) = 1/((1 − p) + p/N). S rises with N, is always less than 1/(1 − p), and heads to 1/(1 − p) as N grows. Its accepted name is Amdahl's law.

\text{time on } N:\ (1 - p) + \tfrac pN @@ the part that cannot be shared, plus the shared part divided N ways
S(N) = \tfrac{1}{(1 - p) + p/N} @@ ﹀ “speedup”
\tfrac pN \gt 0 \Rightarrow S(N) \lt \tfrac{1}{1 - p} @@ the bottom is more than 1 − p (when p > 0); 8d
\tfrac pN \to 0 \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{S(N) \to \tfrac{1}{1 - p}} \quad \blacksquare @@ rung 10's limits of sums and quotients
  
p = 0.95:\ \ S(8) \approx 5.9,\ \ S(64) \approx 15.4,\ \ \text{cap } 20 @@ a job 5% unshared never runs more than 20 times faster
\text{merge sort on two processors}:\ \ M(\tfrac n2) + (n - 1) @@ the halves at once, then one merge: the merge is the unshared part

In 1967 a designer of large computers argued that effort spent on making many processors work at once is wasted. It is wasted unless the part of the work done one step at a time is sped up by nearly as much. That part, he said, soon sets the pace.

Who said it first

The word algorithm is from the name of Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwārizmī. Merge sort was written out by John von Neumann in 1945.

Knuth reports that computer makers of the 1960s estimated more than a quarter of their machines’ running time went to sorting, and argues that sorting is an ideal subject for learning how algorithms are designed and their costs analysed.

Donald Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, vol. 3 (1973); paraphrase

Amdahl argued that effort spent on making many processors work at once is wasted unless the part of the work done one step at a time is sped up by nearly as much, because that part soon sets the pace.

Gene Amdahl, Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large scale computing capabilities (1967); paraphrase
