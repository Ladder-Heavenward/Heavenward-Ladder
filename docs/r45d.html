45d · proof

Pinned Down by Arrows

Rung two built a pair in order out of sets, and then proved the one fact anyone ever uses about it: two pairs are equal exactly when their first parts are equal and their second parts are equal. Its standing questions tried other ways of building a pair, and found that some work and some do not. The ones that work all have that one fact. This step turns the fact into a description that names no members at all. A product of two things is a thing with an arrow to each, such that any other thing with an arrow to each has exactly one arrow into it that agrees with both. For sets it is the set of pairs. For statements ordered by if, it is and. For groups it is pairs multiplied entry by entry. Turn every arrow round and the same words describe a sum: for sets two copies side by side, for statements or. Such a description does not say what the thing is made of. It pins the thing down anyway, up to exactly one matching, and that is the sameness the rung is about.

A product of two things is a thing with an arrow to each, through which every other such pair of arrows runs in exactly one way. A sum is the same with every arrow turned round. Sets give pairs and side-by-side copies, statements give and and or, groups give pairs. Any two products of the same two things are matched by exactly one arrow that agrees with the two arrows out.

45d1A Product, by Its Arrows

Let A and B be things of a category C. A product of A and B is a thing P with arrows p₁ : P → A and p₂ : P → B, such that for every thing X and arrows f : X → A and g : X → B there is exactly one arrow u : X → P with p₁ ∘ u = f and p₂ ∘ u = g. Write ⟨f, g⟩ for that u. A description of this kind, a thing with arrows such that every rival runs through it in exactly one way, is a universal property.

Pair-map technique: in Set, the set of pairs A × B, with the maps to the first and the second entry, is a product of A and B.

p_1(x, y) = x,\ \ p_2(x, y) = y @@ 2g4: reading a pair back
f : X \to A,\ g : X \to B;\ \ u(z) = (f(z), g(z)) @@ a function into A × B (2g10: the pair exists)
p_1(u(z)) = f(z),\ \ p_2(u(z)) = g(z) @@ u runs f and g through P
u' \text{ with } p_1 \circ u' = f,\ p_2 \circ u' = g \Rightarrow u'(z) \text{ has parts } f(z), g(z) @@ suppose a second
\htmlClass{new}{u' = u} \quad \blacksquare @@ the equal-pairs technique (2g5), at each z; equal functions (9a3)

The product in Grp is G × H with (g, h)(g′, h′) = (g g′, h h′): the same u keeps products when f and g do. In Top it is 34b3’s box product. In the category of statements ordered by ⇒ (45a2), an arrow X → A is “X ⇒ A”, and the product of P and Q is P ∧ Q: P ∧ Q ⇒ P and P ∧ Q ⇒ Q by keep either part, and from X ⇒ P and X ⇒ Q, X ⇒ P ∧ Q by join (1c). In any order, the product of two things is their greatest lower bound, when there is one.

45d2Unique up to One Matching

The description names no members, so it cannot say which set P is. It says something stronger instead.

One-matching technique: if P and P′ are both products of A and B, exactly one arrow from P′ to P agrees with the arrows out, and that arrow is a matching.

u = \langle p'_1, p'_2 \rangle : P' \to P,\ \ u' = \langle p_1, p_2 \rangle' : P \to P' @@ each is a product: run the other’s arrows through it; exactly one each
p_1 \circ (u \circ u') = p'_1 \circ u' = p_1,\ \ p_2 \circ (u \circ u') = p_2 @@ brackets free; each runs p₁, p₂ through
p_1 \circ \mathrm{id}_P = p_1,\ \ p_2 \circ \mathrm{id}_P = p_2 @@ so does the do-nothing arrow
u \circ u' = \mathrm{id}_P @@ exactly one arrow P → P runs p₁ and p₂ through P
u' \circ u = \mathrm{id}_{P'} @@ the same, at P′
\htmlClass{new}{u \text{ is a matching, and the only arrow that agrees}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 45a3

So any two products are matched, and in exactly one way that respects what makes them products. The pair of 2g, and any other build whose pairs have the equal-pairs property, give matched products; nothing about the product can tell them apart. That is what “pinned down by arrows” means: not one thing, but one thing up to exactly one matching. The trick of the proof, using “exactly one” on the thing itself to force an arrow to be id, is the whole engine of this rung.

45d3A Sum, the Arrows Turned Round

A sum of A and B is a product of A and B in C^op (45b3): a thing S with arrows i₁ : A → S and i₂ : B → S, such that for every X and arrows f : A → X and g : B → X there is exactly one u : S → X with u ∘ i₁ = f and u ∘ i₂ = g. Write [f, g] for it. The one-matching technique, turned round, says any two sums are matched in exactly one way.

Cases-map technique: in Set, two copies side by side, A ⊔ B, with each member sent to its copy, is a sum of A and B: a function out of it is a function on A and one on B, taken case by case.

i_1(x) = (x, 0),\ \ i_2(y) = (y, 1) @@ 35e1: A ⊔ B = (A × {0}) ∪ (B × {1})
u(x, 0) = f(x),\ \ u(y, 1) = g(y) @@ each member of A ⊔ B is in one copy only: one output each (9a1)
u \circ i_1 = f,\ \ u \circ i_2 = g @@ by the definition
u' \circ i_1 = f,\ u' \circ i_2 = g \Rightarrow u'(x, 0) = f(x),\ u'(y, 1) = g(y) @@ suppose a second
\htmlClass{new}{u' = u} \quad \blacksquare @@ they agree on each copy, and the copies are all of A ⊔ B

In statements ordered by ⇒, the sum of P and Q is P ∨ Q: add an option gives P ⇒ P ∨ Q, and from P ⇒ X and Q ⇒ X, cases worked inside the line (1c) give P ∨ Q ⇒ X. A thing with exactly one arrow to every thing is the sum of nothing at all: in Set it is ∅, with the empty function, and in statements it is ⊥, since ⊥ ⇒ X for every X (1b: when nothing is true, everything is permitted). In Grp the sum of ℤ and ℤ is not ℤ × ℤ. It is the free group on two letters, the loop group of 34i3’s figure eight, and 45i counts arrows to prove the two differ.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A universal property defines an object up to unique isomorphism; the one-matching technique is that uniqueness. A product is a categorical product, ⟨f, g⟩ the pairing; a sum is a coproduct, [f, g] the copairing, A ⊔ B the disjoint union. The empty product is a terminal object; the empty sum an initial object. In a partial order the product is the meet and the coproduct the join; in the category of statements and implications they are conjunction and disjunction, and the initial object is falsum. The coproduct of groups is the free product, ℤ ∗ ℤ for the figure eight.

Who said it first

Products and coproducts by arrows were set out by Saunders Mac Lane in “Duality for groups” (1950), where he noticed that the direct product and the free product of groups are dual. Pierre Samuel described universal mappings in 1948, and Nicolas Bourbaki gave “universal problems” a chapter in the 1950s. Kazimierz Kuratowski’s pair {{x}, {x, y}} of 1921 is the build of 2g, and Norbert Wiener’s of 1914, {{{x}, ∅}, {{y}}}, is another that has the equal-pairs property.

A proof that is not allowed

“A × B and B × A are both products of A and B, so A × B = B × A.” Not allowed: with the arrows out swapped, B × A is a product of A and B, and the one-matching technique gives the matching (x, y) ↦ (y, x), which is not the sameness of rung 0. For A = {1} and B = {2} the two sets are {(1, 2)} and {(2, 1)}, which are different sets.
