45d · technique

The Zero-Place Technique

Over the line of real numbers, x² + 1 = 0 has no answer, though 1 is not in the ideal (x² + 1). Over the turning numbers that cannot happen: if equations have no common zero, the ideal they make holds 1, so a combination of them is the plain contradiction 1 = 0. This is Hilbert’s zero-place technique, and it makes shapes and ideals a true dictionary. This step states it, matches points with largest ideals, and finds which ideals come back from their shapes.

Over ℂ: V(J) is empty exactly when 1 ∈ J. The points of ℂⁿ match the largest ideals (x₁ − a₁, …, xₙ − aₙ). And f vanishes on V(J) exactly when some power fᵐ lies in J: I(V(J)) is the root of J.

45d1No Zeros Means 1 Is in the Ideal

1 \in J \Rightarrow V(J) = \varnothing @@ 1 is never 0
V(J) = \varnothing \Rightarrow 1 \in J @@ the hard direction; held in trust
J = (x, \ xy - 1):\ \ 1 = y\cdot x - (xy - 1) @@ x = 0 and xy = 1 cannot both hold
\htmlClass{new}{\text{over } \mathbb R:\ (x^2 + 1) \text{ has no zeros, and } 1 \notin (x^2 + 1)} @@ the turning numbers are needed

Weak zero-place technique: over the turning numbers, polynomial equations have no common answer exactly when 1 is a combination of them. Held in trust: the proof needs that a field made by finitely many members over ℂ is ℂ itself.

45d2Points Are Largest Ideals

M_a = (x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n) = \{f : f(a) = 0\} @@ divide by each xᵢ − aᵢ in turn (44c)
\mathbb C[x]/M_a \cong \mathbb C,\ f \mapsto f(a) @@ a field, so Mₐ is largest (44b)
M \text{ largest} \Rightarrow V(M) \ne \varnothing \Rightarrow M \subseteq M_a \Rightarrow M = M_a @@ the weak technique, then largest
\htmlClass{new}{\text{points of } \mathbb C^n \leftrightarrow \text{largest ideals of } \mathbb C[x_1, \ldots, x_n]} @@ a one-to-one matching

Point-ideal technique: the largest ideals of ℂ[x₁, …, xₙ] are exactly the ideals of points, (x₁ − a₁, …, xₙ − aₙ).

45d3What Comes Back From a Shape

f^m \in J \Rightarrow f^m = 0 \text{ on } V(J) \Rightarrow f = 0 \text{ there} @@ the root of J vanishes on V(J)
f = 0 \text{ on } V(J) \Rightarrow f^m \in J \text{ for some } m @@ add a letter: V(J, 1 − tf) = ∅, then the weak technique
J = (x^2):\ V(J) = \{0\},\ I(V(J)) = (x) @@ x is not in (x²), but x² is
\htmlClass{new}{I(V(J)) = \sqrt J = \{f : f^m \in J \text{ for some } m\}} @@ the strong technique

Strong zero-place technique: over the turning numbers, a polynomial vanishes on the shape of J exactly when one of its powers lies in J; so shapes match, one to one, the ideals equal to their own roots.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

This is Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, weak and strong forms; the root of J is its radical, and ideals equal to their radicals are radical ideals. Adding the letter t is the Rabinowitsch trick.

Who said it first

David Hilbert proved it in 1893; J. L. Rabinowitsch gave the one-line passage from weak to strong in 1929.
