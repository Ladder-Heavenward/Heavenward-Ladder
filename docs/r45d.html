45d · proof

Order Without a Clock

With no shared clock, “before” has to come from the messages. This step defines the only before that matters, the one along which one event can affect another, and two ways to stamp events with it.

e comes before f if a chain of steps and messages leads from e to f. A counter respects that order; a list of counters captures it.

45d1Happened Before

Say e → f when e and f are on one machine and e comes first, or e sends a message that f receives, or a chain of these leads from e to f. Two events with neither e → f nor f → e are concurrent: neither could have affected the other.

The arrow is an order that leaves some pairs uncompared. It never runs from an event to itself, and chains of it are again arrows.

45d2Counters

Each machine keeps a counter. It adds 1 before each event, and puts the counter on each message it sends. On receipt it sets its counter to the larger of its own and the one received, then adds 1.

Counter technique: if e → f then L(e) < L(f).

Along a machine the counter rises at each event. Across a message the receipt is stamped above the sending. So along any chain the stamps rise. The converse fails: L(e) < L(f) can hold for concurrent events.

45d3Lists of Counters

Each machine keeps a list of n counters, one per machine. It adds 1 to its own entry at each event, and sends its list on each message. On receipt it takes the larger of each pair of entries, then adds 1 to its own.

List-stamp technique: e → f exactly when V(e) ≤ V(f) in every entry and V(e) ≠ V(f).

Entry i of V(f) counts the events of machine i that lead to f. So V(e) ≤ V(f) everywhere says every event leading to e leads to f, and that holds just when e itself leads to f. Two lists neither of which is below the other mark concurrent events.

45d4Why Not Just Clocks

Real clocks drift apart. Order by clock times can put a reply before the question it answers. Stamps follow cause instead of time, and cause is what a program’s correctness depends on.
