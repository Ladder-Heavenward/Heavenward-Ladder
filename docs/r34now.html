34·now · the research front

Can loops tell you the shape of space?

Can loops tell you the shape of space? An ant on a closed surface, which can never leave it or see it from outside, can still learn its shape. It lays a loop of thread, and tries to slide the loop to a point without lifting it off the surface. On a sphere every loop slides to a point. On a doughnut a loop round the hole never does. 34f listed every closed surface, and 34i’s counts tell them apart, so on a surface the loops decide: if every loop slides to a point, the surface is a sphere. Space round us is three-dimensional, and could be closed, like the surface of a ball but one dimension up. In 1904 the question was asked whether loops decide there too. It stood for a century. The answer came by a road no one expected: let the ruler of the space flow, the way heat flows in 33a, smoothing its bends, until the shape shows. Where the flow pinches, cut. This page builds the parts that can be built here and states the rest.

A closed surface whose loops all slide to a point is a sphere: the surface list and the turn-about count leave nothing else. The three-dimensional sphere is two solid balls glued along their rims, and its loops all slide to a point; whether it is the only closed three-dimensional shape with that property was asked in 1904. Letting the ruler change at a rate set by its bends makes a heat flow for the ruler. Under it a round sphere shrinks, with r² falling at a steady pace, 2 for a surface and 4 in three dimensions, and a thin neck pinches before the round end it joins when its radius is below 1/√2 of the end’s. Cutting at the pinches and flowing on was carried through in 2002–03, and settled the question: yes.

34now1On a surface, loops decide

34f put every closed surface in one piece, cut into triangles, on one list: the sphere, g tori summed, k cross-caps summed. 34i counted their loop groups with the letters allowed to change places.

Sphere-loops technique: a closed surface in one piece, cut into triangles, on which every loop slides to a point, is the same shape as the sphere.

S \text{ is the sphere, or } g \text{ tori summed, or } k \text{ cross-caps summed} @@ the surface-list technique (34f)
g \text{ tori}:\ \text{the loop group, letters changing places, is lists of } 2g \text{ whole numbers} @@ the turn-about-count technique (34i)
k \text{ cross-caps}:\ k - 1 \text{ whole numbers and a member of a group of two} @@ the same; for k = 1, that member alone
\text{every loop slides to a point} \Rightarrow \text{the loop group has one member} @@ so its count with letters changing places has one member too
\htmlClass{new}{2g = 0 \text{ and no cross-caps: } S \text{ is the sphere}} \quad \blacksquare @@ every other entry on the list has a loop that does not slide

So an ant needs no outside view. Lay threads, try to slide them: the sphere is told from everything else by its loops alone. And the loops do more. The count of the loop group, letters changing places, gives 2g or k, and with it 34f’s characteristic, so loops tell every closed surface from every other.

34now2One dimension up

The three-sphere is the set of points at distance 1 from 0 in the space of arrows with four entries: x² + y² + z² + w² = 1. Near each of its points it looks like ordinary space, flat up close as in 29h, but it is closed: finitely covered, with no rim. A space that is flat up close in three directions, closed and in one piece is a closed three-dimensional shape.

Ball-pair technique: the three-sphere is two solid balls glued point by point along their rim spheres, and every loop on it slides to a point.

w \ge 0 \text{ and } w \le 0:\ \text{two halves, each the ball } x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1 @@ drop w: each half matches the ball, same shape (34e); the rims meet on w = 0, a sphere
U = \text{all but } (0,0,0,-1),\ V = \text{all but } (0,0,0,1) @@ two open sets that cover it
U \text{ and } V \text{ are the same shape as ordinary space} @@ the push from the missing point onto the space w = 0, as a map of the globe is drawn from a pole
\text{the loop groups of } U \text{ and } V \text{ have one member} @@ the straight-slide technique (34g)
U \cap V \text{ is joined by paths} @@ a sphere times an open stretch
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the loop group of the three-sphere has one member}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gluing rule (34i): no letters, so nothing

The same proof, one dimension down, shows the ordinary sphere’s loops all slide to a point. In 1904 the question was put: is every closed three-dimensional shape whose loops all slide to a point the same shape as the three-sphere? The analogue in five dimensions and more was settled in 1961, and in four dimensions in 1982, both yes. Three dimensions held out. There is too much room to cut along, as on a surface, and too little to move things out of each other’s way, as in five.

34now3A ruler that flows like heat

A closed three-dimensional shape can carry many rulers ds² (29d), each making it lumpy in its own way. The plan is to pick one, then let it change by its own bends, smoothing them, until the shape is plain to see. For each direction at a point, add the surface bends (29e) of the planes through that direction and each other main direction: call this the direction bend. Then let the ruler change at the rate of minus twice its direction bend: ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric, where g is the ruler and Ric the direction bends, in their accepted letters. A direction bent like a sphere’s shrinks, and one bent like a saddle’s grows.

Heat-ruler technique: on a surface, with a ruler of the form e^(2u)(dx² + dy²), the flow of the ruler by its bends is ∂u/∂t = e^(−2u) Δu, a heat equation.

\text{on a surface, each direction lies in one plane: Ric} = K\,g @@ the direction bend is the surface bend K
g = e^{2u}(dx^2 + dy^2) \Rightarrow K = -e^{-2u}\Delta u @@ the remarkable technique (29e) with E = G = e^{2u}, F = 0: a page of partial slopes, held in trust here
\partial_t g = 2\,\partial_t u\,e^{2u}(dx^2 + dy^2) = -2K\,e^{2u}(dx^2 + dy^2) @@ the flow, written out
\htmlClass{new}{\partial_t u = -K = e^{-2u}\Delta u} \quad \blacksquare @@ 33a’s heat equation, with a pace e^(−2u) that changes from point to point

So the bends of a ruler spread out like heat in a bar. A hot spot of bend cools into its neighbours, and on a closed surface the flow, held in trust here, carries every ruler, after a shrinking that the next footing measures, to one of constant bend. In three dimensions the flow is still a heat equation for the ruler, but no longer the plain one, and it can make spots of bend run away to infinity in finite time.

34now4Round spheres shrink, necks pinch

A round sphere of radius r has surface bend 1/r² in every plane at every point. In n dimensions each direction lies in n − 1 main planes with the others, so its direction bend is (n − 1)/r², times the ruler.

Shrinking-sphere technique: under the flow a round sphere of n dimensions stays round, with r² = r₀² − 2(n − 1)t: it vanishes at t = r₀²/(2(n − 1)).

g = r^2 \times (\text{the ruler of the sphere of radius } 1) @@ a round sphere’s ruler, scaled
\text{Ric} = \tfrac{n-1}{r^2}\,g = (n-1) \times (\text{the ruler of radius } 1) @@ the direction bend, at every point alike
\tfrac{d(r^2)}{dt} = -2(n-1) @@ the flow: ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric, read off the factor
\htmlClass{new}{r^2 = r_0^2 - 2(n-1)\,t} \quad \blacksquare @@ for a surface 2t, for the three-sphere 4t

Now a long neck: a thin tube in three dimensions whose cross-sections are spheres of radius ρ, a sphere times a line. Along the sphere the direction bend is 1/ρ², from the one plane inside the sphere, and 0 from the plane with the line. Along the line every plane through it is flat. So the line keeps its length and the sphere factor shrinks as a surface does: ρ² = ρ₀² − 2t.

Neck-pinch technique: a thin neck of radius ρ joining two round ends of radius R pinches before the ends vanish when ρ < R/√2. The neck goes at ρ²/2 and an end, a round three-sphere, at R²/4.

This is the trouble and the cure. A dumbbell does not flow to a round sphere: its neck pinches first. But at a pinch the shape is nearly a sphere times a line, so it can be cut there, the two cut ends capped with balls, and the flow run on. The pieces are simpler than the whole, and the whole is them, glued back along spheres.

34now5Cut, flow on, and what is still open

The road was laid out in 1982: start from any ruler on a closed three-dimensional shape whose loops all slide to a point, flow it, and show that it shrinks to a round point, or pinches only in ways that can be cut. What could not be shown for twenty years was that nothing worse than necks ever forms, and that the cutting does not have to be done infinitely often in finite time.

Both were proved in 2002–03, in three papers posted where anyone could read them, without submission to a journal. Several teams spent three years checking and filling in the details, and by 2006 the proof was accepted. With it came more: every closed three-dimensional shape cuts, along spheres and along tori, into pieces each carrying one of eight kinds of even geometry, the round, the flat, the saddle and five more. For a shape whose loops all slide to a point there are no tori to cut along and the flow ends in round pieces glued by spheres: it is the three-sphere. A prize for the solution was offered in 2010 and declined, and so was a medal in 2006.

Held in trust, then: a closed three-dimensional shape whose loops all slide to a point is the same shape as the three-sphere. In four dimensions the question has a second form, smooth: is every smooth four-dimensional shape that is the same shape as the four-sphere also the same smooth shape? It is open. In every other dimension the answers are known, and in some they are no.

And space? If the universe is closed, light from far away could reach us round two ways at once, and the map of the oldest light in the sky would carry matching circles. The searches have found none, out to the scale of the visible universe. So loops could tell us the shape of space, if they are short enough to see round.

The accepted names

The sphere-loops technique is the case of surfaces of the Poincaré conjecture, which follows from the classification of surfaces; a shape whose loops all slide to a point is simply connected. The three-sphere is S³, and a closed three-dimensional shape is a closed 3-manifold. The 1904 question is the Poincaré conjecture, and its higher analogues are the generalized Poincaré conjecture. The direction bend is the Ricci curvature, and the flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric is Ricci flow; the cutting and capping is surgery. The cutting along spheres and tori into eight geometries is Thurston’s geometrization conjecture, now a theorem. The open question in four dimensions is the smooth four-dimensional Poincaré conjecture. The matching circles are the circles-in-the-sky test of cosmic topology.

Who said it first

Henri Poincaré asked the question in 1904. Stephen Smale settled five dimensions and up in 1961, and Michael Freedman four dimensions in 1982. Richard Hamilton introduced Ricci flow in 1982, and showed in 1988 that on a surface it makes the ruler round, with Bennett Chow completing the case of the sphere in 1991. William Thurston proposed geometrization in 1982. Grigori Perelman posted three papers in 2002 and 2003; Bruce Kleiner and John Lott, John Morgan and Gang Tian, and Huai-Dong Cao and Xi-Ping Zhu published checks by 2006. Perelman declined the Fields Medal in 2006 and the Millennium Prize of the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2010. Neil Cornish, David Spergel, Glenn Starkman and Eiichiro Komatsu searched the microwave sky for matching circles in 2004.

Wrong moves, worked

“A closed three-dimensional shape has characteristic 0, so the characteristic cannot tell it from the three-sphere; but the loop group of the three-sphere has one member, and so does every shape with no holes, so they are all the three-sphere.” Not allowed: the first half is right, and the second assumes what was asked. Whether one member is enough was the whole question, and it took the flow to answer.
