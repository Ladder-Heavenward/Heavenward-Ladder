Where will a loaded beam crack?

Where will a loaded beam crack? Builders asked it long before they could answer it, because a beam gives no second try: the test that finds its breaking point breaks it. What makes the question hard is that the load sits all along the beam, and the beam fails at one place. That place is not always under the heaviest load. This page cuts a beam in thought at every point, and asks what keeps each piece still. The answer comes out as two quantities, the shear and the bending, and each is found from the one before by 16c: the shear is an area under the load, and the bending is an area under the shear. The bending is greatest where the shear crosses zero, and that is where a plain beam cracks. Then one more area, taken across the beam's depth, shows why floor joists stand on edge.

The force across the beam at a point is the support’s push less the load so far. The bending there is the area under that force so far. So the force falls as fast as the load is laid on, and the bending changes as fast as the force. The bending is greatest where the force changes from positive to negative. For an even load, the greatest bending is at the middle, and it grows with the square of the span. Across the beam, the greatest strain grows with the width but shrinks with the square of the depth.

A beam, its load, and its supports

Lay a beam of length L on two supports, one at each end. Measure x in metres from the left support. A load spread along the beam presses down with w(x) kilonewtons on each metre: the weight of a floor, of snow, of the beam itself. The supports push up, with R₀ at the left end and R_L at the right.

Two measured laws, from 12·eng, keep a body still. The pushes up equal the pushes down. And the turning effects about any point cancel, where a push P at a distance d from the point turns with P · d. Their accepted names are the balance of forces and the balance of moments. For the whole beam they give the two supports.

R_0 + R_L = \textstyle\int_0^L w(s)\,ds @@ the pushes balance. The load on a thin strip of width Δs near s is about w(s)Δs; strip sums (15h)
R_L \cdot L = \textstyle\int_0^L s\,w(s)\,ds @@ the turning effects about the left end balance: each strip turns with arm s
w \text{ even: } R_0 = R_L = \tfrac{wL}{2} @@ ∫₀ᴸ s ds = L²/2 (the table, 16f)

Cut it in thought

Cut the beam, in thought, at x. The left piece stays still. The support and the load push on it, and the right piece holds it across the cut. Whatever the right piece must supply, the beam must carry at x.

From here on, the shear at x is V(x) = R₀ − ∫₀ˣ w(s) ds: the net push up on the left piece from the support and the load. The bending moment at x is M(x) = R₀x − ∫₀ˣ (x − s) w(s) ds: the net turning effect of those same pushes about the cut. Their accepted names are shear force and bending moment.

The right piece supplies −V across the cut, and a turning effect −M. A positive M makes the beam sag: its bottom face is stretched and its top face squeezed.

Shear-from-load technique: the load is the slope of the shear, with its sign turned. If w is continuous on [0, L], then V′(x) = −w(x) there. It is area-slope (16b), applied to the area under w.

Moment-from-shear technique: the bending at a cut is the area under the shear up to it. If w is continuous on [0, L], then M′(x) = V(x), and M(x) = ∫₀ˣ V(s) ds.

M(x) = R_0 x - x\textstyle\int_0^x w(s)\,ds + \int_0^x s\,w(s)\,ds @@ (x − s)w(s) = x·w(s) − s·w(s); areas-add and areas-scale (15e), with x fixed inside the integral
M'(x) = R_0 - \textstyle\int_0^x w(s)\,ds - x\,w(x) + x\,w(x) @@ product for slopes (13c) on x · ∫₀ˣ w; area-slope (16b) twice, w and s·w continuous
= V(x) @@ the meaning of V
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{M(x) = M(0) + \textstyle\int_0^x V(s)\,ds = \int_0^x V(s)\,ds}\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ area-is-a-change (16c); M(0) = R₀ · 0 − 0 = 0

So the load is run backwards twice, one area at a time: from w to V, then from V to M. The far end checks the books. V(L) = R₀ − ∫₀ᴸ w = −R_L, the right support's push seen from the left piece. And M(L) = L · V(L) + ∫₀ᴸ s w(s) ds = −R_L L + R_L L = 0, as it must be at a free end.

Where it bends most

Most-bending technique: a beam bends most where the shear crosses zero. Let w be continuous with w ≥ 0 on [0, L], and not 0 everywhere. Then V falls, M rises while V > 0 and falls once V < 0, and M is greatest at the point where V changes from + to −.

V(x) = \tfrac{wL}{2} - wx @@ shear-from-load; R₀ = wL/2
M(x) = \textstyle\int_0^x \big(\tfrac{wL}{2} - ws\big)\,ds = \tfrac{w}{2}\,x(L - x) @@ moment-from-shear; the table
V = 0 \text{ at } x = \tfrac L2,\ + \text{ before},\ - \text{ after} @@ most-bending; first-test (14f)
\htmlClass{new}{M_{\max} = M\big(\tfrac L2\big) = \tfrac{wL^2}{8}} @@ the middle bends most

A floor joist in a house in Salinas: Douglas fir, sold as a 2 × 10, which is 38 mm by 235 mm. It spans 4.2 m, with joists 0.4 m apart. Houses are designed for a floor load of about 2.4 kPa, which is 2.4 kN on each square metre: 1.9 for people and furniture, 0.5 for the floor itself. Each joist carries a strip 0.4 m wide, so w = 2.4 × 0.4 = 0.96 kN/m.

w = 0.96 \text{ kN/m},\ \ L = 4.2 \text{ m} @@ 2.4 kPa on a strip 0.4 m wide
R_0 = R_L = \tfrac{0.96 \cdot 4.2}{2} \approx 2.02 \text{ kN} @@ each end bears half
M_{\max} = \tfrac{0.96 \cdot 4.2^2}{8} \approx 2.12 \text{ kN m} @@ at the middle, 2.1 m from each wall

Where the stress gathers

The bending moment is carried across the cut by the wood itself. Above the middle of its depth the wood is squeezed, and below it the wood is stretched. Its accepted name for push per unit of area across a cut is stress, measured in megapascals: 1 MPa is 1 newton on each square millimetre. A measured law, called the stretch law here, says how the stress spreads. While the wood still springs back, the stress at a depth y below the middle is in proportion to y: σ(y) = (2y/h) σ_m for −h/2 ≤ y ≤ h/2, with σ_m the stress at the bottom face.

Deep-beam technique: depth helps a beam by its square, and width only by itself. Let a beam of width b and depth h carry a bending moment M at a cut. Under the stretch law, the greatest stress at the cut is σ_m = 6M/(bh²), at the top and bottom faces.

\text{a strip of depth } \Delta y \text{ at } y:\ \text{push } \sigma(y)\,b\,\Delta y,\ \text{arm } |y| @@ each strip turns about the middle; squeeze above and stretch below turn the same way
M = \textstyle\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} \sigma(y)\,y\,b\,dy = \tfrac{2b\sigma_m}{h} \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} y^2\,dy @@ strip sums (15h); the stretch law; areas-scale (15e)
= \tfrac{2b\sigma_m}{h} \cdot 2 \textstyle\int_0^{h/2} y^2\,dy = \tfrac{2b\sigma_m}{h} \cdot \tfrac{h^3}{12} @@ odd-and-even-areas (16d): y² is even; the table: 2 · (h/2)³/3 = h³/12
\htmlClass{new}{\sigma_m = \tfrac{6M}{bh^2}} \quad \blacksquare @@ rearrange
\textstyle\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} \sigma(y)\,b\,dy = 0 @@ odd-and-even-areas: σ is odd, so squeeze and stretch balance and the beam is not pushed along
  
\text{on edge: } \sigma_m = \tfrac{6 \cdot 2.12 \text{ kN m}}{0.038 \cdot 0.235^2} \approx 6.05 \text{ MPa} @@ deep-beam; b = 38 mm, h = 235 mm
\text{laid flat: } \sigma_m = \tfrac{6 \cdot 2.12 \text{ kN m}}{0.235 \cdot 0.038^2} \approx 37.4 \text{ MPa} @@ the same wood, b and h swapped
\tfrac{37.4}{6.05} \approx 6.2 = \tfrac{235}{38} @@ the ratio of the sides

The design tables give a common grade of Douglas fir, No. 2, a base value of 900 pounds per square inch in bending, about 6.2 MPa. The joist on edge sits just under it. Laid flat, the same piece of wood would carry six times that stress. So joists are tall and thin: the strength grows as bh², and the wood used only as bh. Doubling the depth gives four times the strength for twice the wood. They cannot be made thinner without end. A joist too thin for its depth rolls over sideways under load, which is why carpenters nail short blocks between joists.

Where does a plain beam crack, then? At the face that is stretched, where the bending moment is greatest. For a beam on two supports that is the bottom face, near the middle. Concrete is strong when squeezed and weak when stretched. So a concrete beam on two supports carries steel bars near its bottom face. A balcony that juts from a wall bends the other way, with its top face stretched and its greatest moment at the wall. Its steel goes near the top.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The strength of wood is a law in the ladder's sense: a measured regularity with a known exception rate. No two boards are alike. Knots, the slope of the grain and the moisture move each board's breaking stress up or down. So a grade's strength is not an average. It is set from tests of many boards, at the stress that about 1 board in 20 fails below. That value is then divided by a further factor, a little over 2 for bending, before it goes into the design tables.

State the rule, its range, and the direction it fails past the edge.

The stretch law has its range too. It is true while the wood springs back. Near breaking, the squeezed side yields first, and the stress spreads in a way that is no longer in proportion to depth. The page's 6M/(bh²) is a design rule inside that range, not a prediction of the breaking load.

Test 200 joists of one grade, and 14 break below the stress that 1 in 20 should. Is the grade's value wrong, or this batch?

Who said it first

Galileo set a beam into a wall and asked what weight at its far end would break it. He took the break to happen by turning about the lowest edge of the section in the wall, with all the fibres of the section resisting alike, and concluded that the strength of a beam on edge is to its strength laid flat as its depth is to its width.

Galileo Galilei, Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences (1638), the second day; paraphrase

In 16·eng.4.4, the mathematician of 1638 is Galileo. Edme Mariotte (1686) and Antoine Parent (1713) saw that the fibres near the edge are stretched less than those far from it. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1773) put the neutral line in the middle and found this page's factor. Claude-Louis Navier's lectures of 1826 made the stretch law the engineer's working rule.

Accepted names: the stretch law is the beam's flexure formula, resting on the assumption that plane sections stay plane, and on Hooke's law; the bending and shear drawn along a beam are its shear and moment diagrams. The 1 board in 20 is the fifth percentile, or 5% exclusion value, of ASTM D245 and D1990.
