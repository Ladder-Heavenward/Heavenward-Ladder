29d · proof

The Ruler That Changes

A map of the world in a school atlas puts latitude up the side and longitude along the bottom. A step of one degree of longitude near the equator is about 111 km of ground. Near the top of the Salish Sea it is only about 71 km. The map is the same paper everywhere, but the ruler you need to read real distances off it changes from place to place. This step says exactly what such a ruler is. At each point it is a small grid of three numbers, and from it come every length, every angle and every area. The sphere has one. The disc plane of 29c has one too, and it turns out to be the ruler that made its distances work.

At each point of a surface, three numbers E, F, G give the length of every small step: ds² = E du² + 2F du dv + G dv². Lengths, angles and areas all come from them, and changing the coordinates changes the grid in one fixed way.

29d1Length From Three Numbers

Let r(u, v) be a patch of a surface in space, as in 28n, with edge arrows ru and rv. Put E = ru · ru, F = ru · rv, G = rv · rv. These three functions of (u, v) are the surface’s ruler, written ds² = E du² + 2F du dv + G dv², and the grid [[E, F], [F, G]] is the ruler grid.

Ruler-length technique: a curve t ↦ r(u(t), v(t)) on the patch, for a ≤ t ≤ b, has length ∫ₐᵇ √(E u′² + 2F u′v′ + G v′²) dt.

\frac{d}{dt}\,r(u(t), v(t)) = u^{\prime}\,r_u + v^{\prime}\,r_v @@ chains along several roads (28c)
|u^{\prime} r_u + v^{\prime} r_v|^2 = u^{\prime 2}\,r_u\cdot r_u + 2u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,r_u\cdot r_v + v^{\prime 2}\,r_v\cdot r_v @@ the dot product spreads over sums (27i)
\text{speed} = \sqrt{E u^{\prime 2} + 2F u^{\prime}v^{\prime} + G v^{\prime 2}} @@ E, F, G by name
\htmlClass{new}{\text{length} = \int_a^b \sqrt{E u^{\prime 2} + 2F u^{\prime}v^{\prime} + G v^{\prime 2}}\,dt} \quad \blacksquare @@ distance travelled is the total of speed (18i)

On the sphere of radius R, with latitude φ and longitude λ, r = R(cos φ cos λ, cos φ sin λ, sin φ) (21o’s two turns). Then E = R², F = 0, G = R² cos² φ. A step of dλ along a circle of latitude has length R cos φ dλ, which is why a degree of longitude shrinks toward the poles.

29d2Angles and Areas From the Same Grid

Ruler-angle technique: two directions (a, b) and (c, d) in the uv-plane leave a point of the surface at the angle θ with cos θ = (Eac + F(ad + bc) + Gbd) / (√(Ea² + 2Fab + Gb²) √(Ec² + 2Fcd + Gd²)).

Ruler-area technique: the patch has area ∬ √(EG − F²) du dv.

p = a\,r_u + b\,r_v,\ \ q = c\,r_u + d\,r_v @@ the two directions as arrows on the surface (29d1)
p \cdot q = Eac + F(ad + bc) + Gbd @@ the dot product spreads over sums
\cos\theta = \frac{p \cdot q}{|p|\,|q|} @@ angle-between (27i)
|r_u \times r_v|^2 = |r_u|^2|r_v|^2\sin^2\theta = |r_u|^2|r_v|^2 - (r_u\cdot r_v)^2 @@ cross technique (27i), and sin² = 1 − cos²
|r_u \times r_v| = \sqrt{EG - F^2} @@ E, F, G by name
\htmlClass{new}{\text{area} = \iint \sqrt{EG - F^2}\,du\,dv} \quad \blacksquare @@ area of a patch is ∬|r_u × r_v| (28n)

So everything the surface knows about lengths, angles and areas is in three numbers per point. Nothing in these formulas mentions the space around the surface. A creature living in the surface, measuring with a tape, could find E, F and G without ever leaving it.

29d3Changing the Coordinates

The same surface can be traced with other coordinates (s, t), through (u, v) = T(s, t). The ruler grid changes, but the lengths it gives must not.

Ruler-change technique: if (u, v) = T(s, t) with slope grid J, the ruler grid in s and t is Jᵀ g J, where g is the ruler grid in u and v; and both give the same length to every curve.

q(s,t) = r(T(s,t)),\ \ [\,q_s\ q_t\,] = [\,r_u\ r_v\,]\,J @@ chains (28c): each new edge arrow is a mix of the old
\begin{bmatrix} q_s\cdot q_s & q_s\cdot q_t \\ q_t\cdot q_s & q_t\cdot q_t \end{bmatrix} = J^{\mathsf T} \begin{bmatrix} E & F \\ F & G \end{bmatrix} J @@ dot products of mixes, written as grids (27)
\text{a curve's speed is the same in both} @@ its velocity is the same arrow in space, written two ways
\htmlClass{new}{\text{new grid} = J^{\mathsf T} g J,\ \text{same lengths}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ruler-length (29d1)

And √(det(Jᵀ g J)) = |det J| √(det g), since determinants multiply (27). That is 28n’s patch technique again: the area does not depend on the coordinates.

29d4A Ruler With No Surface Around It

Turn it round. Start with a region of the plane and any grid g = [[E, F], [F, G]] of continuous functions with E > 0 and EG − F² > 0 at every point. Define the length of a curve by the ruler-length technique, and angles and areas by 29d2. No surface in space is needed. The region with its ruler is a geometry of its own.

The disc plane of 29c is one. Give it the ruler ds² = 4(dx² + dy²)/(1 − x² − y²)², that is E = G = 4/(1 − |z|²)², F = 0.

Disc-ruler technique: the disc plane’s moves keep its ruler, and along a diameter the length from 0 to a point at distance ρ from the centre is ln((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)), the distance 29c2 used.

1 - |f_p(z)|^2 = \frac{(1 - |z|^2)(1 - |p|^2)}{|1 - \bar p z|^2} @@ 29c1, line 1, divided by |1 − p̄z|²
|c| = \frac{1 - |p|^2}{|1 - \bar p z|^2} @@ the size of fₚ’s slope number (29c1, product-size 25c)
\frac{2\,|c|\,|dz|}{1 - |f_p(z)|^2} = \frac{2\,|dz|}{1 - |z|^2} @@ divide the two lines: a small step’s ruler length is the same after the move
\int_0^{\rho} \frac{2\,ds}{1 - s^2} = \int_0^{\rho} \Big(\frac{1}{1+s} + \frac{1}{1-s}\Big) ds = \ln\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho} @@ split the fraction (17k), then the log techniques (17)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{moves keep lengths; } d(0,\rho) = \ln\tfrac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the lines above

The length grows without bound as ρ heads to 1, so the edge is endlessly far away. To a creature in the disc plane, its world has no edge. It is only our flat drawing that squeezes it into a disc.

The accepted names

The ruler is the metric, or first fundamental form, and E, F, G are its coefficients; ds² is the line element. A region with a ruler of its own is a Riemannian metric on that region, and the disc plane’s ruler is the Poincaré metric. The rule Jᵀ g J is how the metric transforms as a tensor. The ruler-area total is the area element √(EG − F²) du dv.

Who said it first

Carl Friedrich Gauss wrote ds² with E, F and G in his Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas of 1827, after years of surveying. Bernhard Riemann’s lecture of 1854, On the hypotheses which lie at the foundations of geometry, took the ruler as the starting point for spaces of any number of dimensions, with no surrounding space needed. Henri Poincaré gave the disc and its ruler in 1882.
