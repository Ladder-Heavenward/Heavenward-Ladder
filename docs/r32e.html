32e · proof

Breaking a Signal Into Frequencies

Most signals do not repeat. A clap in a hall, one word spoken into a phone, the flash of a lighthouse seen once: each starts, does its business and fades. The turnings of this rung were built for things that go round again. What happens when the repeat is made longer and longer, a minute, an hour, a year, until it never comes? The speeds of the turnings that fit a repeat crowd closer together as it grows, and in the end every speed is allowed, not just the whole ones. The list of coefficients becomes a function of the speed, found by one total over the whole line. That function says how much of each frequency the signal holds. This step builds it, shows that it can be undone to get the signal back, and proves that it keeps energy just as the coefficients did. Along the way the bell curve of the statistics floor turns up in a new part: of all signals, it is the one whose frequencies have the same shape as itself.

For a signal with a finite total size, the transform at a frequency is the total of the signal times a turning of that frequency, run backwards. It is bounded and continuous. The bell e to the minus π x squared is its own transform, because both obey the same rate rule with the same start. A transform can be undone by the forward total with the turning run forwards, and the total of the square of a signal equals the total of the square of its transform.

It is what 32a’s coefficients become as the repeat grows. Cut f off outside [−L, L] and repeat it every 2L: the turnings that fit have frequencies n/(2L), and its coefficients are f̂(n/(2L))/(2L), up to the part of f beyond L. As L grows, the frequencies fill the line. The accepted name of the transform is the Fourier transform.

32e3Undoing the Transform

Undo-transform technique: if f and f̂ both have finite total sizes, then f(x) = ∫ f̂(ξ) e^(2πixξ) dξ at every x where f is continuous. The turning runs forwards to undo what it did backwards.

\int \hat f(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i x\xi}\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,d\xi = \int f(y)\Big(\int e^{2\pi i(x - y)\xi}\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,d\xi\Big)dy @@ a bell put in to tame the total; either order (30e): |f(y)|e^(−πε²ξ²) has a finite total on the plane
\int e^{2\pi i u\xi}\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,d\xi = w_\varepsilon(u) = \tfrac1\varepsilon\,e^{-\pi u^2/\varepsilon^2} @@ slide ξ = η/ε; the own-transform technique (32e2); the bell is even, so the turn’s direction does not matter
w_\varepsilon \ge 0,\ \ \int w_\varepsilon = 1,\ \ w_\varepsilon(u) \le \tfrac1\varepsilon e^{-\pi\delta^2/\varepsilon^2} \text{ for } |u| \ge \delta @@ a stretched bell
\int f(x - u)\,w_\varepsilon(u)\,du \to f(x) \text{ as } \varepsilon \to 0 @@ as in 32d3: near 0 by continuity at x, far off by the bound times ∫|f|
\htmlClass{new}{\int \hat f(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i x\xi}\,d\xi = f(x)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the left side heads there too: the fenced-limit technique, fence |f̂|

So a signal and its transform carry the same information: from either one the other is found, by the same kind of total. The bell was used as a smoothing weight, the job 32d3 gave its never-negative weight, and was then let go.

32e4The Transform Keeps Energy

On the line the blend is (f * g)(x) = ∫ f(x − t) g(t) dt, with no weight in front, and the blend-coefficients proof (32d1) runs unchanged: the transform of a blend is the product of the transforms.

Transform-energy technique: if f has a finite total size and a finite energy, then the total of |f̂|² over the line equals the total of |f|².

\tilde f(x) = \overline{f(-x)},\ \ \hat{\tilde f} = \overline{\hat f} @@ the flip passes through the total; slide x to −x
h = f * \tilde f,\ \ \hat h = \hat f\,\overline{\hat f} = |\hat f|^2 \ge 0 @@ the transform of a blend is the product (32d1, on the line)
h(0) = \int f(-t)\,\overline{f(-t)}\,dt = \int|f|^2 @@ the blend at 0
\int |\hat f(\xi)|^2\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,d\xi = \int h(u)\,w_\varepsilon(u)\,du \to h(0) @@ the undo-transform proof (32e3), at x = 0; h is continuous, a blend of two functions of finite energy
\int |\hat f(\xi)|^2\,e^{-\pi\varepsilon^2\xi^2}\,d\xi \to \int|\hat f|^2 @@ the rising-limit technique (30d): the bell rises to 1 as ε falls

The accepted names

The transform is the Fourier transform, here in the convention f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x)e^(−2πixξ) dx, with ξ in cycles per unit, which makes e^(−πx²) exactly its own transform. The transform-bound technique is the L¹ bound with continuity; the undo-transform technique is the Fourier inversion theorem; the transform-energy technique is Plancherel’s theorem (Parseval’s on the line). The box’s transform sin(πξ)/(πξ) is the sinc function. The bell as a tamer that is let go is a Gaussian summability method.

Who said it first

Joseph Fourier wrote the transform and its undoing in his book on heat (1822), with the repeat grown without bound, as on this page. Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Siméon Denis Poisson used it in the 1810s for waves on water. Michel Plancherel proved the energy equality in 1910. Norbert Wiener built the theory on the line in the 1930s, and Laurent Schwartz extended the transform to the Dirac delta and its kin in the 1940s.

A proof that is not allowed

“f(x) = ∫ f̂(ξ)e^(2πixξ)dξ = ∫∫ f(y)e^(2πi(x − y)ξ) dy dξ; swap the order; ∫ e^(2πiuξ)dξ is a spike at u = 0; so the total is f(x).” Not allowed: |f(y)| over the plane has an endless total, so the order cannot be swapped, and ∫ e^(2πiuξ)dξ is no function at all. The bell inside the total is what makes each line true.
