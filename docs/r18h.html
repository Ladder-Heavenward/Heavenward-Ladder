18h · technique

Circles and Conics

18d proved what a circle is, and 18e what the other conics are. This step works them by hand. It finds a circle from what you know about it. It finds the one number that every line through a point shares with a circle, and from it the length of a touching line. It sorts any equation of the second degree with no xy term into its conic. It finds the lines that touch a conic by a double root. And it finds how near and how far a planet comes to the sun.

Complete the squares to find a centre, or to sort a conic. From any point, every line that meets a circle twice cuts two lengths whose product is the same, the gap between the squared distance to the centre and the squared radius. A touching segment from outside is the square root of that same product. A line touches a conic where the meeting equation has a double root. On an ellipse, the distance to a focus runs between two bounds set by its long half-width and how stretched it is.

18h1A Circle From What You Know

Circle-equation (18d) turns x² + y² + Dx + Ey + F = 0 into a centre and a radius. So finding a circle means finding D, E and F. Given the centre and radius, expand (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r². Given three points, each point gives one equation of the first degree in D, E and F; solve the three. Circle-through-three says there is exactly one answer when the points are not collinear.

Given the ends of a diameter, there is a shorter way. Let A and B be points, A ≠ B. Diameter technique: the circle with diameter AB is the set of points X with (X − A) · (X − B) = 0. In coordinates, with A = (p₁, p₂) and B = (t₁, t₂), it is (x − p₁)(x − t₁) + (y − p₂)(y − t₂) = 0.

M = \tfrac{A + B}{2},\ \ w = \tfrac{B - A}{2} @@ the midpoint (part-of-the-way); then A = M − w and B = M + w
(X - A)\cdot(X - B) = \big((X - M) + w\big)\cdot\big((X - M) - w\big) @@ adding-keeps-answers
= |X - M|^2 - |w|^2 @@ the dot technique: the two cross terms cancel
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{(X - A)\cdot(X - B) = 0 \iff d(X, M) }\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{= \tfrac12 d(A, B)} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ both distances are not negative (powers-keep-order); |w| = ½ d(A, B) (distance-laws)
  
x^2 + y^2 - 10x + 4y + 13 = 0:\ \ (x - 5)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 16 @@ circle-equation: centre (5, −2), radius 4
\text{through } (0, 0), (6, 0), (0, 8):\ \ F = 0,\ 36 + 6D = 0,\ 64 + 8E = 0 @@ D = −6, E = −8: centre (3, 4), radius 5
\text{diameter from } (-1, 2) \text{ to } (5, 6):\ \ (x + 1)(x - 5) + (y - 2)(y - 6) = 0 @@ diameter: x² + y² − 4x − 8y + 7 = 0; centre (2, 4), radius √13

18h2One Product for Every Line Through a Point

Take a circle with centre O and radius r. Take a point P, and let d = d(P, O). Draw any line through P that meets the circle twice, at A and B. The lengths d(P, A) and d(P, B) change as the line turns about P. Their product does not.

Same-product technique: every line through P that meets the circle at A and B has d(P, A) · d(P, B) = |d² − r²|. When P is outside the circle, A and B lie on the same side of P. When P is inside, they lie on opposite sides.

X = P + se,\ \ |e| = 1 @@ two-descriptions-agree, with the direction scaled to length 1 (as in line-meets-circle)
|X - O|^2 = d^2 + 2s\,(P - O)\cdot e + s^2 @@ right-triangle, with u = P − O and v = se; |e| = 1
X \text{ on the circle} \iff s^2 + 2\beta s + \gamma = 0 @@ ﹀ “on the circle”, squared; β = (P − O) · e and γ = d² − r²
s = -\beta \pm \sqrt{\beta^2 - \gamma} @@ quadratic-formula; two meeting points, so β² − γ > 0
s_1 s_2 = \beta^2 - (\beta^2 - \gamma) = \gamma @@ the difference of squares
\htmlClass{new}{d(P, A)\,d(P, B) = |s_1|\,|s_2| = |d^2 - r^2|} \quad \blacksquare @@ distance-laws: d(P, P + se) = |s|; γ > 0 outside, so the roots share a sign, and γ < 0 inside

The number d² − r² has the accepted name the power of the point P. Inside the circle, same-product says that two chords crossing at P cut each other into pieces with equal products.

Now let P be outside, and let a line through P touch the circle at T. Touching-length technique: the touching segment from P has length d(P, T) = √(d² − r²). Line-meets-circle says the touching line is perpendicular to the radius OT. So right-triangle gives d² = r² + d(P, T)². Same-product says the same thing: the meeting equation has one root, so s₁ = s₂ and s₁² = d² − r².

How many touching lines are there, and where do they touch? Two-touching-lines technique: from a point P outside a circle, exactly two lines touch the circle, and they touch it where it meets the circle with diameter OP. Put O at (0, 0) and P = (p₁, p₂). Then both touching points lie on the line p₁x + p₂y = r², and the line touching at T = (t₁, t₂) is t₁x + t₂y = r².

\text{the line through } P, T \text{ touches at } T \iff T \text{ on the circle},\ (T - O)\cdot(T - P) = 0 @@ line-meets-circle one way; the other way, T is then the foot from O (nearest-point), at distance r
\begin{aligned}&\iff T \text{ on the circle and}\\ &\quad\text{on the circle with diameter } OP\end{aligned} @@ diameter, with A = O and B = P
M = \tfrac{O + P}{2}:\ \ d(O, M) = \tfrac d2 \text{ and } \big|r - \tfrac d2\big| \lt \tfrac d2 \lt r + \tfrac d2 @@ part-of-the-way; the left inequality is true since 0 < r < d
\text{the two circles meet at exactly two points} @@ two-circles-meet
|X|^2 = r^2 \text{ and } |X|^2 - P \cdot X = 0 \Rightarrow P \cdot X = r^2 @@ O = (0, 0): the second circle is diameter's equation expanded; subtract
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the line touching at } T \text{ is } T \cdot X = r^2} \quad \blacksquare @@ two-descriptions-agree: its normal is T − O, and T · T = r² puts T on it
  
x^2 + y^2 = 25,\ \ P = (7, 1):\ \ d(P, T) = \sqrt{50 - 25} = 5 @@ touching-length
7x + y = 25 \text{ and } x^2 + y^2 = 25:\ \ x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0 @@ two-touching-lines: T = (3, 4) and (4, −3)
3x + 4y = 25,\ \ 4x - 3y = 25 @@ the touching lines; both pass through P = (7, 1)
\text{the line through } O, P:\ \ d(P, A)\,d(P, B) = (\sqrt{50} - 5)(\sqrt{50} + 5) = 25 @@ same-product: d(P, T)² again

The sea's edge is a touching line. Treat the Earth as a ball of radius R, about 6371 km. Cut it through its centre and your eye, a height h above the sea. The cut is a circle, your eye is a point at distance d = R + h from its centre, and your line of sight to the sea's edge touches the circle. By touching-length, the sea's edge is √((R + h)² − R²) = √(2Rh + h²) away, if light went straight. From the top of Mount Diablo, 1173 m up, that is about 122 km.

18h3Sorting an Equation of the Second Degree

Take Ax² + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0, with A and C not both 0. Completed-squares technique: complete both squares, and the signs of A and C say which conic the equation is. Write h = −D/(2A) when A ≠ 0, k = −E/(2C) when C ≠ 0, and K = Ah² + Ck² − F.

A and C of one sign. Then A(x − h)² + C(y − k)² = K. If K has their sign too, it is an ellipse with centre (h, k), and a circle when A = C. If K = 0, it is the one point (h, k). Otherwise it holds no point.

A and C of opposite signs. If K ≠ 0, it is a hyperbola with centre (h, k). If K = 0, it is two lines crossing at (h, k).

C = 0 and E ≠ 0. Then y is a quadratic in x, and its graph is a parabola (18e). If E = 0 too, there is no y in it, and it is two upright lines, one, or none. With A = 0, exchange x and y.

A(x - h)^2 + C(y - k)^2 = K @@ completing the square twice (8i); the constants Ah² + Ck² cross to the right
A\cdot C \gt 0,\ \tfrac{K}{A} \gt 0:\ \ \tfrac{(x - h)^2}{K/A} + \tfrac{(y - k)^2}{K/C} = 1 @@ divide by K; ellipse, moved by (h, k): moving keeps distances (parabola's last line)
A\cdot C \lt 0,\ K \ne 0:\ \ \text{one denominator is below } 0 @@ hyperbola, moved; it opens along the axis whose denominator is above 0
A\cdot C \lt 0,\ K = 0:\ \ \sqrt{|A|}\,(x - h) = \pm\sqrt{|C|}\,(y - k) @@ the difference of squares: two lines
\htmlClass{new}{C = 0,\ E \ne 0:\ \ y = -\tfrac{A}{E}x^2 - \tfrac{D}{E}x - \tfrac{F}{E}} \quad \blacksquare @@ parabola: every quadratic's graph; exchanging x and y keeps distances (18e), so the larger denominator, or the other letter, can take x's place

The cases that are a point, lines or nothing have the accepted name degenerate conics. Equations with an xy term are conics too, tilted; they are not sorted here.

4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 54y + 61 = 0:\ \ 4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 3)^2 = 36 @@ sorting: an ellipse, a = 3, b = 2, c = √5; foci (2 ± √5, −3), e = √5/3
x^2 - 4y^2 + 4x + 8y - 4 = 0:\ \ \tfrac{(x + 2)^2}{4} - (y - 1)^2 = 1 @@ a hyperbola, a = 2, b = 1, c = √5; foci (−2 ± √5, 1); asymptotes y − 1 = ±(x + 2)/2
y^2 - 8x - 6y + 25 = 0:\ \ (y - 3)^2 = 8(x - 2) @@ a parabola opening along x: 4p = 8; vertex (2, 3), focus (4, 3), directrix x = 0

18h4Touching a Conic: The Double Root

For a circle, a touching line is one that meets it once. For a parabola that fails: the line x = 2 meets x² = 4y once, at (2, 1), and crosses it there. So for conics the hand method looks at the meeting equation instead. From here on, a line touches a conic at P when substituting the line into the conic leaves an equation of the second degree with a double root at P. For a circle this is the old meaning. There the meeting equation is s² + 2βs + γ = 0, always of the second degree, and it has one root exactly when the line meets once. Here β and γ (little beta and little gamma) are two numbers fixed by the line and the circle.

Double-root technique: to find the lines of slope m that touch a conic, substitute y = mx + q and set the discriminant to 0. If the equation left has no x² term, the line meets at most once and touches nowhere.

x^2 = 4y,\ \text{the line through } (2, 1):\ \ x^2 - 4mx - 4(1 - 2m) = 0 @@ q = 1 − 2m; discriminant 16m² − 32m + 16 = 16(m − 1)²
m = 1:\ \ y = x - 1 @@ double-root: x² − 4x + 4 = (x − 2)²
\tfrac{x^2}{25} + \tfrac{y^2}{9} = 1:\ \ (9 + 25m^2)x^2 + 50mqx + 25q^2 - 225 = 0 @@ discriminant 0 ⟺ q² = 25m² + 9; slope 1: y = x ± √34
\tfrac{x^2}{16} - \tfrac{y^2}{9} = 1:\ \ (9 - 16m^2)x^2 - 32mqx - 16q^2 - 144 = 0 @@ discriminant 0 ⟺ q² = 16m² − 9; slope 1: y = x ± √7; slopes ±¾ (the asymptotes' slopes) leave no x² term, and touch nowhere

18h5Nearest and Farthest From a Focus

Take the ellipse x²/α² + y²/b² = 1 with α > c > 0, focus F₂ = (c, 0), and e = c/α. Orbit-ends technique: on an ellipse, the distance to a focus is least, α(1 − e), at the near end of the long axis, and greatest, α(1 + e), at the far end.

d(P, F_2) = \alpha - \tfrac ca x = \alpha - ex @@ ellipse, the converse's lines
-\alpha \le x \le \alpha @@ ellipse: y²/b² ≥ 0
\alpha - e\alpha \le \alpha - ex \le \alpha + e\alpha @@ order-and-arithmetic-agree: times e > 0 keeps the order; negative-reverses-order for the minus
\htmlClass{new}{\text{least at } x = \alpha,\ \text{greatest at } x = -\alpha} \quad \blacksquare @@ (α, 0) and (−α, 0) are on the ellipse, and only they reach the ends

Run backwards: if the least distance is q and the greatest is Q, then α = (q + Q)/2 and e = (Q − q)/(Q + q). A measured law, found by an astronomer in 1609, says that each planet moves on an ellipse with the sun at one focus. Its range is the planets and the comets that return; its regular exceptions are small, such as Mercury's ellipse slowly turning about the sun, explained only in 1915.

\text{Earth: } \alpha \approx 149.6 \text{ million km},\ e \approx 0.0167 @@ orbit-ends: nearest about 147.1, farthest about 152.1 million km; nearest in early January
\text{a comet at } 0.59 \text{ and } 35.1 \text{ times Earth's } \alpha @@ run backwards: α ≈ 17.8, e ≈ 0.967

Who said it first

Euclid has same-product in Book III, propositions 35 and 36 (about 300 BCE): for two chords crossing inside a circle, and for a cutting line and a touching line from outside. Jakob Steiner named d² − r² the power of the point in 1826. Apollonius of Perga, around 200 BCE, found the touching lines of every conic in his Conics. René Descartes, in La Géométrie (1637), found the line at right angles to a curve by asking when a circle meets the curve in a double root; double-root is the same idea with a line.

In 18h.4.5, the circles riding on circles are Ptolemy's (about the year 150), kept by Nicolaus Copernicus in 1543 with the sun at the middle. The measurer is Tycho Brahe, the astronomer, who is also the one who found the measured law of 1609, is Johannes Kepler (Astronomia nova, 1609), and the colleague who never accepted ellipses is Galileo Galilei. Isaac Newton, in 1687, derived the ellipse from a force falling off as the square of the distance.

Accepted names: same-product is the intersecting chords theorem inside and the secant theorem outside, and touching-length the tangent–secant theorem. The nearest and farthest points of an orbit about the sun are its perihelion and aphelion. The measured law that each planet moves on an ellipse with the sun at one focus is Kepler's first law.
