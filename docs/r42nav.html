How Does a Robot Map a Room It Is Lost In?

How does a robot map a room it is lost in? A vacuum robot, a Mars rover or a phone showing furniture through its camera must know where it is to build a map, and must have a map to know where it is. The way out is to solve for both at once. Every wheel turn and every sighting of a landmark is a measurement linking two unknowns, and the measurements form a graph. When the robot comes back to a place it has seen, the loop closes and the errors spread out round it. This page builds the graph, solves it by 31’s least squares, closes a loop by hand, and finds why the solve stays quick for millions of measurements.

Unknowns: the robot’s poses and the landmarks’ places. Each measurement says one unknown minus another is about some value. Choose all unknowns to make the sum of squared misfits least. A loop closure adds one more link; least squares spreads its error over the loop. Each measurement touches two unknowns, so the grid of the solve is mostly zeros.

42nav1Where Am I, and Where Is Everything?

Claire’s robot rolls along a corridor. Its wheels count how far it went, a little wrong each time, and its camera sees landmarks, a little wrong too.

\text{wheels only: errors add up} @@ the drift of 24·nav
\text{landmarks only: no map to look them up in} @@ the map is unknown
\text{each needs the other} @@ a loop

From here on, on this page, a pose xk is where the robot is at the k-th moment; a link is a measurement of the difference of two unknowns.

42nav2Each Measurement a Link

\text{wheels: } x_{k+1} - x_k \approx u_k @@ a link between two poses
\text{camera: } \ell - x_k \approx z_k @@ a link from a pose to a landmark
\text{dots} = \text{unknowns},\ \text{links} = \text{measurements} @@ 42a’s dots and links
\htmlClass{new}{\text{misfit of a link} = (\text{difference} - \text{measured})} @@ zero if the measurement were perfect

Map-building technique: write each measurement as a link saying the difference of two unknowns is about the measured value, and choose all the unknowns at once so that the sum of squared misfits is least.

42nav3Closing the Loop

The robot starts at x0 = 0, rolls forward and its wheels say 1.0, rolls again and they say 1.0. Then it sees the start and measures it 1.8 behind.

(x_1 - 1)^2 + (x_2 - x_1 - 1)^2 + (x_2 - 1.8)^2 @@ the sum to make least
\partial_{x_1}:\ 2x_1 - x_2 = 0;\ \ \partial_{x_2}:\ 2x_2 - x_1 = 2.8 @@ least where the slopes are zero (31m)
x_1 = 0.933,\ x_2 = 1.867 @@ solve the pair
\htmlClass{new}{\text{each link off by } 0.067} @@ the 0.2 of disagreement split three ways

The wheels alone said x2 = 2.0; the sighting alone said 1.8. Least squares spreads the disagreement round the loop, each link giving way a little. With weights from each sensor’s spread, the more trusted links give way less, as 31·nav blended guesses.

42nav4A Grid Mostly Zeros

\text{slopes zero} \Rightarrow A^{\mathsf T}\! A\, x = A^{\mathsf T} b @@ 31m’s normal equations
\text{each link touches two unknowns} @@ one row of A has two entries
A^{\mathsf T}\! A:\ \text{nonzero only where two unknowns share a link} @@ the graph’s own pattern
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a million poses: a few million nonzeros, not } 10^{12}} @@ solved in seconds

Because each measurement links only two unknowns, the grid of the solve has the shape of the graph, nearly all zeros, and stays quick to solve as the map grows.

42nav5Seeing With a Camera

A camera sees corners and spots on walls, not labelled landmarks. Software must decide that a corner seen now is the one seen a minute ago.

\text{a point in a picture: a direction, not a distance} @@ rays through the lens, 21·comp
\text{two views from two poses: the point where the rays meet} @@ its place, by 21’s angles
\text{a wrong match: a false link} @@ one bad link can bend the whole map
\htmlClass{new}{\text{down-weight links with large misfits}} @@ trust the many over the few

Phones place furniture in a room this way, and Mars rovers check their wheel counts against the rocks they see, because wheels slip in sand. The graph and its least squares are the same.

Accepted names

Doing both at once is simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM); the graph is a pose graph or factor graph, solving it graph optimization, coming back a loop closure. Wheel counts are odometry, from a camera visual odometry; a mostly-zero grid is sparse; down-weighting large misfits is a robust fit. Solving a camera’s poses and points together is bundle adjustment.

Who said it first

Smith, Self and Cheeseman set out the joint estimate of robot and map in 1986, and the name SLAM arose at a robotics meeting in 1995. Lu and Milios solved the whole graph of poses by least squares in 1997.
