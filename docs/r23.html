Rebuilding From Point One

A tangent line knows a curve at one point, and only roughly nearby. This rung asks how much more one point can tell. A smooth curve has a slope at the point, and a slope of that slope, and so on forever, and each one is a number you can find. Put them all into one polynomial that never ends, and for the growth function, sine, cosine and the logarithm, that endless polynomial is the function itself, rebuilt from what happened at a single point. The rung measures how wrong each cut-off version is, finds how far from the point the endless version reaches, and proves you may take its slopes and areas one piece at a time. Then it finds the catch: a smooth function so flat at one point that the point tells you nothing at all. And it uses the endless sums to prove that neither e nor π is a fraction.

You could stop at the tangent line. You won’t. A point holds more than a slope, and digging it out is the kind of work you now go looking for.

At one point a smooth function has a height and slopes of every order. The polynomial built from them matches the function near the point, with an error you can measure. For many functions the endless version is the function, within a reach. Not for every smooth function.

What it means to say it

To match a function at a point is to agree with it there in height, in slope, in second slope, and so on, up to some count. For each count there is exactly one polynomial that does it. Rebuilding asks more: that as the count grows without end, the matching polynomials settle at the function’s value, not just at the point but at every input near it.

A function is rebuilt from a point when its endless matching polynomial settles at the function’s own values, near that point. The rung finds out when that happens, and how near is near.

Where it comes from

A mathematician of Sangamagrama, in Kerala, wrote the endless sums for sine, cosine and the arctangent around 1400. In Europe, a Scottish mathematician and an English one found such sums in the 1660s and 1670s, and another Englishman gave the general recipe in 1715. For eighty years mathematicians believed every function was its sum, and a mathematician in Paris built a calculus on that belief in 1797. A French mathematician found the function that refuses in 1823, and with a Norwegian and a German put the subject on the footing this rung uses.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things, each from the ones before.

First, for each count of slopes there is exactly one polynomial that matches a function at a point. Each slope, over a factorial, is the number in front of a power (23a2).

Second, the error of that polynomial is the next term it left out, with its slope taken somewhere in between (23b1). So the growth function, sine and cosine are their endless sums at every input.

Third, an endless power sum settles inside some distance from its centre and wanders outside it (23c2).

Fourth, there is a kind of settling that works everywhere at once (23d2), and it keeps curves without breaks (23d3). Power sums settle that way inside their reach (23d4).

Fifth, inside its reach a power sum may be differentiated and integrated one piece at a time (23e3).

Sixth, one smooth function is flat at a point in every order, so its endless sum is zero, and it is not zero anywhere else (23f2).

Seventh, the growth number e (23g1) and the circle number π (23g2) are not fractions.

Anexact, yet rigorous. Beside the proofs, the annexes carry green boxes. 23·phys carries the cloud, focused: a round lens gathers light to a short stretch, never a point, and the question is how wide the blur is. 23·nav carries the cloud, at sea: the arithmetic is fenced to a millionth, and the doubt lives in the air, which sets the fence’s width.

What follows

With those seven, the techniques compute. The sums for the growth function, the waves, the logarithm and any power of one plus x are built and combined by hand. The error bounds say how many terms give six places. A whole list of numbers, such as the rabbit sequence, can be stored as the coefficients of one function, and found again in closed form. Some sums that wander are still the best way to compute, if you stop at the right term; that is how the size of n factorial is finally proved. And areas are computed to many places by fitting parabolas through a few points.

The annexes put the cut-off sums to work. 23·phys bends light through a lens and finds the distance to a star. 23·eng finds where an engine gives its most power. 23·nav finds how far away the horizon is.

What you can do because you know it

You can compute e, π, sines and logarithms to any accuracy with nothing but arithmetic, and say how accurate you are. You can replace a hard function by a polynomial near a point and know how far to trust it. You can solve counting problems by multiplying sums, and compute areas no antiderivative can reach.

Every calculator and computer does what this rung teaches. The rung has handed you the method itself, along with its error.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 22’s endless sums and its tests, rung 14’s mean value technique, rung 16’s areas, and the slopes of rungs 13, 17 and 21. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say a smooth function is rebuilt from one point, when it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
