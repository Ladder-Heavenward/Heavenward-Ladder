One Technique for Every Boundary

Rung twenty-eight ended with three trades. Along a path, the total of an uphill field is the change between the two ends. Round the edge of a rectangle, the circulation is the total of the swirl inside. Out of a box, the flux is the total of the spread inside. Rung sixteen had made the first trade on a line, long before. Each proof on 28j was the same two moves, and each looked like a lucky accident of its own shape. This page asks whether they are one technique. The trouble is that the three totals are of different kinds: a function at two points, a vector along a curve, a vector across a surface, a number through a solid. The page builds one kind of thing to total for each of them, with one slope that takes each kind to the next. Taking that slope twice always gives nothing. Then the three trades become one technique, proved once, and it carries over to any curved surface. Last, the technique turns round and reads the holes of a region: the field round a power line has no swirl anywhere near it, and still its circulation round the line is never zero.

A thing to total along a curve reads one vector at each point; a thing to total across a surface reads two, and changes sign when they swap. One slope takes a function to its uphill vector, a vector field to its swirl, and a swirl to its spread. Its second use is always nothing: an uphill field has no swirl, and a swirl has no spread, because mixed slopes agree. The total of a thing over the edge of a region equals the total of its slope over the region, whether the region is a segment, a rectangle, a box, or a curved patch. And where the slope of a thing is nothing but its total round a loop is not, the loop goes round a hole.

29·cb28.1Things Made to Be Totalled

28j totalled P x′ + Q y′ + R z′ along a path. Write that thing to total as P dx + Q dy + R dz. At each point it reads one vector v, and gives P v₁ + Q v₂ + R v₃: dx reads the first entry of v, dy the second, dz the third. Along a path, it reads the path’s own vector r′(t), and its total is the circulation of (P, Q, R).

Across a surface, each small piece is spanned by two vectors. Write dx dy for the thing that reads two vectors u and v and gives u₁v₂ − u₂v₁. That is the determinant of their first two entries (27e): the area, with a sign, of their parallelogram seen from straight above.

dx\,dy\,(u, v) = u_1v_2 - u_2v_1 @@ the determinant with rows (u₁, u₂) and (v₁, v₂)
dy\,dx\,(u, v) = u_2v_1 - u_1v_2 = -\,dx\,dy\,(u, v) @@ swapping the rows turns the sign (27e)
dx\,dx\,(u, v) = u_1v_1 - u_1v_1 = 0 @@ two equal rows
\big(dy\,dz,\ dz\,dx,\ dx\,dy\big)(u, v) = u \times v @@ the cross technique (27i): its entries are these three determinants
\htmlClass{new}{dy\,dx = -dx\,dy,\ \ dx\,dx = 0;\ \ \textstyle\iint (A\,dy\,dz + B\,dz\,dx + C\,dx\,dy) = \text{flux of } (A, B, C)} \quad \blacksquare @@ read on the patch’s edge vectors r_u, r_v: (A, B, C) · (r_u × r_v), 28n3’s flux

From here on, a thing that reads k vectors at each point, adding in each vector and turning its sign when two of them swap, is a form of degree k. A function is a form of degree 0. P dx + Q dy + R dz is of degree 1, and is totalled along curves. A dy dz + B dz dx + C dx dy is of degree 2, and is totalled across surfaces. f dx dy dz reads three vectors by their determinant, and is totalled through solids.

Two-vector technique: dx dy reads two vectors by a determinant, so dy dx = −dx dy and dx dx = 0; the total of a form of degree 2 across a patch is the flux of its vector (A, B, C). The order in which the marks are written is now part of the form. In the books dx dy is written with the mark ∧ between them; on this ladder ∧ means “and” (1c), so the page leaves it out.

29·cb28.2One Slope for Every Degree

Give every form a slope, d. On a function, d f = f_x dx + f_y dy + f_z dz: the uphill vector of 28g, read as a form of degree 1. On any other form, take d of each function in front, and keep the marks after it: d(f dx) = (d f) dx. Then tidy with the two-vector technique.

d(P\,dx) = (P_x\,dx + P_y\,dy + P_z\,dz)\,dx = -P_y\,dx\,dy + P_z\,dz\,dx @@ dx dx = 0, dy dx = −dx dy
d(Q\,dy) = Q_x\,dx\,dy - Q_z\,dy\,dz,\ \ d(R\,dz) = R_y\,dy\,dz - R_x\,dz\,dx @@ the same two moves
d(P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz) = (R_y - Q_z)\,dy\,dz + (P_z - R_x)\,dz\,dx + (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy @@ add, and gather
d(A\,dy\,dz + B\,dz\,dx + C\,dx\,dy) = A_x\,dx\,dy\,dz + B_y\,dy\,dz\,dx + C_z\,dz\,dx\,dy @@ only the missing mark survives in each piece
\htmlClass{new}{= (A_x + B_y + C_z)\,dx\,dy\,dz} \quad \blacksquare @@ dy dz dx and dz dx dy are each two swaps from dx dy dz

From here on, (R_y − Q_z, P_z − R_x, Q_x − P_y) is the swirl vector of the field (P, Q, R). Its third entry is 28j’s swirl in the plane, and each entry is the swirl in one of the three flat directions, as 28·phys took it. One-slope technique: d takes a function to its uphill vector, a form of degree 1 to its swirl vector, and a form of degree 2 to its spread. The uphill vector, the swirl and the spread of rung twenty-eight are one slope, used on forms of three degrees.

d(x^2y + z) = 2xy\,dx + x^2\,dy + dz @@ the uphill vector (2xy, x², 1)
d(-y\,dx + x\,dy) = -dy\,dx + dx\,dy = 2\,dx\,dy @@ 28j’s whole-flow turn has swirl 2
\htmlClass{new}{d(x\,dy\,dz + y\,dz\,dx + z\,dx\,dy) = 3\,dx\,dy\,dz} @@ the field (x, y, z) spreads at 3 everywhere, as on 28n3

29·cb28.3d of d Is Nothing

Take the slope twice. On a function, the first d gives its uphill vector, and the second gives the swirl vector of that. On a form of degree 1, the first gives its swirl vector, and the second gives the spread of that.

d(df) = (f_{zy} - f_{yz})\,dy\,dz + (f_{xz} - f_{zx})\,dz\,dx + (f_{yx} - f_{xy})\,dx\,dy @@ the one-slope technique with (P, Q, R) = (f_x, f_y, f_z)
= 0 @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d), when f’s second slopes are continuous
d(d\omega) = (R_y - Q_z)_x + (P_z - R_x)_y + (Q_x - P_y)_z @@ the spread of the swirl vector, times dx dy dz
= (R_{yx} - R_{xy}) + (P_{zy} - P_{yz}) + (Q_{xz} - Q_{zx}) @@ regroup by P, Q, R
\htmlClass{new}{d(d\omega) = 0 \text{ for every form whose second slopes are continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap-knobs technique, three times; a form of degree 2 or 3 in space gives degree 4, which reads four vectors in three directions and is 0

Twice-nothing technique: for every form with continuous second slopes, d(dω) = 0: an uphill field has no swirl, and a swirl vector has no spread. It is one fact, the swap of the knobs, said once for each degree.

It cuts both ways in the world. Every compass ever read agrees with a measured law: the magnetic field B has no spread. No one has ever found a lone north pole. On a region with no holes, a field with no spread is the swirl vector of some field A: stated here, not proved, as 28n named the same converse one degree down. For the earth’s field near Seattle, about 54 μT and taken as steady over a city block, one such A can be found by hand.

B = (0,\ 0,\ B_0),\ \ B_0 \approx 54\ \mu\text{T} @@ the field over a block, with z along it
A = \tfrac12 B_0\,(-y,\ x,\ 0) @@ a guess: 28j’s whole-flow turn, scaled
\text{swirl vector of } A = \big(0 - 0,\ 0 - 0,\ \tfrac12B_0 + \tfrac12B_0\big) = (0, 0, B_0) @@ the one-slope technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread of } B = 0} @@ as the twice-nothing technique demands of any swirl vector

29·cb28.4One Technique for Every Boundary

Each region of 28j has an edge one degree down. A segment has two ends. A rectangle has four sides. A box has six faces. Turn each piece of edge outward: the far end of a segment counts with +, the near end with −; the sides of a rectangle are walked with the inside on the left; the faces of a box read their two vectors so that their cross points out. Then all three of 28j’s trades say one thing.

\text{segment, } \omega = f:\ \ f(b) - f(a) = \textstyle\int_a^b f'\,dx = \int d f @@ the area-is-a-change technique (16c); along a path, 28j1’s uphill-total technique
\text{rectangle, } \omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy:\ \ \textstyle\oint P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy = \iint d\omega @@ the swirl technique (28j2)
\text{box, } \omega = C\,dx\,dy:\ \ d\omega = C_z\,dx\,dy\,dz,\ \ \textstyle\iiint C_z = \iint \big(C(\text{top}) - C(\text{bottom})\big)\,dx\,dy @@ totals in either order (28h), then 16c along z
dx\,dy = 0 \text{ on the four upright faces},\ \ \text{the bottom reads } dx\,dy \text{ with } - @@ an upright face’s vectors have no part in x, or none in y; the bottom’s outward cross points down
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_{\text{edge of } M} \omega = \int_M d\omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for A dy dz along x and B dz dx along y; then add

Boundary technique: for a segment, a rectangle or a box M, and a form ω one degree below M, the total of ω over the edge of M, turned outward, is the total of dω over M. Every proof on 28j was this proof: d adds one slope in one direction, and totalling along that direction undoes it, leaving the values on two opposite pieces of edge.

Put the twice-nothing technique beside it. The edge of a box is its six faces, and those faces have no edge of their own: each side of each face is shared with a neighbour, walked once each way. The edge of an edge is nothing, as d of d is nothing. One fact about regions, one about forms, and the boundary technique ties them.

29·cb28.5Carried Onto a Curved Surface

A patch r(u, v) = (x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v)) lays a rectangle S of the uv-plane onto a curved surface. Carry a form back along it: put x(u, v) for x, and x_u du + x_v dv for dx, and the same for y and z. Its total over S is its total over the surface, by 28n’s retrace and patch techniques.

\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz \to U\,du + W\,dv,\ \ U = Px_u + Qy_u + Rz_u,\ \ W = Px_v + Qy_v + Rz_v @@ carried back to S
W_u - U_v = P_ux_v - P_vx_u + Q_uy_v - Q_vy_u + R_uz_v - R_vz_u @@ the product technique; the x_uv, y_uv, z_uv parts cancel by the swap-knobs technique (28d)
P_u = P_xx_u + P_yy_u + P_zz_u,\ \ P_v = P_xx_v + P_yy_v + P_zz_v @@ the roads-add technique (28c)
P_ux_v - P_vx_u = P_y(y_ux_v - y_vx_u) + P_z(z_ux_v - z_vx_u) @@ the P_x parts cancel; the same for Q and R
W_u - U_v = (\text{swirl vector of } F)\cdot(r_u \times r_v) @@ gather by the entries of r_u × r_v (27i)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\oint_{\text{edge}} F\cdot dr = \oint_{\partial S} U\,du + W\,dv = \iint_S (W_u - U_v) = \text{flux of the swirl vector through the patch}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swirl technique on S, then 28n3

Curved-swirl technique: the circulation of a field round the edge of a patch, walked with the patch on the left, equals the flux of its swirl vector through the patch. The carried slope of the carried form is the carried slope of the form: d and carrying can be done in either order. So the boundary technique rides on every patch, however curved.

F = (-y,\ x,\ 0),\ \ \text{swirl vector } (0, 0, 2) @@ 28j’s whole-flow turn, in space
\text{half sphere of radius } a:\ \ \text{flux of } (0, 0, 2) = 2\cdot\pi a^2 @@ (0, 0, 2) · (r_u × r_v) totals to 2 times the area seen from above
\text{rim}:\ \ \textstyle\oint F\cdot dr = 2\pi a^2 @@ the flat disc under the dome has the same edge and the same total
\text{swirl vector of } B = \mu_0 J @@ 28·phys’s wire-field technique, in each of the three flat directions
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\oint B\cdot dr = \mu_0\,(\text{current through any patch with that edge})} @@ the twice-nothing technique: J has no spread, so every patch with the same edge carries the same current

A Bonneville Power Administration line carrying 1000 A has circulation μ₀ · 1000 ≈ 1.26 × 10⁻³ T·m round any loop that goes once round it, flat or bent, near or far. On a circle 15 m from the line, that is a field of about 13.3 μT, a quarter of the earth’s.

29·cb28.6Holes, Read by Forms

28·phys’s wire field, read as a form, is ω = (−y dx + x dy)/(x² + y²), times μ₀I/(2π). Away from the wire, at the line x = y = 0, its slope is nothing. Round the wire its total is not.

d\omega = \Big(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\tfrac{x}{x^2 + y^2} + \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\tfrac{y}{x^2 + y^2}\Big)\,dx\,dy = \tfrac{(y^2 - x^2) + (x^2 - y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}\,dx\,dy = 0 @@ the one-slope technique, off the wire
\textstyle\oint_{\text{circle round } 0} \omega = 2\pi @@ 28j4: on the circle of radius ρ, ω reads ρ² dθ/ρ²
\text{if } \omega = dg \text{ on the region, every loop total is } 0 @@ the uphill-total technique (28j1)
\text{if the loop is the edge of a patch in the region, its total is } \textstyle\iint d\omega = 0 @@ the boundary technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no loop round the wire is the edge of a patch that misses it, and } \omega \text{ is } d \text{ of nothing there}} \quad \blacksquare @@ both would make the total 0

Hole-test technique: if dω = 0 on a region, and the total of ω round some loop in it is not 0, then that loop is the edge of no patch in the region, and ω is the slope of no function there. The form finds the hole without looking for it. Its total round a loop counts how many times the loop goes round: twice round gives 4π, and a loop that misses the wire gives 0. The picture walks a loop past the wire.

29·cb28.7Forms of Any Degree, in Any Number of Directions

Forms in space have degrees 0 to 3 (29·cb28.1). Nothing ties them to three directions. In lists of n numbers, with places x₁, …, xₙ, write dxᵢ for the form that reads place i of one vector. For places i₁, …, iₖ, the form dxᵢ₁ ⋯ dxᵢₖ reads k vectors: write the vectors as the rows of a grid, keep the columns i₁, …, iₖ, and take the determinant (27e).

A form of degree k is a sum of such forms, each with a function in front. Its slope d is taken as before: d of each function in front, with the marks kept after it. Two forms multiply by multiplying out, with the marks kept in order.

Any-degree-forms technique: swapping two marks turns the sign, and a repeated mark gives 0. So the forms with i₁ < ⋯ < iₖ build every form of degree k; there are (n choose k) of them; and above degree n every form is 0. For ω of degree k and η of degree l, ηω = (−1)kl ωη, and d(ωη) = (dω)η + (−1)k ω(dη).

dx_j\,dx_i = -dx_i\,dx_j,\ \ dx_i\,dx_i = 0 @@ column-slide (27e): swapped columns turn the sign, and equal columns give 0
\text{sorted marks: } \tbinom{n}{k} \text{ forms; none above } n @@ one for each choice of k places out of n (4k); above n some mark repeats
\eta\,\omega = (-1)^{kl}\,\omega\,\eta @@ each of the l marks of η moves past the k marks of ω: kl swaps
d(f\,dx_I\cdot g\,dx_J) = (g\,df + f\,dg)\,dx_I\,dx_J @@ the product technique for slopes; dx_I is short for a string of k marks
\htmlClass{new}{d(\omega\eta) = (d\omega)\,\eta + (-1)^k\,\omega\,(d\eta)} \quad \blacksquare @@ dg moves past the k marks of dx_I: k swaps

In space the counts are 1, 3, 3, 1: a function, three parts, three parts, a function. That is how three directions could hide forms inside vectors (29·cb28.2). In four directions the counts are 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, and a form of degree 2 has six parts. No vector of four entries holds it. The electric and the magnetic field, taken together in space and time, are such a form.

The signs do work. Two forms of degree 1 swap with a minus, so ωω = 0 for every form of degree 1. A form of degree 2 swaps with everything, with no sign. And d(dω) = 0 as before, by the swap of the knobs (29·cb28.3), in any number of directions.

29·cb28.8Pieces and Their Edges

The boundary technique (29·cb28.4) turned each piece of edge outward by hand. For a box of any dimension, a sign does it. Take the unit box of k places, 0 ≤ tᵢ ≤ 1. It has 2k faces: for each place i, the back face tᵢ = 0 and the front face tᵢ = 1, each a unit box of k − 1 places. Give the front face of place i the sign (−1)i + 1, and the back face the sign (−1)i.

A piece of dimension k is a smoothly sloped map c of the unit box into lists of n numbers. Its faces are c on the faces of the box. A chain is a sum of pieces with whole numbers in front, and its edge ∂c is the sum of its faces, each with its sign.

Edge-of-an-edge technique: for every chain c, ∂(∂c) = 0: every face of a face comes twice, with opposite signs.

\text{fix place } i \text{ at end } \alpha \text{ and } j \text{ at end } \beta,\ \ i \lt j @@ a face of a face: a box of k − 2 places; α and β are 0 for back, 1 for front
\text{route 1: fix } j \text{, then } i @@ place i is still the ith place: the signs are (−1)^(j+β) and (−1)^(i+α)
\text{route 2: fix } i \text{, then } j @@ place j has moved down to be the (j − 1)th: the signs are (−1)^(i+α) and (−1)^(j−1+β)
(-1)^{i+\alpha+j+\beta} + (-1)^{i+\alpha+j-1+\beta} = 0 @@ the two products differ by one factor of −1
\htmlClass{new}{\partial(\partial c) = 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ each face of a face is reached by exactly these two routes

Check the square. Its edge is the right side, less the left side, less the top, plus the bottom. Walked that way, the inside is on the left, as 28j asked. The edge of that edge is the four corners, each twice, with opposite signs.

With these signs the boundary technique reads the same for a piece of any dimension: the total of ω over ∂c is the total of dω over c. The proof is the box proof of 29·cb28.4, one place at a time, carried along c as in 29·cb28.5. Then two nothings answer each other. The total of ω over ∂(∂c) is the total of dω over ∂c, which is the total of d(dω) over c. The first is 0 because of the region, and the last because of the form.

29·cb28.9A Form With Slope 0 Is a Slope

If ω = dg then dω = 0 (29·cb28.3). The wire form showed that the converse can fail round a hole (29·cb28.6). On a region with no room for a hole, it holds. Call a region star-shaped from 0 when, with each of its points x, it holds the whole segment from 0 to x.

Star technique: on an open region star-shaped from 0, a form ω of degree 1 with dω = 0 is the slope of a function: ω = dg, where g(x) is the total of ω along the segment from 0 to x.

\text{triangle } 0,\ x,\ x + he_j \text{ lies in the region} @@ for small h: the region is open, and star-shaped from 0; eⱼ is the step along place j
\textstyle\oint_{\text{triangle}} \omega = \iint d\omega = 0 @@ the triangle is a patch: the curved-swirl proof (29·cb28.5) with dω in place of the swirl
g(x + he_j) - g(x) = \textstyle\int_0^h \omega_j(x + se_j)\,ds @@ two sides of the triangle are g’s segments; the third reads only the part ωⱼ of ω
\tfrac{g(x + he_j) - g(x)}{h} \to \omega_j(x) @@ the area-slope technique (16b)
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{\partial g}{\partial x_j} = \omega_j \text{ for every } j:\ \ dg = \omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ the partial slopes are continuous, so g is smoothly sloped (28b)

The plane without the origin is star-shaped from no point: the origin is in the way of some segment. Cut away a half-line from the origin and what is left is star-shaped from a point across from the cut. There the wire form is a slope: of the angle θ. Round the cut, θ cannot be mended.

One degree up, 29·cb28.3 said, without proof, that a field with no spread on a region with no holes is the swirl vector of some field. On a box it can now be built. First a tool: for smoothly sloped B, the slope along x of ∫ from 0 to z of B(x, y, s) ds is ∫ from 0 to z of Bx(x, y, s) ds. Write B as the total of Bx along x, swap the two totals (28h2), and take the slope along x (16b).

A_1 = \textstyle\int_0^z B_2(x, y, s)\,ds @@ B = (B₁, B₂, B₃) has no spread on a box round 0
A_2 = -\textstyle\int_0^z B_1(x, y, s)\,ds + \int_0^x B_3(s, y, 0)\,ds,\ \ A_3 = 0 @@ a guess, to be checked
-\tfrac{\partial A_2}{\partial z} = B_1,\ \ \tfrac{\partial A_1}{\partial z} = B_2 @@ the area-slope technique (16b): two entries of the swirl vector (29·cb28.2)
\tfrac{\partial A_2}{\partial x} - \tfrac{\partial A_1}{\partial y} = B_3(x, y, 0) - \textstyle\int_0^z \big(\tfrac{\partial B_1}{\partial x} + \tfrac{\partial B_2}{\partial y}\big)ds @@ the tool above, twice
= B_3(x, y, 0) + \textstyle\int_0^z \tfrac{\partial B_3}{\partial z}\,ds = B_3(x, y, z) @@ no spread; then area-is-a-change (16c)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{swirl vector of } A = B} \quad \blacksquare @@ all three entries

For the earth’s field over a block, B = (0, 0, B₀), this gives A = (0, B₀x, 0). The A of 29·cb28.3 was ½B₀(−y, x, 0). Both have swirl vector B. They differ by the uphill vector of ½B₀xy, which has no swirl: A is fixed only up to a slope, as an antiderivative is fixed only up to a constant.

29·cb28.10A Surface of Any Dimension

A curve is spread by one number, and the surfaces of 28n and 29h by two. In lists of n numbers, a surface of dimension k is a set M in which every point has a patch round it: a smoothly sloped, one-to-one map r from an open region of lists of k numbers onto all of M near that point, whose slope grid Dr has k free columns at every point (27b), and whose undoing is continuous. The undoings are the charts of 29h1, with k in place of 2.

The flat space of M at p = r(a) is everything the grid Dr(a) reaches: the space built by its k columns, the edge vectors ∂r/∂u₁, …, ∂r/∂uₖ at a. It has dimension k. It holds the velocity at p of every curve r(u(t)) that stays on M.

Flat-space technique: the flat space of M at p is the same from every patch round p.

r = q \circ T \text{ near } a,\ \ T = q^{-1}\circ r @@ two patches r and q round p; T is the change of page (29h2)
T \text{ is smoothly sloped, both ways} @@ fill q out to n places with n − k straight directions that Dq does not reach; local-undo and undo-slope (28e2, 28e3) undo that map, and T is its undoing after r
Dr(a) = Dq(T(a))\cdot DT(a) @@ chain-of-flat-maps (28c)
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{Dr(a) \text{ and } Dq(T(a))}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\text{reach the same space}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ DT(a) can be undone, so it loses no direction

The sphere x² + y² + z² = 1 is a surface of dimension 2 in lists of three numbers, by the two charts of 29h1, and its flat space at p is the plane of vectors at right angles to p. A smooth curve with speed never 0 that never crosses itself is a surface of dimension 1, and its flat space is the line along its velocity.

One equation makes a surface one dimension down. Where F is smoothly sloped on lists of n numbers and ∇F ≠ 0 at every point of the set F = 0, that set is a surface of dimension n − 1: near each of its points, one place is fixed by the others (28f), and the others are the patch. Its flat space at p is the vectors at right angles to ∇F(p) (28k).

29·cb28.11An Edge, and One Way Round All Over

A disc has a rim and a sphere has none. A surface with an edge allows a second kind of patch: one spread from half a region, the lists with uₖ ≥ 0. The points that come from uₖ = 0 are the edge, ∂M. The edge is a surface of dimension k − 1, with no edge of its own: drop the last place from each edge patch.

Two patches agree in turn when their change of page T has det DT > 0 wherever both show the surface. That was the test for keeping a flux (28n3). M is turned one way all over when it has an atlas whose patches all agree in turn.

The sphere is. The change of page between its two charts has determinant −1/(X² + Y²)² (29h2), below 0 everywhere. Swap X and Y on one page, and it is above 0 everywhere.

Turned-all-over technique: the twisted band r(u, v) = ((2 + v cos(u/2)) cos u, (2 + v cos(u/2)) sin u, v sin(u/2)), for −1 < v < 1, cannot be turned one way all over.

r(u + 2\pi, -v) = r(u, v) @@ cos(u/2 + π) = −cos(u/2), and the same for sin: once round, the band comes back turned over
n(u) = \tfrac{r_u \times r_v}{|r_u \times r_v|} \text{ at } (u, 0) @@ a side of the band along its middle circle, continuous in u
n(0) = (0, 0, -1),\ \ n(2\pi) = (0, 0, 1) @@ at the one point (2, 0, 0): r_u = (0, 2, 0) both times, and r_v = (1, 0, 0), then (−1, 0, 0)
\text{a one-way atlas picks a side } m @@ at each point: the cross of a patch’s edge vectors, over its length: the same from every patch of the atlas (28n3), and continuous
m\cdot n(u) = \pm 1 \text{, so one sign all the way} @@ continuous in u, and it takes no value between (12a)
\htmlClass{new}{m\cdot n(0) = -\,m\cdot n(2\pi):\ \bot} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same point and the same m, with opposite n

So the band has one side only, and no flux through it can be defined. On a surface turned one way, every form of degree k has a total, patch by patch, with no sign trouble.

The turn passes to the edge. An edge patch counts as agreeing when the direction out of M, followed by the edge’s own directions, agrees with M. For a disc in the plane that is the rim walked with the disc on the left (28j). For a solid ball it is the sphere with its outward side.

29·cb28.12The Size of a Piece of Surface

The area of a patch in space was the total of |r_u × r_v| (28n3), and that is the total of √(EG − F²) (29d): the root of the determinant of the ruler grid. The ruler grid is Drᵀ Dr, the grid of dot products of the edge vectors. Read that way it needs no cross product, so it works in any dimension.

From here on, the size of the piece r(S) of a surface of dimension k is the total over S of √det(Drᵀ Dr).

Piece-size technique: the size of a piece is the same from every patch.

q = r \circ T:\ \ Dq = Dr\cdot DT @@ a second patch of the same piece, from S′; chain-of-flat-maps (28c)
Dq^{\mathsf T}Dq = DT^{\mathsf T}\,\big(Dr^{\mathsf T}Dr\big)\,DT @@ the ruler-change technique (29d), in k places
\sqrt{\det(Dq^{\mathsf T}Dq)} = |\det DT|\,\sqrt{\det(Dr^{\mathsf T}Dr)} @@ det-product (27e); a grid and its flip have one determinant
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_{S'}\sqrt{\det(Dq^{\mathsf T}Dq)} = \int_{S}\sqrt{\det(Dr^{\mathsf T}Dr)}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the change-of-variables technique (28i)

Three checks. For k = 1 the grid is the one number |r′|², and the size is the length of the curve (18i). For k = 2 in space it is the area of 28n3. For k = n the grid Dr is square, det(Drᵀ Dr) = (det Dr)², and the size is the volume that change-of-variables gives.

A graph xₙ = f(x₁, …, xₙ₋₁) has Drᵀ Dr = I + ∇f ∇fᵀ, with determinant 1 + |∇f|². So its size is the total of √(1 + |∇f|²): the size of its shadow, made larger where the graph is steep.

With a size, a function on M has a total: carry it to a page and multiply by √det(Drᵀ Dr). A form of degree k needs no ruler. Its total over a piece is the total over S of ω read on the k edge vectors, kept by a change of page with det DT > 0 and turned in sign by one with det DT < 0, as in 28n3.

29·cb28.13Bumps That Add to 1

A total over a whole surface has to be put together from totals on patches, and patches overlap. The tool that shares a function out among the patches, with nothing counted twice, is a family of bumps.

Let F(t) = e−1/t² for t > 0 and F(t) = 0 for t ≤ 0. It is smooth, by the flat-point technique (23f2): every slope at 0 is 0 from both sides. On a page, b(u) = F(ρ² − |u − c|²) is then a smooth bump: above 0 inside the ball of radius ρ round c, and 0 outside it.

Bumps technique: let M be covered by finitely many patches, each spread from a ball, so that the balls of half the radius already cover M. Then there are smooth functions φ₁, …, φm on M, each at least 0, with φᵢ = 0 outside patch i, and φ₁ + ⋯ + φm = 1 at every point of M.

b_i \gt 0 \text{ on the } \tfrac34 \text{ ball of page } i,\ 0 \text{ beyond it} @@ the bump above with ρ three quarters of the radius; carried to M by patch i, and 0 on the part of M outside the patch
b = b_1 + \cdots + b_m \gt 0 \text{ on all of } M @@ the half balls cover M, and bᵢ > 0 on half ball i
\varphi_i = b_i/b @@ smooth: a quotient with a bottom that is never 0
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\varphi_1 + \cdots + \varphi_m = 1,}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{\varphi_i = 0 \text{ outside patch } i} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ the tops add to the bottom

Now any function or form on M splits: ω = φ₁ω + ⋯ + φmω, and each part lives inside one patch, where it can be carried to a page and totalled. The total of ω over M is the sum of those. It does not depend on the bumps. With a second family ψⱼ, the total of φᵢω is the sum over j of the totals of φᵢψⱼω, and adding over i and j in either order gives the same number.

For the sphere, take the two charts of 29h1 on the balls of radius 4, with half balls of radius 2. The page from the north pole shows every point with z ≤ 3/5 inside its half ball, since X² + Y² = (1 + z)/(1 − z), and the other shows every point with z ≥ −3/5.

Which surfaces have such a finite cover is a question for rung 34. The surfaces of this page do: the sphere, the torus, the disc with its rim.

29·cb28.14The Boundary Technique on a Surface

The boundary technique (29·cb28.4) was proved for a segment, a rectangle and a box, and carried onto one patch (29·cb28.5). The bumps carry it onto a whole surface.

Boundary technique, on a surface: let M be a surface of dimension k with an edge, turned one way all over, with a finite cover as in 29·cb28.13, and let ω be a smoothly sloped form of degree k − 1. Then the total of ω over ∂M is the total of dω over M. If M has no edge, the total of dω over M is 0.

\omega = \varphi_1\omega + \cdots + \varphi_m\omega @@ the bumps technique (29·cb28.13)
\text{carry } \varphi_i\omega \text{ to its page, in a box } Q @@ it is 0 near every face of Q, but for the face uₖ = 0 when patch i is an edge patch
\textstyle\int_Q d(\varphi_i\omega) = \int_{u_k = 0} \varphi_i\omega @@ the boundary technique for a box (29·cb28.4, with the signs of 29·cb28.8); the other faces give 0
\textstyle\int_M d(\varphi_i\omega) = \int_{\partial M}\varphi_i\omega @@ carrying keeps d and keeps totals (29·cb28.5); for an inside patch both sides are 0
\textstyle\sum_i d(\varphi_i\omega) = d\omega @@ d(φᵢω) = (dφᵢ)ω + φᵢ dω (29·cb28.7), and the dφᵢ add to d(1) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_{\partial M}\omega = \int_M d\omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ add over i

Every trade of a total for a total one dimension down, on this ladder, is this line. Area-is-a-change (16c) is M a segment. The swirl and spread techniques (28j) are M a rectangle or a box. Curved-swirl (29·cb28.5) is M a patch in space. The proof was never more than 16c along one place, with the bookkeeping done by forms, signs and bumps.

On a surface with no edge the left side is empty. So the total of a slope dω over a sphere is 0, whatever ω is. Turn that round, as the hole test did: a form whose total over a sphere is not 0 is the slope of nothing there. The next footing has one.

29·cb28.15The Area a Surface Shows to a Point

Stand at 0 and look at a small piece of surface. How much of your view does it fill? Push each of its points along its ray onto the sphere of radius 1 round you, and take the area of the shadow. That is the area the piece shows to 0. A whole sphere round you shows 4π (21o).

A form measures it. Write ρ for the length of (x, y, z), and σ = (x dy dz + y dz dx + z dx dy)/ρ³: the flux form of the field that points straight out from 0 with size 1/ρ². (σ is a little sigma.)

Shown-area technique: dσ = 0 away from 0. The total of σ over a closed surface, turned outward, is 4π when 0 is inside it and 0 when 0 is outside.

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\tfrac{x}{\rho^3} = \tfrac{\rho^2 - 3x^2}{\rho^5} @@ the quotient technique, with ∂ρ/∂x = x/ρ
d\sigma = \tfrac{3\rho^2 - 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)}{\rho^5}\,dx\,dy\,dz = 0 @@ the one-slope technique (29·cb28.2): the spread of the field, away from 0
\text{sphere of radius } a:\ \ \textstyle\iint\sigma = \tfrac{1}{a^2}\cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi @@ the field is straight out, of size 1/a², and the skin has area 4πa² (21o)
\textstyle\iint_S \sigma - \iint_{\text{small sphere}}\sigma = \iiint d\sigma = 0 @@ 0 inside S: the boundary technique (29·cb28.14) on the solid between S and a small sphere round 0
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\iint_S \sigma = 4\pi \text{ with } 0 \text{ inside},}\\ &\quad \htmlClass{new}{0 \text{ with } 0 \text{ outside}} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ 0 outside S: the boundary technique on the whole solid inside S, where dσ = 0

So a closed surface round you fills your whole view, 4π, whatever its shape and wherever you stand inside it. One you are outside of shows 0 in all: its near side and its far side fill the same patch of view, one with each sign.

This is the wire form (29·cb28.6), one degree up. Its slope is 0 away from one point, and its total round that point is not 0. So it is the slope of nothing there, by 29·cb28.14, and it finds the hole. Every field that falls off as the square of the distance is σ times a number. That is why the flux of such a field through a closed surface counts only what is inside.

The accepted names

A form of degree k is a differential k-form, and dx dy is written dx ∧ dy, the wedge product, so that the forms make an exterior algebra. d is the exterior derivative. The swirl vector is the curl, curl F or ∇ × F; the spread is the divergence, div F or ∇ · F. The twice-nothing technique is d² = 0: curl grad f = 0 and div curl F = 0. The boundary technique, for every region and every form, is the generalized Stokes theorem, ∫_∂M ω = ∫_M dω; carrying a form back along a patch is the pullback, and the curved-swirl technique is the Kelvin–Stokes theorem. A form with dω = 0 is closed, and one that is d of another is exact; that closed forms are exact on a region with no holes is the Poincaré lemma, and the closed forms that are not exact, counted, make the de Rham cohomology of the region. The number of times a loop goes round the wire is its winding number. A field whose swirl vector is B is a vector potential for B, and ∮ B · dr = μ₀I is Ampère’s law. Forms of degree k are differential k-forms; their product is the wedge product, or exterior product, and d is the exterior derivative. A piece is a singular cube, a sum of pieces is a chain, and ∂ is the boundary operator. A form with dω = 0 is closed, and a form that is a slope is exact; the star technique is Poincaré’s. The freedom in A is gauge freedom. A surface of dimension k is a k-dimensional manifold in ℝⁿ; its flat space at a point is the tangent space; a patch is a parametrization, or coordinate system. Turned one way all over is orientable; the twisted band is the Möbius band; a surface with an edge is a manifold with boundary. √det(Drᵀ Dr) du₁ ⋯ duₖ is the volume element; Drᵀ Dr is the Gram matrix of the edge vectors, or the first fundamental form. Bumps that add to 1 are a partition of unity. The boundary technique on a surface is the general Stokes formula; Green’s, Gauss’s and the fundamental one of calculus are its cases. The area a surface shows to a point is the solid angle it subtends, in steradians; the count of what is inside is Gauss’s law.

Who said it first

Hermann Grassmann built the algebra in which dx dy = −dy dx in 1844. Élie Cartan wrote forms with d in 1899, and Henri Poincaré used them in his work on celestial mechanics in the same decade. The curved-swirl technique was sent by William Thomson to George Stokes in a letter of 1850, and Stokes set it as a Cambridge examination question in 1854. Vito Volterra stated a version for any number of directions in 1889, and the form ∫_∂M ω = ∫_M dω was set out by Cartan and others in the early 1900s. Georges de Rham proved in 1931 that forms count holes exactly. In 29·cb28.17.5, the four-part numbers are William Rowan Hamilton’s quaternions (1843), defended by Peter Guthrie Tait; the professor at Yale is Josiah Willard Gibbs and the engineer Oliver Heaviside, whose vector analysis won the textbooks; the third way is Cartan’s.
