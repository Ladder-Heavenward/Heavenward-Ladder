One Rule for Every Boundary

Rung twenty-eight ended with three trades. Along a path, the total of an uphill field is the change between the two ends. Round the edge of a rectangle, the circulation is the total of the swirl inside. Out of a box, the flux is the total of the spread inside. Rung sixteen had made the first trade on a line, long before. Each proof on 28j was the same two moves, and each looked like a lucky accident of its own shape. This page asks whether they are one rule. The trouble is that the three totals are of different kinds: a function at two points, an arrow along a curve, an arrow across a surface, a number through a solid. The page builds one kind of thing to total for each of them, with one slope that takes each kind to the next. Taking that slope twice always gives nothing. Then the three trades become one rule, proved once, and it carries over to any curved surface. Last, the rule turns round and reads the holes of a region: the field round a power line has no swirl anywhere near it, and still its circulation round the line is never zero.

A thing to total along a curve reads one arrow at each point; a thing to total across a surface reads two, and changes sign when they swap. One slope takes a function to its uphill arrow, an arrow field to its swirl, and a swirl to its spread. Its second use is always nothing: an uphill field has no swirl, and a swirl has no spread, because mixed slopes agree. The total of a thing over the edge of a region equals the total of its slope over the region, whether the region is a segment, a rectangle, a box, or a curved patch. And where the slope of a thing is nothing but its total round a loop is not, the loop goes round a hole.

29·cb28.1Things Made to Be Totalled

28j totalled P x′ + Q y′ + R z′ along a path. Write that thing to total as P dx + Q dy + R dz. At each point it reads one arrow v, and gives P v₁ + Q v₂ + R v₃: dx reads the first entry of v, dy the second, dz the third. Along a path, it reads the path’s own arrow r′(t), and its total is the circulation of (P, Q, R).

Across a surface, each small piece is spanned by two arrows. Write dx dy for the thing that reads two arrows u and v and gives u₁v₂ − u₂v₁. That is the determinant of their first two entries (27e): the area, with a sign, of their parallelogram seen from straight above.

dx\,dy\,(u, v) = u_1v_2 - u_2v_1 @@ the determinant with rows (u₁, u₂) and (v₁, v₂)
dy\,dx\,(u, v) = u_2v_1 - u_1v_2 = -\,dx\,dy\,(u, v) @@ swapping the rows turns the sign (27e)
dx\,dx\,(u, v) = u_1v_1 - u_1v_1 = 0 @@ two equal rows
\big(dy\,dz,\ dz\,dx,\ dx\,dy\big)(u, v) = u \times v @@ the cross technique (27i): its entries are these three determinants
\htmlClass{new}{dy\,dx = -dx\,dy,\ \ dx\,dx = 0;\ \ \textstyle\iint (A\,dy\,dz + B\,dz\,dx + C\,dx\,dy) = \text{flux of } (A, B, C)} \quad \blacksquare @@ read on the patch’s edge arrows r_u, r_v: (A, B, C) · (r_u × r_v), 28n3’s flux

From here on, a thing that reads k arrows at each point, adding in each arrow and turning its sign when two of them swap, is a form of degree k. A function is a form of degree 0. P dx + Q dy + R dz is of degree 1, and is totalled along curves. A dy dz + B dz dx + C dx dy is of degree 2, and is totalled across surfaces. f dx dy dz reads three arrows by their determinant, and is totalled through solids.

Two-arrow technique: dx dy reads two arrows by a determinant, so dy dx = −dx dy and dx dx = 0; the total of a form of degree 2 across a patch is the flux of its arrow (A, B, C). The order in which the marks are written is now part of the form. In the books dx dy is written with the mark ∧ between them; on this ladder ∧ means “and” (1c), so the page leaves it out.

29·cb28.2One Slope for Every Degree

Give every form a slope, d. On a function, d f = f_x dx + f_y dy + f_z dz: the uphill arrow of 28g, read as a form of degree 1. On any other form, take d of each function in front, and keep the marks after it: d(f dx) = (d f) dx. Then tidy with the two-arrow technique.

d(P\,dx) = (P_x\,dx + P_y\,dy + P_z\,dz)\,dx = -P_y\,dx\,dy + P_z\,dz\,dx @@ dx dx = 0, dy dx = −dx dy
d(Q\,dy) = Q_x\,dx\,dy - Q_z\,dy\,dz,\ \ d(R\,dz) = R_y\,dy\,dz - R_x\,dz\,dx @@ the same two moves
d(P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz) = (R_y - Q_z)\,dy\,dz + (P_z - R_x)\,dz\,dx + (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy @@ add, and gather
d(A\,dy\,dz + B\,dz\,dx + C\,dx\,dy) = A_x\,dx\,dy\,dz + B_y\,dy\,dz\,dx + C_z\,dz\,dx\,dy @@ only the missing mark survives in each piece
\htmlClass{new}{= (A_x + B_y + C_z)\,dx\,dy\,dz} \quad \blacksquare @@ dy dz dx and dz dx dy are each two swaps from dx dy dz

From here on, (R_y − Q_z, P_z − R_x, Q_x − P_y) is the swirl arrow of the field (P, Q, R). Its third entry is 28j’s swirl in the plane, and each entry is the swirl in one of the three flat directions, as 28·phys took it. One-slope technique: d takes a function to its uphill arrow, a form of degree 1 to its swirl arrow, and a form of degree 2 to its spread. The uphill arrow, the swirl and the spread of rung twenty-eight are one slope, used on forms of three degrees.

d(x^2y + z) = 2xy\,dx + x^2\,dy + dz @@ the uphill arrow (2xy, x², 1)
d(-y\,dx + x\,dy) = -dy\,dx + dx\,dy = 2\,dx\,dy @@ 28j’s whole-flow turn has swirl 2
\htmlClass{new}{d(x\,dy\,dz + y\,dz\,dx + z\,dx\,dy) = 3\,dx\,dy\,dz} @@ the field (x, y, z) spreads at 3 everywhere, as on 28n3

29·cb28.3d of d Is Nothing

Take the slope twice. On a function, the first d gives its uphill arrow, and the second gives the swirl arrow of that. On a form of degree 1, the first gives its swirl arrow, and the second gives the spread of that.

d(df) = (f_{zy} - f_{yz})\,dy\,dz + (f_{xz} - f_{zx})\,dz\,dx + (f_{yx} - f_{xy})\,dx\,dy @@ the one-slope technique with (P, Q, R) = (f_x, f_y, f_z)
= 0 @@ the swap-knobs technique (28d), when f’s second slopes are continuous
d(d\omega) = (R_y - Q_z)_x + (P_z - R_x)_y + (Q_x - P_y)_z @@ the spread of the swirl arrow, times dx dy dz
= (R_{yx} - R_{xy}) + (P_{zy} - P_{yz}) + (Q_{xz} - Q_{zx}) @@ regroup by P, Q, R
\htmlClass{new}{d(d\omega) = 0 \text{ for every form whose second slopes are continuous}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swap-knobs technique, three times; a form of degree 2 or 3 in space gives degree 4, which reads four arrows in three directions and is 0

Twice-nothing technique: for every form with continuous second slopes, d(dω) = 0: an uphill field has no swirl, and a swirl arrow has no spread. It is one fact, the swap of the knobs, said once for each degree.

It cuts both ways in the world. Every compass ever read agrees with a measured law: the magnetic field B has no spread. No one has ever found a lone north pole. On a region with no holes, a field with no spread is the swirl arrow of some field A: stated here, not proved, as 28n named the same converse one degree down. For the earth’s field near Seattle, about 54 μT and taken as steady over a city block, one such A can be found by hand.

B = (0,\ 0,\ B_0),\ \ B_0 \approx 54\ \mu\text{T} @@ the field over a block, with z along it
A = \tfrac12 B_0\,(-y,\ x,\ 0) @@ a guess: 28j’s whole-flow turn, scaled
\text{swirl arrow of } A = \big(0 - 0,\ 0 - 0,\ \tfrac12B_0 + \tfrac12B_0\big) = (0, 0, B_0) @@ the one-slope technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{spread of } B = 0} @@ as the twice-nothing technique demands of any swirl arrow

29·cb28.4One Rule for Every Boundary

Each region of 28j has an edge one degree down. A segment has two ends. A rectangle has four sides. A box has six faces. Turn each piece of edge outward: the far end of a segment counts with +, the near end with −; the sides of a rectangle are walked with the inside on the left; the faces of a box read their two arrows so that their cross points out. Then all three of 28j’s trades say one thing.

\text{segment, } \omega = f:\ \ f(b) - f(a) = \textstyle\int_a^b f'\,dx = \int d f @@ the area-is-a-change technique (16c); along a path, 28j1’s uphill-total technique
\text{rectangle, } \omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy:\ \ \textstyle\oint P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy = \iint d\omega @@ the swirl technique (28j2)
\text{box, } \omega = C\,dx\,dy:\ \ d\omega = C_z\,dx\,dy\,dz,\ \ \textstyle\iiint C_z = \iint \big(C(\text{top}) - C(\text{bottom})\big)\,dx\,dy @@ totals in either order (28h), then 16c along z
dx\,dy = 0 \text{ on the four upright faces},\ \ \text{the bottom reads } dx\,dy \text{ with } - @@ an upright face’s arrows have no part in x, or none in y; the bottom’s outward cross points down
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\int_{\text{edge of } M} \omega = \int_M d\omega} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same for A dy dz along x and B dz dx along y; then add

Boundary-rule technique: for a segment, a rectangle or a box M, and a form ω one degree below M, the total of ω over the edge of M, turned outward, is the total of dω over M. Every proof on 28j was this proof: d adds one slope in one direction, and totalling along that direction undoes it, leaving the values on two opposite pieces of edge.

Put the twice-nothing technique beside it. The edge of a box is its six faces, and those faces have no edge of their own: each side of each face is shared with a neighbour, walked once each way. The edge of an edge is nothing, as d of d is nothing. One fact about regions, one about forms, and the boundary rule ties them.

29·cb28.5Carried Onto a Curved Surface

A patch r(u, v) = (x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v)) lays a rectangle S of the uv-plane onto a curved surface. Carry a form back along it: put x(u, v) for x, and x_u du + x_v dv for dx, and the same for y and z. Its total over S is its total over the surface, by 28n’s retrace and patch techniques.

\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz \to U\,du + W\,dv,\ \ U = Px_u + Qy_u + Rz_u,\ \ W = Px_v + Qy_v + Rz_v @@ carried back to S
W_u - U_v = P_ux_v - P_vx_u + Q_uy_v - Q_vy_u + R_uz_v - R_vz_u @@ the product rule; the x_uv, y_uv, z_uv parts cancel by the swap-knobs technique (28d)
P_u = P_xx_u + P_yy_u + P_zz_u,\ \ P_v = P_xx_v + P_yy_v + P_zz_v @@ the roads-add technique (28c)
P_ux_v - P_vx_u = P_y(y_ux_v - y_vx_u) + P_z(z_ux_v - z_vx_u) @@ the P_x parts cancel; the same for Q and R
W_u - U_v = (\text{swirl arrow of } F)\cdot(r_u \times r_v) @@ gather by the entries of r_u × r_v (27i)
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\oint_{\text{edge}} F\cdot dr = \oint_{\partial S} U\,du + W\,dv = \iint_S (W_u - U_v) = \text{flux of the swirl arrow through the patch}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the swirl technique on S, then 28n3

Curved-swirl technique: the circulation of a field round the edge of a patch, walked with the patch on the left, equals the flux of its swirl arrow through the patch. The carried slope of the carried form is the carried slope of the form: d and carrying can be done in either order. So the boundary rule rides on every patch, however curved.

F = (-y,\ x,\ 0),\ \ \text{swirl arrow } (0, 0, 2) @@ 28j’s whole-flow turn, in space
\text{half sphere of radius } a:\ \ \text{flux of } (0, 0, 2) = 2\cdot\pi a^2 @@ (0, 0, 2) · (r_u × r_v) totals to 2 times the area seen from above
\text{rim}:\ \ \textstyle\oint F\cdot dr = 2\pi a^2 @@ the flat disc under the dome has the same edge and the same total
\text{swirl arrow of } B = \mu_0 J @@ 28·phys’s wire-field technique, in each of the three flat directions
\htmlClass{new}{\textstyle\oint B\cdot dr = \mu_0\,(\text{current through any patch with that edge})} @@ the twice-nothing technique: J has no spread, so every patch with the same edge carries the same current

A Bonneville Power Administration line carrying 1000 A has circulation μ₀ · 1000 ≈ 1.26 × 10⁻³ T·m round any loop that goes once round it, flat or bent, near or far. On a circle 15 m from the line, that is a field of about 13.3 μT, a quarter of the earth’s.

29·cb28.6Holes, Read by Forms

28·phys’s wire field, read as a form, is ω = (−y dx + x dy)/(x² + y²), times μ₀I/(2π). Away from the wire, at the line x = y = 0, its slope is nothing. Round the wire its total is not.

d\omega = \Big(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\tfrac{x}{x^2 + y^2} + \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\tfrac{y}{x^2 + y^2}\Big)\,dx\,dy = \tfrac{(y^2 - x^2) + (x^2 - y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}\,dx\,dy = 0 @@ the one-slope technique, off the wire
\textstyle\oint_{\text{circle round } 0} \omega = 2\pi @@ 28j4: on the circle of radius ρ, ω reads ρ² dθ/ρ²
\text{if } \omega = dg \text{ on the region, every loop total is } 0 @@ the uphill-total technique (28j1)
\text{if the loop is the edge of a patch in the region, its total is } \textstyle\iint d\omega = 0 @@ the boundary-rule technique
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no loop round the wire is the edge of a patch that misses it, and } \omega \text{ is } d \text{ of nothing there}} \quad \blacksquare @@ both would make the total 0

Hole-test technique: if dω = 0 on a region, and the total of ω round some loop in it is not 0, then that loop is the edge of no patch in the region, and ω is the slope of no function there. The form finds the hole without looking for it. Its total round a loop counts how many times the loop goes round: twice round gives 4π, and a loop that misses the wire gives 0. The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

A form of degree k is a differential k-form, and dx dy is written dx ∧ dy, the wedge product, so that the forms make an exterior algebra. d is the exterior derivative. The swirl arrow is the curl, curl F or ∇ × F; the spread is the divergence, div F or ∇ · F. The twice-nothing technique is d² = 0: curl grad f = 0 and div curl F = 0. The boundary-rule technique, for every region and every form, is the generalized Stokes theorem, ∫_∂M ω = ∫_M dω; carrying a form back along a patch is the pullback, and the curved-swirl technique is the Kelvin–Stokes theorem. A form with dω = 0 is closed, and one that is d of another is exact; that closed forms are exact on a region with no holes is the Poincaré lemma, and the closed forms that are not exact, counted, make the de Rham cohomology of the region. The number of times a loop goes round the wire is its winding number. A field whose swirl arrow is B is a vector potential for B, and ∮ B · dr = μ₀I is Ampère’s law.

Who said it first

Hermann Grassmann built the algebra in which dx dy = −dy dx in 1844. Élie Cartan wrote forms with d in 1899, and Henri Poincaré used them in his work on celestial mechanics in the same decade. The curved-swirl technique was sent by William Thomson to George Stokes in a letter of 1850, and Stokes set it as a Cambridge examination question in 1854. Vito Volterra stated a version for any number of directions in 1889, and the form ∫_∂M ω = ∫_M dω was set out by Cartan and others in the early 1900s. Georges de Rham proved in 1931 that forms count holes exactly. In 29·cb28.8.5, the four-part numbers are William Rowan Hamilton’s quaternions (1843), defended by Peter Guthrie Tait; the professor at Yale is Josiah Willard Gibbs and the engineer Oliver Heaviside, whose vector analysis won the textbooks; the third way is Cartan’s.
