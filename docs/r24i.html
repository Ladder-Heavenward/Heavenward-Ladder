24i · technique

Slope fields and stepping forward

Most rules on rates have no formula for their solution. The rule still says, at every place, which way the solution must head. Draw that as a short stroke at each point of a grid and you have a field of slopes, and every solution is a curve that runs along the strokes. To follow one, start at the start, step a short way along the stroke you are standing on, and look again. This step makes that walk exact enough to trust. It counts how far one step can slip, and how the slips of many steps add up, using the matching polynomials of the last rung and the drift of nearby starts from this one. It finds a better step that looks ahead before it moves. And it meets rules that punish the plain walk however carefully it is taken, because the step must be tiny to keep the walk steady long after the solution itself has gone quiet.

A rule on rates draws a field of slopes, and a solution runs along it. Step along the stroke you stand on, and look again. Each step slips by about the square of its length, and the slips of a whole walk add to about one step length. Looking ahead before you step makes the slips much smaller. Some rules make the plain walk swing wildly unless the steps are tiny, and a step backward cures it.

24i1Walking the field

From here on, the slope field of y′ = F(x, y) is the picture with, at each point (x, y), a short stroke of slope F(x, y). The forward walk with step h from (x₀, y₀) is xₙ₊₁ = xₙ + h, yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h·F(xₙ, yₙ). Accepted name: a direction field, and the forward step method.

A solution through a point leaves it along the stroke there, since its slope is F at that point. The walk does the same, but keeps that slope for a whole step, while the solution bends away. So each step lands a little off the solution it set out on.

Small-step technique: for y′ = ky from (0, A), n forward steps of length x/n land at A(1 + kx/n)ⁿ, which heads to Ae^(kx) as n grows. The walk closes in on the one solution of 24a3.

y_{j+1} = y_j + h\,k\,y_j = (1 + hk)\,y_j @@ one forward step of y′ = ky
y_n = A(1 + hk)^n = A\big(1 + \tfrac{kx}{n}\big)^n @@ n steps, with h = x/n
\big(1 + \tfrac{u}{n}\big)^n \to e^{u} \text{ for every real } u @@ n·ln(1 + u/n) heads to u, since ln has slope 1 at 1 (17b1); exp carries the limit through (11e)
\htmlClass{new}{y_n \to Ae^{kx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the one solution, by the own-rate technique (24a3)

With k = 1, A = 1 and x = 1: four steps give 1.25⁴ = 2.4414, eight give 2.5658, sixteen give 2.6379, against e = 2.71828. The errors are 0.277, 0.152, 0.080. Doubling the steps about halves the error. The next footing says why.

24i2The slip of one step, and of the walk

Walk-error technique: let F be tame with constant L on a region holding the walk and the solutions through its points, and let every such solution have second slope at most K in size. Then the forward walk with step h lands, at x, within (Kh/2L)(e^(L(x − x₀)) − 1) of the solution through the start. Each step slips by at most Kh²/2; each slip moves the walk onto a nearby solution; and nearby solutions drift apart no faster than the drift technique allows.

z_j = \text{the solution through } (x_j, y_j);\ \ z_0 = y \text{, the solution through the start} @@ the walk's own points, each a start (24d2)
z_j(x_{j+1}) = y_j + h\,F(x_j, y_j) + \tfrac12 z_j''(c)\,h^2 = y_{j+1} + \tfrac12 z_j''(c)\,h^2 @@ the match-error technique (23b1), with n = 1: z_j′(x_j) = F(x_j, y_j)
|z_j(x_{j+1}) - z_{j+1}(x_{j+1})| \le \tfrac12 K h^2 @@ the error-ceiling technique (23b2): one step's slip
|z_j(x_N) - z_{j+1}(x_N)| \le \tfrac12 K h^2\, e^{L(x_N - x_{j+1})} @@ the drift technique (24d3), from x_{j+1} to x_N
|y_N - y(x_N)| \le \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} |z_{j+1}(x_N) - z_j(x_N)| @@ y_N = z_N(x_N); add the gaps (8h, the triangle technique)
\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} h\,e^{L(x_N - x_{j+1})} \le \int_{x_0}^{x_N} e^{L(x_N - t)}\,dt = \tfrac{1}{L}\big(e^{L(x_N - x_0)} - 1\big) @@ each box sits under the falling curve (22f1)
\htmlClass{new}{|y_N - y(x_N)| \le \tfrac{Kh}{2L}\big(e^{L(x_N - x_0)} - 1\big)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the last two lines, times Kh/2

Read the two sizes apart. One step slips by about h². A walk to a fixed x takes (x − x₀)/h steps, so the slips add to about h. Halve the step, and each slip falls to a quarter, the count of steps doubles, and the walk's error halves. For y′ = y on [0, 1], K = e and L = 1, and the bound at h = 0.25 is 0.584, against a true error of 0.277. The bound is honest, not tight.

The factor e^(L(x − x₀)) is the drift technique's. The walk does not fail because one step is bad. It fails because every slip becomes a new start, and starts drift.

24i3Looking ahead

From here on, a look-ahead step from (xₙ, yₙ) takes the slope s₁ = F(xₙ, yₙ), takes a trial step to ỹ = yₙ + h·s₁, reads the slope s₂ = F(xₙ₊₁, ỹ) there, and then steps with the average: yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h(s₁ + s₂)/2. Accepted name: the improved (trapezoidal) step, a second-order step method.

Look-ahead technique: for y′ = ky, one look-ahead step multiplies by 1 + hk + (hk)²/2, the matching polynomial of e^(hk) of degree 2; for k > 0 the walk lands within Axk³h²e^(kx)/6 of Ae^(kx). And for y′ = f(x) the look-ahead walk is the trapezoid sum of 23l1.

s_1 = ky_n,\ \ \tilde y = (1 + hk)\,y_n,\ \ s_2 = k(1 + hk)\,y_n @@ ﹀ “look-ahead step”, with F(x, y) = ky
y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\big(ky_n + k(1 + hk)y_n\big) = \big(1 + u + \tfrac{u^2}{2}\big)\,y_n,\ \ u = hk @@ collect
0 \le e^{u} - \big(1 + u + \tfrac{u^2}{2}\big) \le \tfrac{u^3}{6}e^{u} \ \ (u \ge 0) @@ the error-ceiling technique (23b2) for exp, n = 2: its third slope is at most e^u
b^n - a^n = (b - a)(b^{n-1} + \cdots + a^{n-1}) \le n\,(b - a)\,b^{n-1} \ \ (0 \le a \le b) @@ the difference-of-powers technique (13h2)
\htmlClass{new}{0 \le Ae^{kx} - y_n \le A\,n\tfrac{u^3}{6}e^{u}e^{(n-1)u} = \tfrac{A\,x\,k^3h^2}{6}\,e^{kx}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a = 1 + u + u²/2, b = e^u, nh = x
F = f(x):\ \ y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}\big(f(x_n) + f(x_{n+1})\big) @@ the trial value ỹ is never used: one trapezoid strip per step (23l1)

Now each step slips by about h³, and the walk by about h². For y′ = y on [0, 1]: four look-ahead steps give 2.6949, an error of 0.023, against 0.277 for four forward steps. Eight give 2.7118, an error of 0.0064: halving the step quartered the error, as h² says. The same holds for every rule with enough slopes; the proof is the same matching, with more letters, and the build challenge asks for one case.

A second look-ahead takes its slope at the middle of the step: a half step to the middle, read the slope there, and use it for the whole step. For y′ = ky it multiplies by the same 1 + u + u²/2. For y′ = f(x) it is the midpoint box sum.

24i4Rules that punish the plain walk

Stiff-step technique: for y′ = −ky with k > 0, forward steps shrink toward 0, as the solution does, exactly when hk < 2; backward steps, yₙ₊₁ = yₙ + h·F(xₙ₊₁, yₙ₊₁), shrink toward 0 for every h > 0.

y_{n+1} = y_n - hk\,y_n = (1 - hk)\,y_n,\ \ y_n = (1 - hk)^n y_0 @@ forward steps
|1 - hk| < 1 \iff 0 < hk < 2 @@ unpack the size
hk > 2:\ |y_n| = |1 - hk|^n\,|y_0| \text{ passes every ceiling, with alternating sign} @@ a power of a number above 1 in size (22h2, turned over)
y_{n+1} = y_n - hk\,y_{n+1} \Rightarrow y_{n+1} = \tfrac{1}{1 + hk}\,y_n @@ backward steps: the new value appears on both sides; solve for it
\htmlClass{new}{0 < \tfrac{1}{1 + hk} < 1 \text{ for every } h > 0,\ \text{so backward steps settle at } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ the standard sequences (22h2)

A rule is stiff when it holds a part that dies fast beside a part that moves slowly. Take y′ = −50(y − cos x) from y(0) = 0. Within a tenth of a unit the solution lies on cos x, nudged a little, and after that it is as gentle as cos x. A step of 0.1 would follow cos x well. But near the solution the rule is y′ ≈ −50y plus a slow part, so the forward walk needs 50h < 2, that is h < 0.04, to stay steady at all. At h = 0.03 it gives 0.5654 at x = 1, against the true 0.5569. At h = 0.05 it gives −3325. Backward steps of 0.1 give 0.5563.

So small steps are not enough, or rather they are enough at a price that can be ruinous. A reaction in a flame can hold a step that dies in a millionth of a second beside one that takes a second. Forward steps would need half a million of them for each second, every one of them forced by a part of the solution that vanished long ago. The backward step costs more per step, since the new value must be solved for, and wins by taking steps sized to what is still happening.

24i5Illegal moves

\text{halve the step, and each step's slip halves}\,? @@ a slip is about h², so it falls to a quarter; the walk's error halves, since there are twice as many slips
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,F(x_{n+1}, \tilde y) \text{ is the look-ahead step}\,? @@ the slope at the trial point alone is as crude as the forward step; the average of the two slopes is what matches degree 2
\text{a smaller step always gives a better walk}\,? @@ every step also rounds; with many tiny steps the rounding slips add up, and can swamp the rest
\text{the solution is gentle, so a step that follows it is steady}\,? @@ on a stiff rule the plain walk swings wildly unless hk < 2, however gentle the solution

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The forward walk is Euler's method; the look-ahead step is Heun's method, or the improved Euler method, and the step that reads the slope at the middle is the midpoint method; both are second-order Runge–Kutta methods. The backward step is the backward (implicit) Euler method. One step's slip is the local truncation error, the walk's error is the global error, and a method whose walk error is about hᵖ has order p. The interval of h on which a method stays steady for y′ = −ky is its region of absolute stability.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler described stepping along the field in his Institutiones calculi integralis (1768). Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved, around 1824, that the walks settle on a solution as the step shrinks, the first proof that solutions exist. Karl Heun (1900) and Carl Runge (1895) and Wilhelm Kutta (1901) built the look-ahead steps. Charles Curtiss and Joseph Hirschfelder named stiff equations in 1952, studying the chemistry of flames.
