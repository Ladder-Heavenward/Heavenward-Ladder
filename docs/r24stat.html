How Long Will It Last, When Some Leave Before the End?

How long will it last, when some leave before the end? A clinic tries a new treatment and follows its patients for a year. Some die during the year, most do not, and some move away halfway, still alive. A machine shop tests bearings until they wear out, and stops the test before the last ones fail. Both want the share still going at each time. This page writes that share as the solution of a rate statement of rung 24, finds the time by which half have failed, lets the rate change with age, counts correctly the ones who left early, and compares two groups by a single ratio.

Let S(t) be the share still going at time t, and the hazard h the rate of failing among those still going: S′ = −hS. For a steady hazard, S = e^(−ht) and half are gone by ln 2/h; for a changing one, S = e^(−∫h). For those who leave early, multiply, at each failure, by the share of those still watched who come through it. Two groups with hazards in a fixed ratio r have S₁ = S₀ʳ.

24stat1The Share Still Going

From here on, on this page, the survival curve S(t) is the share of a group still going at time t, with S(0) = 1. The hazard h(t) is the rate at which those still going fail, per one still going: out of S(t), about h(t)·S(t)·Δt fail in the next short time Δt.

S(t + \Delta t) - S(t) \approx -h(t)\,S(t)\,\Delta t @@ the failures in Δt
\htmlClass{new}{S\prime = -h\,S,\ \ S(0) = 1} @@ divide by Δt and let it shrink (13)
h = -S\prime/S @@ read the other way: the hazard from the curve

The hazard is not the chance of failing by time t. It is the rate among the ones still going: a bearing that has lasted 5 years may be more likely to fail this month than a new one, or less.

24stat2A Steady Hazard

Say the bearings fail at a steady hazard h, 0.1 a year, whatever their age.

S\prime = -hS,\ S(0) = 1 @@ the rate statement
S(t) = e^{-ht} @@ the own-rate technique (24a): exactly one solution
S(T) = \tfrac12 \iff T = \ln 2 / h @@ the log undoes exp (17e)
h = 0.1:\ T = 0.693/0.1 \approx 6.9 \text{ years} @@ half are gone
\htmlClass{new}{\text{average life} = \textstyle\int_0^\infty e^{-ht}\,dt = 1/h = 10} @@ the area under S, out to forever (16i)

Steady-hazard technique: with a steady hazard h, the share still going is e−ht, half are gone by ln 2/h, and the average life is 1/h. A part with a steady hazard does not age: having lasted 5 years, it faces the same next 5 as a new one, since S(t + 5)/S(t) = e−5h.

24stat3A Hazard That Grows

Bearings wear: their hazard grows with age. Say h(t) = 2ct, rising in a straight line. Separate the variables as in 24f.

S\prime / S = -h(t) @@ the side-by-side technique (24f)
\ln S(t) = -\textstyle\int_0^t h @@ area is a change (16c), ln S(0) = 0
S(t) = e^{-\int_0^t h} @@ any hazard
\htmlClass{new}{h = 2ct:\ S(t) = e^{-ct^2}} @@ a curve that falls slowly, then fast

Any-hazard technique: the share still going is e to the minus the area under the hazard so far. A falling hazard fits things that fail early if they fail at all; a rising one fits things that wear out. Many machines show both, early faults and late wear, with a flat stretch between.

24stat4Those Who Leave Early

The clinic followed 8 patients. Some died, at the months shown; some left, still alive, at the months marked +. Leaving says only that the patient lived at least that long.

months23+455+79+10
left in view before87654321
2:\ 1 \text{ of } 8 \text{ dies}:\ S = \tfrac78 = 0.875 @@ the share who came through
4:\ 1 \text{ of } 6:\ S = 0.875 \cdot \tfrac56 \approx 0.729 @@ the one who left at 3 is no longer counted
5:\ 1 \text{ of } 5:\ S \approx 0.729 \cdot \tfrac45 \approx 0.583 @@ a death before a leaving at the same month
7:\ 1 \text{ of } 3:\ S \approx 0.583 \cdot \tfrac23 \approx 0.389 @@ two have left since
\htmlClass{new}{S(6) \approx 0.583} @@ not 2/5, which drops those who left

Dropping the three who left leaves 5 with known ends, 3 dead by month 6: a share of 2/5. That throws away months of life the leavers were seen to have. Product technique: at each death, multiply by the share of those still in view who come through it; each leaver counts while in view and then drops out of the count.

24stat5Two Groups, One Ratio

The treatment is compared with the old care. Often the hazard of one group is a fixed multiple r of the other’s at every time, whatever the shape of the hazard.

h_1(t) = r\,h_0(t) @@ the same shape, scaled
S_1(t) = e^{-r\int_0^t h_0} @@ the any-hazard technique
\htmlClass{new}{S_1 = S_0^{\,r}} @@ exp(rx) = (exp x)ʳ (17e)
r = \tfrac12,\ S_0(t) = 0.36:\ S_1(t) = 0.6 @@ half the hazard, not half the deaths

When two hazards keep a fixed ratio r, the curves obey S₁ = S₀r, and one number r sums up the difference at every time. So a trial can report r without knowing the shape of either hazard. Whether the ratio really is fixed must be checked from the curves.

The cellar · there if you want it

Accepted names

The share still going is the survival function, and the rate among those still going the hazard function or failure rate. A steady hazard gives the exponential distribution, which is memoryless; h = 2ct gives a case of the Weibull distribution, and early faults with late wear make the bathtub curve. Leaving early is right censoring, and the product is the Kaplan-Meier estimator. Hazards in a fixed ratio are proportional hazards, r is the hazard ratio, and fitting it is Cox regression.

Who said it first

Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier published the product in the Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1958. David Cox set out proportional hazards in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, 1972.
