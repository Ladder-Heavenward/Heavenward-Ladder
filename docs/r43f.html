43f · proof

Field extensions

The complex numbers hold the reals, and every complex number is a real plus a real times i: two real numbers fix it. The fractions with the square root of two put in hold the fractions, and two fractions fix each member. That is the dimension of rung twenty seven, spent on a new kind of space: a big field, read as arrows that the small field stretches. The count of arrows in a basis is how far the big field reaches past the small one. When the big field is made by putting in one root of an unsplittable polynomial, the count is the degree of that polynomial, and the field is the polynomials divided by it, as step 43b found the complex numbers to be the real polynomials divided by x squared plus one. And when one field sits inside a second, inside a third, the counts multiply. That one rule settles questions that look hard: the cube root of two is not made from square roots of square roots alone, and the square root of two is not made from the cube root of two.

A field inside a bigger one makes the bigger one a space of arrows, and its dimension is the degree. Putting in one root of an unsplittable polynomial of degree d gives a field of degree d, a copy of the polynomials divided by it. Along a tower of fields the degrees multiply, so the degree of any member divides the degree of the field it lies in.

43f1A big field over a small one

Let K be a field inside a field L, with the same plus and times. Read members of L as arrows, added in L, and stretched by members of K, multiplied in L. 27a’s eight rules hold, because they are rules of the field L.

Field-as-space technique: a field L holding a field K is a space of arrows over K, and its dimension is the degree [L : K]. The degree may be endless.

a + bi = a \cdot 1 + b \cdot i,\ \ a, b \in \mathbb R @@ 1 and i build ℂ, and are free: a + bi = 0 only when a = b = 0
[\mathbb C : \mathbb R] = 2 @@ the same-size technique (27b)
[\mathbb Q(\sqrt2) : \mathbb Q] = 2 @@ 1 and √2 free over ℚ, since √2 is no fraction (5f)
\htmlClass{new}{[\mathbb R : \mathbb Q] \text{ is endless}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a space of n arrows over ℚ is listed like ℚⁿ (35a); ℝ cannot be listed (35b)

Here ℚ(√2) is the smallest field inside ℝ holding ℚ and √2. Its members are a + b√2 with a, b fractions, and 43k divides them by hand.

43f2Putting in one root

Let α in L be a zero of some polynomial over K other than 0. Putting α in, p ↦ p(α), is a ring map from K[x] to L. What it loses is an ideal other than {0}, so by the one-maker technique it is (m) for one polynomial m, chosen with top coefficient 1. This m is α’s least polynomial, as 27g’s least-obeyed technique found one for a grid. It cannot be split: if m = gh, then g(α)h(α) = 0, so g(α) or h(α) is 0, and that one is a multiple of m.

Root-field technique: if α has least polynomial m of degree d over K, then K(α) is a copy of K[x]/(m), with basis 1, α, …, α^(d − 1), and [K(α) : K] = d.

K[x]/(m) \text{ is a copy of what } p \mapsto p(\alpha) \text{ reaches} @@ the lost-ideal technique (43b)
(m) \text{ is largest, so that copy is a field} @@ m cannot be split; 43d1’s proof of the unsplittable-is-prime technique; the largest-ideal technique
\text{so what is reached is } K(\alpha) @@ a field holding K and α, made of polynomials in α
p = mq + r,\ \ \deg r < d \Rightarrow p(\alpha) = r(\alpha) @@ division (43c); m(α) = 0
\htmlClass{new}{1, \alpha, \ldots, \alpha^{d-1} \text{ build } K(\alpha) \text{ and are free}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a mix equal to 0 is r(α) = 0 with deg r < d, so r is a multiple of m, so r = 0

∛2 has least polynomial x³ − 2 over ℚ, since 43d’s prime-wedge technique showed it cannot be split, and a polynomial that cannot be split with top 1 and zero ∛2 is the least. So [ℚ(∛2) : ℚ] = 3, with basis 1, ∛2, ∛4. And ℝ[x]/(x² + 1) gave ℂ in 43b by exactly this.

43f3Towers

Tower technique: if K ⊆ L ⊆ M are fields with [M : L] = m and [L : K] = n, then [M : K] = mn.

u_1, \ldots, u_n \text{ a basis of } L \text{ over } K;\ \ v_1, \ldots, v_m \text{ of } M \text{ over } L @@ given
z = \textstyle\sum_j b_j v_j,\ \ b_j = \sum_i c_{ij} u_i,\ c_{ij} \in K @@ write z over L, then each bⱼ over K
z = \textstyle\sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\, u_i v_j @@ so the mn products build M over K
\textstyle\sum_{i,j} c_{ij} u_i v_j = 0 \Rightarrow \sum_i c_{ij} u_i = 0 \text{ for each } j @@ the vⱼ are free over L
\htmlClass{new}{c_{ij} = 0 \text{ for all } i, j:\ [M : K] = mn} \quad \blacksquare @@ the uᵢ are free over K

ℚ(√2, √3), the fractions with both roots put in, sits over ℚ(√2), which sits over ℚ. √3 is not in ℚ(√2), since (a + b√2)² = 3 forces 2ab = 0, and neither a² = 3 nor 2b² = 3 has a fraction answer. So the top step has degree 2, the bottom 2, and the whole tower 4, with basis 1, √2, √3, √6.

43f4Degrees divide

Degree-divides technique: if α lies in a field L of finite degree over K, the degree of α’s least polynomial divides [L : K].

K \subseteq K(\alpha) \subseteq L @@ K(α) is the smallest field holding K and α
[L : K] = [L : K(\alpha)]\,[K(\alpha) : K] @@ the tower technique
\htmlClass{new}{\deg m_\alpha = [K(\alpha) : K] \text{ divides } [L : K]} \quad \blacksquare @@ the root-field technique
  
\sqrt2 \in \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2) \Rightarrow 2 \mid 3 @@ √2 has degree 2; the field has degree 3
\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2) @@ 2 does not divide 3
L \text{ reached by square roots, one at a time} \Rightarrow [L : \mathbb Q] = 2^k @@ each step has degree 1 or 2 (the tower technique)
\htmlClass{new}{\sqrt[3]2 \notin L:\ 3 \nmid 2^k} \quad \blacksquare @@ the degree-divides technique

The second consequence is the heart of 44i, where square roots stand for what a straightedge and compass can reach: doubling a cube means building ∛2, and the degrees say no.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

A field L holding K is an extension L/K, and [L : K] is its degree; extensions of finite degree are finite extensions. A member with a least polynomial is algebraic over K, and the least polynomial is the minimal polynomial. The root-field technique says K(α) ≅ K[x]/(m_α); the tower technique is the tower law, and the degree-divides technique is often stated as: the degree of an algebraic element divides the degree of any finite extension containing it.

Who said it first

Richard Dedekind read fields as spaces over smaller fields in his supplements to Dirichlet’s lectures (1871 to 1894), and the word degree for [L : K] is his. Leopold Kronecker built K(α) as the polynomials divided by m in 1882. Pierre Wantzel proved in 1837 that doubling the cube and trisecting an angle cannot be done with straightedge and compass, by an argument close to the degree-divides technique. Ernst Steinitz wrote the theory of fields as a whole in 1910.

A proof that is not allowed

“√2 + √3 is a sum of two members of degree 2, so it has degree 2 or 4, and since it is no fraction, it has degree 2.” Not allowed: being no fraction rules out degree 1 only. The least polynomial of √2 + √3 is x⁴ − 10x² + 1, of degree 4.
