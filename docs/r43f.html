43f · proof

Squares on a Clock

On the clock of eleven, the squares of the hours land on only five of the ten hours that are not twelve: one, three, four, five and nine. Seven is not a square there, however long you search. On a prime clock exactly half the units are squares, and the full hand of the last step says which: its even powers. A test follows that needs no search: raise to half the count of units, and see whether one comes out. Then the step proves the deepest fact of this rung, found by a young man of eighteen in 1796 after years of trying. Whether one odd prime is a square on the clock of another is tied, by a simple sign, to whether the second is a square on the clock of the first. Two questions about two different clocks turn out to be one. The proof counts points under a slanted line in a box, and nothing else, and it has the feel of a trick until you see why the count must split that way.

On an odd prime clock half the units are squares, the even powers of a full hand. Raising to half the count of units gives one exactly for squares. A square is told by counting how many multiples land in the upper half of the clock. And for two odd primes, each is a square on the other’s clock in the same way, unless both are three more than a multiple of four, when exactly one is.

43f1Half the Units Are Squares

Let p be an odd prime. An hour [a] with p ∤ a is a square on the clock of p when [x]² = [a] for some [x]. On the clock of 11 the squares of 1 to 10 are 1, 4, 9, 5, 3, 3, 5, 9, 4, 1.

Half-squares technique: on the clock of an odd prime p, exactly (p − 1)/2 of the units are squares, and they are the even powers of any full hand.

[x]^2 = [y]^2 \iff ([x] - [y])([x] + [y]) = [0] \iff [x] = \pm[y] @@ a prime clock is a field: no zero product (43b4)
[x] \ne -[x] \text{ for } [x] \ne [0] @@ else [2][x] = [0], and p is odd
\text{the } p - 1 \text{ units pair off as } \pm[x],\ \text{each pair one square} @@ the line above
\htmlClass{new}{(p - 1)/2 \text{ squares}} @@ one for each pair
[g] \text{ a full hand: } [g]^{2j} = ([g]^j)^2 @@ the full-hand technique (43e4): every unit is a power of [g]
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the squares are } [g]^0, [g]^2, \ldots, [g]^{p-3}} \quad \blacksquare @@ (p − 1)/2 even powers, each a square, and no more squares exist

From here on, for an odd prime p and an integer a, the mark (a ∕ p), written in display lines as a over p inside round brackets, is +1 when [a] is a square unit, −1 when [a] is a unit and not a square, and 0 when p ∣ a. It is not a fraction: the round brackets around it are part of the mark. Read it “a on p”. So (3 ∕ 11) = +1 and (7 ∕ 11) = −1.

43f2The Test for a Square

Square-test technique: for an odd prime p and p ∤ a, a^((p − 1)/2) ≡ (a ∕ p) (mod p).

h = (p - 1)/2,\ \ ([a]^h)^2 = [a]^{p-1} = [1] @@ the little technique (43d1)
[a]^h = \pm[1] @@ the roots of x² = 1 on a prime clock (43f1)
[a] = [g]^k:\ \ [a]^h = [g]^{kh} @@ a full hand [g] (43e4)
[g]^h \ne [1],\ \text{so } [g]^h = -[1] @@ [g] has order p − 1 = 2h, not h
[a]^h = (-[1])^k @@ powers of a power
\htmlClass{new}{[a]^h = [1] \iff k \text{ even} \iff [a] \text{ a square}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the half-squares technique

Two things follow at once. The mark multiplies: (ab ∕ p) = (a ∕ p)(b ∕ p), since (ab)ʰ = aʰbʰ. And −1 is a square on the clock of p exactly when (p − 1)/2 is even, so exactly when p ≡ 1 (mod 4): 2² = 4 ≡ −1 on the clock of 5, 5² = 25 ≡ −1 on the clock of 13, and on the clock of 7 no square is 6.

43f3Counting the Multiples That Land High

Write h = (p − 1)/2. Each hour but [0] is ±r for exactly one r from 1 to h: call r its fold. An hour lands high when its least member between 0 and p is above p/2, so it is −r.

High-count technique: for an odd prime p and p ∤ a, (a ∕ p) = (−1)^μ, where μ is the number of a, 2a, …, ha that land high on the clock of p.

[ka] = \varepsilon_k [r_k],\ \ \varepsilon_k = \pm1,\ \ 1 \le r_k \le h @@ each multiple, folded
r_j = r_k \Rightarrow [ja] = \pm[ka] \Rightarrow j = \pm k \Rightarrow j = k @@ cancel [a] (43a4); j + k lies between 2 and p − 1
\{r_1, \ldots, r_h\} = \{1, \ldots, h\} @@ h different folds, each from 1 to h
[a]^h\,[h!] = \textstyle\prod_k [ka] = \prod_k \varepsilon_k\,[r_k] = (-1)^\mu\,[h!] @@ multiply the h lines
\htmlClass{new}{[a]^h = (-1)^\mu \text{, and so } (a \,/\, p) = (-1)^\mu} \quad \blacksquare @@ [h!] is a unit; the square-test technique

For a = 2 the multiples 2, 4, …, p − 1 land high when they pass p/2. On the clock of 7: 2, 4, 6, and 4 and 6 land high, so μ = 2 and 2 is a square: 3² = 9 ≡ 2. On the clock of 11: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and three land high, so 2 is not a square there. Counting in general gives the rule: 2 is a square on the clock of p exactly when p ≡ 1 or 7 (mod 8).

43f4Each Prime on the Other’s Clock

Square-swap technique: for different odd primes p and q, (p ∕ q)(q ∕ p) = (−1) to the power ((p − 1)/2)((q − 1)/2): each is a square on the other’s clock in the same way, unless both are 3 more than a multiple of 4, and then exactly one is.

First, μ can be counted with whole parts. Write ⌊t⌋ for the greatest integer at most t, its whole part; so ⌊17/5⌋ = 3. For odd a with p ∤ a, put T = ⌊a/p⌋ + ⌊2a/p⌋ + ⋯ + ⌊ha/p⌋. Then μ and T are both even or both odd. Let s be the sum of the remainders of ka by p that land low, and l the sum of those that land high, and H = 1 + ⋯ + h. The folds are 1 to h, so s + (μp − l) = H. And by 4j7, aH = pT + s + l. Taking the first from the second, (a − 1)H = pT − μp + 2l. Here a − 1 is even, and p is odd, so T − μ is even.

\text{the box: } 1 \le x \le h_p,\ 1 \le y \le h_q,\ \ h_p = \tfrac{p-1}2,\ h_q = \tfrac{q-1}2 @@ h_p h_q whole-number points
qx = py \text{ at no point of the box} @@ p ∣ qx would need p ∣ x, and x < p
\text{below the line } y = qx/p \text{ in column } x:\ \lfloor qx/p \rfloor \text{ points} @@ the whole part; all lie in the box, since qx/p < q/2
\textstyle\sum_x \lfloor qx/p\rfloor + \sum_y \lfloor py/q\rfloor = h_p h_q @@ the points above the line, counted by rows, the same way
\left(\tfrac qp\right)\left(\tfrac pq\right) = (-1)^{T(q, p)}(-1)^{T(p, q)} @@ the high-count technique with μ and T alike in evenness, twice
\htmlClass{new}{= (-1)^{h_p h_q}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the sum of the two counts

With p = 5 and q = 19, h_p h_q = 2 · 9 is even, so 5 is a square on the clock of 19 exactly when 19 ≡ 4 is a square on the clock of 5, and 4 = 2². So 5 is a square on the clock of 19: 9² = 81 = 4 · 19 + 5.

43f5Deciding a Square by Swapping

With the mark multiplying, the rules for −1 and 2, and the square-swap technique, a question on a big clock shrinks to questions on small ones, much as the gcd algorithm shrinks a pair. Is 219 a square on the clock of 383? Both 383 and 3 are prime.

219 = 3 \cdot 73:\ \ \left(\tfrac{219}{383}\right) = \left(\tfrac{3}{383}\right)\left(\tfrac{73}{383}\right) @@ the mark multiplies (43f2)
\left(\tfrac{3}{383}\right) = -\left(\tfrac{383}{3}\right) = -\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) = -(-1) = 1 @@ both are 3 more than a multiple of 4; 383 = 3 · 127 + 2; 3 ≡ 3 (mod 8)
\left(\tfrac{73}{383}\right) = \left(\tfrac{383}{73}\right) = \left(\tfrac{18}{73}\right) @@ 73 ≡ 1 (mod 4); 383 = 5 · 73 + 18
\left(\tfrac{18}{73}\right) = \left(\tfrac{2}{73}\right)\left(\tfrac{3}{73}\right)^2 = 1 @@ 73 ≡ 1 (mod 8); a square of ±1 is 1
\htmlClass{new}{\left(\tfrac{219}{383}\right) = 1:\ 219 \text{ is a square on the clock of } 383} \quad \blacksquare @@ the two factors

The proof says a square root exists and does not find it. Searching 191 candidates would. 43f.7.1 asks for a quicker road on clocks like 383, which are 3 more than a multiple of 4. And squares on the clock of 8 settle an old question of rung 7: the squares there are 0, 1 and 4, so three squares add to 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 on the clock of 8, never 7. No number 8k + 7 is a sum of three squares. That every other number not of the form 4ʲ(8k + 7) is one is stated here, and its proof is much higher up the ladder.

The accepted names

A square unit modulo p is a quadratic residue, a non-square a quadratic non-residue. The mark (a ∕ p) is the Legendre symbol. The square-test technique is Euler’s criterion; the rules for −1 and 2 are the first and second supplementary laws. The high-count technique is Gauss’s lemma, and its counting form with T is Eisenstein’s. The square-swap technique is the law of quadratic reciprocity, and the box proof is Eisenstein’s (1844). The whole part ⌊t⌋ is the floor function. Fermat’s theorem on sums of two squares is 43f.7.2. Numbers 4ʲ(8k + 7) are exactly those that are not sums of three squares: Legendre’s three-square theorem.

Who said it first

Leonhard Euler guessed reciprocity from tables in the 1740s to 1783. Adrien-Marie Legendre stated it in 1785 and published a proof in 1798 that assumed what Dirichlet proved in 1837, primes in progressions; Gauss criticised it. Carl Friedrich Gauss found the first full proof on 8 April 1796, aged eighteen, published it in 1801, and gave eight proofs in all. Gotthold Eisenstein’s lattice proof is from 1844. The three-square theorem is Legendre’s (1798), completed by Gauss (1801). Fermat claimed the two-squares theorem in 1640; Euler proved it in 1749.

A proof that is not allowed

“3 is a square on the clock of 13, since 4² = 16 ≡ 3. So by the square-swap technique 13 is a square on the clock of 3, so 13 is a square on every clock.” Not allowed: the technique ties two clocks, 3 and 13, and says nothing about a third. 13 ≡ 1 is a square on the clock of 3, and 13 is not a square on the clock of 5, where the squares are 1 and 4 and 13 ≡ 3.
