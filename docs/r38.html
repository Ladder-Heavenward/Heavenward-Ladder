Arithmetic describes its own proofs

Rung 0 began with the rules of reasoning, and every rung since has used them: a proof is a list of lines, each one given or got from earlier lines by a move. This rung turns round and looks at the rules themselves. First it writes down a language exact enough that a statement is a string of marks and a proof is a list of strings, so that whether something is a proof is a matter of checking, which always stops. Then it asks how proofs and truth meet. What the moves reach is true in every world where the axioms hold, and, the other way, what is true in every such world is reached: proof and truth everywhere are one. That has a price. Any collection of axioms with an endless world has a countable one, so the axioms of the line have a world whose points can be listed, and the axioms of arithmetic have a world with numbers past every numeral. Then the rung writes statements as numbers, so that arithmetic can talk about its own strings and proofs, and builds a statement that says of itself that it is not reached. If arithmetic is clash-free, that statement is true and unreached; no listed collection of true axioms reaches every truth of arithmetic; arithmetic cannot prove its own freedom from clash; and truth in arithmetic cannot be said in arithmetic’s own language. The last steps quantify over sets, number formulas by hand, and do proofs by hand in the shape a tree takes.

A statement is a string and a proof a list of strings, so checking a proof is a recipe. What is reached is true in every world of the axioms, and what is true in every world is reached. Every endless world has a countable one beside it, and arithmetic has worlds with numbers past every numeral. Coded as numbers, arithmetic speaks of its own proofs, and a statement saying it is not reached is true and unreached; arithmetic cannot certify itself, and cannot define its own truth.

What it means to say it

To say that arithmetic describes its own proofs is to say that statements and proofs, written exactly, are finite strings of marks; that strings can be numbered; and that “this number is the code of a proof of that statement” is then a fact about numbers, which arithmetic’s own statements can express. Arithmetic, which proves things about numbers, thereby proves things about proofs, including its own.

What is reached from a collection of axioms is exactly what is true in every world where they hold; and arithmetic, describing its own proofs, has a true statement it does not reach, cannot reach its own freedom from clash, and cannot define its own truth. The rung proves it, and some steps of the proof are held in trust where it says so.

Where it comes from

In 1879 a calculus of statements with “every” and “some” was written out in full for the first time. In 1889 the axioms of arithmetic were set down, and from 1910 to 1913 a large part of mathematics was derived in a calculus by hand. In 1915 and 1922 it was seen that a collection with an endless world has a countable one, which made the countable line a puzzle. In 1929 and 1930 it was proved that what holds in every world is reached. In September 1930 a programme to prove mathematics free of clash by finite means was announced at a meeting, and at the same meeting the first sign was given that it could not be done; the full result appeared in 1931. Truth was shown to be undefinable within in 1933, natural deduction was written out in 1934, and in 1961 numbers past every numeral were used to rebuild the calculus with endlessly small numbers. The names are in the basements of the steps.

The principles, in plain words

The rung proves seven things.

First, a statement is a string of marks with one reading, and a proof is a list of statements, each an axiom or got from earlier ones by a move. Whether a list is a proof is settled by checking, which always stops, and the proofs from a listed collection can themselves be listed.

Second, each move keeps truth in a world, so what is reached from a collection is true in every world where the collection holds.

Third, the other way: a collection free of clash has a world, built from its own terms, so what is true in every world of a collection is reached from it. Proof and truth everywhere are one, and a statement follows from a collection exactly when it follows from a finite part of it.

Fourth, a collection with an endless world has a countable one, and one bigger than any given size. The axioms of the line have a countable world, and the axioms of arithmetic have worlds with numbers past every numeral.

Fifth, strings and lists can be coded as numbers, and the axioms of arithmetic can say of a number that it codes a proof. So there is a statement that says, through its code, that it is not reached.

Sixth, if arithmetic is free of clash, that statement is true and not reached, and no listed collection of true axioms reaches every truth of arithmetic. Nor can arithmetic reach the statement that it is free of clash.

Seventh, no formula of arithmetic is true of exactly the codes of the true statements: arithmetic’s truth cannot be said inside it.

What follows

With those, the techniques work by hand. 38l asks what changes when the calculus may say “every set of numbers”, and finds that the axioms then pin down the numbers and the line, at the cost of any recipe for checking that reaches every truth. 38m codes formulas and pairs as numbers by hand, and reads them back. 38n does proofs by hand in the shape of a tree, and finds that every proof can be straightened so that nothing is built only to be taken apart. The call back to rung 29 finds a game that always ends, in arithmetic’s own words, which arithmetic cannot prove ends. The annexes ask why two electrons cannot share a place, whether a machine can check a proof, and what happens when a machine writes one.

What you can do because you know it

You can write a statement exactly, check a proof as a machine would, and say why what is proved is true and what is true everywhere is proved. You can build worlds of axioms that are not the intended one, and say what a countable line or a number past every numeral means. You can code statements as numbers, and follow the argument that a true statement of arithmetic is unreached, that arithmetic cannot certify itself, and that its truth cannot be defined inside.

And you can say exactly what those results do not show: not that some truths are unknowable, but that no single listed collection of axioms reaches them all.

What it costs

Nothing new is laid down. The rung spends rung 0’s moves and rung 7’s tables, rung 4’s numbers and primes, rung 6’s choice to grow a collection to a full one and to build a bigger world, and rung 35’s lists. Four things are held in trust: that arithmetic can say of a number that it codes a proof, which rung 40 makes good; two properties of the provable mark that 38j uses; on 38n, that cutting every detour from a tree stops; and, on the call back, the order of the labels and a result of 1982. At the end of the rung you will know what it means to say arithmetic describes its own proofs, why it is true, and what you can do because you know it.
