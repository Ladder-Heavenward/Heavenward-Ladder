∞68 · research front

Can Every True Statement Be Proved Without Telling Why?

Can every true statement be proved without telling why? Step 41l proved that a map has a three-colouring while showing nothing of the colouring, and by the reduction of 41k it did the same for every statement that a short certificate settles. It held one thing in trust: a locked box that cannot be changed once sent and cannot be read until opened. This page builds the box on a clock, runs the proof with it, and then asks what it costs. A conversation of hundreds of rounds convinces one checker once. In 1986 the coin of the checker was replaced by a fingerprint, which turns the conversation into a written proof anyone can check later; and in 2025 a protocol of a kind used in practice was shown to accept a false statement once a real fingerprint stood in for the coin. Since 2013 a second change has come: proofs shorter than the computation they speak for, a few points on a curve that vouch for a billion steps, checked in a fixed number of steps however long the computation was. The trick at their heart is a box for a whole polynomial, opened at one point by dividing, as 44c divided. The boxes of the shortest kind need a secret number made once and destroyed, and in 2023 over a hundred and forty thousand people took part in making one, because one honest person among them is enough. Whether such proofs can be built on beliefs that a quantum computer would not break, and still be short, is open.

A reduction that carries certificates forward carries zero knowledge. The box gᵐhʳ on a clock with h’s log unknown hides m perfectly and binds it unless the log is found. With a fingerprint in place of the coins, a cheat who can try T times wins with chance at most T ε, so ε must be about 2⁻¹²⁸: 398 rounds on a map of 5 links. A polynomial f is opened at z by y and w = (f − y)/(x − z), checked at a random point: a false y passes with chance at most d/s. A secret made as a product of shares is unknown if one share is.

inf68.1Reductions Carry the Secret

41l4’s through-colouring technique sent every statement of NP through 41k4’s map. Here the difference: what a reduction must do so that the secret survives the trip. Being a reduction is not enough; the prover must also be able to turn its own certificate into one for the new question, fast.

Certificate-carry technique: if f turns each x into f(x) fast, with x in A exactly when f(x) is in B, and a fast g turns every certificate for x into one for f(x), then a proof for B that shows nothing gives a proof for A that shows nothing.

\text{both sides compute } f(x);\ \text{the prover computes } g(\text{its certificate}) @@ fast, so the honest prover can play
x \notin A \Rightarrow f(x) \notin B \Rightarrow \text{caught at B’s rate} @@ the reduction, backwards
\text{the record is a record of B’s proof on } f(x) @@ which the checker could make alone (41l3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a proof for A that shows nothing}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the checker adds only what it can compute itself
  
3 + 2n + 6m = 21 \text{ dots},\ \ 3 + 3n + 12m = 36 \text{ links} @@ 41k4’s map
x = y = 1,\ z = 1 \Rightarrow \text{a colouring} @@ 41k4, forwards: the certificate carried
36 \times 14 = 504 \text{ rounds} @@ 41l2: fooled with chance at most e⁻¹⁴
\htmlClass{new}{e^{-14} \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-7}} \quad \blacksquare @@ for a statement of two clauses

Every reduction of 41k carries certificates, since 41k4 built the colouring from the row. That is the 1986 result, which 41l4 used: every statement of NP has a proof that shows nothing. Its weight is in the 504: a statement of two clauses needs five hundred rounds of boxes.

inf68.2Boxes From a Clock

41l2 held its locked box in trust until rung 43. 43·comp3 built the fingerprint gˣ¹hˣ², and its collision-gives-log technique said two inputs with one fingerprint give the clock log of h. Read that fingerprint as a box: to lock a colour m, pick r at random and send gᵐhʳ; to open it, send m and r. Binding is the collision-gives-log technique. Here the other half.

Box-hides technique: if g has exactly q powers, q prime, and h is one of them other than 1, then for each m, as r runs over the q exponents, gᵐhʳ takes each of the q powers once; so a box with r random is equally likely to be any power, whatever m is.

h = g^x,\ x \ne 0 \text{ on the clock of } q @@ h is a power of g, and not 1
g^m h^r = g^{m + xr} @@ powers add
r \mapsto m + xr \text{ hits every exponent once} @@ x can be undone on the prime clock of q (43b)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every box equally likely, for every } m} \quad \blacksquare @@ the box shows nothing, even to unlimited time
  
2^1, \ldots, 2^{11} = 2, 4, 8, 16, 9, 18, 13, 3, 6, 12, 1 @@ h = 13 = 2⁷; the prover is not told the 7
\text{box}(1, 4) = 2 \cdot 13^4 \equiv 2 \cdot 18 = 36 \equiv 13 @@ 13² = 169 ≡ 8, 8² = 64 ≡ 18
\text{box}(2, 7) = 4 \cdot 13^7 \equiv 13 @@ the same box, opened as another colour
\htmlClass{new}{x \equiv (1 - 2)(7 - 4)^{-1} \equiv -4 \equiv 7 \pmod{11}} \quad \blacksquare @@ two openings give the log: 3 · 4 = 12 ≡ 1

So the box is perfectly hiding and binding only by belief: a prover who could find the clock log of 13 could open a box as any colour. That is the right way round for the proof. The checker’s side, that nothing is learned, holds against any checker forever; the prover’s side, that a cheat is caught, rests on the clock log being slow while the proof runs.

“Nothing learned” is a picture of ∞13.3’s: the box from the secret colouring to what the checker sees equals a box that ignores the secret, so the picture falls into two pieces. The reductions of inf68.1 carry that picture from one question to another as maps in a row. The plan is the reduction; the secret, its arrangement, is exactly what the boxes let go.

inf68.3A Fingerprint for the Coins

∞34.5 replaced the checker’s coin by a fingerprint H of the message and the commitment, and turned a round into a signature. Do the same to the colouring proof: the prover locks the boxes of all the rounds at once, computes H of all of them, reads the links to open from its digits, and writes down the openings. Anyone can check the written proof later. Here the difference: the cheat now plays alone, and can try again.

Many-tries technique: if a cheat passes one run of the rounds with chance at most ε, and each try at a fresh set of boxes gives a fresh fingerprint that acts as fresh coins, then T tries pass with chance at most Tε.

\text{try } i \text{ passes with chance at most } \varepsilon @@ the fingerprint acts as coins (held in trust, as in ∞34.5)
P(\text{some try passes}) \le \textstyle\sum_{i \le T} P(\text{try } i \text{ passes}) @@ the chance of a union is at most the sum (30f)
\le T\varepsilon @@ T terms
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a cheat with } T \text{ tries wins with chance at most } T\varepsilon} \quad \blacksquare @@ so ε must beat every T a cheat can afford
  
20 \text{ rounds}:\ \ \varepsilon = 0.8^{20} \approx 0.0115 @@ caught at 1/5 a round
1/0.0115 \approx 87 \text{ tries} @@ the cheat writes a passing proof in about 87 tries
\varepsilon \le 2^{-128}:\ \ 0.8^R \le 2^{-128} \iff R \ge 128\ln 2/\ln(1.25) \approx 397.6 @@ taking logs (17)
\htmlClass{new}{398 \text{ rounds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ where 20 were enough with a live checker for a one in a hundred risk

The technique holds only while the fingerprint acts as fresh coins, and no real fingerprint is proved to. That was ∞34.oq.2. In January 2025 it was answered for one protocol of a kind used in practice: a protocol sound with a live checker, given a real fingerprint, was made to accept a false statement, by building the statement around the fingerprint itself. The attack does not touch the colouring proof of this page; it showed that “acts as coins” must be checked protocol by protocol.

inf68.4Opening a Polynomial at One Point

Short proofs need a box for a whole polynomial f of degree d, small whatever d is, that can be opened at one point z to show y = f(z) without showing f. The commitment itself, a single point of a curve standing for f at a secret place τ, is held in trust; its arithmetic is 44c3’s, done once.

Quotient-check technique: y = f(z) exactly when x − z divides f(x) − y; so a prover shows y = f(z) by sending w = (f(x) − y)/(x − z), and the checker tests f(τ) − y = w(τ)(τ − z) at one random τ from a set of s values; a false y passes with chance at most d/s.

f(x) - y \text{ has remainder } f(z) - y \text{ on division by } x - z @@ 44c3: the remainder is the value
y = f(z) \iff x - z \mid f(x) - y @@ so the honest w exists exactly when y is true
y \ne f(z):\ \ f(x) - y - w(x)(x - z) \ne 0 \text{ for every } w,\ \text{degree} \le d @@ else x − z would divide f(x) − y
\htmlClass{new}{\text{passes at a random } \tau \text{ with chance} \le d/s} \quad \blacksquare @@ 41i2’s random-point technique
  
f(5) = 3 + 10 + 25 = 38 \equiv 4 @@ y = 4
f(x) - 4 = x^2 + 2x - 1 \equiv (x + 7)(x - 5) @@ (x + 7)(x − 5) = x² + 2x − 35, and −35 ≡ −1
\tau = 9:\ \ f(9) - 4 = 102 - 4 \equiv 13,\ \ w(9)(9 - 5) = 16 \cdot 4 = 64 \equiv 13 @@ the check passes
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a false } y = 5 \text{ passes for at most } 2 \text{ of the } 17 \text{ values of } \tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ d/s = 2/17; a search of every w agrees

On a clock of about 2²⁵⁵ hours and polynomials of degree a million, d/s is about 1.7 × 10⁻⁷¹. One opening is one curve point for w, whatever the degree. That is the seed of a proof shorter than the thing it proves.

inf68.5Proofs Shorter Than the Computation

A computation of N gates leaves a table: the value on every wire. Write each column of the table as a polynomial through its values. Every rule of every gate becomes one polynomial identity, which 41i2 checks at a random point, and the quotient-check technique opens each polynomial at that point with one curve point. Since 2013 this has been made to work, with proofs of a few curve points whatever N is. A scheme of 2016 needs three. Told, not proved: the exact identities, and the beliefs about curves that make a single curve point a binding box.

The box of inf68.4 needs the curve points for 1, τ, τ², …, τᵈ, with τ itself destroyed: anyone who knew τ could open a box as anything. So τ is made by many people together.

One-honest technique: if τ = τ₁τ₂ ⋯ τₖ on a prime clock, each τᵢ chosen by one person and not 0, and one person’s τⱼ is uniform and destroyed, then τ is uniform to everyone else, whatever the others chose.

\tau = \tau_j \cdot c,\ \ c = \textstyle\prod_{i \ne j} \tau_i \ne 0 @@ the others’ part, known to them
t \mapsto t\,c \text{ hits every nonzero hour once} @@ c can be undone on a prime clock (43b)
\tau_j \text{ uniform} \Rightarrow \tau \text{ uniform} @@ each τ comes from exactly one τⱼ
\htmlClass{new}{\text{one honest share hides } \tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ whatever the others did
  
\tau_1 = 5,\ \tau_3 = 3 \text{ known to a cheat};\ \ c = 15 @@ the cheat’s part
\tau_2 = 1, 2, \ldots, 16 \Rightarrow \tau = 15, 13, 11, 9, 7, 5, 3, 1, 16, \ldots, 2 @@ times 15, on the clock of 17
\text{every nonzero hour once} @@ the list is a shuffle
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the cheat learns nothing of } \tau} \quad \blacksquare @@ if τ₂ was honest

In 2023 a ceremony of this kind for a large digital currency’s network ran for 208 days and gathered 141 416 contributions. Its makers argued that one honest one among them is near certain. Doubters asked how anyone can know a contribution was destroyed. Proofs built only on fingerprints, from 2018, need no ceremony at all, and are much longer.

inf68.6What a Short Proof Costs

39k’s counts, on both sides. The checker of a short proof does a fixed number of curve steps, the same for a computation of a thousand gates or of a billion. The prover does far more than the computation itself: every wire becomes part of a polynomial, and every polynomial is boxed.

\text{plain checking of } N \text{ gates: about } N \text{ steps} @@ 39k: a line in N
\text{short proof: a fixed count of curve steps} @@ the same for every N
N = 10^9:\ \ \text{one proof of three curve points stands for } 10^9 \text{ gates} @@ the 2016 scheme
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the checker’s count does not grow with } N} \quad \blacksquare @@ the prover’s does, by much more than N

That is why short proofs are used where one prover serves many checkers, as a digital currency has since 2016 to hide who paid whom (∞34’s history). The arguments are about trust, not arithmetic: the ceremony of inf68.5, the fingerprints of inf68.3, and bugs in the programs that write the identities, which a short proof cannot see.

Open questions · nobody has the answer yet

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞68.oq.1Can short written proofs rest on standard beliefs? Every scheme with proofs a few points long rests on a belief of an unusual kind, that whoever writes a passing proof must “know” a certificate, which cannot be checked by any test. A result of 2011 showed that no proof of the usual kind, from any belief that can be tested, establishes such schemes. Whether some other road gives short written proofs from beliefs like learning with errors is open.
∞68.oq.2Can short proofs survive quantum computers and stay short? The curve schemes would fall to ∞27. Schemes built only on fingerprints, from 2018, are believed to survive, but their proofs are far longer. Short proofs from ∞66’s lattices are being built; how short they can be made, against the belief they rest on, is open.
∞68.oq.3Which uses of the fingerprint are safe? After January 2025 (inf68.3) the question of ∞34.oq.2 is sharper: a class of protocols used in practice is not safe with a real fingerprint in general. Is there a test, from the shape of a protocol alone, that says when a fingerprint may stand in for the coins?
∞68.oq.4Can a written proof show nothing without any shared setup? With a shared random string, written proofs that show nothing exist for every statement of NP, since 2019 even from learning with errors. With no shared string and no fingerprint at all, they are known to be impossible except for easy statements. How little setup is enough, and who should be trusted to make it, is argued.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

The 1985 definitions (∞34’s history) left a question: which statements have proofs that show nothing? In 1986 a group in Israel and the United States answered: every statement of NP, through three-colouring, if locked boxes exist. Some readers found the result almost too strong, since it needs nothing about the statement but a certificate. Others noted its weight: hundreds of rounds for a small statement, and a box for every dot.

The fingerprint of 1986 made proofs that can be written down, and for twenty years the field argued over it. A paper of 1998 built protocols that are safe with coins and unsafe with every real fingerprint. One side called these artificial, built only to fail. The other said a proof of safety that is false in general is not a proof. The January 2025 attack (inf68.3) moved the argument: the protocol it broke was not built to fail.

The practical turn for short proofs came with polynomial boxes in 2010 and a working system in 2013, from a software company’s research arm. A 2016 scheme brought proofs to three curve points, and the same year a digital currency used short proofs to hide payments, after a ceremony whose destruction of its shares nobody could check (∞34’s history). In 2019 its makers disclosed a flaw, found in 2018 and quietly fixed, that would have let anyone make coins unseen: the mathematics held, and the program did not.

Since then the ceremonies have grown. A large digital currency’s 2023 ceremony gathered over a hundred and forty thousand contributions so that its secret would need only one honest person. Critics of short proofs argued that a proof is only as good as the program that writes its identities; defenders answered that this is true of every program, and that the mathematics is not where the flaws have been.

What is argued now: whether the beliefs behind the shortest proofs, which cannot be tested, are acceptable foundations; whether the fingerprint can be trusted after 2025; how much to pay in length for proofs that survive quantum computers; and whether proofs that show nothing, used to prove an age or a payment, protect privacy or make checking easier to demand (∞34.oq.4).

The accepted names

The certificate-carry technique is the closure of zero knowledge under witness-preserving (Levin) reductions; the 1986 result is that every language in NP has a computational zero-knowledge proof, assuming one-way functions. The box gᵐhʳ is the Pedersen commitment, perfectly hiding and computationally binding. Replacing the coins by a fingerprint is the Fiat–Shamir transform, and the many-tries technique the union bound behind grinding attacks; the 1998 protocols are those of Canetti, Goldreich and Halevi. The quotient-check technique is the opening of a KZG polynomial commitment, the curve points being a structured reference string checked with pairings. Short written proofs are SNARKs (succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge), and with zero knowledge zk-SNARKs; the 2016 scheme is Groth16, the 2013 system Pinocchio, the fingerprint-only proofs of 2018 STARKs. The ceremony is a powers-of-tau multi-party computation, here Ethereum’s KZG ceremony; the currency of 2016 is Zcash. The “know a certificate” belief is a knowledge assumption, which is not falsifiable; the 2011 result is the Gentry–Wichs separation. Written proofs with a shared random string are NIZK proofs in the common reference string model, from LWE by Peikert and Shiehian (2019); without one they exist only for BPP (Goldreich–Oren).

Who said it first

Oded Goldreich, Silvio Micali and Avi Wigderson proved that all of NP has zero-knowledge proofs (FOCS 1986). Amos Fiat and Adi Shamir gave the fingerprint (1986). Torben Pedersen gave the commitment (1991). Ran Canetti, Oded Goldreich and Shai Halevi built the 1998 counterexamples. Aniket Kate, Gregory Zaverucha and Ian Goldberg gave constant-size polynomial commitments (Asiacrypt 2010). Bryan Parno, Jon Howell, Craig Gentry and Mariana Raykova built Pinocchio (2013), and Jens Groth the three-element proofs (Eurocrypt 2016). Eli Ben-Sasson and coworkers gave STARKs (2018). Craig Gentry and Daniel Wichs proved the 2011 separation; Chris Peikert and Sina Shiehian gave NIZK from LWE (CRYPTO 2019). Dmitry Khovratovich, Ron Rothblum and Lev Soukhanov gave the January 2025 attack, on a GKR-based protocol.

A proof that is not allowed

“The written proof passed the check, and a cheat passes a run of rounds with chance 0.8²⁰, so the statement is true but for a one in eighty-seven chance.” Not allowed: with a fingerprint the cheat chose which run to show, after trying about eighty-seven. A chance per run says nothing about a proof picked from many runs; only a chance small enough to beat every count of tries does.
