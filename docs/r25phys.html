How Long Can a Star Shine?

How long can a star shine? The sun has poured out the same light for billions of years, and some stars burn out in a few million. No one has been inside one, yet a handful of measured numbers and a few lines of algebra say how big a star is, how hot its middle must be, and how long its fuel will last. This page finds the size of a star from its brightness and colour, the heat at its centre from the pull of its own weight, and its life from the fuel it carries, and then finds why heavy stars die young.

A hot surface gives off σT⁴ per square metre, so a star’s light is L = 4πR²σT⁴, and its size follows from L and T. For a particle to stay up against the star’s pull, its heat must match the pull’s energy: kT ≈ GMm/R. Fuel over light gives the life: about 0.7% of the mass in the core turned to energy by E = mc², divided by L. If L grows as M^3.5, the life falls as M^(−2.5).

25phys1Brightness, Heat and Size

A hot body glows, and the hotter it is the more it gives off: each square metre of a surface at temperature T, in kelvins, gives off σT⁴ watts, with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸. A star is a ball of radius R, so its whole light, its luminosity L, is its area times that.

L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4 @@ area times the glow per square metre
R = 6.96 \times 10^8 \text{ m},\ T = 5772 \text{ K} @@ measured
\htmlClass{new}{L \approx 3.8 \times 10^{26} \text{ W}} @@ the sun’s luminosity, written L⊙

From here on, on this page, ⊙ marks the sun’s value: M⊙, R⊙, L⊙, T⊙. A star’s colour gives its T: red stars are cool, blue ones hot.

25phys2A Star’s Size From Its Light and Colour

Distance and brightness give a star’s L; its colour gives T. Divide by the sun’s, and σ and 4π drop out.

\dfrac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\dfrac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2 \left(\dfrac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4 @@ the sun’s line divided out
\dfrac{R}{R_\odot} = \sqrt{\dfrac{L}{L_\odot}} \left(\dfrac{T_\odot}{T}\right)^2 @@ solve for R
L = 10\,000\,L_\odot,\ T = \tfrac12 T_\odot:\ \sqrt{10\,000} \cdot 2^2 @@ a star cool and very bright
\htmlClass{new}{R = 400\,R_\odot} @@ it would swallow the earth’s orbit

Size technique: a star’s radius is the square root of its luminosity over the sun’s, times the square of the sun’s temperature over its own, in sun radii. A cool star that is still bright must be huge; a hot star that is faint must be tiny.

25phys3How Hot the Middle Must Be

A star does not fall in on itself because the hot gas inside pushes out. A particle of mass m deep in the star is held by a pull whose energy is about GMm/R. For the gas to hold up, the particles’ heat energy, about kT with k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³, must be of the same size.

kT \approx \dfrac{GMm}{R} @@ heat holds up weight
T \approx \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \cdot 2.0 \times 10^{30} \cdot 1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{1.38 \times 10^{-23} \cdot 6.96 \times 10^8} @@ m the mass of a hydrogen nucleus
\htmlClass{new}{T \approx 2 \times 10^7 \text{ K}} @@ the best models say 1.6 × 10⁷

Weight technique: a star’s middle is as hot as it must be to hold up its own weight, about GMm/(kR). That is hot enough for hydrogen nuclei to collide hard enough to fuse into helium, which is where the light comes from.

25phys4How Long the Fuel Lasts

Fusing hydrogen into helium turns about 0.7% of the mass into energy, by E = mc². Only the core, about a tenth of the star’s mass, gets hot enough to burn.

E \approx 0.007 \cdot 0.1 \cdot M_\odot c^2 @@ the fuel’s energy
= 0.0007 \cdot 2.0 \times 10^{30} \cdot (3 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{44} \text{ J} @@ multiply out
E / L_\odot \approx 3.3 \times 10^{17} \text{ s} @@ fuel over light
\htmlClass{new}{\approx 10^{10} \text{ years}} @@ the sun is about halfway through

Fuel technique: a star’s life is its fuel’s energy divided by its luminosity. Chemical burning, at about 30 million joules a kilogram, would have run the sun out in a few thousand years: only nuclear fuel lasts billions.

25phys5Heavy Stars Die Young

Stars that burn hydrogen in their cores follow a rule read off measured pairs: luminosity grows about as the mass to the power 3.5. Fuel grows only as the mass.

L \propto M^{3.5},\ \ E \propto M @@ measured, and the fuel
t \propto M / M^{3.5} = M^{-2.5} @@ the fuel technique
M = 10\,M_\odot:\ t \approx 10^{10} \cdot 10^{-2.5} \approx 3 \times 10^7 \text{ years} @@ thirty million years
\htmlClass{new}{M = \tfrac12 M_\odot:\ t \approx 10^{10} \cdot 2^{2.5} \approx 6 \times 10^{10} \text{ years}} @@ longer than the universe has existed

Ten times the mass burns about three thousand times as bright and lives about three hundred times shorter. So every small red star ever born is still shining, and every heavy blue star we see is young. In a cluster of stars born together, the heaviest ones still burning tell the cluster’s age.

Accepted names

σT⁴ is the Stefan-Boltzmann law, and σ the Stefan-Boltzmann constant. Heat holding up weight is hydrostatic equilibrium, and kT ≈ GMm/R comes from the virial theorem. Stars burning hydrogen in their cores lie on the main sequence, and L ∝ M^3.5 is the mass-luminosity relation. A chart of luminosity against temperature is the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, and the heaviest stars left on it in a cluster mark its turnoff point.

Who said it first

Josef Stefan found the T⁴ law from measurements in 1879, and Ludwig Boltzmann derived it in 1884. Arthur Eddington worked out how a star holds up and found the mass-luminosity relation in 1924; Hans Bethe showed in 1939 how hydrogen fuses in stars.
