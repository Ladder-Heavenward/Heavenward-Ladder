24b · proof

Building the solution by repeated integration

A rule on rates says what the slope is. The sixteenth rung says that a function is its starting value plus the area under its slope. Put those together and the rule becomes a statement about areas: the solution equals its start plus the area under the rule applied to itself. That sounds circular, but it suggests a way to build an answer. Start with a guess, the flat line at the starting value. Put the guess into the rule, take the area, and get a better guess. Do it again. For the rule of the growth function, the guesses are exactly the matching polynomials of the last rung, closing in on the answer.

A rule with a start is the same as a statement about areas: the solution is its start plus the area under the rule applied to itself. Feed a guess in, take the area, and repeat. The guesses close in on the solution.

24b1The rule as an area

Area-form technique: let F be continuous on a rectangle and y continuous on an interval I holding x₀, with its points in the rectangle. Then y solves y′ = F(x, y) with y(x₀) = y₀ exactly when y(x) = y₀ + ∫ₓ₀ˣ F(t, y(t)) dt for every x in I.

t \mapsto F(t, y(t)) \text{ is continuous on } I @@ continuous after continuous (11c)
y' = F(x, y),\ y(x_0) = y_0 \Rightarrow y(x) - y_0 = \int_{x_0}^{x} y'(t)\,dt = \int_{x_0}^{x} F(t, y(t))\,dt @@ evaluating by antiderivatives (16c)
y(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x} F(t, y(t))\,dt \Rightarrow y'(x) = F(x, y(x)) @@ the slope of an area function is the height (16b)
\htmlClass{new}{y(x_0) = y_0 + 0 = y_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ an area from x₀ to x₀ is 0

The area form has two gifts. It holds the start inside it, so the rule and its start are one equation. And it asks only that y be continuous: a slope is no longer assumed, it is produced.

24b2Feed the guess back in

From here on, the repeated integration for y′ = F(x, y), y(x₀) = y₀ is the list of functions with y₀(x) = y₀ and yₙ₊₁(x) = y₀ + ∫ₓ₀ˣ F(t, yₙ(t)) dt.

y_0(x) = 1 @@ the flat line at the start
y_1(x) = 1 + \int_0^x 1\,dt = 1 + x @@ feed y₀ in
y_2(x) = 1 + \int_0^x (1 + t)\,dt = 1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}{2} @@ feed y₁ in
y_n(x) = 1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots + \tfrac{x^n}{n!} \Rightarrow y_{n+1}(x) = 1 + \int_0^x y_n = 1 + x + \cdots + \tfrac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} @@ integrate term by term: ∫₀ˣ tᵏ/k! dt = xᵏ⁺¹/(k + 1)!
\htmlClass{new}{y_n = T_n,\ \text{the matching polynomial of } e^x \text{ at } 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ induction (4h)
  
y_0 = 0,\ \ y_1 = \int_0^x t\,dt = \tfrac{x^2}{2} @@ F(t, 0) = t
y_2 = \int_0^x \big(t + \tfrac{t^2}{2}\big)dt = \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \tfrac{x^3}{6} @@ feed y₁ in
\htmlClass{new}{y_n = \tfrac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots + \tfrac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \to e^x - x - 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same induction; the sum for eˣ less its first two pieces (23b2)

Check the last one in the rule: (eˣ − x − 1)′ = eˣ − 1, and x + (eˣ − x − 1) = eˣ − 1. It starts at 0. So the guesses settled on a solution. By 24a3's kind of argument (look at the gap between two solutions) it is the only one.

24b3Why the guesses settle, for y′ = y

|y_{n+1}(x) - y_n(x)| = \tfrac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \le \tfrac{r^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \text{ on } [-r, r] @@ 24b2
\textstyle\sum \tfrac{r^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} \text{ settles} @@ the ratio test (22e2)
y_n \to y \text{ everywhere at once on } [-r, r] @@ y_n = y₀ + Σ (y_{k+1} − y_k); the at-once comparison technique (23d4)
\int_0^x y_n \to \int_0^x y @@ the area-at-once technique (23e1)
\htmlClass{new}{y(x) = 1 + \int_0^x y(t)\,dt} \quad \blacksquare @@ let n grow on both sides of yₙ₊₁ = 1 + ∫₀ˣ yₙ

That is the whole plan of the next two steps, seen once on an easy rule. The gaps between guesses shrink like a factorial, so the guesses crowd and settle all at once, and the area form passes to the limit. 24c finds the general reason the gaps shrink. 24d runs the plan for every tame rule.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

The area form is the integral equation of the initial value problem; repeated integration is Picard iteration, or the method of successive approximations.

Who said it first

Joseph Liouville used successive approximations in 1838 for a special case, and Émile Picard made them a general method in 1890. Ernst Lindelöf sharpened the conditions in 1894.
