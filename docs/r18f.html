18f · technique

Triangles and parallels by hand

18a to 18e built the plane from pairs of numbers. This step works the old geometry's own techniques about triangles, parallels and similar figures, the way they are worked on paper and on tests, and checks each one in coordinates. It says what an angle is and when two angles are equal, then measures how big one is by halving the quarter turn again and again. Degrees are counted quarter turns, so nothing new is laid down. Along the way it earns three things held in trust since earlier rungs: the drawing of 8i's old algebra book, the seesaw's trade of force for distance, and an animal's skin against its bulk.

In any triangle, the square of a side is the sum of the squares of the other two, less twice their product times how far the angle between them opens. So two sides and the angle between them fix the third side (two-sides-and-the-angle), and three sides fix every angle (three-sides). Parallels make equal alternate angles, so a triangle's angles add to 180°. Two angles equal makes two triangles similar: every side in one ratio.

18f1Angles, and when two are equal

From here on, for points A ≠ B, the ray from A through B is {A + t(B − A) : t ≥ 0}. For points B and C, each different from A, the angle ∠BAC is the pair of rays from A through B and through C, with A its vertex. ∠BAC is a name, for that pair of rays. With e₁ = (B − A)/|B − A| and e₂ = (C − A)/|C − A|, call the number e₁ · e₂ the opening of the angle. By dot-bound it lies between −1 and 1. It is 1 when the rays are one ray, and −1 when they point opposite ways along one line. Two angles are equal when their openings are equal; the accepted word for equal angles, and for figures whose sides and angles all match, is congruent.

This is the old geometry's own test, done with numbers. Mark the point at distance 1 along each ray. The two marks are e₁ and e₂ after moving A to (0, 0), and by right-triangle their distance is √(2 − 2 e₁ · e₂). So two angles are equal exactly when a compass opened to 1 at each vertex cuts off equal chords.

Saying when two angles are equal is not yet saying how big one is. The next three footings measure that, with nothing laid down: a size, from the turns of the plane. For a line Ax + By = C, its two sides are the points with Ax + By > C and those with Ax + By < C.

18f2Turns, and the quarter turn

From here on, a turn is a pair (a, b) of reals with a² + b² = 1. Turning the point (x, y) by (a, b) sends it to (ax − by, bx + ay). The product of two turns is (a, b)(c, d) = (ac − bd, ad + bc). These are the pairs of 9·cb8 with absolute value 1, and this is 9·cb8’s product. Turning by (c, d) and then by (a, b) is turning once by their product.

Turn-keeps technique: a turn keeps every distance and every opening, and the product of two turns is a turn.

(ax - by)^2 + (bx + ay)^2 = (a^2 + b^2)(x^2 + y^2) = x^2 + y^2 @@ multiply out; the terms −2abxy and +2abxy cancel
(ax - by)(ax' - by') + (bx + ay)(bx' + ay') = xx' + yy' @@ the same, with two points; a² + b² = 1
|(a, b)(c, d)|^2 = (a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = 1 @@ the first line, with (x, y) = (c, d)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{distances, openings kept; products are turns}} \quad \blacksquare @@ a distance is the root of a dot (18a); an opening is a dot of directions (18f1)

Turn-between technique: for two directions e₁ = (u₁, u₂) and e₂ = (v₁, v₂) of length 1, exactly one turn sends e₁ to e₂. It is (e₁ · e₂, u₁v₂ − u₂v₁).

a = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2,\ \ b = u_1v_2 - u_2v_1 @@ the pair to try
au_1 - bu_2 = v_1(u_1^2 + u_2^2) = v_1 @@ multiply out; the u₁u₂v₂ terms cancel
bu_1 + au_2 = v_2(u_1^2 + u_2^2) = v_2 @@ likewise; so (a, b) sends e₁ to e₂
a^2 + b^2 = (u_1^2 + u_2^2)(v_1^2 + v_2^2) = 1 @@ multiply out; the 2u₁u₂v₁v₂ terms cancel; so (a, b) is a turn
\htmlClass{new}{(a', b') \text{ another} \Rightarrow a' = a,\ b' = b} \quad \blacksquare @@ subtract the two and multiply by u₁ and by u₂: (a − a′)(u₁² + u₂²) = 0, and the same for b

So the opening of an angle is the first number of the turn from one of its rays to the other. From here on, that turn is the turn between the rays.

From here on, 1 is the turn (1, 0), which leaves every point where it is, and −1 is the turn (−1, 0), the half turn, which sends every direction to its opposite. i is the turn (0, 1), the quarter turn: it sends (1, 0) to (0, 1). Then i · i = (0 − 1, 0 + 0) = −1. Turning a quarter turn twice is a half turn, so i is a square root of −1.

18f3Halving the quarter turn

Halving technique: a turn (a, b) with b ≥ 0 has exactly two halves, turns that give it when multiplied by themselves: h and −h, where h = (√((1 + a)/2), √((1 − a)/2)). The half is h, the one with no number below 0.

h \cdot h = \left(\tfrac{1 + a}{2} - \tfrac{1 - a}{2},\ 2\sqrt{\tfrac{1 + a}{2}}\sqrt{\tfrac{1 - a}{2}}\right) = \left(a,\ \sqrt{1 - a^2}\right) @@ the product of turns (18f2)
\sqrt{1 - a^2} = b @@ a² + b² = 1 and b ≥ 0
(p, q)^2 = (a, b) \Rightarrow p^2 - q^2 = a,\ p^2 + q^2 = 1,\ 2pq = b @@ the product of turns; (p, q) is a turn
\htmlClass{new}{p^2 = \tfrac{1 + a}{2},\ q^2 = \tfrac{1 - a}{2},\ pq \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ add and subtract; p and q share a sign, so (p, q) is h or −h

From here on, i1/2 is the half of i, i1/4 the half of i1/2, and so on: i1/2ⁿ is i halved n times. For whole k, ik/2ⁿ is i1/2ⁿ multiplied by itself k times. One fraction written two ways gives one turn, since a half multiplied by itself gives back what was halved: i2k/2ⁿ⁺¹ = ik/2ⁿ. Fractions with a power of 2 below the line have the accepted name dyadic fractions.

Halving-keeps-order technique: as s runs up through the dyadic fractions from 0 to 2, the first number of is falls from 1 to −1, and its second number never drops below 0. Write is = (C(s), S(s)). The proof halves once more at each line, so it runs by induction (4c) on n, the number of halvings.

i^0 = 1,\ \ i^1 = i,\ \ i^2 = -1 @@ no halving: first numbers 1, 0, −1
s \le 1:\ \ i^s \cdot i^s = i^{2s},\ \ S(2s) \ge 0 @@ 2s needs one halving fewer; the induction
i^s = \pm\,(\text{the half of } i^{2s}) @@ the halving technique
\text{the sign is } + \text{ from } s = 0 \text{ to } s = 1 @@ neighbours differ by the turn i^(1/2ⁿ), whose first number is above 0, and turn-keeps gives them that opening; a half and minus a half have opening at most 0
C(s) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 + C(2s)}{2}} \text{ falls as } s \text{ rises to } 1 @@ C(2s) falls, by the induction, and the root rises with what is under it
1 \le s \le 2:\ \ i^s = i \cdot i^{s - 1} = (-S(s - 1),\ C(s - 1)) @@ the product with (0, 1)
\htmlClass{new}{C \text{ falls on } [0, 2],\ S \ge 0} \quad \blacksquare @@ on [0, 1] both numbers are at least 0, so S(s − 1) = √(1 − C(s − 1)²) rises as C(s − 1) falls

The steps between neighbours shrink too. With c = C(1/2ⁿ), the next half has 1 − √((1 + c)/2) = (1 − c)/(2(1 + √((1 + c)/2))), at most half of 1 − c. So 1 − C(1/2ⁿ) ≤ 1/2ⁿ, and neighbouring powers come as close as you like.

18f4How big an angle is

From here on, the size of an angle whose opening is a is the least upper bound of the dyadic fractions s in [0, 2] with C(s) ≥ a. It is counted in quarter turns. The set has 0 in it and 2 above it, so by completeness (8c3) the size exists. An angle of size t measures 90t degrees, written 90t°: x° is the size x/90, a power of i.

1° = \text{the size } \tfrac{1}{90} = i^{1/90} @@ x° is the size x/90
\tfrac{1}{128} \lt \tfrac{1}{90} \lt \tfrac{2}{128} @@ 7 halvings: between 0.70° and 1.41°
\tfrac{11}{1024} \lt \tfrac{1}{90} \lt \tfrac{12}{1024} @@ 10 halvings: between 0.967° and 1.055°
\tfrac{91}{8192} \lt \tfrac{1}{90} \lt \tfrac{92}{8192} @@ 13 halvings: between 0.9998° and 1.0107°
\htmlClass{new}{\tfrac{1}{90} \ne \tfrac{d}{2^k} \text{ for every } d, k} \quad \blacksquare @@ 2ᵏ = 90d would need the odd 45 to divide 2ᵏ: squeezed by halvings, never landed on

Size technique: equal angles, and only they, have equal sizes. One ray alone has size 0, two opposite rays size 2, which is 180°. For a ray from A and either side of its line, each size between 0 and 2 belongs to exactly one angle made with a ray from A on that side. And if D = A + s(B − A) + t(C − A) with s, t > 0, the sizes of ∠BAD and ∠DAC add to the size of ∠BAC. The last clause has the accepted name angle addition. When the rays through B and C are opposite, the angles BAD and DAC are a linear pair, and their sizes add to 2, which is 180°.

a < a' \Rightarrow C(s) \text{ strictly between them for some } s @@ neighbouring powers come as close as you like (18f3), and C falls
\text{different openings, different sizes; equal openings, equal sizes} @@ the size depends only on the opening
C(0) = 1,\ C(2) = -1 @@ one ray has opening 1, opposite rays −1 (18f1)
\text{size } t:\ (a, \pm\sqrt{1 - a^2}),\ a = \text{the greatest lower bound of } C(s),\ s < t @@ two turns with that opening, one to each side (turn-between); the − one turns back
i^{s} \cdot i^{s'} = i^{s + s'} @@ for dyadic s and s′, k halves times m halves is k + m halves
\text{turn }(B \to C) = \text{turn }(D \to C) \cdot \text{turn }(B \to D) @@ turn-between: both send the direction of B to that of C; D inside the angle keeps the sizes adding to at most 2
\htmlClass{new}{\text{size}(BAD) + \text{size}(DAC) = \text{size}(BAC)} \quad \blacksquare @@ squeeze each size between neighbouring powers; their products are powers, and the gaps shrink

Right-angle technique: if (B − A) · (C − A) = 0, then ∠BAC measures 90°. Its opening is 0, which is C(1), the first number of i. So its size is one quarter turn. Its accepted name is a right angle. An angle under 90° is acute, one over 90° is obtuse.

Nothing here was laid down. Degrees are counted quarter turns, and the rules a measure of angles needs are proved from turning. Rung 21 finds the same size a second way, by walking round a circle, and shows the two agree.

18f5Two sides and the angle between

Write d = |BC|, b = |CA|, c = |AB| for the sides of a triangle ABC: b and c are named by the small letter of the corner each faces, and d is the side facing A. Two-sides-and-the-angle technique: in every triangle, d² = b² + c² − 2bc · (the opening at A). So if two triangles have AB = A′B′, AC = A′C′ and ∠BAC = ∠B′A′C′, then BC = B′C′, and the angles at B and B′, and at C and C′, are equal.

|C - B|^2 = |(C - A) - (B - A)|^2 @@ adding-keeps-answers
= |C - A|^2 + |B - A|^2 - 2(B - A) \cdot (C - A) @@ right-triangle and the dot technique, with u = C − A and v = −(B − A)
(B - A) \cdot (C - A) = cb\,(e_1 \cdot e_2) @@ ﹀ “opening”
d^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\,(e_1 \cdot e_2) @@ so equal b, c and opening give equal d
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the opening at } B = \tfrac{d^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2dc}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same line, said at B; three equal sides, three equal openings

So three sides fix the angles too. Three-sides technique: if two triangles have their three sides equal in pairs, they have their three angles equal in pairs, and are congruent. Each opening is the last line of two-sides-and-the-angle, a fraction of the sides alone.

A Greek geometer, about 300 BCE, proved his fourth proposition, two sides and the angle, by picking up one triangle and laying it on the other: put A on A′ and AB along A′B′, and B falls on B′, because the lengths are equal. Nothing in his postulates says a figure can be carried about without changing. That move has the accepted name superposition, and it is the old geometry's, not this ladder's. A German mathematician, in 1899, made two-sides-and-the-angle an axiom rather than prove it that way. Here it was a calculation.

Equal-sides-equal-angles technique: in a triangle, b = c exactly when the angles at B and C are equal. One way is two-sides-and-the-angle's last line at B and at C, with b = c. For the other, set the two openings equal and clear the fractions: (b − c)(d² − (b + c)²) = 0, and d < b + c by triangle in the plane, so b = c. A triangle with two equal sides has the accepted name isosceles; with three, equilateral. Medieval students called the old geometry's fifth proposition, this one, the bridge of asses, the pons asinorum, the first proof that the slow could not cross.

18f6Crossing lines and parallels

Opposite-angles technique: when two lines cross at O, the angles across from each other are equal. With directions ±u and ±v from O, the angles across are between u and v and between −u and −v, and (−u) · (−v) = u · v. They have the accepted name vertical angles.

Alternate-angles technique: if a line crosses two parallel lines at P and Q, the angles it makes on opposite sides, one at P and one at Q, are equal. The parallels have one direction v by 18c, and the crossing line has a direction w from P towards Q. The angle at P between w and v and the angle at Q between −w and −v have the same opening. They have the accepted name alternate angles. The old geometry needed its fifth postulate for its twenty-ninth proposition; here parallel-through-a-point already did that work.

Angle-sum technique: the angles of a triangle add to 180°.

D = C - (B - A),\ \ E = C + (B - A) @@ the parallel to AB through C (parallel-through-a-point); rays from C through D and E are opposite
m\angle DCA = m\angle CAB,\ \ m\angle ECB = m\angle CBA @@ alternate-angles, with AC and BC as the crossing lines
A - C = (D - C) + (B - C) @@ adding-keeps-answers; so A lies inside ∠DCB, with s = t = 1
m\angle DCA + m\angle ACB = m\angle DCB @@ the size technique: angle addition
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{m\angle CAB + m\angle ACB + m\angle CBA = 180°}\\ &\quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ ∠DCB and ∠BCE are a linear pair (the size technique)

So an equilateral triangle has three angles of 60° (equal-sides-equal-angles twice, then angle-sum), and the two angles of a right triangle other than the right one add to 90°. Halve an equilateral triangle of side 2 along the line from a corner to the middle of the opposite side: by three-sides the halves are congruent, the angles at the middle are a linear pair and so 90° each, and right-triangle gives the third side √(4 − 1) = √3. That is the 30°, 60°, 90° triangle, with sides 1, √3, 2. Halving a square along its diagonal gives the 45°, 45°, 90° triangle, with sides 1, 1, √2.

Parallelogram technique: if AB is parallel to CD and AD is parallel to BC, then D = A + C − B, so AB = CD and AD = BC. The point A + C − B is on the line through A with direction C − B and on the line through C with direction A − B; those two lines are not parallel, so by two-lines-meet they meet only once, at D. The figure has the accepted name parallelogram. A parallelogram with a right angle is a rectangle, and a rectangle with equal sides is 18a's square.

18f7Same shape, another size

Scaling technique in the plane: for a point O and k > 0, the map X ↦ O + k(X − O) multiplies every distance by k, keeps every opening, sends each line to a line parallel to it, and multiplies every area by k². The distances are distance-laws with the k taken out of the root. The openings keep, since each eᵢ is unchanged. For areas, put O at (0, 0): the region between the graphs of f ≤ g over [α, β] goes to the region between x ↦ kf(x/k) and x ↦ kg(x/k) over [kα, kβ], whose area is ∫ k(g − f)(x/k) dx = k² ∫ (g − f)(u) du by substitution for areas, with u = x/k. Figures that one such map carries onto figures congruent to others have the accepted name similar, and k is the scale factor.

Parallel-cut technique: in a triangle ABC, for t > 0 let D = A + t(B − A). The line through D parallel to BC meets the line AC at E = A + t(C − A), and DE = t · BC. The point A + t(C − A) is on AC, and it minus D is t(C − B), so it is on the parallel through D; by two-lines-meet the two lines meet only there. Its accepted name is the intercept theorem.

Similar-triangles technique: if two triangles ABC and A′B′C′ have ∠A = ∠A′ and ∠B = ∠B′, then ∠C = ∠C′, and A′B′/AB = B′C′/BC = C′A′/CA.

t = A'B'/AB,\ \ D = A + t(B - A),\ \ E = A + t(C - A) @@ parallel-cut: DE is parallel to BC, AD = A′B′, AE = t · AC, DE = t · BC
\angle ADE = \angle ABC = \angle A'B'C' @@ alternate-angles's twin on one side: D − A and B − A share a direction, and so do E − D and C − B
E' \text{ on the ray } A'C',\ A'E' = AE @@ then two-sides-and-the-angle for ADE and A′B′E′: A′B′ = AD, A′E′ = AE, ∠A′ = ∠A
\angle A'B'E' = \angle ADE = \angle A'B'C' @@ two-sides-and-the-angle's last clause
E' = C' @@ the size technique: one ray on that side has that size; it meets the line A′C′ once (two-lines-meet)
\begin{aligned}&\htmlClass{new}{C'A' = t \cdot CA,}\\ &\htmlClass{new}{B'C' = B'E' = DE = t \cdot BC} \quad \blacksquare\end{aligned} @@ two-sides-and-the-angle gave B′E′ = DE; ∠C = ∠C′ by angle-sum

Right-triangle technique by similarity: if ∠C is right and F is the foot of the perpendicular from C to AB, then b² = c · |AF| and d² = c · |FB|, so d² + b² = c². The triangles AFC and ACB share ∠A and each has a right angle, so similar-triangles gives |AF|/b = b/c; likewise at B. F lies between A and B, since the angles at A and B are acute by angle-sum, so |AF| + |FB| = c by equality-on-the-segment. This is the second proof promised on the survey. The first was 18a's, by algebra on the coordinates.

Angle-in-a-semicircle technique: for a circle with diameter AB, a point X ≠ A, B lies on the circle exactly when ∠AXB is a right angle. Put the centre at (0, 0), so B = −A: then (A − X) · (B − X) = |X|² − |A|², which is 0 exactly when |X| = |A|.

18f8Three things earned at last

The book from ninth-century Baghdad, in 8i, solved a square and ten roots make thirty-nine, and justified its recipe with a drawing. Here it is. Draw a square of side x, its area x² by square's-area. Along two of its sides lay rectangles x by 5, together 10x. The figure has area x² + 10x = 39, and it is a square of side x + 5 missing its corner, 5 by 5. Fill the corner: the whole is a square of area 39 + 25 = 64, so its side is 8 and x = 3. The drawing finds only the root 3. The root −13 of 8i's formula is missing, because no side has a length below 0, and its author had no number for it. That closes the flag 8i opened for its drawing.

On 12·phys, a stiff bar turning on its pivot moved each place in proportion to its distance from the pivot, taken from watching a seesaw. That closes the premise 12·phys took from measurement. Put the pivot at O = (0, 0) and the bar along a unit direction u, with the hands at P = pu and the stone at Q = −qu. After the turn the bar lies along a unit direction u′, so P′ = pu′ and Q′ = −qu′. Then P′ − P = p(u′ − u) and Q′ − Q = −q(u′ − u): the two moves are parallel, opposite, and in the ratio p to q. The triangles OPP′ and OQQ′ are similar, by scaling in the plane with k = q/p after opposite-angles turns one onto the other's lines.

On 9·bio, the surface law asked why an animal's skin should go as its bulk to the ⅔ power. Take two animals of one shape, the second k times as long in every direction. By scaling in the plane every area of skin is k² times as big. Bulk is counted in cubes of 13g, and a cube of side s becomes a cube of side ks, holding k³ times as much. So skin goes as k² and bulk as k³, and skin goes as bulk to the ⅔ power. That closes the flag 9·bio left for this rung.

Who said it first

The Greek geometer is Euclid, whose Elements (about 300 BCE) proves two-sides-and-the-angle as Book I, proposition 4, by laying one triangle on the other. Equal-sides-equal-angles is his proposition 5, the bridge of asses, and alternate-angles his proposition 29, which needed his fifth postulate. Similar-triangles is his Book VI, proposition 4, and the compass test for equal angles is his.

If the triangle ABC is applied to the triangle DEF, with the point A placed on D and the line AB on DE, then B will fall on E, since AB equals DE; and since the angle at A equals the angle at D, AC will fall on DF.

Euclid, Elements, Book I, proposition 4 (about 300 BCE); paraphrase

The German mathematician of 1899 is David Hilbert, who made two-sides-and-the-angle an axiom. The book from ninth-century Baghdad whose drawing 18f8 earns is al-Khwārizmī’s.

Accepted names: right-triangle is Pythagoras' theorem, and angle-in-a-semicircle is Thales' theorem.
