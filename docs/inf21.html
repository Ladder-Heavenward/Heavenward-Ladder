Can a Group Be Rebuilt From the Ways It Acts?

Can a group be rebuilt from the ways it acts? Forget the group. Keep only its representations, the ways it acts on spaces of vectors as rung 46 built them, the maps between them that respect the group, how two of them combine side by side, and the forgetful map that sends each representation to its plain space. Then the group comes back: its members are exactly the families of grids, one on every representation, that respect all of this. For a turn-about group this is ∞20’s double turning; for the shuffles of three it is read off three pieces and their side-by-side table. The forgetful map matters: two different groups of eight members have the same table. Since 2005 such tables have been studied with no group behind them at all.

Rep(G): representations, maps respecting G, side by side V ⊗ W, unit the one representation. A family u = (u_V), one grid on each V, that commutes with every map respecting G and has u_{V⊗W} = u_V ⊗ u_W, u_1 = 1, is ρ(g) for exactly one g in G. Clock of 3: u on χₖ is zᵏ with z³ = 1. S₃: pieces 1, ε, W with ε ⊗ ε = 1, ε ⊗ W = W, W ⊗ W = 1 + ε + W. The square’s symmetries and the quaternion group of 8 share their table.

inf21.1A Clock From Its Turnings

A turn-about group acts wholly only on lines, by 46·cb27.3, and its whole representations are its turnings. For the clock of 3, ∞20.1 found three: χ₀, χ₁, χ₂ with χₖ(j) = ωʲᵏ, ω a third of a full turn. Side by side, two turnings multiply: χₖ ⊗ χₗ = χₖ₊ₗ. Now forget the clock and keep only that table.

Clock-back technique: a choice of a number uₖ on each χₖ with u₀ = 1 and uₖ₊ₗ = uₖ uₗ is uₖ = zᵏ for a z with z³ = 1, and these three choices are the three members of the clock.

u_k = u_1^k @@ uₖ₊ₗ = uₖ uₗ, from u₀ = 1
u_1^3 = u_3 = u_0 = 1 @@ χ₃ is χ₀, since 3 is 0 on the clock
z \in \{1, \omega, \omega^2\},\ \ z = \omega^j @@ the three cube roots of 1
\htmlClass{new}{u_k = \omega^{jk} = \chi_k(j)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the family is “read every turning at j”: ∞20.4’s double turning

∞20.4 called this the turnings of the turnings. Here it reads differently: the members of the group are the families of numbers, one per representation, that respect side by side. No member was kept, and every member came back.

inf21.2The Shuffles of Three From Three Pieces

46·cb27.1 gave the shuffles of three three whole pieces: the one representation 1, the sign ε, and the plane W of vectors with x + y + z = 0. On the three families of shuffles, the do-nothing, the three swaps and the two turns, their characters are (1, 1, 1), (1, −1, 1) and (2, 0, −1). Side by side, characters multiply.

Shuffle-table technique: ε ⊗ ε = 1, ε ⊗ W = W and W ⊗ W = 1 + ε + W.

\chi_\varepsilon^2 = (1, 1, 1) = \chi_1 @@ signs squared
\chi_\varepsilon \chi_W = (2, 0, -1) = \chi_W @@ the sign is ±1 where W is 0 or −1, and +1 at the do-nothing
\chi_W^2 = (4, 0, 1) = (1,1,1) + (1,-1,1) + (2,0,-1) @@ add the three characters
\htmlClass{new}{W \otimes W = 1 + \varepsilon + W} \quad \blacksquare @@ a representation is fixed by its character, by <a href="r46cb27.html#21d96b">46·cb27.4</a>

The dimensions agree: 1 + 1 + 2 = 4 = 2 × 2. A member of the group is then a choice of grid on each piece, a 1 × 1 on 1 and ε and a 2 × 2 on W, respecting this table and every map that respects the group. The six members are the six ways to do that.

inf21.3The Technique in General

Let G be a finite group. A family u gives each representation V a grid u_V on its plain space, so that u commutes with every map that respects G, u_{V ⊗ W} = u_V ⊗ u_W, and u is 1 on the one representation. Each member g gives such a family, u_V = ρ_V(g).

Rebuild technique: every such family is ρ(g) for exactly one g in G.

\mathbb{C}[G] \to V,\ \ e \mapsto v \text{ respects } G \text{ for every } v @@ the group acting on its own sums, ℂ[G]
u \text{ is fixed by } a = u_{\mathbb{C}[G]}(e) = \textstyle\sum_g a_g\, g @@ <a href="r47g.html#7c833a">47g2</a>’s arrows-into technique: the do-nothing arrow decides
a \otimes a = \textstyle\sum_g a_g\, g \otimes g @@ u respects side by side, and e in ℂ[G] ⊗ ℂ[G] goes along g ↦ g ⊗ g
\htmlClass{new}{a_g a_h = 0 \ (g \ne h),\ a_g^2 = a_g,\ \textstyle\sum a_g = 1 \Rightarrow a = g} \quad \blacksquare @@ compare coefficients; u is 1 on the one representation

For groups that are not finite, the circle or the turns of space, the same holds when representations are taken with care, and that is the form in which the technique was first found. The finite case already shows the shape: a natural family is decided by one arrow, and respecting side by side picks out the members.

inf21.4Without the Forgetful Map

Drop the plain spaces and keep only the pieces and how they combine: the table. Is the group still there? The symmetries of a square, eight members, and the quaternion group, eight members made of ±1, ±i, ±j, ±k, give the answer.

Same-table technique: the square’s symmetries and the quaternion group have the same side-by-side table.

\text{each: four pieces of dimension 1, one of dimension 2} @@ 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 8
\text{the four of dimension 1 multiply as the four-group} @@ each group, with its centre {±1} divided out, is the four-group
\chi_\rho = (2, -2, 0, 0, 0) \text{ on the five families, in both} @@ the two-dimensional piece
\htmlClass{new}{\rho \otimes \rho = \text{sum of the four of dimension 1, in both}} \quad \blacksquare @@ χ_ρ² = (4, 4, 0, 0, 0), and that is the sum of the four

So the table alone does not rebuild a group. What tells the two apart is the forgetful map together with how side by side swaps its two factors: told here, not proved. Tables that obey all the rules of a table and come from no group at all exist, and they are what the 2005 work studied.

Open questions

These are open: the field is still asking them. There is no key; a good attempt is a reason, a guess, or a better question.

∞21.oq.1Which tables of pieces exist? A table that obeys every rule of side by side need not come from a group, and since 2005 it has been known that for a given table only finitely many ways to realise it exist. Listing all the small ones, those with few pieces, is the open question this page leaves.
A short history · told from more than one side

How we got here

Two mathematicians, whose names the technique now carries, first showed that a turning-round of this kind rebuilds a group that is not finite but finitely covered, like the circle or the turns of space, from its representations, and said which tables of representations come from such a group.

In 2005 three mathematicians published a general study of tables of pieces with all the rules of side by side, whether or not a group stands behind them, and showed among much else that such a table can be realised in only finitely many ways.

The accepted names

The representations with their maps and side by side are the representation category Rep(G), a symmetric monoidal category; the forgetful map is the fibre functor; a family u is a monoidal natural transformation of the fibre functor to itself, and the rebuild technique is Tannaka–Krein duality or Tannaka reconstruction. ℂ[G] is the group algebra, and a with a ⊗ a = Δ(a) is a group-like element. The table is the fusion ring or Grothendieck ring; D₄ and Q₈ have isomorphic character tables and fusion rings. A table with all the rules of side by side is a fusion category; finitely many realisations is Ocneanu rigidity.

Who said it first

Tadao Tannaka and Mark Krein, reconstruction for compact groups (dates not yet revisited). Pavel Etingof, Dmitri Nikshych and Viktor Ostrik, “On fusion categories” (Annals of Mathematics 162, 2005).
