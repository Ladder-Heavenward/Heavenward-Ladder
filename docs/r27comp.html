How Does a Screen Turn a Shape?

A game redraws a whole scene sixty times a second, and every frame the shapes in it have turned, grown or moved a little. The screen does not redraw them by hand. It does one thing to every corner: multiply by a grid.

27d made a straight map into a grid and 27e gave the grid an area factor. This page puts both to work on a screen: a shape is a list of corners, one grid moves them all, the determinant says what happens to area, several maps multiply into one grid, and a third number lets a grid shift a shape as well.

A screen moves a shape by multiplying every corner by one grid.

27comp1A Shape Is a List of Corners

A screen does not hold a house. It holds the corners of the house, each one a list of two numbers, and it draws lines between them. This house has seven corners: five for the walls and roof, and two more for the door. Move the corners and the house moves.

27comp2One Grid Moves Every Corner

27d’s columns technique says a straight map is one grid, and its columns are where (1, 0) and (0, 1) go. So to turn, stretch, slide or flip the whole house, the screen needs four numbers. It applies the same grid to every corner.

One-grid technique: a straight map of the plane moves every corner of a shape by one grid of four numbers, whose first column is where (1, 0) goes and whose second column is where (0, 1) goes.

Dashed: where the shape started. The two arrows are the grid’s columns, where (1, 0) and (0, 1) are sent.

Read the grid off the picture: the first column is where the arrow from (1, 0) now points, and the second is where the arrow from (0, 1) points.

27comp3The Area Factor

27e’s determinant is the number every area is multiplied by. Stretch across by 2 and the house covers twice the screen. A turn and the slide both give 1: the house changes place or shape and covers the same amount. A flip gives −1, and the door changes sides. A determinant of 0 squashes the house onto a line. Many corners now share a place, so no grid can send them back, and the map is not undoable (27d3).

Area-factor technique: a grid multiplies the area of every shape by its determinant’s size; a determinant below 0 also flips the shape, and a determinant of 0 lays it on a line and cannot be undone.

griddeterminantwhat happens to the shape
a quarter turn [0 −1; 1 0]1turned, same area
a stretch across [2 0; 0 1]2twice the area
a shrink [½ 0; 0 ½]¼a quarter of the area
the slide [1 1; 0 1]1leans over, same area
a flip [−1 0; 0 1]−1mirrored, same area
a squash flat [1 1; 1 1]0on a line, not undoable

27comp4One After Another Is One Grid

27d2: doing one map after another is multiplying their grids. A screen that must slide a shape and then turn it multiplies the two grids once, and then applies the one product to every corner. The order matters. Multiplying grids is not turn-about.

Right-first technique: the grid AB does B first and A second; so one map after another is one grid, worked out once, and AB and BA are in general different maps.

T = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ L = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix} @@ the quarter turn (20j) and the slide
TL = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} @@ slide first, then turn: the right-hand grid acts first (27d2)
LT = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix} @@ turn first, then slide
\htmlClass{new}{TL \ne LT} \quad \blacksquare @@ the columns differ

27comp5A Shift Needs a Third Number

A straight map sends (0, 0) to (0, 0). Shifting the house three to the right and two up moves (0, 0), so no grid of four numbers can do it. Screens get round this by writing every corner with a third entry that is always 1, and using a grid of nine numbers.

Third-number technique: write each corner (x, y) as (x, y, 1); then a grid of three rows whose last row is (0, 0, 1) can shift as well as turn and stretch, and shifts multiply with the other maps into one grid.

\begin{bmatrix}1&0&3\\0&1&2\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix} @@ a grid of three rows on a list of three
\htmlClass{new}{= (x + 3,\ y + 2,\ 1)} \quad \blacksquare @@ each row times the list (27d1)

The corner shifted by (3, 2), and the 1 is still a 1, ready for the next grid. Now a turn, a stretch and a shift can all be multiplied into one grid.

27comp6A Million Corners, Sixty Times a Second

For one corner, a grid of four numbers costs four multiplications and two additions. A scene in a game has about a million corners and is redrawn sixty times a second. That is 240 million multiplications a second, every one of the same kind, and none waiting on another. A graphics chip is a machine built to do that one job in many places at once.

4 \times 10^6 \times 60 = 2.4\times 10^8 @@ multiplications per corner × corners × frames each second

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The corners of a shape are its vertices, and a shape stored as corners and lines between them is a mesh. A straight map of the plane is a linear transformation; the slide is a shear and the squash flat a projection onto a line. A shift is a translation, and a straight map followed by a shift is an affine transformation. Writing (x, y) as (x, y, 1) is using homogeneous coordinates, and the grid of nine numbers is a transformation matrix. Multiplying the grids once before touching the corners is concatenating transformations. A graphics chip is a GPU, and the corner-moving step it runs is the vertex shader.

What was counted

“About a million corners, sixty times a second” is an order of size for a scene in a modern game, not a measured value; the 240 million is the arithmetic on it.
