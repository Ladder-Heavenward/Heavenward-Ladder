30j · proof

The Bell Curve

Heights of people, errors of measurement, the total of a hundred dice: plot how often each value turns up, and the same shape appears again and again, high in the middle and falling away on both sides like a bell. This step explains why. First it finds the total area under the bell, which takes a trip into the plane and back. Then it stores a chance variable in a function of a new kind, built from the growth function, that works for any chance variable, not only counts. With that function, it shows that adding many independent copies of almost anything and rescaling gives a function that heads to the bell’s own. One last matching step, which turns the meeting of these functions into the meeting of chances, is held in trust; its proof waits much higher up the ladder.

The bell’s total is the root of pi. The average-function of a sum of independent copies, rescaled, heads to the bell’s, so sums of many independent parts take the bell’s shape.

30j1The Bell’s Total

Bell-total technique: ∫ e^(−x²) dx over the whole line is √π.

I = \textstyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx,\ \ I^2 = \int\!\!\int e^{-x^2 - y^2}\,dx\,dy @@ either-order (30e3); the function is at least 0
x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta,\ \ dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta @@ changing the variables (28i)
I^2 = \textstyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta @@ x² + y² = r²
\textstyle\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr = \big[-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\big]_0^{\infty} = \tfrac12 @@ the slope of e^(−r²) is −2r e^(−r²) (16)
\htmlClass{new}{I^2 = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac12 = \pi,\ \ I = \sqrt{\pi}} \quad \blacksquare @@ I > 0

From here on, the bell is the chance variable Z with chances P(a < Z ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ φ(x) dx, where Here φ (little phi) means the bell's density, not 2e's condition.

φ(x) = e^(−x²/2) / √(2π).

φ is its density: chance per unit length. Its total is 1, putting x = √2 u in the bell-total. Its average is 0, since φ is the same on both sides of 0. Its spread is ∫ x² φ(x) dx = 1, by undoing the slope of φ, which is −xφ (16). Its accepted names are the standard normal distribution and the normal density.

30j2The Average-Function

From here on, the average-function of a chance variable X is M(t) = E e^(tX), for the t where this average is finite. M(0) = 1 always. Its accepted name is the moment generating function.

Average-function technique: if M is finite on an interval around 0, then near 0, M(t) = 1 + t E X + t² E X²/2 + r(t) with r(t)/t² heading to 0. If X and Y are independent, MX+Y = MX MY.

e^{tX} = \sum_k t^k X^k/k! @@ the growth sum (23)
\big|\textstyle\sum_{k \le n} t^k X^k/k!\big| \le e^{|tX|} \le e^{tX} + e^{-tX} @@ a fence with a finite average, for |t| inside the interval
M(t) = \sum_k t^k\,E\,X^k/k! @@ fenced-limit (30d3)
\htmlClass{new}{M(t) = 1 + t\,E\,X + \tfrac{t^2}{2}E\,X^2 + r(t),\ \ r(t)/t^2 \to 0} @@ a power sum with reach above 0 (23c); how wrong the match is (23b)
e^{t(X + Y)} = e^{tX}e^{tY} \Rightarrow \htmlClass{new}{M_{X+Y} = M_X M_Y} \quad \blacksquare @@ independent-averages-multiply (30g3)
  
M_Z(t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\textstyle\int e^{tx - x^2/2}\,dx @@ the average as a total against the density
tx - x^2/2 = t^2/2 - (x - t)^2/2 @@ complete the square (8i)
\htmlClass{new}{M_Z(t) = e^{t^2/2}} @@ sliding keeps the total (30a3), and φ has total 1

So n independent bells have a sum with average-function e^(nt²/2), and their average (Z₁ + ⋯ + Zₙ)/n has e^(t²/(2n)): the average-function of a bell narrowed to spread 1/n. With the matching step that 30j3 holds in trust, the average of independent bell readings is itself a bell, narrower by √n.

30j3Every Sum Heads to the Bell

Let X₁, X₂, … be independent with the same law, average μ, spread width σ > 0, and an average-function finite on an interval around 0. Rescale their sum to average 0 and spread 1:

Zₙ = (X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ − nμ) / (σ√n).

Central limit technique: the average-function of Zₙ heads to e^(t²/2), the bell’s, at every t near 0; so P(a < Zₙ ≤ b) heads to ∫ₐᵇ φ(x) dx.

Y_k = (X_k - \mu)/\sigma,\ \ E\,Y = 0,\ \ E\,Y^2 = 1 @@ Var(aX + b) = a² Var X (30g2)
M_Y(u) = 1 + u^2/2 + r(u),\ \ r(u)/u^2 \to 0 @@ average-function (30j2)
M_{Z_n}(t) = M_Y(t/\sqrt n)^n = \big(1 + \tfrac{t^2/2 + n\,r(t/\sqrt n)}{n}\big)^n @@ Zₙ = (Y₁ + ⋯ + Yₙ)/√n; sums multiply (30j2)
n\,r(t/\sqrt n) = t^2 \cdot \dfrac{r(t/\sqrt n)}{(t/\sqrt n)^2} \to 0 @@ for t ≠ 0; at t = 0 both sides are 1
(1 + x_n/n)^n \to e^x \text{ when } x_n \to x @@ many-small-chances (22j1): n ln(1 + xₙ/n) → x, by the slope of ln at 1 (17b)
\htmlClass{new}{M_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2}} @@ xₙ = t²/2 + n r(t/√n) → t²/2
\text{matching average-functions give matching limits} @@ held in trust: its proof waits much higher up the ladder
\htmlClass{new}{P(a < Z_n \le b) \to \textstyle\int_a^b \varphi(x)\,dx} \quad \blacksquare @@ the bell (30j1)

Nothing about the shape of X survived except its average and its spread. A coin, a die, a waiting time: add enough of them, rescale, and the bell appears. The finite average-function shortens the proof. The same holds with only a finite spread, and that proof also waits much higher up the ladder.

Anexact, yet rigorous · the middle of a change

13a found the slope in the middle of a change: not the before, not the after, but what the chords settle on as they close in. The bell is a middle of this kind. Each part’s own shape is a before. The bell is what every sum passes through as the parts grow many and each grows small.

The bell is the shape a sum passes through when its many small parts have faded into their spread.

In the proof, every term of MY past t²/2 fades as n grows. Only the middle term, the spread, is left, and the bell is the shape of that term alone.

Heights of adults are close to a bell; weights of adults are not, and lean to the right. What in their parts might make the difference?

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

The bell is the standard normal, or Gaussian, distribution, written N(0, 1), and φ is the normal density. The bell-total is the Gaussian integral. The average-function is the moment generating function, and E Xᵏ is the kth moment. The central limit technique is the central limit theorem. The matching step held in trust is Curtiss’ continuity theorem for moment generating functions; its proof needs the inversion of the Laplace transform.

Who said it first

Abraham de Moivre found the bell as the limit of coin tosses in 1733. Pierre-Simon Laplace extended it in 1812, and Carl Friedrich Gauss used it for errors of measurement in 1809. The squaring trick for the bell’s total is credited to Siméon Denis Poisson. Aleksandr Lyapunov proved a general central limit theorem in 1901; Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg gave the form with only a finite variance in 1922. Francis Galton built a board of pins, the quincunx, that shows the bell in falling beads.

A proof that is not allowed

“M of Zₙ is (1 + t²/(2n))ⁿ, which heads to e^(t²/2).” Not allowed as written: MY(u) is 1 + u²/2 only up to the error r(u). The proof must show that n times the error at u = t/√n heads to 0.
