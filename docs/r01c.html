1c · proof

And, and the ors

“And” joins statements; “or” offers options. They look like partners, and in a way they are, but their techs are not alike at all, and the difference is the thing to learn in this step. An “and” hands you both of its parts. An “or” hands you neither: it makes you work inside each option in turn, in the same line, before you may write anything in its place. The step gives each word its mark, its making tech and its using tech. It earns the earliest techniques about them, and shows the tempting step that an “or” most invites and most firmly refuses. Before the or’s tech it sets down cases: one statement from every way the givens allow.

To say “and”, have both; having “and”, keep either. To say “or”, have either; having “or”, work inside each option, reach the same thing in each, and only then write it once.

1c1And, and its mark

“Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown” claims both: that Tibbles is grey, and that Rex is brown. It is so exactly when each part is so. Here is the mark:

P ∧ Q is a statement, made of two. Read P ∧ Q as “P and Q”.

When the parts are themselves built from pieces, brackets show what the ∧ joins. For example, (P ∧ Q) ∧ R joins “P ∧ Q” with R. ¬P ∧ Q joins ¬P with Q, because ¬ grabs only the statement right after it, as 1b2 said. This ladder writes its brackets rather than relying on conventions about which mark binds tighter.

1c2Join, and keep either part

The techs for “and” are the plainest on the rung, because the word hides nothing. There are two: one makes an “and”, and one uses it.

  • Join. From P and Q, you may write P ∧ Q. This makes an “and”. For example: Tibbles is grey; Rex is brown; so, by join, Tibbles is grey ∧ Rex is brown. You already do this whenever you report two things at once: “I fed the cat, and I locked the door.”
  • Keep either part. From P ∧ Q, you may write P, and you may write Q. This uses an “and”. Keeping the part before the mark is “keep left”; the part after, “keep right”. For example: from Tibbles is grey ∧ Rex is brown, keep right: Rex is brown. You do this too. Told “I fed the cat and locked the door”, you can answer “did you lock the door?” with yes.

Join needs both parts in hand. Keep needs the whole and in hand. Neither tech ever reaches past what it was handed.

1c3And turns about

Said of pets first: Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown, so Rex is brown and Tibbles is grey. Of course. The earliest technique about ∧ says that in marks, and it gets a name so later arguments can name it.

Turn-about technique for And: from P ∧ Q, Q ∧ P. The parts of an “and” may be written in either order.

Start from P ∧ Q. Keep the right part: Q. Keep the left part: P. Join them, the other way about: Q ∧ P.

Take it apart, put it together the other way about. Every argument about ∧ has that shape, and most are as short as this.

1c4An and is not a sameness

With two lines joined, it is tempting to treat them as one thing. Rung 0's = does make two into one (0b2); ∧ does nothing of the kind. An and says both parts are true, not that they are the same: each part keeps its own meaning, and each can be refuted on its own.

Take “Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown”. Keep the left part, and you have “Tibbles is grey”. Keep the right part, and you have “Rex is brown”. Neither keep ever puts one part where the other was. Now Rex rolls in flour. “Rex is brown” is refuted, so the and is refuted with it, and “Tibbles is grey” is still true. Two names for one thing could never come apart like that. The parts of an and are equally true for as long as neither is refuted, and that is all the and says.

An = is different. Tibbles = the cat on the mat. If “Tibbles is asleep” is refuted, so is “the cat on the mat is asleep”, and the = itself still stands. An = keeps itself, and whatever is refuted of one side is refuted of the other. To bring down an = you need something true of one side and false of the other.

1c5Or, and its mark

“Ann is at the bakery or at the library” claims something weaker than either part. It claims only that one of its two options is true, and it is content if both are. That it is content with both matters: on this ladder, and throughout mathematics, “or” is the generous word. “This or that but not both” must be said in full, as an “or” joined with a denial of the “and”. Here is the mark:

P ∨ Q is a statement, made of two.

Read P ∨ Q as “P or Q”. Each side of an “or” is called a disjunct, from the Latin for “set apart”. The word is worth having because “side” is also what people call the parts of other marks.

No key for these marks? Write P and Q and P or Q, as 4a17 wrote end.

1c6Add an option

Add an option: from P, you may write P ∨ Q, or Q ∨ P, for any statement Q whatever. This makes an “or”. You have thrown away the news of which disjunct is true, and that is allowed: a vaguer statement that follows from one you have is still earned. You already do this when you are vague on purpose. Ann is at the bakery, so “Ann is at the bakery or the library” is still true, just less helpful.

1c7Cases: one statement from every way

Sometimes the givens say that things are one of a few ways, without saying which. A shelter card reads “Rex is a puppy or Rex is a kitten”, and the ink has run on the rest. Neither way can be said outright. But each way can be worked where it stands, inside the or, while the line keeps both. The argument below does that.

Work inside an option: in a line that holds an or, you may work one option by any tech, using that option and the other statements of the line, while the other options stand as they were. Nothing written inside one option is in reach in another, or outside the or. Say it once: an or whose options are all the same statement says that statement, and you may write it alone. So when every way things could be has been worked to the same statement, that statement leans on none of them, and the or gives way to it. This is called reasoning by cases, and each option is a case. Reasoning by cases is guessing done in full: every guess the givens leave open is worked, and what all of them reach is kept, whichever guess is right.

Rex is a puppy or Rex is a kitten. That is given: the card says one of the two, not which. Work inside the first option: Rex is young (﹀ “puppy”). The line now reads Rex is young ∨ Rex is a kitten. Work inside the second: Rex is young (﹀ “kitten”). The line reads Rex is young ∨ Rex is young. Both options say the same, so say it once: Rex is young.

Each option is worked in its own place in the line, so nothing written in one is in reach in another, or outside. The common statement is the exception, and it stands alone once every option says it. The list of ways has to be complete. Leave a way out of the or, and the statement might lean on the one you left out. A card reading “puppy, kitten or parrot” leaves Rex young only if the parrot option reaches it too. It does not: nothing on the card says the parrot is young, so the line reads Rex is young ∨ Rex is a parrot, and it stops there. The next step writes this way of working in marks, as the way an or is used.

1c8Reason by cases, with the mark

Reason by cases: in a line that holds P ∨ Q, work inside the option P until it reads R, and inside the option Q until it reads the same R; then R ∨ R says R once, and you may write R. This is 1c7’s reasoning by cases, now with the mark. It uses an “or”. The or-line is what makes the list of ways complete, so the common R leans on neither option. You already do this. If it rains, you will take the bus. If it shines, you will take the bus. It will rain or shine. So you will take the bus.

Notice what is missing. There is no “keep either disjunct”. From P ∨ Q you may not write P, and you may not write Q. The or-line told you that an option is true, not which, and a line may not pretend to know what it does not say. Picking a side is the illegal move this step exists to name, and it is named again in the illegal moves.

1c9The remaining option

Ann is at the bakery or at the library. She is not at the library. So she is at the bakery. Here is the technique that says so. Remaining-option technique: from P or Q, and not Q, you may write P. It needs the or-line and the denial of one option. The case where Ann is at the library is the case that cannot happen, and 1b's strangest tech is what closes that case.

Ann is at the bakery or at the library. She is not at the library. The line holds both. Work inside the library option, beside “she is not at the library”: a clash, so that option is totally false. When nothing is true, everything is permitted, so the option may be written again as “she is at the bakery”. The line now reads: she is at the bakery ∨ she is at the bakery. Say it once: she is at the bakery.

Note where everything-permitted earns its keep. The library option must still reach “she is at the bakery” (the P), because reasoning by cases needs the same statement (the R) in every option, and from ⊥ it does. That writing stays inside the option. What hands P out is reasoning by cases, from both options at once. This is the everyday use of the tech, and nearly its only one.

An old story tells of a hunting dog at a fork of three roads. It sniffed down two of them, found no scent, and ran down the third without sniffing at all. The dog, the story says, reasons: the hare went this way or that way or the other; not this, not that; so the other. That is the remaining-option technique, used twice, on an or of three.

1c10Or turns about

Said of Ann: she is at the bakery or the library, so she is at the library or the bakery. The technique that says so is the or-version of the turn-about technique for And.

Turn-about technique for Or: from P ∨ Q, Q ∨ P. It is earned by cases, with the making tech in each option.

Start from P ∨ Q. Work inside the option P: add an option in front, Q ∨ P. Work inside the option Q: add an option behind, Q ∨ P. The line reads (Q ∨ P) ∨ (Q ∨ P). Say it once: Q ∨ P.

1c11Or, but not both

Outside mathematics, “or” usually means one or the other, not both. For example, “you may have cake or ice cream” does not offer both. The generous or is the exception. Mathematics keeps it because it is the easier one to build with. The everyday or is built from it, with marks already in this step.

Read P ⊕ Q as “P or Q, but not both”. Its name is the exclusive or. From here on, ∨ has a name too: the inclusive or. The exclusive or says that exactly one of its two options is true. Like 1b's ≠, it is a mark made of marks already earned. This one is made of three: an or, an and, and a not.

English has a second way to say it: “this and not that, or that and not this”. In marks, (P ∧ ¬Q) ∨ (¬P ∧ Q). The first way starts from the generous or and takes away the both. The second builds each option with its denial attached, so it leans on not from the start. From the second way a line reaches the first way’s or, P ∨ Q, by cases. The not-both, and the road back, ask for a denial a line cannot write, and wait for proofs.

1c12Only one

Cake or ice cream, then. A server who brings both has not been generous with the menu’s or. The server has broken it: the table now holds the or, the cake and the ice cream, and that line is totally false.

Only-one technique: a line that holds P ⊕ Q, P and Q equals ⊥. The generous or can never give this. A line that holds P ∨ Q, P and Q stands, since ∨ is content with both.

Start from a line that holds P ⊕ Q, P and Q. Unpack the ⊕: P ∨ Q, and ¬(P ∧ Q). Join P and Q: P ∧ Q. That clashes with ¬(P ∧ Q), and the line equals ⊥. Writing ¬Q alone, from P ⊕ Q and P, is the output a line cannot offer: it waits for proofs.

Going from ¬Q to P works as it does for any or. From P ⊕ Q and ¬Q, keep the first part, P ∨ Q. The remaining-option technique gives P.

So there are two ors, and a reader has to know which one a statement means. In mathematics, a bare “or” is always the inclusive one. In everyday speech, ask whether both could be so. If both could be so and the statement doesn't mind, it is the inclusive or. If the statement would be broken by both, it is the exclusive one.

1c13Running it backwards

Wanting “Ann is on Elm Street” from “Ann is at the bakery or at the library”, ask whether each place is on Elm Street. If both are, each option can be worked to the foot, and cases hand it out. That question, asked of every disjunct, is the whole search.

Wanting P ∧ Q, you know the last move: join. So the line needs P and needs Q, each earned separately. Wanting P ∨ Q, the last move is add an option, so it is enough to reach either disjunct. Pick the easier. If neither is reachable alone, look for an or in the line and reason by cases, working each option to P ∨ Q. Having an or-line and wanting anything at all, ask: can I work each option to it? If an option is denied by something in the line, that option closes through ⊥ for free, which is what the remaining-option technique does. The search for an argument by cases is a search for the common statement; the slides run one from the foot.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • P \land Q is also written P & Q, and in code P && Q or P AND Q.
  • P \lor Q is also written P + Q in some books, and in code P || Q or P OR Q. It is the inclusive or.
  • P \oplus Q is also written P ⊻ Q, or XOR, and in code P ^ Q.

Who said it first

The hunting dog is Chrysippus's, the Stoic, as Sextus Empiricus reports it.

Chrysippus told of a hunting dog at a fork of three roads. It sniffed down two of them, found no scent, and ran down the third without sniffing at all. The dog, he said, reasons: the hare went this way or that way or the other; not this, not that; so the other.

Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.69, reporting Chrysippus; paraphrase

Accepted names

P ∧ Q is the conjunction of P and Q, and P and Q are its conjuncts. P ∨ Q is the disjunction of P and Q.

The accepted word for what the turn-about techniques for And and for Or do is commutative: ∧ and ∨ are commutative.

Splitting an or into its sides and reaching the same line from each has the accepted name proof by cases.
