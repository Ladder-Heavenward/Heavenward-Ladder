1c · proof

And, and the ors

“And” joins statements; “or” offers options. They look like partners, and in a way they are, but their techs are not alike at all, and the difference is the thing to learn in this step. An “and” hands you both of its parts. An “or” hands you neither, and makes you work through each option in turn before you may conclude anything. The step gives each word its mark, its making tech and its using tech. It proves the earliest techniques about them, and shows the tempting step that an “or” most invites and most firmly refuses.

To say “and”, have both; having “and”, keep either. To say “or”, have either; having “or”, suppose each option in turn, reach the same thing from each, and only then have it.

What this stands on, and where it goes

Stands on 1b (¬, ⊥, suppositions, clash, refute, when nothing is true, everything is permitted) and on rung 0. 1d defines “exactly when” with the ∧ built here; 1e matches these techs with “every” and “some”; 1f gives both marks a table; 1g lists their rewriting techniques; 1i makes reasoning by cases a proof shape.

1c1And, and its mark

Stands on 1b1 (the word, then the mark).

“Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown” claims both: that Tibbles is grey, and that Rex is brown. It is so exactly when each part is so. Here is the mark:

P ∧ Q is a statement, made of two. It is called the conjunction of P and Q, and P and Q are its conjuncts. Read P ∧ Q as “P and Q”.

When the parts are themselves built from pieces, brackets show what the ∧ joins. For example, (P ∧ Q) ∧ R joins “P ∧ Q” with R. ¬P ∧ Q joins ¬P with Q, because ¬ grabs only the statement right after it, as 1b said. This ladder writes its brackets rather than relying on conventions about which mark binds tighter.

1c2Join, and keep either part

Stands on 1c1 (and, and its mark).

The techs for “and” are the plainest on the rung, because the word hides nothing. There are two: one makes an “and”, and one uses it.

  • Join. From P and Q, you may write P ∧ Q. This makes an “and”. For example: Tibbles is grey; Rex is brown; so, by join, Tibbles is grey ∧ Rex is brown. You already do this whenever you report two things at once: “I fed the cat, and I locked the door.”
  • Keep either part. From P ∧ Q, you may write P, and you may write Q. This uses an “and”. Keeping the part before the mark is “keep left”; the part after, “keep right”. For example: from Tibbles is grey ∧ Rex is brown, keep right: Rex is brown. You do this too. Told “I fed the cat and locked the door”, you can answer “did you lock the door?” with yes.

Join needs both parts in hand. Keep needs the whole and in hand. Neither tech ever reaches past what it was handed.

1c3And turns about

Stands on 1c2 (join, and keep either part).

Said of pets first: Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown, so Rex is brown and Tibbles is grey. Of course. The earliest technique about ∧ says that in marks, and it gets a name so later arguments can name it.

Turn-about technique for And: from P ∧ Q, Q ∧ P. The parts of an “and” may be written in either order.

Start from P ∧ Q. Keep the right part: Q. Keep the left part: P. Join them, the other way about: Q ∧ P.

Take it apart, put it together the other way about. Every argument about ∧ has that shape, and most are as short as this.

1c4An and is not a sameness

Stands on 1c3 (and turns about) and 0b5 (swap equals).

With two lines joined, it is tempting to treat them as one thing. Rung 0's = does make two into one: “Tibbles = the grey cat” says one cat has two names, and the swap axiom lets either name stand wherever the other stands. ∧ does nothing of the kind. An and says both parts are true, not that they are the same: each part keeps its own meaning, and each can be refuted on its own.

Take “Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown”. Keep the left part, and you have “Tibbles is grey”. Keep the right part, and you have “Rex is brown”. Neither keep ever puts one part where the other was. Now Rex rolls in flour. “Rex is brown” is refuted, so the and is refuted with it, and “Tibbles is grey” is still true. Two names for one thing could never come apart like that. The parts of an and are equally true for as long as neither is refuted, and that is all the and says.

An = is different. Tibbles = the cat on the mat. If “Tibbles is asleep” is refuted, so is “the cat on the mat is asleep”, and the = itself still stands. An = keeps itself, and whatever is refuted of one side is refuted of the other. To bring down an = you need something true of one side and false of the other.

1c5Or, and its mark

Stands on 1c1 (and, and its mark).

“Ann is at the bakery or at the library” claims something weaker than either part. It claims only that one of its two options is true, and it is content if both are. That it is content with both matters: on this ladder, and throughout mathematics, “or” is the generous word. “This or that but not both” must be said in full, as an “or” joined with a denial of the “and”. Here is the mark:

P ∨ Q is a statement, made of two. It is called the disjunction of P and Q.

Read P ∨ Q as “P or Q”. Each side of an “or” is called a disjunct, from the Latin for “set apart”. The word is worth having because “side” is also what people call the parts of other marks.

Typing an answer, with no key for these marks? Write the words they are read as: P and Q in place of P ∧ Q, and P or Q in place of P ∨ Q. They mean the same thing, and the ladder reads them the same way.

1c6Add an option

Stands on 1c5 (or, and its mark).

Add an option: from P, you may write P ∨ Q, or Q ∨ P, for any statement Q whatever. This makes an “or”. You have thrown away the news of which disjunct is true, and that is allowed: a vaguer statement that follows from one you have is still earned. You already do this when you are vague on purpose. Ann is at the bakery, so “Ann is at the bakery or the library” is still true, just less helpful.

For example: Ann is at the bakery. So, by add an option, Ann is at the bakery or at the library. The or-line is true, because its first option is. It is less useful than what you had, because it no longer says which.

1c7Reason by cases, with the mark

Stands on 1c6 (add an option) and 1a14 (cases).

Reason by cases: from P ∨ Q, suppose P and reach R, and drop it; then suppose Q and reach the same R, and drop it; then you may write R. This is 1a's reasoning by cases, now with the mark. It uses an “or”. The or-line is what makes the list of ways complete. So the common R leans on neither case, and it stands once the last case is dropped. You already do this. If it rains, you will take the bus. If it shines, you will take the bus. It will rain or shine. So you will take the bus.

Notice what is missing. There is no “keep either disjunct”. From P ∨ Q you may not write P, and you may not write Q. The or-line told you that an option is true, not which, and a proof may not pretend to know what its lines do not say. Picking a side is the illegal move this step exists to name, and it is named again in the illegal moves.

1c8The remaining option

Stands on 1c7 (reason by cases) and 1b10 (when nothing is true, everything is permitted).

Ann is at the bakery or at the library. She is not at the library. So she is at the bakery. Here is the technique that says so. Remaining-option technique: from P or Q, and not Q, you may write P. It needs the or-line and the denial of one option. The case where Ann is at the library is the case that cannot happen, and 1b's strangest tech is what closes that case.

Ann is at the bakery or at the library. She is not at the library. Take the cases one at a time. Case one: suppose she is at the bakery. Then she is at the bakery; nothing more to do. Case two: suppose she is at the library. That clashes with “she is not at the library”: totally false. When nothing is true, everything is permitted. So this case reaches “she is at the bakery” too. Both cases reach the same thing. So, by cases, she is at the bakery.

Said quickly, the argument is nothing: if she is at the bakery she is at the bakery; and she cannot be at the library, so that case is empty. The argument above replaces “cannot” and “so” with named techs. Note where everything-permitted earns its keep. The library case must still reach “she is at the bakery” (the P), because reasoning by cases requires the same statement (the R) from every case, and from ⊥ it does. Inside the library case, that sentence is a private note, and it goes when the case closes. What hands it out is reasoning by cases, from both cases at once. This is the everyday use of the tech, and nearly its only one.

An old story tells of a hunting dog at a fork of three roads. It sniffed down two of them, found no scent, and ran down the third without sniffing at all. The dog, the story says, reasons: the hare went this way or that way or the other; not this, not that; so the other. That is the remaining-option technique, used twice, on an or of three.

1c9Or turns about

Stands on 1c7 (reason by cases) and 1c3 (and turns about).

Said of Ann: she is at the bakery or the library, so she is at the library or the bakery. The technique that says so is the or-version of the turn-about technique for And.

Turn-about technique for Or: from P ∨ Q, Q ∨ P. It is earned by cases, with the making tech in each case.

Start from P ∨ Q. Case one: suppose P. Add an option in front: Q ∨ P. Case two: suppose Q. Add an option behind: Q ∨ P. Both cases reach Q ∨ P. So, by cases, Q ∨ P.

1c10Neither

Stands on 1c9 (or turns about) and 1b5 (refute).

Ann is neither at the bakery nor at the library: not at the bakery, and not at the library. English says this in a word of its own, “neither”, and it joins this step to 1b. Neither technique: if it is not so that P or Q, then P is not so and Q is not so. It needs one line, ¬(P ∨ Q). It writes ¬P ∧ ¬Q.

Start from ¬(P ∨ Q). Suppose, until we drop it, P. Add an option: P ∨ Q. That clashes with what we have. So drop the supposition, by refute: ¬P. Now suppose Q. Add an option: P ∨ Q. Clash again. Drop it, by refute: ¬Q. Join the two: ¬P ∧ ¬Q.

The neither technique runs the other way too: from ¬P ∧ ¬Q, get ¬(P ∨ Q). Keep both parts, ¬P and ¬Q. Suppose P ∨ Q. Case one: suppose P; it clashes with ¬P, so ⊥. Case two: suppose Q; it clashes with ¬Q, so ⊥. Both cases reach ⊥, so, by cases, ⊥. Refute: ¬(P ∨ Q). The slides write that argument out. So “neither” and “not either” are interchangeable on this rung in both directions, by techs alone. 1g will give the rewrite a name.

1c11Not both

Stands on 1c10 (neither) and 1b12 (the step you cannot take).

There is a near twin of “neither” that is not interchangeable both ways on this rung. From ¬P ∨ ¬Q you can get ¬(P ∧ Q). Suppose P ∧ Q, and keep both parts. Then reason by cases on the or-line: each case clashes, so ⊥. Refute gives ¬(P ∧ Q).

But from ¬(P ∧ Q), “not both”, no tech on this rung produces ¬P ∨ ¬Q, “P fails or Q fails”. The missing direction is a cousin of 1b's gap, and it is settled in the same place, rung 7. 1g's rulebook marks it.

Take a second look, with people. Ann and Ben cannot both be guilty: that is “not both”. It does not hand you an innocent suspect. To say “Ann is innocent or Ben is innocent” is to say that one of them can be pointed to, and “not both” never pointed. That is the space the missing direction leaves open.

1c12Or, but not both

Stands on 1c11 (not both) and 1b7 (not the same thing as).

Outside mathematics, “or” usually means one or the other, not both. For example, “you may have cake or ice cream” does not offer both. The generous or is the exception. Mathematics keeps it because it is the easier one to build with. The everyday or is built from it, with marks already in this step.

Read P ⊕ Q as “P or Q, but not both”. Its name is the exclusive or. From here on, ∨ has a name too: the inclusive or. The exclusive or says that exactly one of its two options is true. Like 1b's ≠, it is a mark made of marks already earned. This one is made of three: an or, an and, and a not.

English has a second way to say it: “this and not that, or that and not this”. In marks, (P ∧ ¬Q) ∨ (¬P ∧ Q). The first way starts from the generous or and takes away the both. The second builds each option with its denial attached, so it leans on not from the start. Each way can be earned from the other with the techniques in this step and 1b.

1c13Only one

Stands on 1c12 (or, but not both) and 1c8 (the remaining option).

Cake or ice cream, then. You have the cake, so no ice cream. A server who brings both has not been generous with the menu's or. The server has refuted it.

Only-one technique: from P ⊕ Q and P, you may write ¬Q. The generous or can never give this. From P ∨ Q and P, nothing at all follows about Q.

Start from P ⊕ Q and P. Unpack the ⊕: P ∨ Q, and ¬(P ∧ Q). Keep the second part. Suppose, until we drop it, Q. Join P and Q: P ∧ Q. That clashes with ¬(P ∧ Q). Drop the supposition, by refute: ¬Q.

Going from ¬Q to P works as it does for any or. From P ⊕ Q and ¬Q, keep the first part, P ∨ Q. The remaining-option technique gives P.

So there are two ors, and a reader has to know which one a sentence means. In mathematics, a bare “or” is always the inclusive one. In everyday speech, ask whether both could be so. If both could be so and the sentence doesn't mind, it is the inclusive or. If the sentence would be broken by both, it is the exclusive one.

1c14Illegal moves

Stands on 1c2 to 1c13.

Six moves look like uses of this step and are not. One picks a side of an or. One reads an or as an and. One joins with only one part in hand. One ends cases that reached different lines. One uses something said under a case after the cases are done. And one writes “this or that but not both” as a bare or.

P \lor Q,\;\text{ so } P\,? @@ picking a side: “Ann is at the bakery or the library, so she is at the bakery”. The or-line never said which
P \lor Q,\;\text{ so } P \land Q\,? @@ an “or” is not an “and”: the or-line allows both, it does not claim both
P,\;\text{ so } P \land Q\,? @@ join needs Q in hand as well; “Tibbles is grey, so Tibbles is grey and Rex is brown” earns nothing about Rex
\begin{aligned}&\text{case } P \text{ reaches } R;\;\text{ case } Q \text{ reaches } S;\\ &\text{so } R\,?\end{aligned} @@ the cases reached different things; nothing stands outside until every case reaches the same line
\begin{aligned}&\text{case } P\text{: } \dots P \land R \dots;\\ &\text{after the cases are dropped: } P \land R\,?\end{aligned} @@ a sentence under a case leaned on that case's supposition; it does not survive the drop. Only the common line does
\begin{aligned}&\text{“this or that but not}\\ &\quad\text{both”, written } P \lor Q\,?\end{aligned} @@ ∨ is the generous or. The “not both” must be written: (P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬(P ∧ Q)

The commonest of these in life is the first, picking a side. It feels like information: someone said “bakery or library”, and the hearer walks to the bakery. In a proof the walk is not licensed. What is licensed is to reason about both places, and if the conclusion wanted is the same either way, to have it. If it is not the same, the or-line was not enough, and the honest thing is to say so and look for another line.

1c15Running it backwards

Stands on 1c7 (reason by cases) and 1a19 (reading the foot).

Wanting “Ann is on Elm Street” from “Ann is at the bakery or at the library”, ask whether each place is on Elm Street. If both are, each case reaches the foot, and cases hand it out. That question, asked of every disjunct, is the whole search.

Wanting P ∧ Q, you know the last move: join. So you need P and you need Q, each earned separately. The argument splits into separate threads that meet at the end. Wanting P ∨ Q, the last move is add an option, so it is enough to reach either disjunct. Pick the easier. If neither is reachable alone, look for an or-line you have and reason by cases, reaching P ∨ Q in each case. Having an or-line and wanting anything at all, ask: can I reach it from each disjunct? If a disjunct is denied by something you have, that case closes through ⊥ for free, which is what the remaining-option technique does. The search for an argument by cases is a search for the common line; the slides run one from the foot.

Other ways the same marks are written

Mathematics writes some of these marks in more than one way, by subject and by century. Each one below means what the mark in this step means; know them on sight.

  • P \land Q is also written P & Q, and in code P && Q or P AND Q.
  • P \lor Q is also written P + Q in some books, and in code P || Q or P OR Q. It is the inclusive or.
  • P \oplus Q is also written P ⊻ Q, or XOR, and in code P ^ Q.

Who said it first

The hunting dog is Chrysippus's, the Stoic, as Sextus Empiricus reports it.

Chrysippus told of a hunting dog at a fork of three roads. It sniffed down two of them, found no scent, and ran down the third without sniffing at all. The dog, he said, reasons: the hare went this way or that way or the other; not this, not that; so the other.

Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.69, reporting Chrysippus; paraphrase

Accepted names

The accepted word for what the turn-about techniques for And and for Or do is commutative: ∧ and ∨ are commutative.

Splitting an or into its sides and reaching the same line from each has the accepted name proof by cases.
