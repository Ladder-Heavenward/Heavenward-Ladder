20e · proof

Two Flips

Stand between two mirrors that meet in a corner and you see yourself turned, not flipped. Stand between two that face each other and you see yourself shifted, again and again. This step proves both. Two flips across lines that cross make a turn about the crossing; two flips across parallel lines make a slide. And every turn and every slide can be made this way, so the flips build the other two kinds.

Flipping across the floor and then across a tilted line through the origin is the turn by twice the tilt. Flipping across two parallel lines is the slide by twice the gap between them. Every turn and every slide is two flips.

20e1A Flip, Written Out

Take the line through the origin along u = (c, s), with c² + s² = 1. Write the flip formula out, coordinate by coordinate. A flip through the origin is fixed by the pair (c² − s², 2cs).

F_u(x, y) = 2(cx + sy)(c, s) - (x, y) @@ the flip formula (20c3)
= \big((2c^2 - 1)x + 2cs\,y,\ \ 2cs\,x + (2s^2 - 1)y\big) @@ share out
= \big((c^2 - s^2)x + 2cs\,y,\ \ 2cs\,x - (c^2 - s^2)y\big) @@ 1 = c² + s²

So with p = c² − s² and q = 2cs, F_u(x, y) = (px + qy, qx − py). Compare the turn by (p, q): (px − qy, qx + py). The two differ only in the sign of y.

20e2Lines That Cross Make a Turn

Crossing-flips technique: two flips across lines that cross at a point make a turn about that point. For the floor and then the line along u = (c, s), it is the turn by (c² − s², 2cs).

F_u(F_0(x, y)) = F_u(x, -y) @@ the floor first
= (px - qy,\ qx + py) @@ 20e1, with −y for y
= R_{(p,q)}(x, y),\ \ p^2 + q^2 = (c^2 + s^2)^2 = 1 @@ a turn, by (c² − s², 2cs)
F_v \circ F_u = (F_v \circ F_0) \circ (F_0 \circ F_u) @@ F₀ ∘ F₀ does nothing; regroup
F_0 \circ F_u = (F_u \circ F_0)^{-1} @@ socks-and-shoes: each flip undoes itself (20c.4.4)
\htmlClass{new}{F_v \circ F_u = R_v \circ R_u^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare @@ R_u⁻¹ is the turn by (p, −q); and a turn by (p, q) after one by (p′, q′) is the turn by (pp′ − qq′, qp′ + pq′), whose squares add to (p² + q²)(p′² + q′²) = 1
\text{about another crossing point} @@ slide it to O, flip twice, slide back (20c4)

Rung 21 shows that the turn by (c² − s², 2cs) is the turn by twice the angle from the floor to u. For now it is enough that it is a turn.

20e3Parallel Lines Make a Slide

Parallel-flips technique: two flips across parallel lines make a slide, at right angles to the lines, by twice the gap between them.

y = 0,\ \ y = d:\ \ (x, y) \mapsto (x, -y) \mapsto (x, 2d - (-y)) @@ the floor, then the line y = d: (x, y) ↦ (x, 2d − y) (20c4)
= (x, y + 2d) @@ a slide by (0, 2d)

20e4Every Turn and Every Slide Is Two Flips

Two-flips technique: every turn and every slide is two flips. For a slide by v, take two lines at right angles to v, half of |v| apart. For the turn by (a, b) about the origin, take the floor and the line along (c, s) with c² − s² = a and 2cs = b.

a \ne -1:\ \ c = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 + a}{2}},\ \ s = \tfrac{b}{2c} @@ then c² − s² = (1 + a)/2 − b²/(2(1 + a)) = a, using b² = 1 − a²
2cs = b,\ \ c^2 + s^2 = \tfrac{1 + a}{2} + \tfrac{1 - a}{2} = 1 @@ so u = (c, s) is a unit displacement
a = -1:\ \ (c, s) = (0, 1) @@ the half turn: the floor, then the upright axis
\htmlClass{new}{R_{(a,b)} = F_u \circ F_0} \quad \blacksquare @@ crossing-flips

The floor was a free choice. Any line through the origin can be the first flip, with the second turned on from it.

The accepted names

The two-flips facts are often stated as: the product of two reflections in intersecting lines is a rotation by twice the angle between them; in parallel lines, a translation by twice the distance between them.

Who said it first

In 20e.4.5, the old version is in Plato's Timaeus and Lucretius; modern rounds include Martin Gardner, The Ambidextrous Universe (1964), Ned Block, “Why do mirrors reverse right/left but not up/down?” (1974), and Richard Gregory, Mirrors in Mind (1997).
