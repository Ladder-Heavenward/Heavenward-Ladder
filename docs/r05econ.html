Should the Faster Worker Do Everything?

Should the faster worker do everything? Joy bakes pies and sews shirts, and she is quicker than Terry at both. It looks as if Terry has nothing to offer her. Yet if each makes what costs them least in the other thing, the two of them together end up with more pies and more shirts than either could make alone. This page measures the cost of a shirt in pies, as a fraction, proves that moving work to whoever’s cost is lower makes more of something and less of nothing, and finds a price at which both gain from a trade.

Write each worker’s hours for each good. The cost of one good is how much of the other good the same hours would have made: a ratio of hours. Compare two costs by cross-multiplying. Whoever’s cost of a good is lower makes it; moving one unit to them keeps that good level and adds to the other. Any price strictly between the two costs leaves both better off, and the fake sum of the two fractions is one such price.

5econ1Hours for Each Thing

Joy takes 1 hour to bake a pie and 2 hours to sew a shirt. Terry takes 3 hours for a pie and 4 for a shirt. Joy is quicker at both.

hours for a piehours for a shirt
Joy12
Terry34

Each of them works 12 hours. Working alone, each splits the hours between pies and shirts. If Joy gives 6 hours to each, she makes 6 pies and 3 shirts; if Terry does the same, he makes 2 pies and 1½ shirts.

Together that is 8 pies and 4½ shirts. The question of the page is whether they can do better by splitting the work differently.

5econ2What a Shirt Costs in Pies

The hours Joy spends on one shirt would have baked 2 pies. So for Joy a shirt costs 2 pies. For Terry, the 4 hours of a shirt would have baked 4/3 of a pie.

From here on, on this page, the cost of a shirt for a worker is the number of pies the same hours would make: hours for a shirt over hours for a pie, a ratio as in 5j.

4/3 < 2/1\ \text{exactly when}\ 4 \cdot 1 < 3 \cdot 2 @@ the compare-by-cross-multiplying technique (5f)
4 < 6 @@ so it holds
\htmlClass{new}{\text{a shirt costs Terry fewer pies than it costs Joy}} @@ the costs compared

Turned over, the cost of a pie in shirts is 1/2 for Joy and 3/4 for Terry, and 1/2 < 3/4. So a pie costs Joy fewer shirts. Whoever has the lower cost of one good has the higher cost of the other. Being quicker at both does not make Joy cheaper at both.

5econ3Give Each Thing to Its Cheaper Maker

Lower-cost technique: if a shirt costs one worker fewer pies than another, moving the making of one shirt from the second worker to the first leaves the shirts the same and adds pies. Let the second worker’s cost be a and the first’s be b, with b < a.

\text{first: one more shirt, } b \text{ fewer pies} @@ the hours come from pies, at the first’s cost
\text{second: one fewer shirt, } a \text{ more pies} @@ the hours go to pies, at the second’s cost
\text{shirts: } +1 - 1 = 0 @@ the shirts are level
\text{pies: } a - b @@ the change in pies
\htmlClass{new}{a - b \text{ is positive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ b < a means a − b is positive (5d)

For Joy and Terry, a = 2 and b = 4/3, and each shirt moved to Terry adds 2/3 of a pie. Keep moving shirts to Terry until he makes nothing but shirts: 12 hours make 3 shirts. Joy makes the rest.

\text{Terry: } 12/4 = 3 \text{ shirts} @@ all his hours
\text{Joy: } 3 \text{ hours} \to 1\tfrac12 \text{ shirts},\ 9 \text{ hours} \to 9 \text{ pies} @@ to keep 4½ shirts in all
\htmlClass{new}{9 \text{ pies},\ 4\tfrac12 \text{ shirts}} @@ one more pie than 8, the same shirts

Nobody worked longer or faster. The extra pie came only from who made what.

5econ4A Price Both Will Take

Terry sews and Joy bakes, but each wants some of both, so they trade. Say a shirt trades for p pies. Terry gains on each shirt he sells when p is more than his cost, 4/3. Joy gains on each shirt she buys when p is less than her cost, 2. So a price between the two costs leaves both better off.

\tfrac43 < \tfrac{4 + 2}{3 + 1} < \tfrac21 @@ the fake-sum-between technique (5h)
\tfrac{6}{4} = \tfrac32 @@ the scaling technique (5c)
\htmlClass{new}{p = 3/2 \text{ pies a shirt}} @@ Terry gains 1/6 of a pie a shirt, Joy 1/2

Outside the two costs, one of them would do better making the shirt alone, and that one would not trade. The two costs are the edges of every deal they could agree on.

5econ5Faster at Everything Is Not Cheaper at Everything

In 1817 David Ricardo wrote this out for two countries, England and Portugal, and two goods, cloth and wine. In his numbers Portugal needed fewer hours of work for both. He showed that both countries still gained if Portugal made wine and England made cloth, and they traded.

hours for clothhours for wine
England100120
Portugal9080
\text{wine in cloth: England } 120/100 = 6/5 @@ scaling
\text{wine in cloth: Portugal } 80/90 = 8/9 @@ scaling
8 \cdot 5 < 9 \cdot 6 @@ 40 < 54: compare by cross-multiplying
\htmlClass{new}{\text{wine is cheaper in Portugal}} @@ so Portugal makes wine, and England cloth

What a country or a worker should make is decided by costs in the other good, not by hours alone. Real trade has more than two goods, and costs that change with how much is made. Each of those changes the numbers. None of them changes the step of 5econ3.

Accepted names

The cost of a good in another good is its opportunity cost. Being quicker at a good is absolute advantage; having the lower opportunity cost is comparative advantage. Making only what you have the comparative advantage in is specialisation, and a price between the two costs lies inside the range of mutually beneficial terms of trade.

Who said it first

David Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, 1817, chapter 7, with the cloth and wine numbers in the table. Robert Torrens published a similar idea in 1815.
