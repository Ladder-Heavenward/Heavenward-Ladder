Does a Bouncing Ball Ever Stop?

Does a bouncing ball ever stop? Drop a ping-pong ball on a table and listen. The clicks come faster and faster, then blur into a buzz, then stop. Each bounce seems to reach the same share of the height before it, so there is always a next bounce, a little lower. Is there a last one? What makes it hard is that two answers both look right. Endlessly many bounces seem to need endless time. Yet a few seconds later the ball is plainly lying still. This page shows, with 22e’s geometric yardstick and 13·phys’s falling stone, how far the ball travels, when the bouncing would end if the share never changed, how to hear the share in the clicks, and why a real ball stops leaving the table after a count of bounces you could write down.

If each bounce reaches the same share of the last height, the heights shrink by that share, and the times in the air shrink by its square root. Both lists make endless ratio sums, so the distance and the time are finite, though the bounces never run out. Two neighbouring gaps between clicks stand in the ratio of that square root, so the ear can measure the share. A real bounce also spends a moment pressed against the table, and that moment does not shrink. Endlessly many such moments would take endless time, so a real ball stops bouncing after a finite count.

22phys1How Far the Ball Travels

The rules of table tennis ask that a table give a bounce of about 23 cm when a ball is dropped on it from 30 cm. Take as a measured law, for heights near those, that each bounce reaches the same share r of the height before it. Here r ≈ 23/30 ≈ 0.767. Drop the ball from height h. The bounces reach h r, h r², h r³, and so on.

h \text{ down; then } rh \text{ up and down; then } r^2i \text{ up and down}; \ \ldots @@ each bounce goes up and comes back
D = h + 2(rh + r^2h + r^3h + \cdots) @@ the whole path as an endless sum
= h + \tfrac{2rh}{1 - r} @@ endless-ratio-sum (15i): first term rh, ratio r
\htmlClass{new}{D = h\,\tfrac{1 + r}{1 - r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ over one denominator

Distance technique: a ball whose bounces each reach a share r of the last height travels h(1 + r)/(1 − r) in all. From 30 cm, with r = 23/30, D = 0.30 × 53/7 ≈ 2.27 m. Endlessly many bounces cover less than two and a half metres.

22phys2How Long the Bouncing Lasts

A stone dropped from height H falls for √(2H/g) seconds, since H = ½gt² (13·phys). A hop up to height H and back takes twice that: the climb is the fall run backwards. Write t₀ = √(2h/g) for the first fall.

\text{hop } k:\ \ 2\sqrt{\tfrac{2r^k h}{g}} = 2t_0\,(\sqrt r)^k @@ height rᵏh; up and down
T = t_0 + 2t_0\big(\sqrt r + (\sqrt r)^2 + (\sqrt r)^3 + \cdots\big) @@ the first fall, then every hop
= t_0 + \tfrac{2t_0\sqrt r}{1 - \sqrt r} @@ endless-ratio-sum (15i): ratio √r < 1
\htmlClass{new}{T = t_0\,\tfrac{1 + \sqrt r}{1 - \sqrt r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ over one denominator

Time technique: if every bounce reaches a share r of the last height, endlessly many bounces end within t₀(1 + √r)/(1 − √r) seconds. From 30 cm, t₀ = √(0.6/9.8) ≈ 0.247 s and √r ≈ 0.876, so T ≈ 0.247 × 1.876/0.124 ≈ 3.73 s. The times shrink more slowly than the heights: by √r, not by r.

22phys3Hearing the Share

Each click is a landing, and the gap between two clicks is one hop: 2t₀(√r)ᵏ. A phone can record the clicks, and a sound editor times the gaps to a thousandth of a second.

\tfrac{\text{gap}_{k+1}}{\text{gap}_k} = \tfrac{2t_0(\sqrt r)^{k+1}}{2t_0(\sqrt r)^k} = \sqrt r @@ the time technique, hop by hop
r = \Big(\tfrac{\text{gap}_{k+1}}{\text{gap}_k}\Big)^2 @@ square both sides
\htmlClass{new}{\text{time left after a click} = \tfrac{\text{next gap}}{1 - \sqrt r}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the gaps still to come, an endless ratio sum (15i)

Click technique: two neighbouring gaps between clicks stand in the ratio √r, so the ear can measure the share. Gaps of 0.400 s and then 0.350 s give √r = 0.875 and r ≈ 0.766, a table that meets the rule. The next gap should be 0.306 s, and the buzz should end 0.306/0.125 ≈ 2.45 s after the click that closed the 0.350 s gap.

22phys4What Really Stops It

The time technique trusts the share law at every height, down to the width of an atom and below. Measure where it can fail. A real bounce is not instant. The ball squashes against the table and springs back, and it stays pressed there for a short time τ. For a ping-pong ball τ is about a thousandth of a second. Unlike the hops, τ does not shrink as the hops do.

\text{bounce } k \text{ takes at least } \tau \gt 0 @@ the ball is pressed to the table each time
n \text{ bounces take at least } n\tau @@ add; the hops only add more
n\tau \text{ passes every bound} @@ everyday-counting-passes (8e)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{endlessly many real bounces take endless time}} \quad \blacksquare @@ yet the ball rests in seconds: so it bounces only finitely often

Contact technique: bounces that each take at least a fixed time cannot be endlessly many in a few seconds. So the share law must fail at some small height. It surely fails once a hop lasts no longer than the contact itself. Below that the table’s shake, the ball’s squash and the air decide, and the ball ends up resting, or rolling.

Anexact, yet rigorous · laws with a known exception rate

The share law was measured from drops of about 30 cm. It is a law in the ladder’s sense: a measured regularity, with a range. Read past its range, it promises endlessly many bounces in under four seconds. Every one of those endlessly many bounces happens below the range. After twenty bounces from 30 cm the hops are under 2 mm high.

A law that promises endlessly many events in a moment has been read past its range.

So keep the law, and mark where it ends. From 30 cm, the first hop under a millimetre is the 22nd, and the time technique says that all the hops from there on take about 0.21 s together. Whatever happens down there, the law places the end of the buzz to within about a fifth of a second.

A basketball dropped from a third-floor window in Tacoma, with r ≈ 0.7: would you trust the time technique for its end, and to within how much?

The accepted names

The ratio of the speed after a bounce to the speed before it is the coefficient of restitution, e; the share of height is r = e². The time τ is the contact time. Endlessly many events in a finite time is Zeno behaviour, in the study of systems that switch; the bouncing ball is its standard example. A task made of endlessly many steps in a finite time is a supertask.

Who said it first

Isaac Newton measured the share of speed kept in collisions of balls hung as pendulums (Principia, 1687). The table tennis rule of a 23 cm bounce from 30 cm is in the sport’s international laws. Karl Johansson, Magnus Egerstedt, John Lygeros and Shankar Sastry named Zeno behaviour (1999). In 22·phys.6.5, James Thomson (1954) argued from the lamp that supertasks cannot be done; Paul Benacerraf (1962) answered that the story of the switching says nothing about the lamp at the end, so no clash follows.
