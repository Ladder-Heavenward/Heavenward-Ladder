41·crypt · cryptography

Can a secret be split so any three of five can open it?

Can a secret be split so any three of five can open it? A bank vault has one combination and five managers, and the rule is that any three of them together may open it, while no two can, and no one alone. Cutting the combination into five pieces fails at once: each piece gives away part of the secret, and the pieces have to be the right ones. There is a way that gives each manager one number, so that any three numbers rebuild the secret exactly and any two give away nothing at all, not one hint. It uses only what this rung built: a curve through points, read on a prime clock. Three points fix a curve that bends once, two points fit every such curve through every possible secret, and on a clock there are no sizes to give a hint. The same trick guards the keys that sign the root notes of the web.

Write the secret as the value at 0 of a curve f(x) = s + a₁x + a₂x² on a prime clock, with a₁ and a₂ chosen at random, and give the five people f(1) to f(5). Any three values fix f and so s. Any two fit exactly one curve through each possible secret, so they say nothing about s.

41crypt1One point for each person

Work on the clock of a prime p larger than the secret and than the number of people. The secret s is an hour. Pick two hours a₁ and a₂ at random, each of the p equally likely, and make the curve f(x) ≡ s + a₁x + a₂x². Then f(0) ≡ s. Person i is given the pair (i, f(i)), for i = 1 to 5, and the curve is thrown away.

On the clock of 101, with s = 57, a₁ = 13 and a₂ = 70, the five shares are f(1) = 39, f(2) = 60, f(3) = 19, f(4) = 17, f(5) = 54. For instance f(3) = 57 + 39 + 630 = 726 = 7 · 101 + 19.

Point-share technique: each share (i, f(i)) is a point of the one curve of degree at most 2 whose value at 0 is the secret, and the five points are at five different hours.

f(x) \equiv s + a_1x + a_2x^2 \pmod p @@ the dealer’s curve
f(0) \equiv s @@ the secret is where the curve meets x = 0
1, 2, 3, 4, 5 \text{ are different hours, none } 0 @@ p > 5
\htmlClass{new}{\text{shares } (i, f(i)),\ i = 1, \ldots, 5,\ \text{ all on } f} \quad \blacksquare @@ the curve is then thrown away

41crypt2Three points fix the curve

41e’s few-roots technique says a curve of degree d on a prime clock, not zero, has at most d roots. That gives the first half of the rule.

Three-points-fix technique: on a prime clock, two curves of degree at most 2 that agree at three different hours are the same curve; and for any three points at different hours there is one, built as a sum of three pieces.

f, g \text{ of degree} \le 2,\ f(x_k) \equiv g(x_k),\ k = 1, 2, 3 @@ two curves through the same three points
f - g \text{ has degree} \le 2 \text{ and three roots} @@ subtract
f - g \equiv 0 @@ the few-roots technique (41e): a nonzero curve of degree 2 has at most 2 roots
\ell_1(x) \equiv \tfrac{(x - x_2)(x - x_3)}{(x_1 - x_2)(x_1 - x_3)} \text{ is } 1 \text{ at } x_1,\ 0 \text{ at } x_2, x_3 @@ the bottom is a unit, since the hours differ (41b)
\htmlClass{new}{f \equiv y_1\ell_1 + y_2\ell_2 + y_3\ell_3 \text{ passes through all three}} \quad \blacksquare @@ ℓ₂, ℓ₃ the same way; and it is the only one

The division in ℓ₁ is the step that needs the clock to be prime. On the clock of 12, 2 has no undoing, and three points can fit two curves or none.

41crypt3Three people open it

The three holders need not rebuild the whole curve, only its value at 0, so they put x = 0 into each piece.

Rebuild-at-zero technique: holders of three shares at the hours x₁, x₂, x₃ get the secret as s ≡ y₁w₁ + y₂w₂ + y₃w₃, with w₁ ≡ x₂x₃ / ((x₁ − x₂)(x₁ − x₃)) and w₂, w₃ alike, weights that depend only on which hours came.

w_1 \equiv \tfrac{(0 - 3)(0 - 5)}{(1 - 3)(1 - 5)} = \tfrac{15}{8},\ \ w_3 \equiv \tfrac{(0 - 1)(0 - 5)}{(3 - 1)(3 - 5)} = -\tfrac54,\ \ w_5 \equiv \tfrac{(0 - 1)(0 - 3)}{(5 - 1)(5 - 3)} = \tfrac38 @@ ℓₖ(0), the three-points-fix technique
s \equiv 39 \cdot \tfrac{15}{8} - 19 \cdot \tfrac{5}{4} + 54 \cdot \tfrac38 = \tfrac{585 - 190 + 162}{8} = \tfrac{557}{8} @@ the three shares
557 \equiv 52,\ \ 8^{-1} \equiv 38 \pmod{101} @@ 8 · 38 = 304 = 3 · 101 + 1 (41b)
\htmlClass{new}{s \equiv 52 \cdot 38 = 1976 \equiv 57} \quad \blacksquare @@ the secret

Holders 2, 3 and 4 have the weights 6, 93 and 3 on the clock of 101, and 60 · 6 + 19 · 93 + 17 · 3 = 2178 ≡ 57 again. Any three of the five, ten groups in all, each have their weights, and each gets 57.

41crypt4Two people learn nothing

Two holders know two points. The question is what those tell them about s, and the answer is: not a thing. They are no better off than someone guessing an hour with no shares at all, one chance in p.

Two-say-nothing technique: for any two shares at different hours and any hour s′, exactly one curve of degree at most 2 passes through both shares and has the value s′ at 0; so every secret is equally likely given two shares.

(0, s'),\ (x_1, y_1),\ (x_2, y_2):\ \text{three points at different hours} @@ the shares’ hours are not 0
\text{exactly one curve of degree} \le 2 \text{ through them} @@ the three-points-fix technique
\text{the dealer’s choice } (a_1, a_2) \text{ is one of } p^2,\ \text{all equally likely} @@ how the curve was made
\text{for each } s', \text{ one } (a_1, a_2) \text{ gives these two shares} @@ the line above
\htmlClass{new}{P(s = s' \mid \text{two shares}) = 1/p \text{ for every } s'} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same count of dealings for every secret

Holders 1 and 2 have 39 and 60. If the secret were 0, the curve would be 48x + 92x²; if 57, the true 57 + 13x + 70x²; if 100, 100 + 100x + 41x². Each passes through (1, 39) and (2, 60). Cutting the secret into digits fails by comparison: holders of two of three digits have ruled out all but ten of a thousand combinations.

The basement · there if you want it

The accepted names

This is Shamir’s secret sharing, a threshold scheme with threshold 3. The pieces ℓₖ are the Lagrange basis polynomials, and the rebuild at zero is Lagrange interpolation at 0. The two-say-nothing technique says the scheme is perfect, or information-theoretically secure. The converse challenge is the bound that shares of a perfect scheme are at least as large as the secret. A split whose shares can be checked is verifiable secret sharing. Root keys of the web’s chains of trust and of domain name signing are held in this way.

Who said it first

Adi Shamir published the scheme in 1979 in a page and a half, and George Blakley gave another, with planes in place of curves, the same year. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the pieces in 1795, and Edward Waring had them in 1779. Paul Feldman (1987) and Torben Pedersen (1991) made shares that can be checked.

A proof that is not allowed

“Two shares give two conditions on three unknowns s, a₁, a₂, so s is not fixed, so two holders learn nothing.” Not allowed: not being fixed is not the same as learning nothing. Over the ordinary numbers s is not fixed either, yet sizes and remainders leak. The claim needs the count of the two-say-nothing technique: the same number of dealings for every secret.
