17k · technique

Partial fractions into logs

17i found that a quotient whose top is the slope of its bottom has a log for its antiderivative. Most quotients of polynomials are not of that shape. But when the bottom splits into different straight factors, the quotient splits into a sum of pieces, a number over each straight factor, and each piece is easy. 9g's two-factors did this for two factors. This step proves that the split exists for any number of them. It finds the A's by one quick tech, and integrates the result.

If the bottom is a product of different straight factors, and the top has lower degree, the quotient is a sum of numbers over those factors. Each number is the top at that factor’s root, over the bottom’s other factors at that root. Then the area is a sum of those numbers times logarithms. If the top’s degree is not lower, divide first.

17k1The split exists

Straight-factors technique: let c₁, …, cₙ be different reals, q(x) = (x − c₁)⋯(x − cₙ), and p a polynomial of degree less than n. Then for x different from every cᵢ, p(x)/q(x) = A₁/(x − c₁) + ⋯ + Aₙ/(x − cₙ), where Aᵢ = p(cᵢ)/qᵢ(cᵢ) and qᵢ is q with the factor (x − cᵢ) left out.

r(x) = p(x) - \textstyle\sum_i A_i\,q_i(x) @@ a polynomial of degree less than n
r(c_k) = p(c_k) - A_k q_k(c_k) = 0 @@ qᵢ(cₖ) = 0 for i ≠ k, since qᵢ keeps the factor (x − cₖ); then the choice of Aₖ
\begin{aligned}&r \text{ has } n \text{ different}\\ &\quad\text{roots, and degree less than } n\end{aligned} @@ each root cₖ gives a factor x − cₖ: difference-of-powers (13h) splits r(x) − r(aₖ)
r = 0 @@ a nonzero r would have n straight factors, so degree n at least
\htmlClass{new}{p(x) = \textstyle\sum_i A_i q_i(x)} @@ for every x
\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \textstyle\sum_i \tfrac{A_i}{x - c_i} \quad \blacksquare @@ divide by q(x) = (x − cᵢ)qᵢ(x), for x not a root

The formula for Aᵢ is a quick tech: cover the factor (x − cᵢ) in the bottom of p/q with a finger, and put x = cᵢ into what is left. Its accepted name is the cover-up rule; the split has the accepted name partial fractions.

17k3How quotients were first integrated

In 1702 one of the inventors of the calculus set out how quotients of polynomials might be integrated, by splitting their bottoms into factors. He claimed that x⁴ + c⁴ cannot be split into real factors of the second degree. So, he concluded, its integral calls for a new kind of quantity beyond logs.

Who said it first

Leibniz, setting out how quotients of polynomials might be integrated by splitting their bottoms into factors, claimed that x⁴ + c⁴ cannot be split into real factors of the second degree, and so concluded that its integral calls for a new kind of quantity beyond logs and the measures of circles.

Leibniz, in the Acta Eruditorum (1702); paraphrase

In 17k.4.4, the one who pointed out the factors in 1719 is Nicolaus I Bernoulli.
