Where Is the Middle of a List?

Where is the middle of a list? 4·stat could say which value of a list comes most often and how far apart its ends are. It could not say where the list's middle is, because the most natural middle, sharing the total out evenly, is a division, and divisions waited for rung 5. Now every division by a number other than 0 has an answer. This page finds two middles, the mean and the median, and asks when they disagree. Then it uses the mean to price a gamble. And it uses fractions to answer a question that most people, doctors included, get wrong: given that a test came back positive, what is the chance you have the disease?

The mean is the total shared out evenly: add the values and divide by how many there are. The median is the middle of the sorted list. The mean always lies between the least and the greatest value; one wild value can drag it, while the median stays put. The expected value of a gamble is the mean of its payouts over equally likely cases. The chance of one thing given another is the count of cases with both, over the count of cases with the second.

5stat1The Mean

From here on, for data x₀, …, x₍ₙ₋₁₎ of rationals with n ≠ 0, the mean is x̄ = (x₀ + ⋯ + x₍ₙ₋₁₎)/n, written with Σ as (Σᵢ xᵢ)/n. Its everyday name is the average. It is the one number that, put in every place instead of the values, keeps the total: n · x̄ = Σᵢ xᵢ.

Mean-between technique: the mean of data lies between its least value and its greatest, and equals either one only when all the values are equal.

\ell \le x_i \le g \text{ for each } i @@ ℓ least, g greatest (least-member, 4·stat's greatest-of-a-finite-set)
n\ell \le \textstyle\sum_i x_i \le ng @@ add n such inequalities, one place at a time (5d: adding keeps order), by induction on n
\ell \le \bar x \le g @@ divide by n > 0, which keeps order (5d)
\htmlClass{new}{\bar x = \ell \Rightarrow \text{every } x_i = \ell} \quad \blacksquare @@ if some xᵢ > ℓ, that place makes the sum strictly greater than nℓ; the same at g

Shift-and-stretch technique: if each value x is replaced by rx + b, for rationals r and b, the mean becomes r x̄ + b. Σᵢ(rxᵢ + b) = r Σᵢ xᵢ + nb, by 5d's rules and induction; divide by n. So the mean of temperatures in degrees Celsius, turned into Fahrenheit, is the Fahrenheit of the mean.

5stat2The Median

Sorting technique: for every list of rationals there is a list with the same tally for every value whose values never go down: y₀ ≤ y₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ y₍ₙ₋₁₎. There is only one such list. By induction on n: put first a place holding the least value, then the rest of the list sorted. Two sorted lists with the same tallies agree place by place, by comparing their first places and then the rest. Its accepted name is the list sorted in order.

From here on, the median of data with sorted list y is y₍ₘ₎ when n = 2m + 1, and (y₍ₘ₋₁₎ + y₍ₘ₎)/2 when n = 2m with m ≥ 1. Median-halves technique: at least half of the values are at most the median, and at least half are at least it. In the odd case, y₀ to y₍ₘ₎ are m + 1 values, more than n/2. In the even case, y₀ to y₍ₘ₋₁₎ are m values, at most the median, since the median lies between y₍ₘ₋₁₎ and y₍ₘ₎ (5f).

The median of the lower half of the sorted list, leaving out the middle value when n is odd, is the first quartile Q₁. The median of the upper half is the third quartile Q₃, and the median itself is Q₂. Other books split the halves in slightly other ways, and get slightly other quartiles; this ladder uses this one. The least value, Q₁, the median, Q₃ and the greatest value have the accepted name the five-number summary, and Q₃ − Q₁ is the interquartile range, or IQR. A statistician in the 1970s drew the five numbers as a box from Q₁ to Q₃, with a line at the median and whiskers out to the ends: the box plot. He flagged a value more than 1.5 · IQR beyond the box as an outlier.

80, 95, 102, 110, 118, 125, 131, 140, 160 @@ nine winter power bills in Redding, in dollars, sorted
\text{median } 118;\ \ Q_1 = \tfrac{95 + 102}{2} = 98.5,\ \ Q_3 = \tfrac{131 + 140}{2} = 135.5 @@ m = 4; the halves leave out y₄
\bar x = \tfrac{1061}{9} \approx 117.9 @@ close to the median: the bills are spread evenly
160 \to 520:\ \ \bar x = \tfrac{1421}{9} \approx 157.9,\ \ \text{median } 118 @@ one bill with a hot tub drags the mean above eight of the nine; IQR 37, and 520 > 135.5 + 55.5: an outlier
Anexact, yet rigorous · two sizes of truth

2b set two sizes of truth beside every and some, and 2d met them again: a set is its members, and a member is not the set. The mean lives at the size of the crowd. The nine bills average about $117.90, and no household paid that. Mean-between says the mean sits among the values, not that it is one of them.

Say which size your statement lives at: the average household is a fact about the crowd, not about any household in it.

The median is a crowd-statement too, of another kind. One bill of $520 drags the mean above eight of the nine, and leaves the median at $118. The two numbers answer two questions about one crowd, and the hot tub shows which question each one answers. 4·stat's tally table kept the crowd's shape; these numbers each keep one fact about it.

A town's mean household income goes up when one very rich family moves in. Did anyone in the town get richer?

5stat3The Price of a Gamble

From here on, when n cases are taken to be equally likely and k of them give an event A, the chance of A is the fraction k/n, written P(A). This is 4·stat's chance, k of n, now written as a fraction (5c). It lies between 0 and 1, since 0 ≤ k ≤ n.

From here on, suppose n cases are taken as equally likely, and each case c brings a payout X(c), a rational. Then the expected value of X, written E X, is the mean of the payouts over the n cases. It is what the gamble is worth on average, case for case.

Expected-values-add technique: E(X + Y) = E X + E Y, and E(rX + b) = r E X + b. Grouping the cases by payout, E X is the sum over the payouts v of v times the chance that X = v. The first two are shift-and-stretch and the rule that a sum of sums can be added in either order (5d, by induction). For the third, the cases with X = v form a pile of size k, contributing kv to the total, and kv/n = v · (k/n).

\text{one die: } \tfrac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = \tfrac72 @@ the mean over 6 cases
\text{two dice, the total: } \tfrac72 + \tfrac72 = 7 @@ expected-values-add, without listing the 36 cases
\text{pay } 1,\ \text{win } 5 \text{ on a total of } 7 @@ chance 6/36 = 1/6: E = 5 · 1/6 − 1 = −1/6 per play

5stat4Chance Given That

From here on, take events A and B among equally likely cases, with B holding at least one case. The chance of A given B, written P(A | B), is the number of cases in both A and B over the number in B. It is the chance of A when the cases are cut down to those in B.

Chance-product technique: P(A and B) = P(B) · P(A | B). With k cases in both, j in B and n in all, k/n = (j/n)(k/j), by 5c's rules. Turned-chance technique: if A and B each hold a case, P(B | A) = P(A | B) · P(B) / P(A). Chance-product twice, with A and B in each order, gives P(A) · P(B | A) = P(B) · P(A | B), and P(A) ≠ 0. The accepted name for the chance of A given B is a conditional probability: a chance under a condition.

\begin{aligned}&1000 \text{ tanoaks; } 20 \text{ infected}\\ &\quad\text{with sudden oak death}\end{aligned} @@ in a stand in Marin, say
\begin{aligned}&\text{the test flags } 18 \text{ of the } 20,\\ &\quad \ \text{and } 49 \text{ of the } 980 \text{ healthy}\end{aligned} @@ it catches 9 in 10, and wrongly flags 1 healthy tree in 20
P(\text{infected} \mid \text{flagged}) = \tfrac{18}{18 + 49} = \tfrac{18}{67} \approx 0.27 @@ chance-product's count; turned-chance gives the same: (9/10)(20/1000)/(67/1000)

So a flagged tree is still more likely healthy than not. Most people asked guess nine in ten, because they hear how often the test catches the disease and forget how many healthy trees there are to flag.

Who said it first

The turned-chance technique is from Thomas Bayes’s essay, published in 1763, after his death, by Richard Price, in a form that historians have read as an answer to David Hume; Price's own example was whether the sun will rise again. The box plot is John Tukey’s, from 1977.

Accepted names

The turned-chance technique is Bayes’ theorem, or Bayes’ rule. The chance of A given B is a conditional probability.
