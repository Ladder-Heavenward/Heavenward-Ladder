44e · proof

Where Factoring Fails

Add one new number to the whole numbers, a square root of minus five, and keep everything you can make from it by adding and multiplying. The result is a ring with no zero products, sitting inside the complex numbers of rung twenty five. In it, six is two times three, and six is also one plus the root times one minus the root. All four of those factors are unsplittable. None of them is a partner of another. So the one-factoring technique, which held for the whole numbers and for polynomials over a field, fails here, and the reason is that this ring has an ideal with no single maker. That looks like the end of factoring. It is not. Two of the ideals with no single maker, multiplied together, give the multiples of two, and the other factorings of six are regroupings of one product of four such ideals. In the right ring, factoring comes back, one level up: not for members, but for ideals. The first people to see this called the missing makers ideal numbers.

In the whole numbers with a root of minus five put in, six has two factorings into unsplittables that are not partners. Two is unsplittable there and not prime, and the ideal made by two and one plus the root has no one maker. Ideals multiply, and six’s ideal is one product of four prime ideals, of which both factorings are regroupings.

44e1A Ring With a Root of Minus Five

Write ω for i√5, so ω² = −5. The members a + bω with a, b whole form a ring inside ℂ, written ℤ[ω]: sums and products of such numbers are such numbers, since (a + bω)(c + dω) = (ac − 5bd) + (ad + bc)ω. It has no zero products, because ℂ has none.

Its size is N(a + bω) = a² + 5b², the square of 25c’s size |a + bω|.

Whole-size technique: N(wz) = N(w)N(z) for w, z in ℤ[ω]; N(w) = 1 exactly when w is undoable; and the undoable members are 1 and −1.

N(wz) = |wz|^2 = |w|^2 |z|^2 = N(w)N(z) @@ the size of a product (25c)
wz = 1 \Rightarrow N(w)N(z) = 1 \Rightarrow N(w) = 1 @@ sizes are whole numbers, never negative
a^2 + 5b^2 = 1 \Rightarrow b = 0,\ a = \pm 1 @@ 5b² is 0 or at least 5
\htmlClass{new}{\text{the undoable members are } \pm 1} \quad \blacksquare @@ and N(±1) = 1

Small sizes: a² + 5b² takes the values 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9, … and never 2 or 3. That gap does all the work of the next footing.

44e2Six, Two Ways

Two-factorings technique: in ℤ[ω], 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + ω)(1 − ω), all four factors are unsplittable, and no two of them are partners.

(1 + \omega)(1 - \omega) = 1 - \omega^2 = 1 + 5 = 6 @@ the difference of squares (5i), ω² = −5
N(2) = 4,\ N(3) = 9,\ N(1 \pm \omega) = 6 @@ a² + 5b²
2 = wz,\ \text{neither undoable} \Rightarrow N(w) = N(z) = 2 @@ sizes multiply, and size 1 means undoable
\text{no member has size } 2 \text{ or } 3 @@ the gap in a² + 5b²
2,\ 3,\ 1 \pm \omega \text{ are unsplittable} @@ the same for 3 (sizes 3 and 3) and for 1 ± ω (sizes 2 and 3)
\htmlClass{new}{\text{partners differ by } \pm 1 \text{, so have equal sizes; } 4, 9, 6 \text{ differ, and } 1 + \omega \ne \pm(1 - \omega)} \quad \blacksquare @@ the whole-size technique

So 2 is unsplittable and is not prime: 2 divides (1 + ω)(1 − ω) = 6, and 2 divides neither factor, since (1 ± ω)/2 is not in ℤ[ω]. By the unsplittable-is-prime technique, ℤ[ω] is not a principal ring. The ideal with no one maker is P = (2, 1 + ω): a maker g would divide 2 and 1 + ω, so N(g) divides 4 and 6, so N(g) is 1 or 2. Size 2 does not exist, so g = ±1 and P would be the whole ring. But every member 2u + (1 + ω)v has a + b even, and 1 does not.

44e3Ideals Multiply

The product IJ of two ideals is the set of sums of products ij with i in I and j in J. It is an ideal, and (g)(h) = (gh). Take P = (2, 1 + ω), Q = (3, 1 + ω) and Q′ = (3, 1 − ω).

Ideal-product technique: in ℤ[ω], P² = (2), QQ′ = (3), PQ = (1 + ω) and PQ′ = (1 − ω).

P^2 = (4,\ 2 + 2\omega,\ (1 + \omega)^2) = (4,\ 2 + 2\omega,\ -4 + 2\omega) @@ the products of the makers; (1 + ω)² = 1 + 2ω − 5
\phantom{P^2} = (2)\,(2,\ 1 + \omega,\ -2 + \omega) @@ each maker is 2 times something
(1 + \omega) - (-2 + \omega) = 3,\ \ 3 - 2 = 1 @@ inside the second ideal
\htmlClass{new}{P^2 = (2)(1) = (2)} \quad \blacksquare @@ an ideal holding 1 is the whole ring (the undoable-fills technique)

The other three go the same way. QQ′ = (9, 3 − 3ω, 3 + 3ω, 6) holds 9 − 6 = 3 and is made by 3. PQ = (6, 2 + 2ω, 3 + 3ω, −4 + 2ω) holds (3 + 3ω) − (2 + 2ω) = 1 + ω, and each maker is a multiple of 1 + ω.

(6) = (2)(3) = P^2 \cdot QQ' @@ the ideal-product technique
(6) = (1 + \omega)(1 - \omega) = PQ \cdot PQ' @@ the ideal-product technique
\htmlClass{new}{(6) = P \cdot P \cdot Q \cdot Q'} \quad \blacksquare @@ the same four ideals, grouped two ways

44e4Prime Ideals Restore It

P, Q and Q′ are prime ideals. For P: every member of ℤ[ω] is a + bω ∼ a + b on dividing by P, since ω ∼ −1 there (1 + ω is in P), and 2 ∼ 0. So ℤ[ω]/P has two members, it is ℤ/2, a field, and P is largest, so prime (44b4). In the same way ℤ[ω]/Q and ℤ[ω]/Q′ are ℤ/3.

Ideal-factoring technique: in ℤ[ω], every ideal other than {0} and the whole ring is a product of prime ideals in exactly one way, up to order. Held in trust. The proof needs more about these rings than the ladder has built, and is much higher up the ladder. The lines below check one case: they show that no regrouping of P, Q, Q′ can make (6) another way.

P,\ Q,\ Q' \text{ have no one maker} @@ a maker of Q would have size dividing 9 and 6, so 3, as a maker of P would have size 2 (44e2); none exist
P^2 = (2),\ \ PQ = (1 + \omega) @@ the ideal-product technique
\text{call two ideals alike when } (g)I = (h)J \text{ for some members } g, h \ne 0 @@ alike up to a made-by-one factor
\htmlClass{new}{\text{every ideal is alike to } (1) \text{ or to } P:\ \text{two kinds}} \quad \blacksquare @@ held in trust beyond these: P ∼ Q ∼ Q′, and P² ∼ (1)

The kinds of ideals multiply like a group of two members: the made-by-one kind and P’s kind. A ring is principal exactly when there is one kind. The ring ℤ[i], one step from here, has one kind, and the whole numbers with a root of minus twenty three put in, (1 + √−23)/2 among them, have three.

The cellar · there if you want it

The accepted names

ℤ[√−5] is the ring of integers of the field ℚ(√−5). The size N is the norm. The ideal-factoring technique says ℤ[√−5] is a Dedekind domain; the kinds of ideal form the ideal class group, and its size, here 2, is the class number. ℤ[i] is the ring of Gaussian integers, and it is a Euclidean domain. The class number of ℚ(√−23) is 3.

Who said it first

Ernst Kummer found in 1844 that factoring into unsplittables fails in the rings of the powers of ζ, and in 1847 brought in ideal numbers to restore it. When Gabriel Lamé announced a proof of Fermat’s claim to the Paris Academy on 1 March 1847, Joseph Liouville objected at once that it assumed one factoring, and in May Kummer wrote that he had shown three years earlier that the assumption is false; it first fails for p = 23. Richard Dedekind replaced ideal numbers by ideals in 1871 and proved the ideal-factoring technique for every ring of algebraic integers. Carl Friedrich Gauss studied ℤ[i] in 1832 and the forms a² + 5b² in 1801.

A proof that is not allowed

“6 = 2 · 3 and 6 = (1 + ω)(1 − ω). Since 2 is unsplittable, it must divide 1 + ω or 1 − ω.” Not allowed: “unsplittable, so divides a factor” is the unsplittable-is-prime technique, which needs a principal ring. ℤ[ω] is not one, and 2 divides neither.
