25b · proof

A Field With No Order

The reals obey twelve techniques: nine about adding and multiplying, and three about which numbers are positive. The new numbers keep the first nine. Can they keep the other three? Could some choice of which complex numbers count as positive make the old rules of order hold? This step shows that no choice can. The new numbers pay for their roots with their order. There is no larger and smaller among them, only larger and smaller sizes.

No choice of positives in the new numbers obeys the three order techniques. They form a field, but not a regular number system.

25b1What an Order Would Need

5d1 listed the twelve techniques of arithmetic. Nine are about adding and multiplying, and 9·cb8.3 proved ℂ obeys all nine: ℂ is a field. The other three are about a set of numbers called positive:

Three-way: every number is 0, positive, or the negative of a positive, and exactly one. Positive-sum: positive plus positive is positive. Positive-product: positive times positive is positive.

From here on, an order on a field is a choice of which members are positive that obeys the three-way, positive-sum and positive-product techniques. Then w < z means z − w is positive. Accepted name: an ordered field; the ladder calls a field with an order a regular number system.

x \ne 0,\ x \text{ positive} \Rightarrow x \cdot x \text{ positive} @@ positive-product
x \ne 0,\ x = -p,\ p \text{ positive} \Rightarrow x\cdot x = (-p)(-p) = p\cdot p \text{ positive} @@ two negatives (5e); positive-product
\htmlClass{new}{x \ne 0 \Rightarrow x^2 \text{ positive}} \quad \blacksquare @@ the three-way technique leaves only these two cases

That is the whole engine of the next footing. In any field with an order, every square other than 0 is positive. 8g used it on the line as square-never-negative.

25b2No Order on ℂ

No-order technique: no choice of positives in ℂ obeys the three order techniques. Whatever you call positive, i or its negative is positive, and either way its square, −1, would be positive.

\text{suppose an order on } \mathbb{C} @@ for contradiction
i \ne 0 @@ i = (0, 1) ≠ (0, 0)
i^2 = -1 \text{ is positive} @@ 25b1: a square other than 0 is positive
1 = 1^2 \text{ is positive} @@ 25b1 again, with x = 1
1 \text{ positive and } 1 \text{ the negative of the positive } -1 @@ −(−1) = 1 (5e)
\text{contradiction} @@ three-way: exactly one of the three
\htmlClass{new}{\text{no order on } \mathbb{C}} \quad \blacksquare

Notice what the proof used: only that i² = −1. Any field with a number whose square is −1 has no order. To gain a square root of −1, a number system must give up larger and smaller. The trade is forced.

25b3What Is Left to Compare

Size-compare technique: sizes are reals, so they compare: |z| < |w| is a claim about two reals, and every inequality in ℂ is an inequality between reals.

|z|,\ |w| \in \mathbb{R},\ |z| \ge 0 @@ the size is a square root of a sum of squares (25a1)
|z| = 0 \iff z = 0 @@ a² + b² = 0 only when a = b = 0 (8g)
\text{floor parts, upright parts, sizes} \in \mathbb{R} @@ each compares by 8g
\htmlClass{new}{z < w \text{ is not a claim in } \mathbb{C};\ |z| < |w| \text{ is}} \quad \blacksquare @@ 25b2

So in ℂ you may write |z − 1| < 0.01, or that the floor part of z is positive. You may never write z < w, unless both are on the floor, where 8g’s order is the old one. Sizes do the work that order did: 25c makes them as useful as absolute values on the line.

The accepted names

A field with an order obeying these techniques is an ordered field. The no-order technique is usually stated as “ℂ cannot be made into an ordered field.” The size-compare technique is why analysis in ℂ is done with |z − w| in place of order.

Who said it first

The proof is folklore, older than any one book. The point that a field containing a square root of −1 admits no order belongs to the theory of ordered fields built by Emil Artin and Otto Schreier in 1927: a field has an order exactly when −1 is not a sum of squares.
